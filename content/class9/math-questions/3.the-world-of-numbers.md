<!-- ===== Counting ===== -->

What is one-to-one correspondence, and how did it give rise to numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Matching one object to another, one for one.**
>
> A herder with no number words could **drop a pebble into a pot for each cow leaving and remove one for each cow returning**. An empty pot at dusk meant nothing was lost.
>
> **That act of matching is the birth of the natural numbers ℕ = {1, 2, 3, …}.**

---

What are the Lebombo and Ishango bones, and why do they matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Lebombo Bone** (~35,000 years old) — **29 deliberate, uniformly sized notches**, probably a **lunar phase counter**.
> - **Ishango Bone** (~20,000 BCE) — one column groups notches as **11, 13, 17, 19** (the **primes** between 10 and 20), another shows **doubling**.
>
> **They show the abstract idea of "number" is tens of thousands of years old** — long before writing or cities.

---

What do 11, 13, 17 and 19 have in common? Give the next three of that kind.

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are all prime numbers.**
>
> The next three are **23, 29, 31**.

---

How large were the numbers named in ancient Indian texts?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **Vedas** name every power of 10 up to **10¹²**, called **parārdha**.
>
> The **Lalitavistara** (4th century BCE) has Buddha naming powers up to **10⁵³**, called **tallakṣhaṇa**.

---

A Lothal merchant gets 15 copper ingots for every 2 bags of spices. How many for 12 bags?

> [!ANSWER]
> `12 ÷ 2 = 6` lots of 2 bags, so `15 × 6 =` **90 ingots**.

---

Are natural numbers closed under subtraction?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> `5 − 3 = 2` ✓ is natural, but `3 − 5 = −2` **is not a natural number**.
>
> **That very gap is why integers had to be invented.**

---

<!-- ===== Zero ===== -->

Babylonians and Mayans used placeholders for zero. Why isn't that the same as having the number zero?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **placeholder** only marks an **empty column** in a written numeral.
>
> **It was not treated as a NUMBER you could add, subtract and multiply.** Zero becomes a number only when it has **rules of arithmetic**.

---

What is Śhūnyatā, and how did it help India arrive at zero?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Śhūnyatā** means **emptiness or nothingness** — in the Upanishads and Buddhist literature it was the goal of yoga and meditation, emptying the mind of all **vṛttis** (fluctuations) to reach perfect stillness. **Patanjali's Yoga Sutras** describe it too.
>
> **Because Indian thinkers already revered this state of emptiness, they had the conceptual framework to welcome "nothingness" as a thing in its own right** — and the idea eventually crystallised into the **mathematical zero**.

---

What is the Bakhśhālī Manuscript, and what does it show?

> [!ANSWER]
> A manuscript dated to the **early centuries CE** which features a bold **dot (bindu)** used to represent **zero**.
>
> It shows the physical transition **from a blank space to a symbol**.

---

How did Brahmagupta define zero, and what rules did he give?

> [!ANSWER]
> In the **Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE)** he defined zero as **the result of subtracting a number from itself: `a − a = 0`**.
>
> His rules: **`a + 0 = a`**, **`a − 0 = a`**, **`a × 0 = 0`**.

<!-- ===== Integers ===== -->

What question led Brahmagupta to negative numbers, and how did he describe them?

> [!ANSWER]
> If `5 − 5 = 0`, **what is `3 − 5`?**
>
> He grounded the answer in commerce:
> - **Fortunes (Dhana)** — positive numbers, wealth
> - **Debts (Ṛiṇa)** — negative numbers, what you owe

---

What are the integers, and what does ℤ stand for?

> [!ANSWER]
> **All natural numbers, their negatives, and zero.**
>
> **ℤ** comes from the German **Zahlen**, meaning "numbers".

---

Explain why a negative times a negative is positive, using debt.

> [!ANSWER]
> A negative number is a **debt**; multiplying by a negative means **REMOVING** those debts.
>
> **If someone takes away four of your ₹3 debts, you are ₹12 richer** — so `(−3) × (−4) = +12`.

---

Why is `10 − (−5) = 15`? Explain with money.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Cancelling a debt makes you richer.**
>
> If you have ₹10 and a ₹5 debt is **wiped out**, you are ₹5 better off — exactly as if you'd been **given** ₹5.
>
> **So subtracting a negative is adding a positive.**

---

Ladakh is 4 °C at noon and drops 15 °C by midnight. What is the midnight temperature?

> [!ANSWER]
> `4 − 15 =` **−11 °C**

---

A trader takes a loan of ₹850, makes ₹1,200 profit, then loses ₹450. Write the equation and find his standing.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(−850) + 1200 + (−450) =` **−₹100**
>
> He ends **₹100 in debt**.

---

Calculate: (−12) × 5, (−8) × (−7), 0 − (−14), (−20) ÷ 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `(−12) × 5 =` **−60** (debt × fortune = debt)
> - `(−8) × (−7) =` **56** (debt × debt = fortune)
> - `0 − (−14) =` **14** (removing a debt)
> - `(−20) ÷ 4 =` **−5**

<!-- ===== Rationals ===== -->

Define a rational number, and say what ℚ stands for.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Any number expressible as `p/q` where p and q are INTEGERS and `q ≠ 0`.**
>
> **ℚ** stands for **quotient**.

---

Why must `q ≠ 0` in the definition?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **division by zero is undefined** — there is no number that, multiplied by 0, gives a non-zero result.

---

Show that every integer is a rational number.

> [!ANSWER]
> Any integer n can be written as **`n/1`**, which is `p/q` with `q = 1 ≠ 0`.
>
> `5 = 5/1` · `−10 = −10/1`
>
> **So natural numbers, whole numbers and integers are all rational.**

---

Is the fraction form of a rational number unique?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** `−1/3 = −2/6 = −3/9 = −10/30 = …` — these are **equivalent fractions**.
>
> **That's exactly why you can cancel a common factor:** `12/30 = 2/5` after dividing both by 6.
>
> **By convention we use the lowest terms**, with p and q **co-prime**.

---

Where can the minus sign go in a negative fraction?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Anywhere:** `−1/5 = (−1)/5 = 1/(−5)` — all three are the same number.

---

State the rule for when two rational numbers are equal, and use it on 3/5 and 6/10.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`a/b = c/d` exactly when `ad = bc`.**
>
> For `−3/5` and `−6/10`: `(−3) × 10 = −30` and `5 × (−6) = −30` — **equal** ✓

---

Find `2/5 + 3/10` and `7/12 + 5/8`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `2/5 + 3/10 = 4/10 + 3/10 =` **7/10**
> - `7/12 + 5/8 = 14/24 + 15/24 =` **29/24**
>
> **The method: make the denominators the same first** (using the LCM), then add the numerators.

---

Find `5/6 − 1/4` and `−7/9 − (−2/3)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `5/6 − 1/4 = 10/12 − 3/12 =` **7/12**
> - `−7/9 + 2/3 = −7/9 + 6/9 =` **−1/9** (subtracting a negative adds)

---

Find `2/3 × 3/10` and `−4/7 × 5/14`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `2/3 × 3/10 = 6/30 =` **1/5**
> - `−4/7 × 5/14 = −20/98 =` **−10/49**

---

Find `2/3 ÷ 3/10` and `−4/7 ÷ 5/14`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Division means multiplying by the reciprocal.**
>
> - `2/3 ÷ 3/10 = 2/3 × 10/3 =` **20/9**
> - `−4/7 ÷ 5/14 = −4/7 × 14/5 = −56/35 =` **−8/5**

---

Which operations are rational numbers closed under?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Addition, subtraction and multiplication — always.**
>
> **And division too, provided you never divide by zero.**
>
> "Closed" means combining two rationals always produces another rational.

---

Verify the distributive law using `(1/2 + 3/4) × 8/3`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Left side:** `(1/2 + 3/4) × 8/3 = (2/4 + 3/4) × 8/3 = 5/4 × 8/3 = 40/12 = 10/3`
>
> **Right side:** `1/2 × 8/3 + 3/4 × 8/3 = 8/6 + 24/12 = 4/3 + 2 = 4/3 + 6/3 = 10/3`
>
> **Equal** ✓ — so `p(q + r) = pq + pr` holds for rationals.

---

Find x such that `(5/6)x + 3/5 = (5/6)x + 1/2`. What goes wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> Subtracting `(5/6)x` from both sides leaves **`3/5 = 1/2`**, which is **false**.
>
> **So there is NO value of x that works** — the equation has no solution. The x cancels completely, leaving a false statement.

<!-- ===== Number line and absolute value ===== -->

How do you place `p/q` on the number line?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Divide each unit interval into q equal parts, then move p parts from 0** — right if positive, left if negative.
>
> For `3/4`: divide 0 to 1 into four parts, move three to the right.

---

Where does 9/4 lie, and how do you mark it?

> [!ANSWER]
> `9/4 = 2¼`, so it lies **between 2 and 3**.
>
> Divide the interval from 2 to 3 into **four** equal parts and move **one** part right of 2.

---

Define absolute value, and evaluate |5/3|, |−5/3| and |0|.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`|x|` is the distance of x from 0 on the number line.**
>
> - `|5/3| =` **5/3**
> - `|−5/3| =` **5/3**
> - `|0| =` **0**
>
> **`|x| ≥ 0` always — a distance is never negative.**

---

How do you find the distance between two numbers on the number line?

> [!ANSWER]
> **`|a − b|`.**
>
> From −4 to 3: `|−4 − 3| = |−7| =` **7** ✓

<!-- ===== Density ===== -->

What does it mean to say rational numbers are dense?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Between any two rational numbers there is another rational number** — and therefore **infinitely many**.
>
> No matter how close two rationals are, you can always squeeze one in between.

---

How do you find a rational number between two given ones? Prove it always works.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Take their AVERAGE: `(a + b)/2`.**
>
> **Why it's rational:** it's a sum and a division of rationals, and rationals are **closed** under both.
>
> **Why it lies between them:** the average of two different numbers is always strictly between them.
>
> Between 1 and 3/2: `(1 + 3/2)/2 =` **5/4** ✓

---

Find three rational numbers strictly between −1/2 and 1/4.

> [!ANSWER]
> Convert to a common denominator: `−4/8` and `2/8`.
>
> **`−3/8`, `−1/8`, `1/8`** all lie between them (and there are infinitely many more).

---

Find six rational numbers between 3 and 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> Write them with denominator 7: `3 = 21/7` and `4 = 28/7`.
>
> **`22/7, 23/7, 24/7, 25/7, 26/7, 27/7`**
>
> **The trick: choose a denominator big enough to leave room.**

---

Find three rational numbers between 3.1415 and 3.1416.

> [!ANSWER]
> Add another decimal place: **3.14151, 3.14153, 3.14158** — all terminating decimals, hence rational.

---

A tailor has 15¾ m of silk and each kurta needs 2¼ m. How many kurtas?

> [!ANSWER]
> `15¾ ÷ 2¼ = 63/4 ÷ 9/4 = 63/4 × 4/9 =` **7 kurtas** exactly, with nothing left over.

<!-- ===== Irrationals ===== -->

Why is the diagonal of a unit square not a rational number?

> [!ANSWER]
> By the **Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem**, `1² + 1² = d²`, so `d² = 2` and **`d = √2`**.
>
> And **√2 cannot be written as a ratio of integers** — it is **irrational**.

---

Who first met such lengths, and when?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Baudhāyana**, around **800 BCE**, while composing his **Śhulbasūtra** — a manual for constructing geometric fire altars.
>
> The **Greeks** hit the same crisis a few centuries later.

---

Give the full proof that √2 is irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> **By contradiction:**
>
> ```
> 1  Assume √2 = p/q in SIMPLEST form (p, q co-prime, q ≠ 0)
> 2  Square:            2 = p²/q²
> 3  Multiply by q²:    2q² = p²
> 4  p² is 2 × an integer → p² is EVEN → p is EVEN.  Let p = 2k
> 5  Substitute:        2q² = 4k²
> 6  Divide by 2:       q² = 2k²
> 7  So q² is EVEN → q is EVEN
> 8  CONTRADICTION: p and q are BOTH even, sharing a factor of 2 —
>    but we said the fraction was in simplest form
> ```
>
> **The logic is flawless, so the assumption must be wrong. √2 is irrational.** ∎

---

Step 4 says "if p² is even then p is even". Why is that true?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **an odd number squared is always odd**: `(2n+1)² = 4n² + 4n + 1`, which is one more than an even number.
>
> So if the square is **even**, the original number cannot have been odd.

---

What is proof by contradiction?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You assume the OPPOSITE of what you want to prove, and show that assumption leads to a logical disaster.**
>
> Since the reasoning is valid, the only thing that can be wrong is the **assumption** — so the opposite must be true.

---

Does the same proof work for √3, √5 and √7?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes** — with 3, 5 or 7 in place of 2 throughout.
>
> It works for **the square root of any number that is not a perfect square**. It fails for √4, because 4 *is* a perfect square and √4 = 2 is rational.

---

Describe how to construct √2 on the number line with ruler and compass.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1  Mark OA = 1 unit on the number line; draw a perpendicular at A
> 2  Mark B on it with AB = 1 unit; join OB.  By Pythagoras, OB = √2
> 3  With centre O and radius OB, draw an arc cutting the line at P
>    Then OP = √2, so P represents √2
> ```
>
> Repeating from there gives **√3, √5, and in general √n** for any positive integer n.

---

What did Āryabhaṭa say about π, and what did Mādhava add?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Āryabhaṭa** (499 CE) gave the very accurate `3927/1250 = 3.1416`, but called it an **āsanna (approximation)** — indicating an exact fraction likely could not be found. **Lambert proved π irrational in 1761.**
>
> **Mādhava of Sangamagrama** (14th century), founder of the **Kerala School**, realised that to express an irrational number you need an **INFINITE SUM**, not a single fraction:
>
> **`π = 4 × (1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …)`**

---

What does it mean to "add infinitely many terms"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Finding the value you get closer and closer to** as you add more and more terms of the sum, starting from the first.
>
> You never finish adding — but the running total **settles towards** a definite value.

<!-- ===== Decimals ===== -->

What are the real numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Rational numbers together with irrational numbers — written ℝ.**
>
> They fill the number line completely, with no gaps.

---

What two things can happen to the decimal expansion of a rational number?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **It TERMINATES** — the remainder eventually becomes 0. `3/8 = 0.375`
> 2. **It REPEATS** — the digits loop forever. `5/11 = 0.454545…`
>
> **There is no third possibility for a rational number.**

---

Why must the decimal of a rational number eventually repeat if it doesn't terminate?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because there are only **finitely many possible remainders**.
>
> Dividing by 7, the remainders can only be **1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6** (0 would mean it terminated). **With only 6 options, one must eventually recur — and once a remainder repeats, the whole division loops.**

---

State the rule for predicting whether `p/q` terminates, and explain it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **For `p/q` in lowest terms, the decimal terminates exactly when the prime factors of q are ONLY 2s, ONLY 5s, or both.**
>
> **Why:** in that case you can multiply numerator and denominator to turn the denominator into a **power of 10**.
>
> `3/20`: `20 = 2² × 5`, so `3/20 = 15/100 =` 0.15 ✓

---

Without dividing, say which of 7/20, 4/15 and 13/250 terminate.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `7/20` — `20 = 2² × 5` → **terminates** (0.35)
> - `4/15` — `15 = 3 × 5`, and the **3** spoils it → **repeats** (0.2̄6̄)
> - `13/250` — `250 = 2 × 5³` → **terminates** (0.052)

---

Convert 0.35 to a fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> `0.35 = 35/100 =` **7/20** (dividing both by 5).

---

Convert 0.6̄ (0.666…) to a fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Let x = 0.6̄
> 1 digit repeats → ×10:   10x = 6.6̄
> Subtract:                 9x = 6
>                            x = 6/9 = 2/3
> ```

---

Convert 0.4̄5̄ (0.454545…) to a fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Let x = 0.4̄5̄
> 2 digits repeat → ×100:  100x = 45.4̄5̄
> Subtract:                 99x = 45
>                            x = 45/99 = 5/11
> ```

---

Convert 0.16̄ (0.1666…) to a fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Let x = 0.16̄            ('1' non-repeating, '6' repeating)
> ×10 (1 non-repeating digit):   10x = 1.6̄
> ×10 again (1 repeating digit): 100x = 16.6̄
> Subtract:                       90x = 15
>                                   x = 15/90 = 1/6
> ```

---

Convert 2.357̄ to a fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Let x = 2.357̄          ('35' non-repeating, '7' repeating)
> ×10² :   100x = 235.7̄
> ×10¹ :  1000x = 2357.7̄
> Subtract: 900x = 2122
>              x = 2122/900 = 1061/450
> ```

---

Give the two-line recipe for converting any repeating decimal.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Pure repeating** — multiply by `10ⁿ` where **n = number of repeating digits**, subtract, solve.
> - **General repeating** — multiply by `10ᵐ` where **m = number of non-repeating digits** to clear them, then by `10ⁿ` again, subtract, solve.

---

What is a cyclic number? Show it for 1/7.

> [!ANSWER]
> `1/7 = 0.142857142857…` The block **142857** multiplied by 1 to 6 gives:
>
> ```
> ×1 = 142857    ×4 = 571428
> ×2 = 285714    ×5 = 714285
> ×3 = 428571    ×6 = 857142
> ```
>
> **The same six digits, simply rotated** — a **cyclic number**.

---

Classify as rational or irrational: √81, √12, 0.3333…, 0.123451234512345…, 1.01001000100001…

> [!ANSWER]
> - **√81 = 9** → **rational** (a perfect square)
> - **√12** → **irrational** (12 isn't a perfect square)
> - **0.3333… = 1/3** → **rational** (repeating)
> - **0.123451234512345…** → **rational**, block `12345` repeats → `12345/99999`
> - **1.01001000100001…** → **irrational** — it has a *pattern* but **no single block that repeats**

---

Why is 1.01001000100001… irrational when it clearly has a pattern?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **a pattern is not the same as a repeating block**.
>
> The gaps between the 1s keep **growing** (one zero, then two, then three…), so no fixed block ever repeats — and only a repeating **block** makes a decimal rational.

---

Show that 0.999… = 1 exactly.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Let x = 0.9̄
> 10x = 9.9̄
> Subtract: 9x = 9  →  x = 1
> ```
>
> **So 0.999… IS 1** — not slightly less. Most people find this surprising.

---

Why isn't the decimal representation of a number unique?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **every terminating decimal has an alternative ending in repeating 9s**:
>
> `1.000… = 0.999…` · `2.47000… = 2.46999…`
>
> Just as `1 = 10/10 = 100/100` as a fraction, a number can wear more than one **decimal** costume.

---

Summarise how the number system was built up, and why each extension was needed.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ℕ  natural numbers      1, 2, 3, …
>    + zero               whole numbers
>    + negatives          ℤ  integers
>    + fractions          ℚ  rational numbers  (p/q, q ≠ 0)
>    + irrationals        ℝ  real numbers      (the unbroken line)
> ```
>
> **Each set was invented because the previous one wasn't CLOSED under some operation:**
> **subtraction** forced integers · **division** forced rationals · **square roots** forced irrationals.
