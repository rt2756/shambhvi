<!-- ===== Identity vs equation ===== -->

Take three consecutive squares, add the smallest and largest, subtract twice the middle. Try 1, 4, 9 and 25, 36, 49.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `(1 + 9) − 2(4) = 10 − 8 =` **2**
> - `(25 + 49) − 2(36) = 74 − 72 =` **2**
>
> **The answer is always 2** — and algebra will show exactly why.

---

Prove that pattern always gives 2.

> [!ANSWER]
> Three consecutive numbers are `n − 1`, `n`, `n + 1`:
>
> ```
> (n − 1)² + (n + 1)² = (n² − 2n + 1) + (n² + 2n + 1) = 2n² + 2
> subtract 2n²                                        = 2
> ```
>
> **The `2n` terms cancel and the `2n²` cancels — leaving 2, whatever n is.** ∎

---

What is the difference between an identity and an equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> - An **IDENTITY** is true for **ALL** values of its variables.
> - An **EQUATION** need not be — it may hold only for particular values.
>
> `x² − 1 = 24` is true only for `x = ±5` → **equation**.
> `(x + y)² = x² + 2xy + y²` is true for **every** x and y → **identity**.

<!-- ===== (a+b)² ===== -->

Draw the picture that shows `(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> A square of side `(a + b)`, cut at distance a along both edges:
>
> ```
>         a       b
>     ┌───────┬──────┐
>   a │  a²   │  ab  │
>     ├───────┼──────┤
>   b │  ab   │  b²  │
>     └───────┴──────┘
> ```
>
> One square `a²`, one square `b²`, and **two** rectangles of `ab`.

---

Why isn't the picture enough to prove the identity?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **a picture only covers positive LENGTHS**.
>
> It says nothing about negative numbers or fractions. **A picture SUGGESTS an identity; algebra PROVES it for every number.**

---

Prove `(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²` using distributivity.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (a + b)² = (a + b)(a + b)
>          = a(a + b) + b(a + b)
>          = a² + ab + ba + b²
>          = a² + 2ab + b²      (since ab = ba)
> ```

---

Check the identity for a = −2, b = −3.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(a + b)² = (−5)² =` **25**
>
> `a² + 2ab + b² = 4 + 2(−2)(−3) + 9 = 4 + 12 + 9 =` **25** ✓

---

Check it for a = −2/3, b = 3/4.

> [!ANSWER]
> `a + b = −8/12 + 9/12 = 1/12`, so `(a + b)² =` **1/144**
>
> `a² + 2ab + b² = 4/9 + 2(−2/3)(3/4) + 9/16 = 4/9 − 1 + 9/16 = (64 − 144 + 81)/144 =` **1/144** ✓

---

When is `(a + b)²` bigger than `a² + b²`, and when smaller?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They differ by exactly `2ab`.**
>
> | Condition | Result |
> |---|---|
> | a, b have the **same sign** → `2ab > 0` | `(a+b)² > a² + b²` |
> | a, b have **opposite signs** → `2ab < 0` | `(a+b)² < a² + b²` |
> | a or b is **0** → `2ab = 0` | **equal** |
>
> `a = 10, b = 2` → `144 > 104` · `a = 5, b = −2` → `9 < 29`

> - `(2.5p + 1.5q)² = 6.25p² + 2(2.5)(1.5)pq + 2.25q² =` **6.25p² + 7.5pq + 2.25q²**

Expand `(1/x + 1/y)²`.

> `= 1/x² + 2(1/x)(1/y) + 1/y² =` **1/x² + 2/(xy) + 1/y²**

Find 64² and 105² using the identity.

<!-- ===== (a−b)² ===== -->

How do you get `(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²` from the first identity?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Replace b by −b.**
>
> `(−b)² = +b²` and `2a(−b) = −2ab`, so `a² + 2ab + b²` becomes **`a² − 2ab + b²`**.

---

Derive `(a − b)²` from a picture instead.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take a square of side a and split a into `(a − b)` and `b`. To reach the small square of side `(a − b)`, **cut off a rectangle of `ab` and another of `b(a − b)`**:
>
> `(a − b)² = a² − ab − b(a − b) = a² − ab − ba + b² =` **`a² − 2ab + b²`** ✓

---

Find 29², 79² and 193² using `(a − b)²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `29² = (30 − 1)² = 900 − 60 + 1 =` **841**
> - `79² = (80 − 1)² = 6400 − 160 + 1 =` **6241**
> - `193² = (200 − 7)² = 40000 − 2800 + 49 =` **37249**

---

Find 299² the quickest way.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(300 − 1)² = 90000 − 600 + 1 =` **89401**
>
> **Always pick the split that gives the roundest numbers** — `(300 − 1)` beats `(290 + 9)` every time.

<!-- ===== Three terms ===== -->

Derive `(a + b + c)²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Group two of them.** Let `d = b + c`:
>
> ```
> (a + d)² = a² + 2ad + d²
>          = a² + 2a(b + c) + (b + c)²
>          = a² + 2ab + 2ac + b² + 2bc + c²
> ```
>
> **`(a + b + c)² = a² + b² + c² + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca`**

---

What does the picture for that identity look like?

> [!ANSWER]
> A square of side `a + b + c` splits into **three squares** (`a²`, `b²`, `c²`) and **six rectangles** — **two each** of `ab`, `bc` and `ca`.
>
> That's exactly why the identity has three squared terms and three doubled products.

---

Find 119² using the three-term identity.

> [!ANSWER]
> `119² = (100 + 10 + 9)²`
> `= 10000 + 100 + 81 + 2(100)(10) + 2(100)(9) + 2(10)(9)`
> `= 10000 + 100 + 81 + 2000 + 1800 + 180 =` **14161**

---

Which identity would you use for 117², 198², 214² and 1104²?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `117² = (100 + 17)²` — the two-term **sum** identity → **13689**
> - `198² = (200 − 2)²` — the **difference** identity → **39204**
> - `214² = (200 + 14)²` — sum → **45796**
> - `1104² = (1100 + 4)²` — sum → **1218816**
>
> **Choose whichever split gives the roundest numbers.**

Factorise `x² + 4x + 4` and `36x² + 12x + 1`.

Factorise `9a² + 4b² + c² − 12ab + 6ac − 4bc`.

> - `2BC = 2(−2b)(c) = −4bc` ✓

<!-- ===== Śhrīdharāchārya ===== -->

State Śhrīdharāchārya's squaring rule and use it on 55².

> [!ANSWER]
> Rearranging `a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)` gives **`a² = (a + b)(a − b) + b²`**.
>
> `55² = (55 + 5)(55 − 5) + 5² = 60 × 50 + 25 = 3000 + 25 =` **3025**
>
> **Choose b to make one factor round.**

---

Compute 35², 65², 85² and 105². What pattern do you see?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1225, 4225, 7225, 11025** — every one ends in **25**.
>
> **The rule for numbers ending in 5:** take the part before the 5, multiply it by **one more than itself**, and put **25** on the end.
> `85²` → `8 × 9 = 72` → **7225** ✓ `105²` → `10 × 11 = 110` → **11025** ✓

---

Prove that "ends in 5" rule.

> [!ANSWER]
> A number ending in 5 is `10n + 5`:
>
> ```
> (10n + 5)² = 100n² + 100n + 25
>            = 100·n(n + 1) + 25
> ```
>
> **`n(n+1)` sits in the hundreds and above; 25 occupies the last two digits.** ∎

<!-- ===== Splitting the middle term ===== -->

How does the algebra-tile picture for `(x + 3)(x + 4)` work?

> [!ANSWER]
> Build a rectangle from **one x²-tile**, **seven x-tiles** (3 to the right, 4 below) and **twelve unit tiles** in a `3 × 4` array.
>
> **The DIMENSIONS of the finished rectangle are the factors:** `x + 3` and `x + 4`.
>
> So `x² + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)`, and the `7x` has been split as `3x + 4x`.

---

State the general method for factorising `x² + bx + c` without tiles.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Compare with `x² + (a + b)x + ab`.**
>
> **Find two numbers whose SUM is the x-coefficient and whose PRODUCT is the constant.**

---

Factorise `x² + 11x + 30`, showing your search.

> [!ANSWER]
> Need `a + b = 11` and `ab = 30`.
>
> - 2 and 15 → product ✓ but sum **17** ✗
> - 3 and 10 → product ✓ but sum **13** ✗
> - **5 and 6** → sum 11 ✓ product 30 ✓
>
> **`= (x + 5)(x + 6)`**

---

Factorise `x² − 5x + 6`.

> [!ANSWER]
> Need `a + b = −5`, `ab = +6`.
>
> **A POSITIVE product with a NEGATIVE sum means both numbers are negative** → **−2 and −3**.
>
> **`= (x − 2)(x − 3)`**

---

Give the sign shortcut for choosing a and b.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **`ab` positive** → a and b have the **SAME** sign, matching the sign of `a + b`
> - **`ab` negative** → a and b have **OPPOSITE** signs
>
> That halves the search before you start.

---

Factorise `r² − r − 42`.

> [!ANSWER]
> Need `a + b = −1`, `ab = −42`. **Negative product → opposite signs.**
>
> `6` and `−7` → sum **−1** ✓ product **−42** ✓
>
> **`= (r + 6)(r − 7)`**

---

Factorise `s² − 11s + 24`.

> [!ANSWER]
> Need `a + b = −11`, `ab = +24` → both negative → **−3 and −8**.
>
> **`= (s − 3)(s − 8)`**

---

Write the general expansion of `(x + a)(x + b)` and of `(px + a)(qx + b)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **`(x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab`**
> - **`(px + a)(qx + b) = pq·x² + (pb + qa)x + ab`**

<!-- ===== Cubes ===== -->

Derive `(a + b)³`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (a + b)³ = (a + b)(a + b)²
>          = (a + b)(a² + 2ab + b²)
>          = a³ + 2a²b + ab² + a²b + 2ab² + b³
>          = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³
> ```

---

What does the cube picture show?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **cube of edge `a + b`** splits into:
>
> - **two cubes** — `a³` and `b³`
> - **six cuboids** — three of `a × a × b` (total **3a²b**) and three of `a × b × b` (total **3ab²**)
>
> Adding the volumes gives exactly `a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³` ✓

---

Write `(a − b)³` and describe its signs.

> [!ANSWER]
> Replacing b by −b: **`(a − b)³ = a³ − 3a²b + 3ab² − b³`**
>
> **The four terms alternate in sign** — plus, minus, plus, minus.

---

A cube has volume `p³ + 6p²q + 12pq² + 8q³`. Find its side.

> [!ANSWER]
> `= (p)³ + 3(p)²(2q) + 3(p)(2q)² + (2q)³ = (p + 2q)³`
>
> **The side is `p + 2q` units.**

---

Factorise `8n³ − 60n²m + 150nm² − 125m³`.

> [!ANSWER]
> The alternating signs point to `(a − b)³`.
>
> `= (2n)³ − 3(2n)²(5m) + 3(2n)(5m)² − (5m)³ =` **`(2n − 5m)³`**

---

Multiply out `(x − y)(x² + xy + y²)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> = x³ + x²y + xy² − x²y − xy² − y³
> = x³ − y³
> ```
>
> **The four middle terms cancel in pairs.**

---

Predict `(x + y)(x² − xy + y²)` and check.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`= x³ + y³`**
>
> ```
> = x³ − x²y + xy² + x²y − xy² + y³ = x³ + y³ ✓
> ```

---

Is `x − y` a factor of `x⁴ − y⁴`? Of `x⁵ − y⁵`?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes to both.**
>
> `x⁴ − y⁴ = (x²)² − (y²)² = (x² − y²)(x² + y²) = (x − y)(x + y)(x² + y²)` — the factor `x − y` is right there.
>
> **In general `x − y` is a factor of `xⁿ − yⁿ` for every n**, because putting `x = y` always makes the expression zero.

<!-- ===== Three-variable identity ===== -->

State the three-variable cube identity.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`(x + y + z)(x² + y² + z² − xy − yz − zx) = x³ + y³ + z³ − 3xyz`**

---

Three numbers sum to 10, multiply to 25, and their squares sum to 38. Find the sum of their cubes.

> [!ANSWER]
> **First find `xy + yz + zx`** from `(x+y+z)² = x² + y² + z² + 2(xy + yz + zx)`:
>
> `100 = 38 + 2(xy + yz + zx)` → `xy + yz + zx =` **31**
>
> **Then the identity:**
> ```
> (10)(38 − 31) = x³ + y³ + z³ − 3(25)
>            70 = x³ + y³ + z³ − 75
> x³ + y³ + z³ = 145
> ```

<!-- ===== Rational expressions ===== -->

Simplify `(x² − 7x + 12)/(5x² + 5x − 100)`, assuming the denominator isn't zero.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Numerator:   a + b = −7, ab = 12  →  −3, −4  →  (x − 3)(x − 4)
> Denominator: take out 5 → 5(x² + x − 20)
>              a + b = 1, ab = −20  →  5, −4   →  5(x − 4)(x + 5)
>
>   (x − 3)(x − 4)          x − 3
> ─────────────────  =  ───────────
>   5(x − 4)(x + 5)       5(x + 5)
> ```

---

Why must the question say "assuming the denominator isn't zero"?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **you may only cancel a factor that is not zero**.
>
> If `x = 4`, the cancelled factor `(x − 4)` would be **zero**, and the original expression would be undefined — so the simplified form wouldn't be equivalent there.

---

A student cancels the x's in `(x + 3)/(x + 5)` to get `3/5`. What's wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You can only cancel FACTORS, never individual TERMS.**
>
> In `(x + 3)`, the x is **added**, not multiplied — so it isn't a factor of the whole numerator and can't be cancelled against anything.

---

Simplify `(4y² − 20yz + 25z²)/(25z² − 4y²)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - Numerator: `(2y)² − 2(2y)(5z) + (5z)² = (2y − 5z)²`
> - Denominator: `(5z)² − (2y)² = (5z − 2y)(5z + 2y)`
>
> Since `(2y − 5z) = −(5z − 2y)`:
>
> `= (2y − 5z)(2y − 5z) / [−(2y − 5z)(5z + 2y)] =` **`−(2y − 5z)/(5z + 2y)`**

---

Simplify `(p⁴ − 16)/(p² − 4p + 4)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - Numerator: `(p²)² − 4² = (p² − 4)(p² + 4) = (p − 2)(p + 2)(p² + 4)`
> - Denominator: `(p − 2)²`
>
> `=` **`(p + 2)(p² + 4)/(p − 2)`**

<!-- ===== Applications ===== -->

Saira uses one x-by-x square, 8 strips of `x × 1`, and 15 unit squares to build a rectangle. Find its dimensions.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Total area** `= x² + 8x + 15`.
>
> Factorising: need `a + b = 8`, `ab = 15` → **3 and 5**.
>
> **The rectangle is `(x + 5)` by `(x + 3)`.**

---

A pool's breadth is 4 m less than its length and its area is 96 m². Find both.

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the length be x, breadth `x − 4`:
>
> ```
> x(x − 4) = 96  →  x² − 4x − 96 = 0
> Split −4x using −12 and 8:
> x² − 12x + 8x − 96 = x(x − 12) + 8(x − 12) = (x − 12)(x + 8) = 0
> ```
>
> So `x = 12` or `x = −8`. **A length can't be negative**, so **length 12 m, breadth 8 m** ✓ (`12 × 8 = 96`)

---

Why does `(x − 12)(x + 8) = 0` mean x is 12 or −8?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **a product is zero only if one of its factors is zero**.
>
> So either `x − 12 = 0` or `x + 8 = 0`. **Then check both against the real situation** and discard any answer that makes no sense — like a negative length.

---

James writes `(a − b)²(a + b) = (a² − 2ab + b²)(a + b)`; Reshma writes `(a − b)[(a − b)(a + b)] = (a − b)(a² − b²)`. Who is right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both** — they give the same answer.
>
> James expands the square first; Reshma **regroups** to use the difference-of-squares identity first. Reshma's route is quicker, because `(a² − b²)` is easier to multiply out than a three-term expression.
>
> **The lesson: identities can be combined in different orders, and some orders are much less work.**

---

Use identities to find `23 × 17`, `18 × 29` and `34 × 43` without long multiplication.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `23 × 17 = (20 + 3)(20 − 3) = 400 − 9 =` **391**
> - `18 × 29`: no neat centre, but `= 18 × 30 − 18 =` **522**
> - `34 × 43`: `= 34 × 40 + 34 × 3 = 1360 + 102 =` **1462**
>
> **The `a² − b²` trick only works when the two numbers are equally spaced either side of a round number** — check first.

List every identity from this chapter.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (a + b)²         = a² + 2ab + b²
> (a − b)²         = a² − 2ab + b²
> (a + b)(a − b)   = a² − b²
> a²               = (a + b)(a − b) + b²          [Śhrīdharāchārya]
> (a + b + c)²     = a² + b² + c² + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca
> (x + a)(x + b)   = x² + (a + b)x + ab
> (a + b)³         = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³
> (a − b)³         = a³ − 3a²b + 3ab² − b³

> (x + y)(x² − xy + y²) = x³ + y³
> (x+y+z)(x²+y²+z²−xy−yz−zx) = x³ + y³ + z³ − 3xyz
> ```
