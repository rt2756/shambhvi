<!-- ===== Sequences ===== -->

Define a sequence and a term, and distinguish finite from infinite sequences.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A SEQUENCE is an ordered list of numbers; each number in it is a TERM.**
>
> - **Infinite:** `1, 2, 3, 4, …` — the three dots mean it continues indefinitely.
> - **Finite:** `6, 12, 24, 48, 96` — exactly five terms and no more.

---

Explain the tₙ notation, and say what `t₄ = 7` means.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`t₁` is the first term, `t₂` the second, and so on — the SUBSCRIPT is the POSITION.**
>
> **`t₄ = 7`** says *"the term in position 4 is 7."*
>
> Different letters (`t`, `s`, `u`) let you talk about several sequences at once.

---

In the notation tₙ, what can n be, and what can the term itself be?

> [!ANSWER]
> **n must be a positive whole number** — a position can't be 2.5 or −3.
>
> **But the TERM can be anything** — negative, fractional, even irrational. `1, ½, ⅓, ¼, …` and `−7, −3, 1, 5, 9, …` are both perfectly good sequences.

---

Show that each triangular number is a sum of natural numbers, and find the 5th.

> [!ANSWER]
> `1 = 1` · `3 = 1+2` · `6 = 1+2+3` · `10 = 1+2+3+4`
>
> **The nth triangular number is the sum of the first n natural numbers.**
>
> The 5th is `1+2+3+4+5 =` **15**.

---

Show that each square number is a sum of odd numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> `1 = 1` · `4 = 1+3` · `9 = 1+3+5` · `16 = 1+3+5+7`
>
> **Each square is the sum of the odd numbers up to that point** — which is why the **differences** between consecutive squares are `3, 5, 7, 9, 11` — the odd numbers themselves.

---

Compare the differences between consecutive terms in the four familiar sequences.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Sequence | Differences |
> |---|---|
> | Natural numbers | 1, 1, 1, … — **constant** |
> | Odd numbers | 2, 2, 2, … — **constant** |
> | Triangular numbers | 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — **growing** |
> | Square numbers | 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 — **the odd numbers** |
>
> **Constant differences are what make the first two arithmetic progressions.**

---

Predict the next four terms of 1, 4, 7, 10, 13, and find the running sums.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each term is **3 more** than the last: **16, 19, 22, 25**.
>
> **Running sums:** `1`, `1+4 = 5`, `1+4+7 = 12`, `+10 = 22`, `+13 = 35`, `+16 = 51`, `+19 = 70`, `+22 = 92`, `+25 = 117`, `+28 = 145`

<!-- ===== Explicit rules ===== -->

What is an explicit rule, and why is it so useful?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A rule giving the nth term DIRECTLY from its position n**, without needing any previous term.
>
> **Why it's useful:** you can jump straight to the **300th term** without computing the 299 before it.

---

Show that uₙ = 2n − 1 generates the odd numbers, and find the 53rd, 108th and 1170th terms.

> [!ANSWER]
> `u₁ = 1`, `u₂ = 3`, `u₃ = 5` ✓
>
> - `u₅₃ = 106 − 1 =` **105**
> - `u₁₀₈ = 216 − 1 =` **215**
> - `u₁₁₇₀ = 2340 − 1 =` **2339**

---

Which position does 137 occupy in the odd-number sequence?

> [!ANSWER]
> Solve `2n − 1 = 137` → `2n = 138` → **n = 69**
>
> **137 is the 69th odd number.**

---

For sₙ = 5n − 2, is 308 a term? Is 471?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **308:** `5n − 2 = 308` → `n = 62` ✓ — **yes, the 62nd term**
> - **471:** `5n − 2 = 471` → `n = 94.6` ✗ — **no**

---

Why must n be a natural number when testing membership?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **n is a POSITION in the list** — there is no 94.6th term, just as there is no 94.6th person in a queue.
>
> A fractional or negative n means the number simply isn't in the sequence.

---

Find the first five terms of tₙ = 3n − 4, tₙ = 2 − 5n and tₙ = n² − 2n + 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `3n − 4` → **−1, 2, 5, 8, 11**
> - `2 − 5n` → **−3, −8, −13, −18, −23** (decreasing)
> - `n² − 2n + 3` → **2, 3, 6, 11, 18**

---

For tₙ = 3n − 7, find the 1st, 12th and 50th terms; find which term is 332; and check whether 557 is a term.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `t₁ = −4` · `t₁₂ = 29` · `t₅₀ = 143`
> - `3n − 7 = 332` → `n = 113` → **the 113th term**
> - `3n − 7 = 557` → `n = 188` ✓ — **yes, the 188th term**

---

For tₙ = 5n − 3, find the 10th and 15th terms, test 97 and 172, and find which term is 607.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `t₁₀ =` **47** · `t₁₅ =` **72**
> - `5n − 3 = 97` → `n = 20` ✓ — **97 is the 20th term**
> - `5n − 3 = 172` → `n = 35` ✓ — **172 is the 35th term**
> - `5n − 3 = 607` → `n = 122` → **the 122nd term**

---

Give the explicit rule for the square numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`tₙ = n²`**

---

Can you find an explicit rule for the primes 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No simple one exists.**
>
> Despite centuries of searching, **there is no simple formula that predicts the next prime** — which is exactly why primes are so interesting, and so useful in cryptography.

<!-- ===== Recursive rules ===== -->

What is a recursive rule, and what does it need that an explicit rule doesn't?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A rule giving each term in terms of PREVIOUS terms.**
>
> **It needs a STARTING VALUE** — without `t₁` you can't get going at all.

---

Give both rules for 1, 4, 7, 10, 13, …

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Explicit:** `tₙ = 3n − 2`
> - **Recursive:** `t₁ = 1`, `tₙ = tₙ₋₁ + 3` for n ≥ 2

---

Find the first four terms of u₁ = 1, uₙ = 2uₙ₋₁ + 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u₂ = 2(1) + 3 = 5
> u₃ = 2(5) + 3 = 13
> u₄ = 2(13) + 3 = 29
> ```
>
> **1, 5, 13, 29, …**

---

Find the first four terms of s₁ = 3, sₙ = sₙ₋₁(sₙ₋₁ − 1).

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> s₂ = 3 × 2 = 6
> s₃ = 6 × 5 = 30
> s₄ = 30 × 29 = 870
> ```
>
> **3, 6, 30, 870, …** — growing explosively.

---

Find the first five terms of t₁ = −5, tₙ₊₁ = tₙ + 3. Is 52 a term?

> [!ANSWER]
> **−5, −2, 1, 4, 7, …**
>
> This is an AP with `a = −5`, `d = 3`, so `tₙ = −5 + 3(n−1) = 3n − 8`.
>
> `3n − 8 = 52` → `n = 20` ✓ — **yes, 52 is the 20th term.**

---

Let T₁ = 1, T₂ = 2, T₃ = 4 and Tₙ = Tₙ₋₁ + Tₙ₋₂ + Tₙ₋₃. Find T₄ to T₈.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> T₄ = 4 + 2 + 1 = 7
> T₅ = 7 + 4 + 2 = 13
> T₆ = 13 + 7 + 4 = 24
> T₇ = 24 + 13 + 7 = 44
> T₈ = 44 + 24 + 13 = 81
> ```
>
> (This is the "tribonacci" sequence — three previous terms instead of two.)

---

What is the trade-off between explicit and recursive rules?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Explicit** — lets you **leap to any term** instantly.
> - **Recursive** — often describes the **pattern more naturally**, but makes you climb there step by step.
>
> Some sequences have an easy recursive rule and a very hard explicit one.

---

Give the Virahānka–Fibonacci rule and the first eight terms.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`V₁ = 1, V₂ = 2, Vₙ = Vₙ₋₁ + Vₙ₋₂` for n ≥ 3** — each term is the **sum of the previous two**.
>
> **1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, …**

---

Who discovered that sequence, and in what context?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Virahānka**, in his *Vṛttajātisamuchaya* (**7th century CE**) — and he discovered it studying **Prakrit metre and poetry**, not numbers.
>
> It was studied further by **Gopāla** (c. 1135) and **Hemachandra** (c. 1150), and later by the Italian **Fibonacci** (c. 1200).

<!-- ===== APs ===== -->

Define an arithmetic progression and the common difference.

> [!ANSWER]
> **An AP is a sequence in which the DIFFERENCE between consecutive terms is CONSTANT.**
>
> That constant is the **common difference, d**.

---

Derive the nth term of 1, 5, 9, 13, 17, …

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> t₁ = 1
> t₂ = 1 + 1×4
> t₃ = 1 + 2×4
> t₄ = 1 + 3×4
> tₙ = 1 + (n − 1)×4 = 4n − 3
> ```

---

Give the explicit and recursive rules for a general AP.

> [!ANSWER]
> With first term **a** and common difference **d**:
>
> - **Explicit:** `tₙ = a + (n − 1)d`
> - **Recursive:** `t₁ = a`, `tₙ = tₙ₋₁ + d` for n ≥ 2

---

Is 11, 7, 3, −1, −5, … an AP? Give a and d.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes** — the difference is **−4** every time.
>
> `a = 11`, `d = −4` → `tₙ = 11 − 4(n−1) =` **15 − 4n**
>
> **A negative common difference gives a DECREASING AP.**

---

Find the nth term of 2, 5, 8, 11, … and of −5, −1, 3, 7, …

> [!ANSWER]
> - `2, 5, 8, 11, …` → `a = 2, d = 3` → **`tₙ = 3n − 1`**
> - `−5, −1, 3, 7, …` → `a = −5, d = 4` → **`tₙ = 4n − 9`**

---

Find the nth term of ½, 5/2, 9/2, 13/2, … and of 1.5, 3.5, 5.5, 7.5, …

> [!ANSWER]
> - `a = ½, d = 2` → `tₙ = ½ + 2(n−1) =` **2n − 3/2**
> - `a = 1.5, d = 2` → `tₙ = 1.5 + 2(n−1) =` **2n − 0.5**

---

Plot the AP 1, 5, 9, 13, 17 as points (position, term). What do you see, and what does it mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> The points `(1,1), (2,5), (3,9), (4,13), (5,17)` **lie on a STRAIGHT LINE**.
>
> **Every AP is a LINEAR pattern** — and **the common difference d IS the SLOPE of the line** (Chapter 2).

---

A taxi charges ₹200 booking plus ₹40 per km. Write the AP and find the fare for 10 km.

> [!ANSWER]
> Fares: `240, 280, 320, …` — an AP with `a = 240`, `d = 40`.
>
> `tₙ = 240 + 40(n−1) =` **200 + 40n**
>
> For 10 km: `200 + 400 =` **₹600**

---

Find the 10th and 26th terms of the AP 3, 8, 13, 18, …

> [!ANSWER]
> `a = 3, d = 5` → `tₙ = 5n − 2`
>
> `t₁₀ =` **48** · `t₂₆ =` **128**

---

Which term of 21, 18, 15, … is −81? Is 0 a term?

> [!ANSWER]
> `a = 21, d = −3` → `tₙ = 24 − 3n`
>
> - `24 − 3n = −81` → `n = 35` → **the 35th term**
> - `24 − 3n = 0` → `n = 8` ✓ — **yes, 0 is the 8th term**

---

Find the nth term and recursive rule for 11, 8, 5, 2, …

> [!ANSWER]
> `a = 11, d = −3`
>
> - **Explicit:** `tₙ = 11 − 3(n−1) =` **14 − 3n**
> - **Recursive:** `t₁ = 11`, `tₙ = tₙ₋₁ − 3`

---

An AP has 50 terms; the 3rd is 12 and the 50th is 106. Find the 29th term.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a + 2d = 12       …(1)
> a + 49d = 106     …(2)
> Subtracting: 47d = 94  →  d = 2
>              a = 12 − 4 = 8
> ```
>
> `t₂₉ = 8 + 28(2) =` **64**

---

Harish starts on ₹5,00,000 with a ₹20,000 annual increment. After how many years does he reach ₹7,00,000?

> [!ANSWER]
> An AP with `a = 500000`, `d = 20000`.
>
> `500000 + 20000(n−1) = 700000` → `20000(n−1) = 200000` → `n − 1 = 10`
>
> **After 10 years** (in his 11th year of salary).

<!-- ===== Sum of naturals ===== -->

Use the reversal trick to find 1 + 2 + … + 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> S = 1  +  2  +  3  + … + 10
> S = 10 +  9  +  8  + … +  1
> ```
>
> **Every column adds to 11**, and there are 10 columns.
>
> `2S = 11 × 10 = 110` → `S =` **55**

---

Derive the general formula, and give the picture that explains it.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> S = 1 + 2 + … + n
> S = n + (n−1) + … + 1
> 2S = n(n + 1)
> ```
>
> **`Sₙ = n(n + 1)/2`**
>
> **The picture:** two staircase arrays of `1 + 2 + … + 6` fit together into a **7 × 6 rectangle**, showing `2 × (1+2+…+6) = 7 × 6`.

---

Where is this result first written down, and what does the source add?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Āryabhaṭa's *Āryabhaṭīya*, Chapter 2, Verse 19.**
>
> It gives **two** methods — the second being: **take the average of the first and last terms and multiply by the number of terms**.
>
> **That version generalises to ANY AP:** `Sum = (number of terms) × (first + last)/2`.

---

Find S₂₀, S₅₀ and S₁₀₀₀.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `S₂₀ = 20 × 21/2 =` **210**
> - `S₅₀ = 50 × 51/2 =` **1275**
> - `S₁₀₀₀ = 1000 × 1001/2 =` **500,500**

---

Find 25 + 26 + 27 + … + 58.

> [!ANSWER]
> `= S₅₈ − S₂₄`
>
> `= (58 × 59)/2 − (24 × 25)/2 = 1711 − 300 =` **1411**
>
> **The trick: subtract the unwanted head of the sum.**

---

Find the 10th, 17th and 80th triangular numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> `tₙ = n(n+1)/2`:
>
> - 10th → `10 × 11/2 =` **55**
> - 17th → `17 × 18/2 =` **153**
> - 80th → `80 × 81/2 =` **3240**

---

A child lays out marbles: 1 in the first row, 2 in the second, up to 25 rows. How many altogether?

> [!ANSWER]
> `S₂₅ = 25 × 26/2 =` **325 marbles**

---

How many 2-digit numbers are divisible by 3, and what is their sum?

> [!ANSWER]
> They run `12, 15, 18, …, 99` — an AP with `a = 12`, `d = 3`.
>
> `12 + 3(n−1) = 99` → `n =` **30 numbers**
>
> `Sum = 30 × (12 + 99)/2 = 30 × 55.5 =` **1665**

<!-- ===== GPs ===== -->

Define a geometric progression and the common ratio.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A GP is a sequence in which each term is the previous term MULTIPLIED by a constant.**
>
> That constant is the **common ratio, r**.

---

Show that 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96 is a GP and find its nth term.

> [!ANSWER]
> `6/3 = 12/6 = 24/12 = 48/24 = 96/48 =` **2** — the same ratio every time ✓
>
> `3, 3×2, 3×2², 3×2³, …` → **`tₙ = 3 × 2ⁿ⁻¹`**
>
> **Recursive:** `t₁ = 3`, `tₙ = 2tₙ₋₁`

---

Give the explicit and recursive rules for a general GP.

> [!ANSWER]
> With first term **a** and common ratio **r**:
>
> - **Explicit:** `tₙ = a·rⁿ⁻¹`
> - **Recursive:** `t₁ = a`, `tₙ = r·tₙ₋₁` for n ≥ 2

---

Are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 1, 3, 9, 27, 81 GPs? Give the ratios and nth terms.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `1, 2, 4, 8, 16, …` → **r = 2**, `tₙ = 2ⁿ⁻¹`
> - `1, 3, 9, 27, 81, …` → **r = 3**, `tₙ = 3ⁿ⁻¹`

---

Is 1, −1, 1, −1, 1, … a GP?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — with `r = −1`.**
>
> `tₙ = (−1)ⁿ⁻¹`. **The negative ratio makes the sign alternate**, which is a perfectly good GP even though the values never grow.

---

Check whether 5, 15/4, 45/16, 135/64, … is a GP.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (15/4) ÷ 5     = 15/20  = 3/4
> (45/16) ÷ (15/4) = 45/16 × 4/15 = 3/4
> (135/64) ÷ (45/16) = 135/64 × 16/45 = 3/4
> ```
>
> **All ratios are 3/4** ✓ — a GP with `a = 5`, `r = 3/4`, so **`tₙ = 5 × (3/4)ⁿ⁻¹`**

---

Check 2, 10, 50, 250, … and 4, 8/3, 16/9, 32/27, …

> [!ANSWER]
> - `2, 10, 50, 250, …` → **r = 5**, `tₙ = 2 × 5ⁿ⁻¹` — increasing
> - `4, 8/3, 16/9, 32/27, …` → **r = 2/3**, `tₙ = 4 × (2/3)ⁿ⁻¹` — **decreasing, because r < 1**

---

Find a recursive rule for tₙ = 3 × 10ⁿ⁻¹.

> [!ANSWER]
> The sequence is `3, 30, 300, 3000, …`
>
> **`t₁ = 3`, `tₙ = 10 tₙ₋₁`** for n ≥ 2

---

How do you test whether a sequence is an AP or a GP?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **For an AP: SUBTRACT** consecutive terms. If every difference is the same, it's an AP.
> - **For a GP: DIVIDE** consecutive terms. If every ratio is the same, it's a GP.

---

Tabulate the differences between APs and GPs.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **AP** | **GP** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Rule | **add** a constant | **multiply** by a constant |
> | Constant | common **difference d** | common **ratio r** |
> | Test | **subtract** | **divide** |
> | Explicit | `tₙ = a + (n−1)d` | `tₙ = a·rⁿ⁻¹` |
> | Recursive | `tₙ = tₙ₋₁ + d` | `tₙ = r·tₙ₋₁` |
> | Graph | a **straight line** | a **curve** |
> | Example | `1, 5, 9, 13, …` | `3, 6, 12, 24, …` |

---

Connect APs and GPs to simple and compound interest.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Simple interest is an AP** — a **fixed amount** is added each year, so the balances have a constant difference.
> - **Compound interest is a GP** — a **fixed multiplier** `(1 + r)` each year, so the balances have a constant ratio.
>
> **That's exactly why compound interest overtakes simple interest so dramatically** — a curve always beats a straight line eventually.

---

The Sierpinski triangle keeps 3 of every 4 triangles at each stage. Which sequences here are GPs?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Number of black triangles: `1, 3, 9, 27, …`** — a GP with **r = 3**
> - **Area remaining: `1, 3/4, 9/16, 27/64, …`** — a GP with **r = 3/4**
>
> **Fractals are geometric progressions made visible** — every "repeat the same operation" rule produces a GP in whatever you count.
