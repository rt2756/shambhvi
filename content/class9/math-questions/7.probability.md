<!-- ===== What it is ===== -->

Probability is described as a kind of measurement. What does it measure?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The LIKELIHOOD of events** — rather than a physical quantity like length, area or volume.
>
> It expresses **how confident we are that a particular event will occur**.

---

What makes an event "random"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **We know all the possible outcomes, but cannot predict which one will occur.**
>
> Tossing a coin: you know it's heads or tails — but not which. Rolling a die: you know it's 1 to 6 — but not which.

---

Define a random experiment.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Something you can REPEAT, where the result may be different each time and you cannot know the outcome in advance.**
>
> Tossing a coin, rolling a die, and drawing a name in a lucky draw are all random experiments.

---

What is subjective probability?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A personal judgement of likelihood based on how someone interprets the evidence** — for example, *"the sun is shining brightly, so it's unlikely to rain."*
>
> Two people can look at the same weather and reach different subjective probabilities. **Objective methods are what this chapter is about.**

---

Why is rainfall considered random?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it depends on **many complex factors** — temperature, humidity, wind patterns, pressure — and is **so sensitive to them** that exact prediction is impossible.
>
> **But we can still estimate its likelihood** from patterns and probabilities derived from past data.

---

Why is tossing a coin considered a fair way to decide who bats first?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Precisely BECAUSE it is unpredictable.**
>
> Neither captain can influence the result, and neither knows it in advance — so neither can complain. **The fairness comes FROM the randomness.**

---

What do we mean by calling a coin "fair" or "unbiased", and by a "random toss"?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Fair / unbiased** — the coin is **symmetrical**, so there is **no reason for it to land on one side more often** than the other.
> - **Random toss** — the coin is allowed to **fall freely, without bias or interference**.

---

<!-- ===== The scale ===== -->

Describe the probability scale.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Probability runs from 0 to 1:**
>
> | Value | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **0** | impossible |
> | 0 to 0.5 | less likely than not |
> | **0.5** | even chance |
> | 0.5 to 1 | more likely than not |
> | **1** | certain |
>
> **Most events fall strictly between 0 and 1.**

---

What does a probability of 0.75 mean? What does 0.5 mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **0.75** — a **75% chance**, so the event is **more likely than not**.
> - **0.5** — a **50% chance**, so it is **equally likely** to happen or not.

---

A deck of six cards contains an unknown number of purple cards. Describe how the probability of drawing purple varies.

> [!ANSWER]
> It slides **smoothly along the scale**:
>
> - **0 purple** → **impossible** (P = 0)
> - a few purple → **less likely**
> - **3 purple** → **even chance** (P = 0.5)
> - most purple → **more likely**
> - **all six purple** → **certain** (P = 1)

---

Rank these and explain: (i) next Monday comes after Sunday; (ii) it snows in Mumbai in July; (iii) an elephant walks through your classroom today; (iv) you greet at least one friend at school tomorrow.

> [!ANSWER]
> - (i) **Certain (P = 1)** — the days of the week are fixed in order.
> - (ii) **Impossible (P ≈ 0)** — Mumbai's July temperatures never approach freezing.
> - (iii) **Less likely, very nearly impossible** — not physically impossible, but extraordinarily unlikely.
> - (iv) **More likely, close to certain** — assuming you go to school and have friends there.

---

Classify: getting a number greater than 6 on a die; a coin landing heads; drawing a 2–10 from a 52-card deck.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Greater than 6 on a die** — **impossible**; a die shows only 1 to 6.
> - **A coin landing heads** — **even chance**; heads and tails are equally likely.
> - **A 2–10 from 52 cards** — **more likely**; **36 of the 52** cards qualify, so P ≈ 0.69.

<!-- ===== Experimental ===== -->

Give the two objective ways of measuring probability.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **EXPERIMENTAL** — from **data**: perform the experiment many times (or analyse past observations) and compute the **relative frequency**.
> 2. **THEORETICAL** — from **reasoning**: assume all outcomes are equally likely, and count favourable against total outcomes. **No data needed.**

---

State the formula for experimental probability.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`Experimental P = (number of times the event occurred) ÷ (total number of trials)`**
>
> This is also called the **relative frequency** of the event.

---

You roll a die 50 times and get a 4 exactly 8 times. What is the experimental probability?

> [!ANSWER]
> `8/50 =` **0.16 or 16%**

---

When must you use experimental probability rather than theoretical?

> [!ANSWER]
> **When you cannot assume the outcomes are equally likely.**
>
> A bent coin, a **paper cup** thrown in the air, whether it will rain, or a survey of people's preferences — **there is no reason to think the outcomes are equally likely**, so data is the only route.

---

You toss a paper cup 100 times and record whether it lands on its bottom, its top, or its side. How would you assign probabilities?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By experimental probability:** divide the count for each landing by 100.
>
> **Why theoretical probability fails here:** the three positions are **clearly not equally likely** — the cup's shape favours the side. Only data can tell you the real proportions.

---

<!-- ===== Theoretical ===== -->

State the formula for theoretical probability and its key assumption.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`P(event) = (number of favourable outcomes) ÷ (number of possible outcomes)`**
>
> **The assumption: all outcomes are EQUALLY LIKELY** — that there's no reason for any one to occur more often than another.

---

Find the theoretical probability of rolling a 4 on a standard die.

> [!ANSWER]
> Favourable outcomes: **1** (just the 4). Possible outcomes: **6**.
>
> `P = 1/6 ≈` **0.167 or 16.7%**

---

A letter is picked at random from PROBABILITY. Find P(B).

> [!ANSWER]
> **PROBABILITY has 11 letters, two of which are B.**
>
> `P(B) = 2/11 ≈` **0.182 or 18.2%**

---

Find the probability of getting an even number on a fair die.

> [!ANSWER]
> Even numbers on a die: **{2, 4, 6}** — three favourable outcomes out of six.
>
> `P = 3/6 =` **0.5**

---

<!-- ===== Sampling ===== -->

Of 50 students, 20 like mango, 15 apples, 10 bananas, 5 grapes. Find the probability a random student likes mango.

> [!ANSWER]
> `20/50 =` **0.4 or 40%**

---

Using that data, estimate how many mangoes to buy for a school of 1500.

> [!ANSWER]
> `40% of 1500 =` **about 600 mangoes**.
>
> The rest would be apples, bananas and grapes in their own proportions.

---

What are a sample and a population, and why do we sample at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **POPULATION** — the whole group you care about (here, 1500 students).
> - **SAMPLE** — the smaller group you actually collect data from (here, 50).
>
> **We sample because collecting data from everyone is usually impractical** — too slow, too costly, sometimes impossible.

---

How would you make the fruit estimate more reliable?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Use a **LARGER sample** — ask 100 or 200 students rather than 50.
> - Make it **more REPRESENTATIVE** — include students from **different classes and grades**, not just one class.
>
> **A big sample that's all from one class can still be badly misleading** — size and representativeness both matter.

---

A sample of 30 sweets contains 10 red, 8 green, 7 yellow and 5 blue. Find P(green), and estimate the yellow sweets in a bag of 600.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `P(green) = 8/30 =` **0.267 or about 27%**
> - `P(yellow) = 7/30`, so in 600 sweets: `7/30 × 600 =` **140 yellow sweets**

---

A sample of 40 students gives 14 Science, 11 Arts, 9 Sports and 6 Debate club. Find P(Arts), and estimate Sports Club members in a school of 800.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `P(Arts) = 11/40 =` **0.275 or 27.5%**
> - `P(Sports) = 9/40`, so in 800 students: `9/40 × 800 =` **180 students**

<!-- ===== Sample spaces ===== -->

Define sample space and sample size, and give the two rules for writing a sample space.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The SAMPLE SPACE S is the list of all possible outcomes**; the **SAMPLE SIZE n(S)** is how many there are.
>
> **Two rules:**
> 1. S must include **EVERY** possible outcome.
> 2. **No outcome may be listed more than once.**

---

Give the sample space and size for: rain tomorrow, a match result, rolling a die, tossing two coins.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Experiment | S | n(S) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Rain tomorrow | {Rain, No Rain} | 2 |
> | Match result | {Win, Lose, Draw} | 3 |
> | Rolling a die | {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} | 6 |
> | Tossing two coins | **{HH, HT, TH, TT}** | **4** |

---

Why does tossing two coins give four outcomes, not three?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **HT and TH are DIFFERENT outcomes**.
>
> "First coin heads, second tails" is **not the same event** as "first coin tails, second heads" — even though both give "one head and one tail".
>
> **Listing them as one is the commonest error in the chapter**, and it makes P(one of each) come out wrong.

---

Define an event, and give three events from rolling a die.

> [!ANSWER]
> **An event is any single outcome or combination of outcomes — a SUBSET of the sample space.**
>
> For `S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}`:
> - "rolling a 4" → **{4}**, `P = 1/6`
> - "rolling an even number" → **{2, 4, 6}**, `P = 1/2`
> - "rolling more than 4" → **{5, 6}**, `P = 1/3`

---

When would {Rain, No Rain} be an inadequate sample space?

> [!ANSWER]
> **When the question asks about HOW MUCH rain.**
>
> Then you'd need **{No Rain, Drizzle, Light Rain, Heavy Rain}**.
>
> **The sample space must be detailed enough to suit the specific problem being studied** — no more, no less.

---

A child has 2 shirts and 3 pairs of trousers. How many outfits, and how would you list the sample space?

> [!ANSWER]
> **6 outfits** — each of the 2 shirts can go with each of the 3 trousers.
>
> With shirts A, B and trousers 1, 2, 3:
> **S = {A1, A2, A3, B1, B2, B3}**, so `n(S) =` **6**

Compare experimental and theoretical probability in a table.

> | | **Experimental** | **Theoretical** |
> |---|---|---|

---

You roll a die 12 times and get a 3 three times. Compare the experimental and theoretical probabilities, and explain the difference.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Experimental:** `3/12 =` **0.25**
> - **Theoretical:** `1/6 ≈` **0.167**
>
> **Why they differ:** with only **12 trials**, chance variation is large — a few extra 3s make a big difference to the fraction.

---

What would happen if you rolled that die 60, 600 or 6000 times?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The experimental probability would get closer and closer to 1/6.**
>
> **This is the LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS:** as the number of trials increases, experimental probability tends towards theoretical probability.

---

State the Law of Large Numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> **As the number of trials increases, the experimental probability tends to get CLOSER to the theoretical probability.**
>
> It says nothing about any individual trial — only about the long-run **proportion**.

<!-- ===== Gambler's Fallacy ===== -->

What is the Gambler's Fallacy?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Believing that because something random has happened several times in a row, the opposite outcome is now "due".**
>
> It is **false**. Past results have **no effect** on the next one.

---

A fair coin has come up heads six times running. What is the probability of tails next?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Still exactly ½.**
>
> **The coin has no memory.** It doesn't keep track of past flips — every toss starts afresh, with the same symmetry and the same probability.

---

You've rolled three 6s in Snakes and Ladders. Is a 6 now less likely?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — it's still 1/6 ≈ 0.166.**
>
> Each roll is an **INDEPENDENT event**: its probability doesn't depend on what came before. **Randomness has no memory**, and the Gambler's Fallacy tricks people into seeing a pattern that isn't there.

---

If the Law of Large Numbers says things "even out", why isn't a tail due after six heads?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **the Law of Large Numbers is about the PROPORTION over thousands of trials, not about a correction on the next throw.**
>
> Six extra heads become negligible after ten thousand tosses — **not because tails "catches up", but because the six get swamped by the sheer number of later results.**
>
> **Probability does not tell you what will happen next; it predicts what happens in the long run.**

---

What is Jñān-Chaupaḍ?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **ancient Indian dice game that Snakes and Ladders evolved from**, used as an **educational tool**.
>
> Each **ladder symbolised a virtue** and each **snake a vice**, so playing it illustrated the consequences of good and bad behaviour. Versions have been found across India; a 19th-century Jain cloth board is in the National Museum.
