<!-- ===== Definitions ===== -->

Define a circle, and define it again using the word "locus".

> [!ANSWER]
> **The set of all points in a plane that are EQUIDISTANT from a given point.**
>
> Using "locus" (the set of points satisfying a condition): **a circle is the locus of points equidistant from a given point.**
>
> That given point is the **centre**; the fixed distance is the **radius**.

---

Define chord, diameter, arc, minor arc and major arc.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Chord** — a segment joining **two points on the circle**
> - **Diameter** — a chord **passing through the centre**
> - **Arc** — a connected portion of the circle, defined by two end points
> - **Minor arc** — the **smaller** of the two arcs between the points
> - **Major arc** — the **larger** one

---

What is meant by "the angle subtended by chord BC at the centre A"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The angle ∠BAC** — formed by joining both ends of the chord to the centre.

---

<!-- ===== Symmetry ===== -->

What kind of rotational symmetry does a circle have, and how does a wheel show it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Complete rotational symmetry** — rotate it by **any** angle about its centre and it looks exactly the same.
>
> **The wheel argument:** watching a rotating wheel, you can never tell whether the point now touching the ground is the same one as before. **A rotating circle looks identical at every instant.**

---

How many lines of reflection symmetry does a circle have?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Infinitely many — every diameter is one.**
>
> Fold a paper circle so its boundaries overlap: the crease is a line of symmetry, and it **always passes through the centre**.

---

Jamuna has a paper circle and wants to find its centre. What should she do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fold it twice** so that the boundary overlaps itself each time.
>
> **Each crease is a diameter** (every line of reflection symmetry passes through the centre), and **two diameters cross at the centre**.

---

What is the length of the longest chord in a circle of radius 5? Is there a shortest chord?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The longest is the DIAMETER, 10 units.**
>
> **There is no shortest chord** — a chord can be made as short as you like without ever reaching zero.

---

What is the locus of points equidistant from two given points A and B?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The perpendicular bisector of AB.**
>
> **Why it's the whole locus:** every point on that line is equidistant from A and B, **and** every point equidistant from A and B lies on it. Both directions must hold for it to be the locus.

---

<!-- ===== Circles through points ===== -->

How many circles pass through two given points A and B?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Infinitely many.**
>
> **Why:** the centre must satisfy `OA = OB`, so it lies on the **perpendicular bisector of AB** — and **every** point on that line can serve as a centre.

---

What is the smallest such circle? Is there a largest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Smallest:** centre at the **midpoint** of AB, radius **½ AB**, with AB as a **diameter**.
>
> **There is no largest** — moving further along the perpendicular bisector makes the radius grow without limit.

---

As you move along the perpendicular bisector away from AB, does the circle look more or less curved?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Less curved.**
>
> The radius grows, and **a bigger circle is flatter** near any given point — which is why the Earth looks flat from the ground.

---

State and prove Theorem 1.

> [!ANSWER]
> **There is a UNIQUE circle passing through three NON-COLLINEAR points.**
>
> **Proof:** the centre O must satisfy `OA = OB`, so it lies on the **perpendicular bisector of AB**; and `OA = OC`, so it also lies on the **perpendicular bisector of AC**.
>
> Because A, B, C are **not collinear**, those two bisectors **intersect at exactly one point** — and two intersecting lines meet only once. That point is the centre, and the radius is OA. ∎

---

Why is there no circle through three collinear points?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC would be **parallel** — they'd never meet, so there is **no possible centre**.
>
> **Consequence: a straight line can cut a circle in at most TWO points.**

---

Define circumcircle, circumcentre, circumscribe and inscribe.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Circumcircle** — the circle through all three vertices of a triangle
> - **Circumcentre** — its centre
> - The circle **circumscribes** the triangle
> - The triangle is **inscribed** in the circle

---

Where does the circumcentre lie for acute, obtuse and right-angled triangles?

> [!ANSWER]
> | Triangle | Circumcentre |
> |---|---|
> | **Acute** | **inside** the triangle |
> | **Obtuse** | **outside** the triangle |
> | **Right-angled** | **at the midpoint of the hypotenuse** |

---

Draw ∆ABC with AB = 6 cm, BC = 7 cm, CA = 7 cm and its circumcircle. What will you find when you measure OA, OB and OC?

> [!ANSWER]
> **All three are equal** — they are radii of the same circle.
>
> That's the whole point of the circumcentre: it is the one point equidistant from all three vertices.

---

Can two different triangles share the same circumcircle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — infinitely many.**
>
> Any three points on a given circle form a triangle inscribed in it. Rotating a triangle about the centre gives another triangle, **congruent to the first**, on the same circumcircle.
>
> (The uniqueness runs the other way: **one triangle has only one circumcircle**, not one circle only one triangle.)

---

<!-- ===== Chords and central angles ===== -->

Show that the triangle formed by a chord and the centre is isosceles.

> [!ANSWER]
> The two sides from the centre to the chord's end points are both **radii**, so they are **equal**.
>
> **A triangle with two equal sides is isosceles.** ∎
>
> **Almost every circle proof rests on this one fact.**

---

State and prove Theorem 2.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre.**
>
> **Proof:** for chords AB and DE with `AB = DE`:
> - `CA = CB = CD = CE = r` (all radii)
> - `AB = DE` (given)
>
> By **SSS**, `∆CAB ≅ ∆CDE`, so the corresponding angles `∠ACB = ∠DCE`. ∎

---

State and prove Theorem 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Chords subtending equal angles at the centre are equal.**
>
> **Proof:** `CA = CD` and `CB = CE` (radii), and the included angles `∠ACB = ∠DCE` are equal.
>
> By **SAS**, `∆ACB ≅ ∆DCE`, so `AB = ED`. ∎

---

Give the intuitive "arms" argument for Theorem 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> Stand at the centre with your arms at a fixed angle, touching the circle at A and B — the distance between your hands is the chord AB.
>
> **Now rotate on the spot without changing the angle.** Your hands still touch the circle, and **the distance between them hasn't changed** — so the new chord has the same length.

---

Two chords make isosceles triangles with the centre. If the triangles have equal bases, are they congruent?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> Both triangles have **two sides equal to the radius**, and equal bases — so **SSS** gives congruence.

---

<!-- ===== Perpendicular from the centre ===== -->

State and prove Theorem 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The line joining the centre to the MIDPOINT of a chord is PERPENDICULAR to the chord.**
>
> **Proof:** `∆CAB` is isosceles (`CA = CB`), so `∠A = ∠B`. With `AM = BM` and CM common, **SAS** gives `∆CMA ≅ ∆CMB`, so `∠CMA = ∠CMB`.
>
> But those two angles lie on a **straight line** and add to **180°** — so **each is 90°**. ∎

---

State and prove Theorem 5 (the converse).

> [!ANSWER]
> **The perpendicular from the centre to a chord BISECTS it.**
>
> **Proof:** in `∆CMA` and `∆CMB`, `∠CMA = ∠CMB = 90°`, `CA = CB` (radii), and CM is common.
>
> By **RHS**, the triangles are congruent, so **`AM = BM`**. ∎

---

What do Theorems 4 and 5 say together?

> [!ANSWER]
> That for any chord, **"the line from the centre through the midpoint" and "the perpendicular from the centre" are the SAME line.**
>
> Find either one and you have found the other.

---

Describe the paper-folding demonstration of these theorems.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fold a paper circle inwards from the boundary** — the crease is a **chord**.
>
> **Fold again so the chord's end points meet** — the second crease **bisects the chord at right angles and passes through the centre**, exactly as the theorems say.

---

An isosceles triangle ABC with AB = AC is inscribed in a circle. Show the altitude from A passes through the centre.

> [!ANSWER]
> Since `AB = AC`, the altitude from A is also the **perpendicular bisector of BC**.
>
> **The centre is equidistant from B and C**, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of BC — which is that very altitude. ∎

---

<!-- ===== Distance from the centre ===== -->

What exactly is meant by "the distance of a chord from the centre"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The PERPENDICULAR distance** — measured along the perpendicular dropped from the centre to the chord.
>
> Any other line from the centre to the chord would be longer.

---

State Theorem 6 and give two different proofs.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Chords of equal length are EQUIDISTANT from the centre.**
>
> **Proof 1 (SSS):** `∆CAB ≅ ∆CFG` by SSS (two radii each, plus `AB = FG`). **Congruent triangles have congruent altitudes**, so `CE = CH`.
>
> **Proof 2 (RHS):** the perpendicular from the centre bisects each chord, so `AE = FH`. With `∠CEA = ∠CHF = 90°` and `CA = CF` (radii), **RHS** gives `∆CEA ≅ ∆CHF`, so `CE = CH`. ∎

---

State Theorem 7.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Chords equidistant from the centre have EQUAL length** — the converse of Theorem 6.

---

Derive the chord-length formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> The perpendicular from the centre **bisects** the chord, creating a right triangle with:
> - hypotenuse **r** (a radius)
> - one leg **d** (the distance from the centre)
> - other leg **half the chord**
>
> By Baudhāyana–Pythagoras, half the chord `= √(r² − d²)`, so
>
> **`chord length = 2√(r² − d²)`**

---

Find the chord of a circle of radius 7 cm at perpendicular distance 6 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> `2√(49 − 36) = 2√13 ≈` **7.21 cm**

---

Two parallel chords of 6 cm and 8 cm lie on opposite sides of the centre of a circle of radius 5 cm. Find the distance between their midpoints.

> [!ANSWER]
> - 8 cm chord: half is 4, so `d = √(25 − 16) =` **3 cm**
> - 6 cm chord: half is 3, so `d = √(25 − 9) =` **4 cm**
>
> They're on **opposite sides**, so the distance between midpoints is `3 + 4 =` **7 cm**.
>
> (On the **same** side it would be `4 − 3 = 1 cm` — read the question carefully.)

---

State and prove Theorem 8.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The LONGER chord is CLOSER to the centre.**
>
> **Proof:** by Pythagoras, `CF² + AF² = r²` and `CG² + GD² = r²`, so `CF² + AF² = CG² + GD²`.
>
> If `AB > DE`, then the half-chords give `AF > GD`, so `AF² > GD²` — which forces **`CF² < CG²`**, hence `CF < CG`. ∎

---

What are the two extreme cases of Theorem 8?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Nearest possible chord:** the one **through the centre** — the **diameter**, at distance **0**. So **the diameter is the longest chord**.
> - **Furthest possible:** push a chord outward until it shrinks to a **single point** — length 0, at distance **r**.

---

If chord AB is twice as far from the centre as chord CD, is CD = 2AB?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> The relationship is `chord = 2√(r² − d²)` — **not proportional**. Doubling d does not halve the chord.
>
> **Example:** with `r = 5`, `d = 2` gives chord `2√21 ≈ 9.17`; `d = 4` gives chord `2√9 = 6`. `9.17` is **not** twice 6.

---

<!-- ===== Arcs and angles ===== -->

How is the angle subtended by an arc at the centre defined?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The angle swept as you move from one radius to the other ALONG THAT ARC.**
>
> - **Minor arc** → angle at the centre is **less than 180°**
> - **Major arc** → angle at the centre is **more than 180°** (reflex)

---

State Theorem 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The angle subtended by an arc at the CENTRE is DOUBLE the angle it subtends at any point on the circle OUTSIDE the arc.**
>
> **`angle at centre = 2 × angle at circumference`**

---

Sketch the proof of Theorem 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> Join D to the centre C and extend to meet the circle at E.
>
> - `∆DCB` is **isosceles** (`CB = CD`), so `∠CBD = ∠CDB`. By the **exterior angle theorem**, `∠BCE = ∠CBD + ∠CDB = 2∠BDC`.
> - Similarly `∆ADC` is isosceles, giving `∠ACE = 2∠CDA`.
>
> Adding: `∠BCA = 2(∠BDC + ∠CDA) = 2∠BDA` ∎
>
> **The two isosceles triangles are what make it work** — and they exist because all radii are equal.

---

State the "angles in the same segment" result and explain why it's true.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wherever you place a point D on the circle outside a given arc AB, ∠ADB is exactly the same.**
>
> **Why:** every such angle is **half the SAME fixed central angle** — and half of a fixed number is a fixed number.

---

Why does this property distinguish the circle from other shapes?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **for points INSIDE or OUTSIDE the circle, the angles all differ**.
>
> Only for points **on the circle, in the same segment**, are the angles all equal. No other shape has that property.

---

State and prove the semicircle corollary.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The angle subtended by a DIAMETER at any point on the circle is 90°.**
>
> **Proof:** a diameter's arc subtends a **straight angle, 180°**, at the centre. By Theorem 9, the angle at the circumference is **half of that — 90°**. ∎
>
> (A **corollary** is a fact that follows immediately from an already-proved result.)

---

In a circle of radius 12 cm, a central angle AOB is 60°. Find the chord AB.

> [!ANSWER]
> `∆AOB` has `OA = OB = 12` (radii) with the included angle **60°**.
>
> Being isosceles, its other two angles are equal and sum to `180 − 60 = 120`, so **each is 60°** — the triangle is **EQUILATERAL**.
>
> **AB = 12 cm.**

---

X and Y are on the same side of chord AB, both on the circle. Can ∠AXB differ from ∠AYB?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No** — they are angles in the **same segment**, so they are **equal**.
>
> (If X and Y were on **opposite** sides, the angles would be supplementary rather than equal.)

---

<!-- ===== Concyclic points ===== -->

What does "concyclic" mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Lying on the same circle.**

---

State Theorem 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> **If a segment AB subtends EQUAL angles at two points C and D on the SAME SIDE of AB, then A, B, C and D are concyclic.**

---

Prove Theorem 10 by elimination.

> [!ANSWER]
> A, B, C are non-collinear, so a **unique circle** passes through them (Theorem 1). Suppose D is **not** on it.
>
> - **If D is OUTSIDE:** AD cuts the circle at E. Then `∠AEB` is an **exterior angle** of `∆BED`, so `∠AEB > ∠ADB`. But `∠AEB = ∠ACB` (same segment) and `∠ACB = ∠ADB` (given) — giving **`∠ACB > ∠ACB`**, impossible.
> - **If D is INSIDE:** the same argument with the roles reversed is equally impossible.
>
> **Both options are eliminated, so D must lie ON the circle.** ∎

---

What is a cyclic quadrilateral?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A quadrilateral whose four vertices are concyclic** — all lying on one circle.

---

State and prove Theorem 11.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The OPPOSITE angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°.**
>
> **Proof:** `∠BAD` is half the angle arc BCD subtends at the centre (the **reflex** ∠BOD); `∠BCD` is half the angle arc BAD subtends (the ordinary ∠BOD).
>
> Those two central angles together make a **complete rotation, 360°**.
>
> So `∠BAD + ∠BCD = ½ × 360° =` **180°** ∎

---

State Theorem 12.

> [!ANSWER]
> **If two opposite angles of a quadrilateral add up to 180°, its vertices are CONCYCLIC** — the converse of Theorem 11.

---

Can a cyclic quadrilateral have angles 80°, 110°, 100° and 70° in order?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> `∠A + ∠C = 80 + 100 =` **180°** ✓ and `∠B + ∠D = 110 + 70 =` **180°** ✓
>
> Both pairs of opposite angles sum to 180°, so by **Theorem 12** such a quadrilateral is cyclic and can be drawn.

---

Could a cyclic quadrilateral have angles 90°, 80°, 100° and 90° in order?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> `∠A + ∠C = 90 + 100 =` **190°** ✗ — not 180°, so the four vertices cannot lie on a circle.
>
> (Notice all four still add to 360°, as any quadrilateral's must — that check alone is **not enough**.)

---

Summarise all twelve theorems.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1   unique circle through 3 non-collinear points
> 2   equal chords  → equal angles at the centre
> 3   equal angles at the centre → equal chords
> 4   centre to midpoint of chord ⟂ chord
> 5   perpendicular from centre bisects the chord
> 6   equal chords are equidistant from the centre
> 7   chords equidistant from the centre are equal
> 8   the longer chord is nearer the centre
> 9   angle at centre = 2 × angle at circumference
>     ⇒ angles in the same segment are EQUAL
>     ⇒ angle in a semicircle is 90°
> 10  equal angles on the same side ⇒ concyclic
> 11  opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°
> 12  opposite angles summing to 180° ⇒ concyclic
>
> chord length = 2√(r² − d²)
> ```
