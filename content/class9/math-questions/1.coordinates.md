<!-- ===== What coordinates are ===== -->

What is a coordinate system?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A structured framework — like the grid lines on a map or graph paper — that lets us use NUMBERS to describe the exact physical location of a point.**

---

How was the Sindhu-Sarasvatī Civilisation already using a coordinate system?

> [!ANSWER]
> Its city streets were laid out with striking precision **North–South and East–West, about 10 metres apart**.
>
> A merchant could find a shop or warehouse by **counting N–S and E–W units of distance from the city centre** — which is exactly what coordinates do.

---

Why would the Cartesian plane be impossible without Brahmagupta's work?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **Brahmagupta formalised zero and negative numbers as algebraic entities**.
>
> In a coordinate system the **origin IS zero** and the **negative axes ARE values less than zero** — without those ideas you could describe only one quadrant.

---

What did Āryabhaṭa and Al-Bīrūnī each contribute to coordinates?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Āryabhaṭa** (c. 499 CE) replaced the Greek 'chords' with **'sines'**, making it far easier to calculate the coordinates of a star or a city, and mapped the sky using **celestial coordinates**.
> - **Al-Bīrūnī** (c. 1000 CE) travelled to India, studied the Siddhāntas, used Indian trigonometric methods to compute the coordinates of cities across Asia, and perfected the **astrolabe** so sailors could find their position from the stars.

---

Why is the coordinate plane called "Cartesian"?

> [!ANSWER]
> After **René Descartes**, who in **1637** (following Fermat's related work in 1636) formalised the fact that **any point in a plane can be defined by just two numbers** — its distances from two perpendicular axes.

---

<!-- ===== The plane ===== -->

Name the parts of the coordinate plane.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **x-axis** — the horizontal line
> - **y-axis** — the vertical line
> - **Origin O** — where they cross, with coordinates **(0, 0)**
> - **Cartesian plane** (also coordinate plane or xy-plane) — the plane they sit in
> - **Quadrants** — the four parts the axes divide it into

---

What do the two numbers in `P (x, y)` actually measure?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **x** is the perpendicular distance of P from the **y-axis**, measured along the x-axis
> - **y** is the perpendicular distance of P from the **x-axis**, measured along the y-axis
>
> **Note the crossover:** the x-coordinate is measured *from the y-axis*, not from the x-axis.

---

State the sign conventions.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Right of O and upwards are POSITIVE; left of O and downwards are NEGATIVE.**

---

Give the sign pattern for each quadrant.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Quadrant | x | y |
> |---|---|---|
> | **I** | + | + |
> | **II** | **−** | + |
> | **III** | **−** | **−** |
> | **IV** | + | **−** |

---

Which quadrant is each point in: (3, −5), (−5, 3), (−2, −6), (7, 2)?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `(3, −5)` → **Quadrant IV**
> - `(−5, 3)` → **Quadrant II**
> - `(−2, −6)` → **Quadrant III**
> - `(7, 2)` → **Quadrant I**

---

What is the x-coordinate of any point on the y-axis? And the y-coordinate of a point on the x-axis?

> [!ANSWER]
> - A point on the **y-axis** has **x = 0** → its form is **(0, y)**
> - A point on the **x-axis** has **y = 0** → its form is **(x, 0)**
>
> **Why:** a point on the y-axis is **zero distance from the y-axis**, and the x-coordinate *is* that distance.

---

Does `(x, y)` ever equal `(y, x)`?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only when x = y.**
>
> `(3, 5)` and `(5, 3)` are two completely different points. The pair is **ordered**, so swapping generally moves the point — except along the 45° line through the origin, where x and y are equal.

---

What are the coordinates of the point where the two axes intersect?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(0, 0)** — the **origin**. It is zero distance from both axes.

---

Point W has x-coordinate −5. What can you say about a point H on the line through W parallel to the y-axis?

> [!ANSWER]
> **H also has x-coordinate −5** — the line is vertical, so every point on it is 5 units to the left of the y-axis.
>
> **H can be in Quadrant II** (if y is positive) or **Quadrant III** (if y is negative), or on the x-axis if y = 0.

---

<!-- ===== Distance ===== -->

How do you find the distance between two points that lie on a horizontal line?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Subtract the x-coordinates and take the absolute value: `|x₂ − x₁|`.**
>
> The y-coordinates are equal, so no vertical distance is involved.

---

State the distance formula and explain where it comes from.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`Distance = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]`**
>
> **Why:** drop a horizontal and a vertical line to form a **right-angled triangle**. Its legs are the **horizontal shift `x₂ − x₁`** and the **vertical shift `y₂ − y₁`**, and the distance is the **hypotenuse**.
>
> The **Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem** (Class 8) then gives the formula directly.

---

Why doesn't it matter whether `x₂ − x₁` comes out negative?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **squaring removes the sign**.
>
> We're only measuring **how far** the shift is along each axis, not in which direction.

---

Find the distance between A (3, 4) and D (7, 1).

> [!ANSWER]
> Horizontal shift `= 7 − 3 = 4`; vertical shift `= 4 − 1 = 3`.
>
> `AD = √(4² + 3²) = √25 =` **5 units**

---

Find DM and MA for D (7, 1), M (9, 6) and A (3, 4).

> [!ANSWER]
> - `DM = √(2² + 5²) =` **√29 units**
> - `MA = √(6² + 2²) =` **√40 units**

---

Find the distance between (−3, −4) and (6, 8).

> [!ANSWER]
> Shifts: `6 − (−3) = 9` and `8 − (−4) = 12`.
>
> `√(9² + 12²) = √(81 + 144) = √225 =` **15 units**

---

<!-- ===== Reflection ===== -->

Triangle ADM with A (3, 4), D (7, 1), M (9, 6) is reflected in the y-axis. Give the images and the new side lengths.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Images:** A′ (−3, 4), D′ (−7, 1), M′ (−9, 6) — the x-coordinates change sign.
>
> **Side lengths are UNCHANGED:** A′D′ = 5, D′M′ = √29, M′A′ = √40.

---

State the reflection rules, and say why lengths are preserved.

> [!ANSWER]
> - Reflection in the **y-axis**: `(x, y) → (−x, y)`
> - Reflection in the **x-axis**: `(x, y) → (x, −y)`
>
> **Lengths are preserved** because the distance formula uses **squared differences** — and the difference of two numbers is unchanged in size when both change sign together.

---

<!-- ===== Midpoint ===== -->

Give the midpoint formula and explain it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The midpoint of `(x₁, y₁)` and `(x₂, y₂)` is `( (x₁+x₂)/2 , (y₁+y₂)/2 )`.**
>
> **Why:** the midpoint is **halfway across and halfway up** — and "halfway between two numbers" is simply their **average**.

---

Is (3, 4) the midpoint of (2, 3) and (4, 5)? Is (0, −2) the midpoint of (−8, 7) and (6, −3)?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `(2+4)/2 = 3` ✓ and `(3+5)/2 = 4` ✓ → **yes**
> - `(−8+6)/2 = −1`, not 0 → **no**

---

Is (0, 0) the midpoint of (−3, 0) and (3, 0)? Is (0, 5) the midpoint of (0, 0) and (0, −10)?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `(−3+3)/2 = 0` ✓ and `(0+0)/2 = 0` ✓ → **yes**
> - `(0 + (−10))/2 = −5`, not 5 → **no** (the midpoint is (0, −5))

---

M (−7, 1) is the midpoint of A (3, −4) and B (x, y). Find B.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (3 + x)/2 = −7  →  3 + x = −14  →  x = −17
> (−4 + y)/2 = 1  →  −4 + y = 2   →  y = 6
> ```
>
> **B = (−17, 6)**

---

P and Q trisect AB with P nearer A. Find them for A (4, 7) and B (16, −2).

> [!ANSWER]
> The total shift is `(16 − 4, −2 − 7) = (+12, −9)`, so **each third is `(+4, −3)`**.
>
> - **P = (4 + 4, 7 − 3) = (8, 4)**
> - **Q = (8 + 4, 4 − 3) = (12, 1)**
>
> Check: Q + (4, −3) = (16, −2) = B ✓

<!-- ===== Collinearity ===== -->

How can you check whether three points lie on a straight line, without plotting them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Find all three distances. The points are collinear exactly when the LONGEST equals the SUM of the other two.**
>
> **Why:** if they're in a line, one point lies between the other two, so the two short journeys add up to the long one. If they form a genuine triangle, the two shorter sides together are **longer** than the third.

---

Are M (−3, −4), A (0, 0) and G (6, 8) collinear?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `MA = √(3² + 4²) =` **5**
> - `AG = √(6² + 8²) =` **10**
> - `MG = √(9² + 12²) =` **15**
>
> `5 + 10 = 15` ✓ → **yes, they are collinear**, with A between M and G.

---

Are R (−5, −1), B (−2, −5) and C (4, −12) collinear?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `RB = √(3² + 4²) =` **5**
> - `BC = √(6² + 7²) = √85 ≈` **9.22**
> - `RC = √(9² + 11²) = √202 ≈` **14.21**
>
> `5 + 9.22 = 14.22`, but `RC = 14.21` — **not equal**, so **not collinear**.
>
> They are very nearly in a line, which is exactly why plotting alone wouldn't settle it — the arithmetic does.

---

<!-- ===== Circles ===== -->

Show that A (1, −8), B (−4, 7) and C (−7, −4) lie on a circle centred at the origin, and give its radius.

> [!ANSWER]
> Distance from O for each:
>
> - `A: √(1 + 64) = √65`
> - `B: √(16 + 49) = √65`
> - `C: √(49 + 16) = √65`
>
> **All three are the same distance from O**, so they lie on a circle of **radius √65 ≈ 8.06**.

---

Do D (−5, 6) and E (0, 9) lie inside, on, or outside that circle?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **D:** `√(25 + 36) = √61 ≈ 7.81` — **less** than 8.06 → **inside**
> - **E:** distance **9** — **more** than 8.06 → **outside**
>
> **The test is simply: compare the distance from the centre with the radius.**

---

A screen 800 pixels wide and 600 high has its origin at the bottom-left. A circle of radius 80 is centred at A (100, 150). Does any part of it fall off the screen?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The circle spans x from `100 − 80 = 20` to `100 + 80 = 180` — inside 0 to 800 ✓ — and y from `150 − 80 = 70` to `150 + 80 = 230` — inside 0 to 600 ✓
>
> **The test:** the circle stays on screen if the centre is at least one radius from every edge.

---

A second circle of radius 100 is centred at B (250, 230). Do the two circles intersect?

> [!ANSWER]
> Distance between centres `= √((250−100)² + (230−150)²) = √(150² + 80²) = √(22500 + 6400) = √28900 =` **170**.
>
> Sum of radii `= 80 + 100 =` **180**. Since `170 < 180`, **the circles DO overlap**.
>
> **The rule:** two circles intersect when the distance between centres is **less than the sum of the radii** (and more than their difference).

<!-- ===== Shapes ===== -->

Plot A (2, 1), B (−1, 2), C (−2, −1), D (1, −2). Is ABCD a square? Find its area.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sides:** `AB = √(3² + 1²) = √10`, `BC = √(1² + 3²) = √10`, `CD = √10`, `DA = √10` — **all equal** ✓
>
> **Diagonals:** `AC = √(4² + 2²) = √20`, `BD = √(2² + 4²) = √20` — **equal** ✓
>
> **Equal sides + equal diagonals ⇒ a square** (Class 8, Chapter 4).
>
> **Area = (side)² = (√10)² =** **10 square units**

---

R (3, 0), A (0, −2), M (−5, −2), P (−5, 2) are joined in order. Which sides are parallel to an axis, and which points are mirror images?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **AM is horizontal** (both have y = −2) → **parallel to the x-axis**
> - **MP is vertical** (both have x = −5) → **parallel to the y-axis**
> - **M (−5, −2) and P (−5, 2)** are mirror images **in the x-axis** — same x, opposite y
>
> AM and MP meet at M and are **perpendicular** to each other.

---

A city's two main roads cross at the centre, running N–S and E–W, with all other streets parallel and 200 m apart, 10 in each direction. How many intersections are labelled (4, 3)? And (3, 4)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Exactly ONE each** — and **they are different intersections**.
>
> `(4, 3)` is where the **4th N–S street** meets the **3rd E–W street**; `(3, 4)` is where the **3rd N–S** meets the **4th E–W**.
>
> **This is the point of ordered pairs:** the label pins down a unique crossing, and swapping the numbers names a different one.

---

What would a coordinate system be like with no negative numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You could describe only ONE quadrant** — everything up and to the right of the origin.
>
> Points to the left or below could not be named at all, so it would **not** let you locate every point on the plane.
>
> That's exactly why **Brahmagupta's formalisation of zero and negatives** was the missing piece.
