<!-- ===== Perimeter ===== -->

Define perimeter, and give the formulas for a square, an equilateral triangle and a rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The total length around a shape's border** — the distance an insect walks going once round without turning back.
>
> - Square, side a → **4a**
> - Equilateral triangle, side a → **3a**
> - Rectangle, sides a and b → **2(a + b)**

---

How is the square's perimeter formula a special case of the rectangle's?

> [!ANSWER]
> Put **`a = b`** in `2(a + b)`:
>
> `2(a + a) = 4a` ✓
>
> A square **is** a rectangle with equal sides, so its formula must fall out of the general one.

---

For a square, what is the ratio of perimeter to side? Does it change with size?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always 4 : 1**, whatever the size.
>
> Double the side and the perimeter doubles too — so the **ratio is fixed**. For an equilateral triangle it's always **3 : 1**.
>
> **That observation is what makes it natural to ask whether circles have a fixed ratio too.**

---

<!-- ===== π ===== -->

State what π is and the two forms of the circumference formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> **π is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter — the SAME for every circle.**
>
> **`C = πd = 2πr`**

---

Describe a home experiment to estimate π.

> [!ANSWER]
> Measure the diameter **D** of a cotton reel as accurately as you can. Wind **thin** thread tightly round it **20 times**, unwind and measure the total length **L**.
>
> Then **`π ≈ L ÷ (20D)`**.
>
> **Why 20 turns:** measuring one turn is too short to be accurate; 20 turns spreads the measurement error over 20 circumferences.

---

How did the Mesopotamians know π must be more than 3?

> [!ANSWER]
> They saw that a circle's perimeter is **slightly larger than that of the hexagon inscribed in it**.
>
> **A regular hexagon inscribed in a circle of radius r has perimeter exactly 6r = 3d** — so the circle's perimeter must exceed 3d, giving **π > 3**. They used `3 + 1/8 = 3.125`.

---

What was Archimedes' method, and what bounds did he get?

> [!ANSWER]
> He **"trapped" π between the perimeters of INSCRIBED and CIRCUMSCRIBED polygons** — the circle must lie between them.
>
> Starting with hexagons he showed `3 < π < 2√3 ≈ 3.46`. Working up to **96-sided polygons**, he got
>
> **`3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7`**

---

Who found 355/113, and why is it remarkable?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Zu Chongzhi** (480 CE), who pushed the polygon method to **24,576 sides**.
>
> `355/113 ≈ 3.1415929` was **the most accurate value in the world for over 800 years** — and it is now known that **no fraction with denominator under 15,000 comes closer to π**.

---

What did Āryabhaṭa mean by calling his value āsanna?

> [!ANSWER]
> **"Approaching" or "approximate".**
>
> He gave `62832/20000 = 3.1416` but explicitly flagged it as approximate — **a profound hint that π cannot be given exactly as a single fraction**, centuries before that was proved.

---

Brahmagupta used √10 for π, which is less accurate than earlier values. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> For its **mathematical elegance and the ease of manipulating it in equations**.
>
> Accuracy isn't the only thing that matters in a working value — and √10 went on to dominate the Arab world and medieval Europe for centuries.

---

What did Mādhava do that everyone before him had not?

> [!ANSWER]
> He realised π is **a LIMIT to be reached, not a fraction to be found** — and gave the **first EXACT formula**, an **infinite series**:
>
> **`π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …`**
>
> **By moving from cutting circles geometrically to summing numbers analytically, he birthed CALCULUS.** His series gave π to **11 decimal places**.

---

Who came after Mādhava on the π problem?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Nīlakaṇṭha** (c. 1500), **Machin** (1706), **Ramanujan** (1914), and the **Chudnovsky brothers** (1988), who extended Ramanujan's ideas.
>
> **Their algorithms let us compute π to hundreds of trillions of digits today.**

---

Who first used the symbol π, and why that letter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **William Jones**, a Welsh mathematician, in **1706** — because π is the first letter of the Greek word **perimetros**, meaning perimeter.
>
> **Leonhard Euler** made the symbol popular.

---

Why is it wrong to write π = 22/7?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **π is IRRATIONAL** — it cannot be written as a ratio of two integers at all (proved by **Lambert, 1761**).
>
> `22/7` is **close but not equal**. Write **`π ≈ 22/7`** and **`π ≠ 22/7`**, exactly as `√2 ≈ 1.414` but `√2 ≠ 1.414`.

---

Why is there no "best fraction" for π?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **however close a fraction gets, another gets closer**.
>
> That's a direct consequence of π being irrational — no fraction ever lands on it, so the sequence of better approximations never ends.

---

Give a mnemonic for the digits of π, and say what Pi Day is.

> [!ANSWER]
> *"How I wish I could recollect pi."* — count the letters: **3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2** → **3.141592**
>
> **14 March** ("3-14") is **Pi Day**; **22 July** ("22-7") is **Pi Approximation Day**.

<!-- ===== Arc length ===== -->

Why must the two semicircles of a circle have equal length?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Reflect the circle in the diameter** — the two semicircles **swap places**.
>
> A reflection doesn't change lengths, so they must be equal. Each is `2πr ÷ 2 =` **πr**.
>
> (Rotating the figure 180° gives the same conclusion.)

---

Give the general arc-length formula and explain it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`arc = 2πr × θ/360`** for an angle θ° at the centre.
>
> **Why:** a semicircle is `2πr × 180/360` and a quarter circle `2πr × 90/360`. **The arc is the same fraction of the circumference as its angle is of 360°.**

---

The perimeter of a circle is 44 cm. Find its radius. (π ≈ 22/7)

> [!ANSWER]
> `2 × (22/7) × r = 44` → `r = 44 × 7 / 44 =` **7 cm**

---

Find the circumference of circles of radius 7 cm, 10 cm and 12 cm, to 3 significant figures.

> [!ANSWER]
> Using `C = 2πr` with π ≈ 22/7:
>
> - `r = 7` → `2 × 22/7 × 7 =` **44.0 cm**
> - `r = 10` → `2 × 22/7 × 10 =` **62.9 cm**
> - `r = 12` → `2 × 22/7 × 12 =` **75.4 cm**

---

Find the arc length for (i) radius 3.5 cm, angle 60°; (ii) radius 6.3 m, angle 120°.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `2 × 22/7 × 3.5 × 60/360 = 22 × (1/6) =` **3.67 cm**
> - `2 × 22/7 × 6.3 × 120/360 = 39.6 × (1/3) =` **13.2 m**

---

Find the perimeter of a sector of radius 14 cm with angle 75°.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A sector's perimeter is the ARC plus TWO RADII.**
>
> Arc `= 2 × 22/7 × 14 × 75/360 = 88 × (75/360) =` 18.33 cm
> Perimeter `= 18.33 + 2(14) =` **46.3 cm**
>
> **Forgetting the two radii is the classic mistake here.**

---

A car tyre has diameter 56 cm. How far does the car go in one revolution, and how many revolutions in 10 km?

> [!ANSWER]
> - One revolution = the circumference `= (22/7) × 56 =` **176 cm = 1.76 m**
> - 10 km = 10,000 m, so revolutions `= 10000 ÷ 1.76 ≈` **5,682 revolutions**

---

The perimeters of two circles are in the ratio 5 : 4. What is the ratio of their radii?

> [!ANSWER]
> **5 : 4**, the same.
>
> **Why:** `C = 2πr`, so circumference is **directly proportional** to radius — the constant `2π` cancels from the ratio.

<!-- ===== The track ===== -->

Verify that a runner 0.3 m from the inner border of the standard track covers 400 m. (Straights 84.39 m, inner radius 36.5 m.)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> straights:  2 × 84.39                          = 168.78 m
> curves:     two semicircles = one full circle
>             radius = 36.5 + 0.3 = 36.8 m
>             2 × 3.1416 × 36.8                  = 231.22 m
> TOTAL                                          = 400.00 m ✓
> ```

---

Why are staggers needed at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> **On the straights every runner covers the same distance.**
>
> **But on the curves the outer runner's semicircles have a bigger radius**, so she travels further. The stagger moves her start forward by exactly that extra distance.

---

Lanes are 1.22 m wide. Find the stagger between adjacent lanes, and say whether it's the same between every pair.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each lane out increases the radius by **1.22 m**, so the extra circumference is
>
> `2π × 1.22 ≈` **7.67 m**
>
> **The same between every pair of adjacent lanes** — because each adds the same 1.22 m to the radius, and the extra `2π × 1.22` doesn't depend on the radius you started from.

---

A school builds a 200 m track instead of a 400 m one. Does it need a smaller stagger for the 4 × 100 m relay?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — the stagger is the same.**
>
> **Why:** the stagger depends only on the **lane WIDTH** (`2π × width`), not on the radius of the track. A smaller track has tighter curves, but the extra distance per lane is unchanged.

<!-- ===== Perimeter puzzles ===== -->

Two circles of radius r each pass through the other's centre. Find the perimeter of the shape they form.

> [!ANSWER]
> The triangle joining the two centres A, B to an intersection point C has `AB = AC = BC = r` — it is **equilateral**, so `∠CAB = 60°`.
>
> Similarly `∠BAD = 60°`, giving `∠CAD =` **120°**.
>
> So each **dotted** arc is `120/360 = ⅓` of its circle, and each **red** arc is **⅔**.
>
> **Total perimeter `= 2 × ⅔ × 2πr =` 8πr/3**

---

Points P and Q are joined by one big semicircle, and also by three smaller semicircles side by side. Which path is longer?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are exactly EQUAL.**
>
> With radii `a′` and `b′, c′, d′`, the paths are `πa′` and `π(b′ + c′ + d′)`.
>
> **But `PQ = 2a′` and also `PQ = 2b′ + 2c′ + 2d′`**, so `a′ = b′ + c′ + d′` — and the two lengths match.
>
> **Why it feels wrong:** the wiggly path *looks* longer. But **arc length is proportional to radius**, so splitting one radius into pieces leaves the total untouched — however many pieces you use.

<!-- ===== Area basics ===== -->

What is the unit of area, and what are the areas of a rectangle, parallelogram and triangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The unit is a 1 × 1 square, of area 1 sq unit.**
>
> - Rectangle → **ab**
> - Parallelogram → **base × height**
> - Triangle → **½ × base × height**

---

Explain why a parallelogram's area is base × height.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Cut off the triangle at one end and slide it to the other.**
>
> The parallelogram becomes a **rectangle with the same base and the same height** — a different shape, but the pieces are unchanged, so the **area is the same**.
>
> Rectangle area = `bh`, so the parallelogram's is too.

---

Can you find a parallelogram's area from its side lengths alone?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> **Push a rectangle over:** the sides stay the same, but the **height shrinks**, so the area falls. Squash it flat and the area goes to zero.
>
> **The angle matters** — so you need the height, not just the sides.

---

Give the "nice" proof of the triangle area formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two congruent copies of any triangle fit together to form a parallelogram** with the same base and height.
>
> So `Area of triangle = ½ × Area of parallelogram =` **½ bh** ∎
>
> (The other route — enclosing the triangle in a rectangle — works too, but needs separate handling for obtuse triangles.)

---

State and prove the median theorem, and say why it's surprising.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A median divides a triangle into two triangles of EQUAL AREA.**
>
> **Proof:** the two halves have **equal bases** (`BD = DC`, since D is the midpoint) and the **same height** — the perpendicular from A to line BC. Area depends on nothing else. ∎
>
> **Why it's surprising:** the two triangles are usually **not congruent** — they can look completely different — yet their areas are exactly equal.

---

In ∆ABC, D is the midpoint of BC and P is any point on median AD. Show area(∆ABP) = area(∆ACP).

> [!ANSWER]
> **PD is a median of ∆PBC** (since D is the midpoint of BC), so `area(∆PBD) = area(∆PCD)`.
>
> **AD is a median of ∆ABC**, so `area(∆ABD) = area(∆ACD)`.
>
> Subtracting: `area(∆ABP) = area(∆ABD) − area(∆PBD) = area(∆ACD) − area(∆PCD) = area(∆ACP)` ∎

---

ABCD is a parallelogram and P, Q are any two points on AB. Compare area(∆PCD) and area(∆QCD).

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are equal.**
>
> Both triangles have the **same base CD** and the **same height** — the distance between the parallel lines AB and CD, which doesn't depend on where P or Q sits.
>
> **Ratio = 1 : 1**

---

Rectangles have perimeter 40 units. Which has the largest area? Which the smallest?

> [!ANSWER]
> With perimeter 40, the sides sum to 20 — so they are `x` and `20 − x`, and `Area = x(20 − x)`.
>
> - **Largest: the SQUARE, 10 × 10, area 100.** (For a fixed sum, the product is greatest when the numbers are equal — Class 8, Chapter 6.)
> - **There is NO smallest.** Make x as tiny as you like and the area approaches 0 without ever reaching it.
>
> **There are infinitely many such rectangles**, since the sides needn't be whole numbers.

<!-- ===== Heron ===== -->

State Heron's formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> For a triangle with sides a, b, c and **semi-perimeter `s = ½(a + b + c)`**:
>
> **`Area = √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)]`**

---

Verify Heron's formula for an equilateral triangle of side a.

> [!ANSWER]
> `s = 3a/2`, so
>
> `Area = √[(3a/2)(a/2)(a/2)(a/2)] = √(3a⁴/16) =` **(√3/4)a²** ✓
>
> Checking by ½ × base × height: `h = (√3/2)a`, so `½ × a × (√3/2)a =` **(√3/4)a²** — the same.

---

Verify Heron's formula for a 3-4-5 triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> `s = 6`, so `Area = √(6 × 3 × 2 × 1) = √36 =` **6 sq units**
>
> **Check:** `3² + 4² = 5²`, so by the converse of Baudhāyana–Pythagoras it's **right-angled**, giving `½ × 3 × 4 =` **6** ✓

---

When is Heron's formula the right tool?

> [!ANSWER]
> **When you know the three SIDES but no height.**
>
> That's exactly the situation for a triangular plot of land measured with a tape — you can walk the boundary but you can't easily measure a perpendicular.

---

Find the area of a triangle with two sides 8 cm and 11 cm and perimeter 32 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> Third side `= 32 − 8 − 11 =` **13 cm**. `s = 16`.
>
> `Area = √(16 × 8 × 5 × 3) = √1920 ≈` **43.8 cm²**

---

A triangular plot has sides in the ratio 3 : 5 : 7 and perimeter 300 m. Find its area.

> [!ANSWER]
> Parts total 15, so each part is `300/15 = 20` → sides **60, 100, 140 m**. `s = 150`.
>
> `Area = √(150 × 90 × 50 × 10) = √6,750,000 ≈` **2598 m²**
>
> (Exactly `1500√3 m²`.)

---

Give the two circle-based area formulas for a triangle, and define the incircle.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **`Area = abc / (4R)`** where R is the radius of the **circumcircle**
> - **`Area = r(a + b + c) / 2`** where r is the radius of the **incircle**
>
> **The INCIRCLE is the one circle that fits tightly inside the triangle, touching all three sides.**

<!-- ===== Brahmagupta ===== -->

Can you find a quadrilateral's area from its four sides? Demonstrate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> A rhombus with all sides 3 can have area **9** (as a square), or **8.01**, or **5.41** — **squash it and the area changes while the sides don't.**
>
> **You need one extra piece of information** — an angle, a diagonal, or a geometric property.

---

State Brahmagupta's formula and its condition.

> [!ANSWER]
> **For a CYCLIC quadrilateral** with sides a, b, c, d and `s = ½(a + b + c + d)`:
>
> **`Area = √[(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)(s − d)]`**
>
> **It only applies to cyclic quadrilaterals** — "cyclic" is the extra information that pins the area down.

---

Verify Brahmagupta's formula for a rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> All rectangles are cyclic. With sides a, b, a, b: `s = a + b`, so
>
> `Area = √[(a+b−a)(a+b−b)(a+b−a)(a+b−b)] = √(b · a · b · a) =` **ab** ✓

---

Show that Heron's formula is a special case of Brahmagupta's.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Set `d = 0`.** The quadrilateral collapses into a **triangle** (vertices A and D coincide), and **every triangle is cyclic** — so Brahmagupta's formula still applies.
>
> Then `s = ½(a + b + c + 0) = ½(a + b + c)` — the triangle's semi-perimeter — and
>
> `√[(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)(s−0)] = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)]`
>
> **which is exactly Heron's formula.** ∎

---

Explain "special case" and "generalisation", with three examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A special case comes from a general result by adding an extra condition; the general result is a generalisation of it.**
>
> - **Square is a special case of rectangle:** `A = a²` from `A = ab` with `b = a`
> - **Isosceles right triangle:** `c = a√2` from `a² + b² = c²` with `a = b`
> - **`(a + b)²` is a special case of `(a + b + c)²`** with `c = 0`
> - **Heron is a special case of Brahmagupta** with `d = 0`

---

A trapezium has parallel sides 40 cm and 20 cm and equal non-parallel sides of 26 cm. Find its area.

> [!ANSWER]
> The overhang on each side is `(40 − 20)/2 =` 10 cm, so the height is
>
> `h = √(26² − 10²) = √(676 − 100) = √576 =` **24 cm**
>
> `Area = ½ × (40 + 20) × 24 =` **720 cm²**

---

A rhombus has one diagonal twice the other and area 128 cm². Find the shorter diagonal.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Area = ½ × d₁ × d₂` (Class 8). With `d₂ = 2d₁`:
>
> `½ × d₁ × 2d₁ = 128` → `d₁² = 128` → `d₁ = √128 ≈` **11.3 cm**
>
> (Exactly `8√2` cm; the longer diagonal is `16√2 ≈ 22.6` cm.)

<!-- ===== Squaring a rectangle ===== -->

What does "squaring a shape" mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Constructing a SQUARE with exactly the same area as the given shape** — using only ruler and compass.
>
> Baudhāyana's *Śhulbasūtra* (800 BCE) gives the construction for a rectangle.

---

Why does Baudhāyana's construction work?

> [!ANSWER]
> It is a **geometric translation of an algebraic identity**:
>
> ```
> AF = (a + b)/2   so   HK = (a + b)/2   (a radius)
> BH = AF − b = (a − b)/2
> By Baudhāyana–Pythagoras in ∆HKP:
> HP² = HK² − BH² = [(a+b)/2]² − [(a−b)/2]²
>     = (a² + 2ab + b²)/4 − (a² − 2ab + b²)/4 = ab
> ```
>
> **So the square on HP has area `ab` — the rectangle's area.** ∎
>
> The identity behind it is **`((a+b)/2)² − ((a−b)/2)² = ab`**.

---

How would you square a triangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **First turn the triangle into a rectangle of the same area** — cut along the mid-height line and rotate the top piece down, giving a rectangle of base b and height `h/2`.
>
> **Then square that rectangle** using Baudhāyana's construction.
>
> **The strategy: reduce the new problem to one you've already solved.**

<!-- ===== Area of a circle ===== -->

Why must a circle's area be proportional to the square of its circumference?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **for any fixed SHAPE, the ratio `P² : A` is the same at every SCALE**:
>
> - Square: `P = 4a`, `A = a²` → `P² : A = 16 : 1`
> - Equilateral triangle: `P = 3a`, `A = (√3/4)a²` → `P² : A = 36 : √3`
>
> The ratio depends only on the shape, **not the size** — so for circles too, `C² : A` must be some fixed constant.

---

What values did the Babylonians and Egyptians use for the circle's area?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Babylonians** (before 1500 BCE): `C² : A ≈ 12 : 1`, so **`A ≈ C²/12`**
> - **Egyptians** (c. 1500 BCE): **`A ≈ (8d/9)² = (256/81)r²`** — and remarkably the **same formula appears in the Baudhāyana Śhulbasūtra** (800 BCE)

---

How did Archimedes state the area of a circle, and what does it give?

> [!ANSWER]
> *"The area of a circle equals the area of a right triangle with legs equal to the radius and the circumference."*
>
> `Area = ½ × circumference × radius = ½ × 2πr × r =` **πr²**

---

What was the key fact behind Archimedes' proof?

> [!ANSWER]
> That **any regular polygon's area is `½ × perimeter × the radius of its incircle`**.
>
> He then asked what happens as **the number of sides grows without limit** — the polygon approaches the circle, its perimeter approaches `2πr`, and its incircle radius approaches r.

---

Describe Nīlakaṇṭha's visual proof of `A = πr²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Slice the circle into thin sectors and lay them alternately point-up and point-down.**
>
> The result approaches a **parallelogram** with:
> - **base = half the circumference = πr** (half the sectors point each way)
> - **height = the radius r**
>
> `Area = base × height = πr × r =` **πr²** ✓
>
> **The thinner the slices, the flatter each arc becomes and the closer the shape is to a true parallelogram.**

---

Define a sector and give its area.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The region bounded by an arc and the two radii to its end points.**
>
> **`Sector area = πr² × θ/360`** — the same fraction of the circle as the arc is of the circumference.

---

Find the area of a quadrant of a circle of radius 14 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> `πr² × 90/360 = (22/7) × 196 × ¼ = 616 × ¼ =` **154 cm²**

---

List every formula from this chapter.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PERIMETER
> square 4a · equilateral triangle 3a · rectangle 2(a + b)
> circle            C = 2πr = πd
> arc (angle θ)     2πr × θ/360
> sector perimeter  2πr × θ/360 + 2r
>
> AREA
> rectangle ab · square a² · parallelogram bh · triangle ½bh
> Heron        √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)],  s = ½(a+b+c)
> Brahmagupta  √[(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)(s−d)],  cyclic only
> triangle     abc/(4R)  and  r(a+b+c)/2
> circle       A = πr²
> sector       πr² × θ/360
> ```
