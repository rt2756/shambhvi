Why do we introduce work and energy when we already have Newton's laws?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because when forces change with time or act in complicated ways, applying Newton's laws directly becomes difficult.**
>
> Work and energy often let us analyse motion **more easily** — for example, they give the speed at the bottom of a curved slide **without following the changing slope at every instant**.
>
> **You only need the start and the end**, not everything in between. That is a genuinely different kind of tool, not just a shortcut.

---

Lifting one bag by 1 m does work W. How much work is done in lifting three such bags together by 1 m, and one bag by 3 m?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both are 3W.**
>
> - **Three bags together by 1 m:** you need a force **3 times larger** over the **same** distance → **3 times the work**.
> - **One bag by 3 m:** the **same** force over a **3 times larger** distance → **3 times the work**.
>
> **The two experiments point the same way, and together they give the definition:** work is proportional to the force **and** to the displacement.

---

Define work done by a constant force and give its SI unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Work done = force applied × displacement IN THE DIRECTION OF THE FORCE.**
> ```
> W = F × s
> ```
> **SI unit: the JOULE (J).**
> ```
> 1 J = 1 N × 1 m = 1 kg m/s² × 1 m = 1 kg m² s⁻²
> ```
> **One joule of work is done when a force of 1 newton displaces an object by 1 metre in the direction of the force.**

---

Why is it not enough to say "30 joules of work was done"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because you must always state WHICH FORCE (or agency) did the work, and ON WHICH OBJECT.**
>
> In the same interaction, one body may do +30 J on another while the second does −30 J on the first. "30 J of work was done" leaves out the two things that make the statement meaningful.
>
> "The goalkeeper did **−30 J of work on the ball**" is complete.

---

How do you find the work done from a force–displacement graph, and why is this method worth knowing?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The work done is the AREA under the line, between the initial and final positions.**
>
> For a constant 10 N acting over 1 m, the area of the rectangle is `10 × 1 =` **10 J**.
>
> **Why it matters: the method still works when the force is NOT constant.** `W = F × s` assumes a single value of F, but the area under a curve needs no such assumption — you just measure the area. That is the graphical view's real payoff.

---

Force and displacement both have direction. Does work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Work is described by **a number with a positive or negative sign**, and nothing more.
>
> The sign tells you whether the displacement was **along** the force (+) or **opposite** to it (−). It is not a direction in space — you cannot say "30 joules towards the east".

---

Name three situations in which the work done is zero.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **F = 0** — no force acts, so no work is done.
> 2. **s = 0** — the object does not move, **however hard the force**. Pushing a rigid wall does **no work on the wall**.
> 3. **The force is PERPENDICULAR to the displacement** — there is no displacement *in the direction of the force*. Carrying a box horizontally while pushing it upwards does no work on the box **by that upward force**.

---

You push a wall with all your strength and get tired, yet the work done is zero. Resolve this.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The work done ON THE WALL is zero, because the wall does not move (s = 0).**
>
> **But you really are spending energy.** To apply a force at all, **the muscles in your body repeatedly expand and contract, using up your body's internal energy.** That is where the tiredness comes from.
>
> **The scientific definition is about the OBJECT, not about your effort.** It measures energy transferred to the wall — and none was.

---

When is work positive and when is it negative? Give an example of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **POSITIVE** — the displacement is in the **same direction** as the force. Pushing a wheelchair forward: your force and its displacement point the same way.
> - **NEGATIVE** — the displacement is **opposite** to the force. A goalkeeper stopping a football applies a force opposite to the ball's motion, so **she does negative work on the ball**.

---

A girl lifts a dumbbell and then slowly lowers it. When does she do positive work and when negative?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Lifting:** her force is **upward** and the displacement is **upward** → **positive work**.
> - **Lowering:** her force is **still upward** (she is holding the dumbbell back, not pushing it down) while the displacement is **downward** → **negative work**.
>
> **The direction of her force never changes** — only the direction of the motion does, and that is what flips the sign.

---

A goalkeeper's hand moves back 15 cm while she applies 200 N to stop a ball. Find the work she does on the ball.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the ball's displacement is opposite to the applied force, so s is negative
>
> W = 200 N × (−0.15 m) = −30 J
> ```
> **She does −30 J of work on the ball**, which is why the ball loses 30 J of kinetic energy and stops.

---

In that same save, does the ball do work on the goalkeeper?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — and it is POSITIVE.**
>
> By Newton's third law, **the ball and the goalkeeper apply equal and opposite forces on each other**, and **both do work on each other**.
>
> - The **ball does positive work on the goalkeeper** — it pushes her hands back, and her hands move in the direction of that push.
> - The **goalkeeper does negative work on the ball** — she pushes forward while the ball moves back.
>
> **Same interaction, opposite signs** — energy leaves the ball and arrives at the goalkeeper.

---

A weightlifter holds a barbell steady above her head for ten seconds. Is she doing work on the barbell?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — zero work, because the displacement is zero.**
>
> She is certainly applying a large force (equal to the barbell's weight), and she will tire, but `W = F × s` with **s = 0** gives **W = 0**.
>
> **Her tiredness is internal energy spent inside her own body**, exactly as with pushing a wall. No energy is being transferred to the barbell — its kinetic and potential energy are unchanged throughout.

---

Is the work done by friction on a stack of coins sliding across a rough surface positive, negative or zero?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Negative.**
>
> **Friction acts opposite to the direction of motion**, so the displacement is opposite to the force. By definition that makes the work negative.
>
> **And the physics matches the sign:** the coins **lose** kinetic energy and come to rest, which is exactly what negative work means.

---

State the work–energy theorem, and explain why it is more powerful than F = ma.

> [!ANSWER]
> **WORK DONE ON AN OBJECT = CHANGE IN ITS ENERGY.**
>
> **Why it is more powerful:**
> - it holds for a **SYSTEM of objects**, not just a single body
> - it holds even when **the forces are NOT constant**, where the kinematic equations fail
> - it lets you jump from the initial state straight to the final state **without tracking the motion in between**
>
> **SI unit of energy is the joule (J)**, the same as for work — as it must be, since work is a change in energy.

---

Where did the energy of a thrown cricket ball and a raised flowerpot come from?

> [!ANSWER]
> **From the work done on them.**
>
> - The ball gained energy from the **work done by the fielder in throwing it**; it can now do work knocking over a wicket.
> - The pot gained energy from the **work done in raising it**; it can now do work damaging something below.
>
> **An object with the capacity to do work is said to possess ENERGY** — and the work–energy theorem says the energy it has is exactly the work that went into it.

---

In a carrom shot the striker hits the white coin, which hits the black coin. Describe the work done and the energy changes.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Interaction | Positive work | Negative work |
> |---|---|---|
> | striker → white coin | **white coin gains energy** | **striker loses energy** |
> | white coin → black coin | **black coin gains energy** | **white coin loses energy** |
>
> **Why the negative half must be there:** by **Newton's third law** the white coin pushes back on the striker, **opposite to the striker's motion** — so it does negative work on the striker.
>
> **Energy is handed on, never created.** Every gain by one body is a loss by another.

---

Name four ways energy can be transferred other than by doing mechanical work.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **As HEAT** — when two objects at different temperatures touch, energy flows from the hotter to the colder.
> - **As RADIATION** — with no contact at all; this is how the **Sun's energy reaches the Earth**.
> - **Through ELECTRIC CIRCUITS.**
> - **Via SOUND WAVES.**
> - **In NUCLEAR REACTIONS**, such as those that power the Sun.
>
> **Doing mechanical work is only one route among several.**

---

Name six forms of energy and say what each is.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Form | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | **Mechanical** | energy due to the **motion or position** of objects |
> | **Thermal** | energy that makes things **warm or hot** |
> | **Light** | energy that allows us to **see** |
> | **Sound** | energy of **vibrations** of air or other molecules |
> | **Electrical** | energy related to the **position or motion of charges** |
> | **Chemical** | energy stored in fuels and food, in the **bonds between atoms** |
> | **Nuclear** | energy stored in the **nuclei of atoms** |

Identify the energy transformation in each: a truck moving uphill, photosynthesis, water flowing from a dam, a firecracker, a microphone, a solar panel.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Situation | Transformation |
> |---|---|
> | **Truck moving uphill** | **chemical** (fuel) → **mechanical** (kinetic + potential) + thermal |
> | **Unwinding of a watch spring** | stored **potential (elastic)** → **mechanical** |
> | **Photosynthesis** | **light** → **chemical** |
> | **Water flowing from a dam** | **potential** → **kinetic** |
> | **Burning matchstick** | **chemical** → **thermal + light** |
> | **Firecracker exploding** | **chemical** → **thermal + light + sound + kinetic** |
> | **Speaking into a microphone** | **sound** → **electrical** |
> | **Glowing electric bulb** | **electrical** → **light + thermal** |
> | **Solar panel** | **light** → **electrical** |

---

When you pedal a bicycle on a flat road, in what forms does your muscular energy appear?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Kinetic energy** of you and the bicycle, as you speed up
> - **Thermal energy** from friction — in the chain, the bearings, the brakes, between the tyres and the road, and in the air pushed aside
> - **Sound energy** — the whirr of the chain and tyres
> - a good deal of **thermal energy in your own body**, which is why you get hot
>
> **On a FLAT road there is no gain in potential energy at all**, and once you are at a steady speed the kinetic energy is not increasing either — so at cruising speed **essentially all your effort is going into overcoming friction and air resistance.**

---

Derive the expression for kinetic energy.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take mass m, initial velocity u, final velocity v, displacement s, under a **constant** force F (so a is constant and the kinematic equations apply):
> ```
> from v² = u² + 2as :      s = (v² − u²) / (2a)
>
> W = F × s = m a × (v² − u²)/(2a)
>
> W = ½ m (v² − u²)
> ```
> By the work–energy theorem this **is** the change in the object's energy. **Setting u = 0 makes it the final kinetic energy:**
> ```
> K = ½ m v²
> ```
> **A stationary object is defined to have zero kinetic energy**, which is what lets us treat the u = 0 case as "the whole of K".

---

If a vehicle's speed doubles, what happens to its kinetic energy?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It becomes FOUR times as large.**
> ```
> ½ m (2v)² = ½ m × 4v² = 4 × ½ m v²
> ```
> **Because kinetic energy depends on the SQUARE of the velocity.**
>
> **This is the same v² that made stopping distance quadruple in Chapter 4** — seen from the energy side. The brakes must dispose of four times as much energy, so they need four times the distance to do it at the same force.

---

Find the kinetic energy of a 0.2 kg cricket ball bowled at 154.8 km/h.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> v = 154.8 km/h = 154.8 × 5/18 = 43 m/s
>
> K = ½ m v² = ½ × 0.2 × 43² = 0.1 × 1849 = 184.9 J
> ```

---

Two objects of mass m and 4m have the same kinetic energy. Find the ratio of their speeds.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ½ m v1²  =  ½ (4m) v2²
>      v1² =  4 v2²
>      v1  =  2 v2
> ```
> **The ratio is 2 : 1** — the lighter object moves twice as fast.
>
> **Why not four times:** kinetic energy depends on **v squared**, so a four-fold mass difference is offset by only a **two-fold** speed difference.

---

Does the kinetic energy of an object moving with constant velocity change as its position changes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> `K = ½ m v²` depends **only on the mass and the speed**, neither of which changes. **Position does not appear in the formula at all.**
>
> (Its **potential** energy would change if the height changed — but that is a different quantity.)

---

A 15,000 kg jet is stopped in 100 m by an arrester wire exerting 367,500 N. Find its landing speed.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> work done by the wire = F × s = 367,500 × (−100) = −36,750,000 J
>                                 (force opposite to the displacement → negative)
>
> change in kinetic energy = 0 − ½ × 15,000 × v²
>
> by the work-energy theorem:
>       −½ × 15,000 × v² = −36,750,000
>                     v² = 2 × 36,750,000 / 15,000 = 4900
>                      v = 70 m/s = 252 km/h
> ```
> **Notice how little you needed to know** — nothing about how the deceleration varied second by second, only the force, the distance and the two end states.

---

Where does the energy that shoots a slingshot stone or a bow's arrow come from?

> [!ANSWER]
> **From the stretched band or the bent bow, which had the capacity to do work BECAUSE OF THEIR SHAPE.**
>
> **The work you did in deforming them was STORED in them.** When released, they return to their original shape and transfer that stored energy to the stone or arrow as **kinetic energy**.
>
> **Why storage is possible:** you had to apply an external force to overcome the **internal forces** in the material. Remove your force and those internal forces **undo the deformation, doing work in the process**.

---

Define potential energy, and give three different ways it can be stored.

> [!ANSWER]
> **POTENTIAL ENERGY is the energy stored by an object because of its DEFORMATION, or by a system of objects because of their RELATIVE POSITIONS.**
>
> - **Deformation:** a stretched rubber band, a bent bow, a compressed spring
> - **Magnetic:** two magnets with unlike poles pulled apart — released, they rush together with enough kinetic energy to crush a pea placed between them
> - **Electric:** two separated electric charges
> - **Gravitational:** a ball raised above the Earth
>
> **The general rule: whenever a system of objects interacts through forces — gravitational, electric or magnetic — it can store energy in the arrangement of those objects.**

---

A heavy ball is dropped into sand from 1 m and then from 2 m. Which makes the deeper depression, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The 2 m drop makes the DEEPER depression.**
>
> **Why:** raising the ball to a greater height requires **more work**, so the ball possesses **more energy** at that height. When released, that extra energy is used to do more work on the sand, digging a deeper hole.
>
> **The depth of the depression is a visible measure of the stored energy** — which is exactly what makes this a useful experiment rather than just a demonstration.

---

Derive U = mgh, and state its limitation.

> [!ANSWER]
> Define the potential energy as **zero on the ground**. To raise an object of mass m slowly to a height h, you must apply an upward force equal to **mg** through a displacement **h**:
> ```
> W = force × displacement = mg × h
> ```
> By the work–energy theorem this work appears as a change in the object's potential energy:
> ```
> U = m g h        (SI unit: joule)
> ```
> **Limitation: valid only NEAR the Earth's surface**, where **g is nearly constant**. Further out, **g decreases** and this formula no longer applies.

---

Does the potential energy of an object change if it moves horizontally at constant velocity? What if it is raised vertically?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Horizontal motion: NO change.** `U = mgh` depends only on the **height**, and h is unchanged.
> - **Raised vertically: YES**, it **increases**, by exactly `mg × (rise)`.
>
> **This is why the work done in carrying a box across a level room is zero** — force up, motion sideways, no change in height, no energy transferred.

---

A 200 g ball is thrown 10 m up. Find its potential energy at the top. (g = 10 m/s²)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> U = mgh = 0.2 kg × 10 m/s² × 10 m = 20 J
> ```

---

Show that for a freely falling object the mechanical energy stays constant.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Drop an object of mass m from height h (so u = 0).**
> ```
> At the start (A):
>     potential energy  = mgh
>     kinetic energy    = 0
>     MECHANICAL ENERGY = mgh
>
> After falling for time t (at B):
>     v  = u + gt = gt
>     h' = h − ½ g t²
>
>     potential energy  = mgh' = mgh − ½ m g² t²
>     kinetic energy    = ½ m v² = ½ m g² t²
>     MECHANICAL ENERGY = mgh − ½ m g² t² + ½ m g² t² = mgh
> ```
> **The kinetic energy GAINED (½ m g² t²) is exactly the potential energy LOST.**
>
> **CONSERVATION OF MECHANICAL ENERGY: as an object moves under gravity, its mechanical energy stays constant provided no other external forces act.**

---

Show that the mechanical energy of a ball dropped from height h is still mgh just before it hits the ground.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Just before impact, h' = 0, so all the potential energy has gone.** Use `v² = u² + 2gh` with u = 0:
> ```
> v² = 2gh
>
> kinetic energy   = ½ m v² = ½ m (2gh) = mgh
> potential energy = 0
>
> MECHANICAL ENERGY = mgh + 0 = mgh
> ```
> **Exactly the value it started with** — the energy has simply moved from the potential column to the kinetic column.

---

Describe the energy changes at the three positions of a swinging pendulum, and say why it eventually stops.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Point | Potential energy | Kinetic energy |
> |---|---|---|
> | **P** — released, at height h | **mgh — maximum** | **zero** |
> | **Q** — the lowest point | **zero** | **maximum** |
> | **R** — the far side, where it stops | **mgh again** | **zero** |
>
> **The bob rising to almost exactly the same height on the other side is the evidence** that its mechanical energy is unchanged.
>
> **Why "almost", and why it eventually stops:** **friction at the support and air resistance** continuously remove energy from the mechanical account, turning it into thermal energy. **Work done against friction is not stored anywhere** and cannot be recovered.

---

Find the speed of a child at the bottom of a frictionless slide of height h, and say what it does and does not depend on.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> potential energy at the top = kinetic energy at the bottom
>
>            m g h = ½ m v²
>                v = √(2 g h)
> ```
> **The MASS CANCELS.** So the speed depends **only on the height h**.
>
> **It does NOT depend on:**
> - the **mass** of the child — two children of different masses arrive at the **same speed**
> - the **shape** of the slide — straight, curved or wavy makes no difference
>
> **Of several slides, the TALLEST gives the largest speed at the bottom, however it is shaped.**

---

Why is the energy method so much easier than Newton's laws for the slide problem?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a slide's slope changes continuously**, so the component of gravity along the motion changes at every point. Using Newton's laws would mean tracking that changing force through every instant of the descent.
>
> **The energy method needs only the START and the END:** the child begins with mgh of potential energy and ends with ½mv² of kinetic energy, and everything in between cancels out.
>
> **By keeping track of the total mechanical energy, we find the final speed without working through the intermediate motion at all.**

---

A 10,000 kg truck at 72 km/h runs onto a 30° sand escape ramp where sand exerts 50,000 N. Find the minimum ramp length. (g = 10 m/s²; the truck rises 1 m for every 2 m along the ramp.)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> v = 72 km/h = 20 m/s
>
> initial energy = ½ × 10,000 × 20² + 0 = 2,000,000 J
> final energy   = 0 + mg(d/2) = 10,000 × 10 × d/2 = 50,000 d
>
> work done by sand = −50,000 d          (opposite to the motion)
>
> work-energy theorem:  −50,000 d = 50,000 d − 2,000,000
>                        2,000,000 = 100,000 d
>                                d = 20 m
> ```
> **The ramp must be at least 20 m long.**
>
> **Notice it does the job in two independent ways at once:** the sand's friction removes energy **and** climbing converts kinetic energy into potential energy. That is why an escape ramp is both **uphill** and **full of gravel** — each halves the length needed.

---

On a science-park roller coaster the ball is released from the highest point A, runs through low point B and rises to C, then D and E which are each lower than the last. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **At A:** maximum **potential** energy, zero **kinetic** energy.
> **At B (the low point):** potential energy has been converted into **kinetic** energy — fastest here.
> **At C:** kinetic energy converts back into potential energy as the ball rises again.
>
> **Why C, D and E get progressively LOWER:** **friction and air resistance** continuously convert some mechanical energy into **thermal energy**, which cannot be recovered. With less mechanical energy left, the ball can only climb to a **lower** height each time.
>
> **On a perfectly frictionless track every hump could be the same height as the first** — the falling heights are a direct measurement of the energy being lost.

---

Define power, give its unit, and explain why running upstairs needs more power than walking.

> [!ANSWER]
> **POWER is the RATE at which work is done.**
> ```
> P = W / t
> ```
> **SI unit: the WATT (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s.**
>
> **Running vs walking upstairs:** the **work done is exactly the same** — same mass, same height, so the same mgh. **But running does it in less time**, and dividing the same work by a smaller t gives a **larger power**.
>
> **More work in the same time, or the same work in less time, both require more power.**

---

A weightlifter raises 75 kg by 2 m in 5 s. Find the power. (g = 10 m/s²)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> W = mgh = 75 × 10 × 2 = 1500 J
> P = W/t = 1500 / 5 = 300 W
> ```

---

A 1000 kg car goes from rest to 72 km/h in 10 s. Find the power of the engine.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> v = 72 km/h = 20 m/s
>
> work done = final KE − initial KE = ½ × 1000 × 20² − 0 = 200,000 J
>
> P = 200,000 / 10 = 20,000 W  (= 20 kW, about 27 hp)
> ```

---

What is horsepower, where does the name come from, and how many watts is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A unit of power still used for car engines and water pumps. 1 hp = 746 W.**
>
> **The name is literal:** in the early days of engines, their power was **compared with that of actual horses** used to pull carriages — the only power source buyers understood.
>
> **The watt itself is named after James Watt**, who invented an efficient steam engine producing rotational motion to turn wheels.

---

What determines the energy needed to raise a flag to the top of a pole? Does raising it faster change the work? What if the speed doubles?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The energy is `mgh`, so it depends only on the mass of the flag, the height of the pole, and g.**
>
> **Raising it slowly or quickly makes NO difference to the work done** — none of those three quantities changes with speed.
>
> **But doubling the speed HALVES the time, so it DOUBLES the power required** (`P = W/t` with the same W and half the t).
>
> **Work and power are genuinely different questions:** *how much* energy, versus *how fast* you deliver it.

---

A crane lifts a mass to the 10th floor in time t, then the same mass to the 20th floor in time 2t. Compare the energy and the power.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> energy:  the height DOUBLES, so the work mgh DOUBLES  →  2W
>
> power:   P1 = W/t      P2 = 2W/2t = W/t     →  THE SAME
> ```
> **Twice the energy, but exactly the same power.**
>
> **Why:** the crane did twice the work, but it also took twice as long, and the two factors cancel in `W/t`. **A more powerful crane is one that could do the second lift in the ORIGINAL time t.**

---

A student of mass 50 kg is lifted 72.5 m by lift, and on another day climbs the same 72.5 m by stairs. Compare the gain in potential energy. (g = 10 m/s²)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> both cases:  U = mgh = 50 × 10 × 72.5 = 36,250 J
> ```
> **The gain is the SAME — 36,250 J — by either route.**
>
> **Conclusion: gravitational potential energy depends only on the CHANGE IN HEIGHT, not on the path taken.** Lift, stairs, ramp, or a long spiral route all give the same answer.
>
> (Climbing the stairs feels far harder because your **muscles** also spend internal energy and work against friction — but the **potential energy gained by the student** is identical.)

---

A man of mass 60 kg rides a 100 kg scooter to a speed v. Next day his 40 kg son rides with him to the same speed in the same time. Find the ratio of fuel used.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> day 1 total mass = 60 + 100 = 160 kg
> day 2 total mass = 60 + 100 + 40 = 200 kg
>
> energy = ½ m v², with the same v both days
>
> ratio = 160 / 200 = 4 / 5
> ```
> **The fuel used is in the ratio 4 : 5.**
>
> **Why the speed does not matter to the ratio:** it is the same on both days, so v² cancels and only the **masses** decide. (This assumes all the fuel energy goes into the scooter's kinetic energy, with no losses to air resistance or friction.)

---

A ball is thrown upwards. At its highest point, is the force on it zero? Is its acceleration zero? What about its kinetic and potential energy?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Force: NOT zero.** Gravity acts on the ball at every instant of the flight, including at the top.
> - **Acceleration: NOT zero.** It is **g, downwards**, throughout.
> - **Kinetic energy: ZERO** — momentarily, because the velocity is zero there.
> - **Potential energy: MAXIMUM** — the ball is at its greatest height.
>
> **The trap is thinking that momentarily zero velocity means zero force or zero acceleration.** It doesn't — the velocity is zero only for an instant precisely *because* the acceleration is still pulling it down.

---

A 2 kg ball is thrown up at 20 m/s and reaches 19.4 m. Find the sign of the work done by gravity each way, and the work done by air resistance. (g = 10 m/s²)

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) Sign of the work done by gravity:**
> - **Upward motion: NEGATIVE** — gravity acts downwards while the displacement is upwards.
> - **Downward motion: POSITIVE** — force and displacement both downwards.
>
> **(ii) Work done by air resistance:**
> ```
> initial kinetic energy = ½ × 2 × 20² = 400 J
> potential energy at the top = mgh = 2 × 10 × 19.4 = 388 J
>
> work done by air resistance = 388 − 400 = −12 J
> ```
> **Air resistance did −12 J of work** — which is why the ball reached 19.4 m instead of the 20 m it would have reached in a vacuum.

---

A 10 kg block has 180 J of kinetic energy at the 0 m mark. A force of 50 N acts along its motion for the first 2 m, then 20 N for the next 2 m. Find its speed at 0 m and at 4 m, and say whether it ever decelerates.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> speed at 0 m:   K = ½ m v²  →  180 = ½ × 10 × v²  →  v² = 36  →  v = 6 m/s
>
> work done = area under the force–displacement graph
>           = (50 × 2) + (20 × 2) = 100 + 40 = 140 J
>
> kinetic energy at 4 m = 180 + 140 = 320 J
>          320 = ½ × 10 × v²  →  v² = 64  →  v = 8 m/s
> ```
> **6 m/s at 0 m, 8 m/s at 4 m.**
>
> **No, the block never decelerates.** The force stays **positive and along the motion** throughout, so the work is always positive and the kinetic energy only ever rises. **It does accelerate less strongly in the second half** — a smaller force means a smaller acceleration — but that is not deceleration.

An astronaut can throw a ball 8 m up on Earth. How high will the same throw go on the Moon, where gravity is one-sixth of Earth's?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> at the top all the kinetic energy has become potential energy:
>       ½ m v² = m g h    →    h = v² / (2g)
>
> the same throw means the same v, so h is inversely proportional to g
>
> g_moon = g/6    →    h_moon = 6 × 8 = 48 m
> ```
> **48 m.**
>
> **Why the mass of the ball is irrelevant:** it cancels on both sides, exactly as it did for the slide.

A 1000 kg car travels at a constant 35 m/s from A to B, then brakes to rest at C. Describe A–B, find the kinetic energy at A, the work done by the brakes, and where the energy goes.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) Between A and B the car moves at a CONSTANT speed of 35 m/s** — this is the driver's **reaction time**, before the brakes are applied at all. Zero acceleration, zero net force.
>
> **(ii)**
> ```
> K at A = ½ × 1000 × 35² = 500 × 1225 = 612,500 J
> ```
> **(iii)** The car ends at rest, so the brakes must remove all of it:
> ```
> work done by the brakes = 0 − 612,500 = −612,500 J
> ```
> **(iv) The kinetic energy transforms mainly into THERMAL energy** — in the brake pads and discs, the tyres and the road surface — plus some **sound**. It is not destroyed, but it is no longer mechanical energy and cannot be recovered.

A 0.5 kg ball starts at rest on a frictionless track where its potential energy is 30 J. Find its speed where the potential energy is 5 J, 21 J and 29 J.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Total mechanical energy = 30 + 0 = 30 J everywhere, since the track is frictionless.**
> ```
> K = 30 − U    and    K = ½ × 0.5 × v² = 0.25 v²,  so  v = 2√K
>
> U = 5 J   →  K = 25 J  →  v = 2√25 = 10 m/s
> U = 21 J  →  K =  9 J  →  v = 2√9  =  6 m/s
> U = 29 J  →  K =  1 J  →  v = 2√1  =  2 m/s
> ```
> **The lower the ball is on the track, the less potential energy it has and the faster it moves** — the two columns always add to 30 J.

A 1.5 kg coconut falls 10 m onto wet sand. Find its speed on impact, and the depth of the depression if the sand resists with 3000 N. (g = 10 m/s²)

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i)**
> ```
> v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 10) = √200 = 14.1 m/s
> ```
> **(ii)**
> ```
> energy of the coconut at impact = mgh = 1.5 × 10 × 10 = 150 J
>
> work done by the sand = resistive force × depth
>                  150 = 3000 × d
>                    d = 0.05 m = 5 cm
> ```
> **The depression is 5 cm deep.**
>
> **Why softer sand would mean a deeper hole:** a smaller resistive force needs a longer distance to absorb the same 150 J — which is the airbag principle once again.

---

What is a simple machine, and what can it and cannot it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A device that makes a task easier by changing the MAGNITUDE or the DIRECTION of the force you have to apply.**
>
> **What it CANNOT do: reduce the total work required.** That is fixed by the task itself.
> ```
> effort = the force you apply
> load   = the force to be overcome
> mechanical advantage = load / effort
> ```
> **Machines do not create energy — they only help us use it more effectively.**

---

What is the mechanical advantage of a fixed pulley, and why use one at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Its mechanical advantage is 1** — the effort and the load are equal in magnitude. **A fixed pulley does not reduce the force required at all.**
>
> **It changes the DIRECTION of the force**, and that is the whole point: **pulling downwards is far easier for us than lifting upwards**, because you can use your own body weight and a natural stance.
>
> **It buys convenience, not force reduction.** (**Movable pulleys and pulley systems**, used in elevators and cranes, do give a mechanical advantage greater than 1.)

---

Derive the mechanical advantage of an inclined plane.

> [!ANSWER]
> Move the load up the plane at constant speed, ignoring friction. Let L be the length of the plane and h the height:
> ```
> work you do on the object         = F' × L
> potential energy the object gains = m g h
>
> by the work-energy theorem:     F' × L = m g h
>                                 mg/F' = L/h
>
> mechanical advantage = load/effort = mg/F' = L / h
> ```
> **Since L is always larger than h, the effort F' is always LESS than mg, so the mechanical advantage is greater than 1.**
>
> **Make L longer — a shallower ramp — and the effort falls further.**

---

A ramp helps raise an object over a 30 cm step and is 40 cm wide. Find its mechanical advantage.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the ramp is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 30 cm and 40 cm:
> L = √(30² + 40²) = √(900 + 1600) = √2500 = 50 cm
>
> mechanical advantage = L/h = 50/30 = 1.67
> ```

---

Why are hill roads built to wind around in gentle slopes rather than going straight up?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a winding road is a LONGER inclined plane with the same height gain, and mechanical advantage = L/h.**
>
> Increasing L while h stays fixed **increases the mechanical advantage**, so the force needed to climb — from the engine, or from your legs — is **much smaller**.
>
> **The price is distance:** you travel far further to reach the same top. **The total work done is unchanged** — the road only trades force for distance, which is the bargain every simple machine makes.

---

Why is climbing an inclined ladder easier than climbing a vertical one to the same floor?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The inclined ladder is a longer path to the same height, so `mechanical advantage = L/h` is greater than 1** and each step needs **less force**.
>
> On a **vertical** ladder, L = h, so the mechanical advantage is exactly **1** — you must lift your entire weight with your arms and legs, with no help at all.
>
> **The work done is the same either way** (`mgh`, same m and h). You simply spread it over more steps.

---

Name the three parts of a lever and write the balance condition.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **FULCRUM** — the fixed point the lever rotates about
> - **LOAD** — the force to be overcome; its distance from the fulcrum is the **LOAD ARM**
> - **EFFORT** — the force applied; its distance from the fulcrum is the **EFFORT ARM**
> ```
> effort × effort arm = load × load arm
>
> mechanical advantage = load/effort = effort arm / load arm
> ```

---

How can a light eraser lift a heavy stapler on a ruler-and-pencil lever?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the eraser is placed far from the fulcrum and the stapler close to it.**
>
> The end where the **smaller force** is applied moves through a **larger distance**, while the end applying the **larger force** moves a **smaller distance**:
> ```
> F1 × d1 = F2 × d2
> ```
> **The work done at one end is transferred to the other.** Increase the effort arm and the same work delivers a much larger force over a shorter distance.
>
> **A lever reduces the FORCE required, not the WORK done.**

---

On a seesaw with seats A, B, D, E and fulcrum C, where AC = EC = 2 m and BC = DC = 1 m, a 15 kg child sits at A. Where should a 30 kg child sit?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> effort × effort arm = load × load arm
> 15 × 2 = 30 × L
>      L = 1 m
> ```
> **At seat D** — 1 m from the fulcrum, on the other side.
>
> **The heavier child sits closer in**, exactly halving the distance because they are twice the mass.

---

A child and an adult of twice the child's weight balance on a sliding-seat seesaw. Describe their positions.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The adult must sit at HALF the child's distance from the fulcrum.**
> ```
> child's weight × d_child = adult's weight × d_adult
>              W × d       = 2W × d_adult
>                 d_adult  = d/2
> ```
> So if the child sits **2 m** from the fulcrum, the adult sits **1 m** on the other side.
>
> **The heavier side is always the shorter arm** — that is the whole content of `effort × effort arm = load × load arm`.

---

Why is it easier to open a can lid using a spoon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The spoon acts as a LEVER.** The rim of the can is the **fulcrum**, the lid is the **load** just beside it, and your hand applies the **effort** at the far end of the handle.
>
> **The effort arm (the spoon's handle) is much longer than the load arm (the short distance from the rim to the lid)**, so `mechanical advantage = effort arm / load arm` is large, and a small push produces a large lifting force on the lid.
>
> **The cost, as always: your hand moves a long way while the lid moves only a little.**

---

Why do you push an object closer to the fulcrum of a pair of scissors when cutting something hard?

> [!ANSWER]
> **To shorten the LOAD ARM, which increases the mechanical advantage.**
> ```
> mechanical advantage = effort arm / load arm
> ```
> Your fingers stay at the handles, so the **effort arm is fixed**. Moving the object closer to the pivot makes the **load arm smaller**, and a smaller denominator gives a **larger mechanical advantage** — so the same squeeze of your fingers delivers a much larger cutting force.
>
> **The blade tip is for delicate cuts; the base of the blades is for hard ones.**

Name the three classes of lever with examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Class | What is in the middle | Examples |
> |---|---|---|
> | **I** | **FULCRUM** | tongs, scissors, crowbar, pliers, balance scale, seesaw |
> | **II** | **LOAD** | lemon squeezer, wheelbarrow, bottle opener |
> | **III** | **EFFORT** | tongs, tweezers, broom, hammer, oar |
>
> **The classification is purely about the ORDER of fulcrum, load and effort along the bar.**

---

Many designs for perpetual motion machines have been proposed but none works. Explain why, in terms of work and energy.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because friction is present in every real machine, and work done against friction is NOT stored anywhere.**
>
> Unlike work done against gravity or a spring — which becomes recoverable potential energy — **work done against friction becomes thermal energy that cannot be converted back into mechanical energy** by the machine.
>
> So at every cycle a machine **loses** some mechanical energy and nothing puts it back. Its total mechanical energy steadily falls, and it **slows down and stops**.
>
> **A machine cannot create energy — it only redirects what it is given.** A perpetual motion machine would have to create energy, which nothing does.

Explain the energy conversions in a traditional gharat or panchakki watermill.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> water high up          POTENTIAL energy
>      ↓ down the pipe
> fast-moving water      KINETIC energy
>      ↓ strikes the wheel
> rotating wheel         MECHANICAL energy
>      ↓ connected shaft
> turning grinding stone  useful WORK — grinding grain
> ```
> **Modern dams do exactly the same thing:** the stored potential energy of water behind the dam becomes kinetic energy, which is then converted into **electricity**.

---

True or false: (i) work is done whenever a force is applied, even if the object does not move; (ii) a motionless stretched rubber band has kinetic energy; (iii) the SI unit of work and energy is the same.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) FALSE.** `W = F × s`, so if s = 0 then W = 0, however large the force. Pushing a wall does no work on the wall.
>
> **(ii) FALSE.** It is motionless, so its **kinetic** energy is zero. What it has is **POTENTIAL** energy, stored by its deformation — which is why it can shoot a stone the moment you release it.
>
> **(iii) TRUE.** Both are measured in **joules (J)** — as they must be, since the work–energy theorem says work done **is** a change in energy.
