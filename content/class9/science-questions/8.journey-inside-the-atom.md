What did Acharya Kanada propose, and where are his ideas recorded?

> [!ANSWER]
> That if matter (**dravya**) is divided again and again, you reach a stage of **smallest particles that can no longer be divided** — which he called **PARMANUS**. They are **infinitely small and cannot be perceived by the senses**, and combine into **dyads** (two) and **triads** (three), from which the whole material universe including living bodies is built.
>
> **Recorded in the Sanskrit text the *Vaisesika Sutras*.**
>
> The Greek philosophers **Leucippus and Democritus** independently proposed the same idea, calling the particles **atomos** — Greek for *indivisible*.

---

Kanada and Democritus had the atomic idea 2000 years before Dalton. Why is Dalton credited with atomic theory?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the earlier concept originated as an IMAGINARY IDEA rather than from experimental observation.** It was a brilliant guess, but there was no way to test it or build on it.
>
> **Dalton's 1808 theory was based on the SCIENTIFIC EXPERIMENTS of his time** — it was the **first scientific description of how matter is made**, and became the starting point for our whole understanding of atomic structure.
>
> **A further gap in Kanada's account:** it does not specify **the proportions in which parmanus combine** to form different substances — and it is exactly those fixed proportions that Dalton's experiments revealed.
>
> **The difference is not the idea. It is the evidence.**

---

What was the first observation showing that atoms are not indivisible?

> [!ANSWER]
> **RADIOACTIVITY** — the discovery that **certain elements emit invisible energy and particles**.
>
> **Why that settles it:** if something comes **out** of an atom, the atom must have been **made of smaller parts**. You cannot get pieces out of something that has no pieces.
>
> Until the late 19th century atoms had been thought to be the smallest, indivisible units of matter.

---

Describe Thomson's cathode ray experiment and what he concluded.

> [!ANSWER]
> **In 1897 J. J. Thomson studied the conduction of electric current through gases at very low pressure**, using a glass tube with two electrodes and a high voltage. He observed **rays travelling from the cathode (negative) to the anode (positive)** — **cathode rays**.
>
> **Studying them in electric and magnetic fields, he concluded they are streams of NEGATIVELY CHARGED particles with a mass MUCH SMALLER than that of atoms.** These were later named **ELECTRONS**.

---

Why did Thomson conclude that electrons are present in ALL atoms?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the nature of the cathode rays did NOT depend on the material of the cathode or the gas filled in the tube.**
>
> **Why that is decisive:** if you get the **identical particle** out of every material you try, that particle **cannot belong to any one material**. It must be a component common to them all.
>
> **So electrons are a fundamental component of every element.** Thomson's was the **first subatomic particle ever identified**, and he received the **1906 Nobel Prize in Physics**.

---

Describe Thomson's model of the atom, and say what problem it was designed to solve.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The problem:** electrons are **negative**, but atoms as a whole are **NEUTRAL**. So where is the positive charge?
>
> **Thomson's answer: the atom is a SPHERE OF POSITIVE CHARGE with electrons distributed throughout it.** Compared to a **pudding with plums in it** — the **plum pudding model**. A **watermelon** is a closer picture: red pulp = the positive charge, seeds = electrons spread through it.
>
> **It was the first genuine attempt to describe how an atom's positive and negative charges stay balanced.**

---

You build a Thomson model from clay (positive) with beads (electrons). What if the clay's positive charge is less than the beads' total negative charge, or the clay itself carries some negative charge?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In both cases the model would no longer represent a NEUTRAL atom.**
>
> - If the clay's positive charge is **less** than the beads' total negative charge, the model would carry a **net NEGATIVE charge** — it would represent an ion, not an atom.
> - If the clay itself carried some negative charge, there would be **nothing positive to balance the electrons at all** — again a net negative object.
>
> **The whole purpose of the model was to explain neutrality**, which requires the positive charge to be **exactly equal and opposite** to the total negative charge.

---

Would an orange or lemon be a good comparison for Thomson's model? Where does it match and where does it fall short?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It matches** in that **small seeds are embedded in a larger soft body**, just as electrons are embedded in a sphere of positive charge.
>
> **Where it falls short:**
> - orange seeds are **clustered near the centre**, not **distributed throughout** as Thomson required
> - the pulp is **divided into segments**, whereas Thomson's positive charge is **uniform** with no internal boundaries
>
> **Judging a model by where it breaks is exactly the right habit** — no analogy is right in every respect, and knowing which respects fail is what stops you drawing false conclusions from it.

---

Describe the gold foil experiment: what was expected, and what was observed?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In 1911 Geiger and Marsden, under Rutherford, aimed a narrow beam of ALPHA (α) PARTICLES — tiny, positively charged particles emitted by radioactive elements — at an extremely thin sheet of GOLD FOIL.**
>
> **Expected (from Thomson's model):** with the positive charge spread out evenly, the α-particles should **pass straight through or be deflected only slightly**.
>
> **Observed:**
> - **most passed through undeflected**
> - **some were sharply deflected**
> - **a few even bounced straight back**
>
> The deflection from a straight path is called **SCATTERING**, so this is also called the **α-ray scattering experiment**.

---

How does each of Rutherford's three conclusions follow from a specific observation?

> [!ANSWER]
> | Observation | Conclusion |
> |---|---|
> | Most α-particles passed **undeflected** | **Most of an atom is EMPTY SPACE** — otherwise they would have hit something |
> | Some were **sharply deflected**, a few **bounced back** | The positive charge is **NOT spread out**; it is **concentrated in an extremely small, dense region — the NUCLEUS**, which also holds **most of the atom's mass** |
> | Only a **few** were deflected | That nucleus must be **extremely small** — most particles miss it entirely |
>
> **Plus:** the electrons revolve around the nucleus, somewhat like planets orbiting the Sun — hence the **PLANETARY MODEL**.

---

How does one bounced-back alpha particle completely rule out the plum pudding model?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because in Thomson's model there is nothing anywhere in the atom massive enough or concentrated enough to turn a fast alpha particle around.**
>
> The positive charge is spread thinly through the whole volume, so at any point the pushing force is **tiny**. A fast, heavy alpha particle would sail through with only a slight nudge.
>
> **To reverse its motion, the alpha particle must meet something both very MASSIVE and very CONCENTRATED.** A single bounce-back therefore proves that the atom's positive charge and mass are **packed into a tiny centre** — which is precisely what Thomson's model denies.
>
> **One result that a model says is impossible is enough to overturn it.**

---

How small is the nucleus compared with the atom? Give the comparison.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> diameter of an atom    ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m
> diameter of a nucleus  ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m
>
> ratio ≈ 100,000 (one lakh) times smaller
> ```
> **If an atom were the size of a cricket ground, about 100 m across, the nucleus would be a single black pepper grain a few millimetres wide at the centre.**
>
> **Everything else is empty space** — which is exactly what the undeflected alpha particles were telling us.

---

Roughly how many atoms are stacked across the thickness of a 0.1 mm sheet of paper?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> sheet thickness = 0.1 mm = 10⁻⁴ m
> atom diameter   ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m
>
> number ≈ 10⁻⁴ ÷ 10⁻¹⁰ = 10⁶
> ```
> **About ONE MILLION atoms.** What feels like a simple sheet of paper is a vast, neatly arranged assembly of tiny building blocks.

---

Predict what would happen in the gold foil experiment if the foil were made much thicker.

> [!ANSWER]
> **More particles would be deflected, and more would bounce back; fewer would pass straight through.**
>
> **Why:** a thicker foil means **more layers of atoms**, so each alpha particle has **more chances of coming close to a nucleus** on its way through. Some would be deflected several times over.
>
> **Why the foil had to be extremely THIN in the first place:** with a thick foil, most particles would be scattered many times, and you could not tell **a single sharp deflection from an accumulation of small ones** — the result would be unreadable.

---

What would happen if the alpha particles were replaced with negatively charged particles?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They would be ATTRACTED to the positive nucleus rather than repelled by it.**
>
> Most would still pass through undeflected (the atom is still mostly empty space), but those approaching a nucleus would be **pulled towards it and bent INWARD**, not pushed away — so **none would bounce straight back** the way alpha particles did.
>
> They would also be **attracted or repelled by the electrons** they pass, since a negative particle interacts with them far more than a heavy alpha particle does.

---

Rutherford's model was better than Thomson's. What could it still not explain?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The STABILITY of the atom.**
>
> **The argument, using Chapter 4:** a particle moving in a **circular path is constantly changing direction, which means it is ACCELERATING**. An **accelerating charged particle should lose energy**. Losing energy, the electron would **spiral inward and fall into the nucleus**.
>
> **If that really happened, atoms would collapse and matter would not exist.** But matter around us is perfectly stable — so Rutherford's model had to be incomplete.

---

What is a proton, and why must the number of protons equal the number of electrons?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A proton is the positively charged particle in the nucleus**, discovered and named by Rutherford. Protons are **much heavier than electrons** and carry a charge **equal and opposite** to an electron's.
>
> **Why the numbers must match: for an atom to be ELECTRICALLY NEUTRAL, the total positive charge must exactly cancel the total negative charge.** Since each proton is +1 and each electron −1, the counts must be equal.
>
> Helium: 2 protons, 2 electrons. Sodium: 11 protons, 11 electrons. **All atoms are electrically neutral.**

---

State Bohr's postulates.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Niels Bohr, 1913:**
> - Electrons do not move randomly but follow **FIXED CIRCULAR PATHS** called **stationary states, orbits or SHELLS**. Each shell corresponds to a **definite amount of energy**, so they are also called **energy levels**.
> - The shells are labelled **K, L, M, N, …** or **n = 1, 2, 3, 4, …**
> - Electrons can revolve **only in these allowed shells, never in between**, and **while in a fixed shell an electron does NOT lose energy**.
> - **K (n = 1) is closest to the nucleus and has the LEAST energy**; energy increases outwards.
> - An electron moves between shells **only by absorbing or releasing a FIXED amount of energy** equal to the difference between the two levels.
> - **Each shell can hold only a certain number of electrons.**

---

How exactly does Bohr's model solve the stability problem that defeated Rutherford's?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By introducing STATIONARY STATES as a postulate: in a stationary state, the energy of an electron remains CONSTANT even though it is moving around the nucleus.**
>
> **Once that is granted, the spiral-in problem cannot arise.** Rutherford's electron collapsed because it **lost energy continuously**. Bohr's electron in an allowed shell **loses no energy at all**, so it has no way to spiral inward.
>
> **Be honest about what this is:** it is **asserted, not derived**. Bohr's model explained many experimental observations and was a major step forward, but the *reason* stationary states exist had to wait for the **quantum mechanical model**.

---

Why are Bohr's shells called K, L, M, N rather than A, B, C, D?

> [!ANSWER]
> **From early X-ray experiments by the physicist Charles Barkla**, who called the first X-ray line he observed **K**.
>
> **He deliberately did not start at A** — he was leaving room in case a series **earlier than the K series** was later discovered. None ever was, and Bohr adopted the same notation for atomic shells.
>
> **A nice reminder that scientific naming often preserves history rather than logic** — like *c* for the speed of light from *celeritas*.

---

Why did the discovery of the neutron become necessary?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of a mass puzzle.** A **hydrogen** atom has **1 proton**; a **helium** atom has **2 protons** — yet helium's mass is about **FOUR times** hydrogen's, **not double**.
>
> **So something else must be in the nucleus, adding MASS without adding CHARGE.** Protons alone could not account for the mass, and electrons are far too light to matter.
>
> **In 1932 James Chadwick discovered exactly such a particle: the NEUTRON**, with a mass nearly equal to a proton's but **no electrical charge**. Helium's 2 protons + 2 neutrons give a mass of about 4. **Chadwick won the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics.**

---

Give the symbol and relative charge of the three subatomic particles, and say which atom has no neutrons.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Particle | Symbol | Relative charge |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Electron** | e⁻ | **−1** |
> | **Proton** | p⁺ | **+1** |
> | **Neutron** | n⁰ | **0** |
>
> **Neutrons are found in the nucleus of all atoms EXCEPT hydrogen** — ordinary hydrogen (protium) has a nucleus of a **single proton** and nothing else.

---

All the protons in a nucleus are positive and squeezed together. Why don't they push each other apart?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every proton DOES repel every other proton — that repulsion is real. Neutrons counteract it in two ways:**
> 1. by **intervening between protons and increasing the distance** between them, which reduces the repulsive force
> 2. by **strengthening the NUCLEAR FORCE**, which binds all the nuclear particles tightly together
>
> **This is why heavier atoms need proportionally MORE neutrons.** Light atoms often have equal numbers — **carbon 6 and 6, oxygen 8 and 8** — but **iron has 26 protons and 30 neutrons**, and **uranium has 92 protons and 146 neutrons**. More protons means far more repulsion to be tamed.

---

Why did the discovery of the uncharged neutron transform nuclear research?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because being uncharged, neutrons are NOT REPELLED by a nucleus and can penetrate it easily.**
>
> A positively charged particle aimed at a nucleus is pushed away long before it arrives — that is what limited earlier experiments. **A neutron sails straight in.**
>
> **The consequences:** the creation of **artificial radioactive elements** and the **splitting of uranium atoms**, giving birth to the **"atomic age"** — and to both nuclear power and nuclear weapons.

---

What work does BARC do with neutrons, and what has it revealed?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC), Mumbai, leads advanced NEUTRON-SCATTERING experiments** using reactors such as **Dhruva**.
>
> These have revealed key insights into the structure of **superconductors, battery electrodes and drug molecules** — helping develop **better medicines, energy storage and industrial alloys** in India.
>
> **It is the gold foil experiment's idea, industrialised:** fire particles at matter, read the scattering pattern, deduce the structure inside.

State the IUPAC rules for writing element symbols, and give an example of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - Many symbols are the **first letter**, or the **first two letters**, of the name — **H** (hydrogen), **Al** (aluminium)
> - **The first letter is ALWAYS capital; the second (if any) is ALWAYS small** — **Al** not AL, **Co** not CO
> - Some use the first letter and a **later** letter, not the second — **Cl** (chlorine), **Zn** (zinc)
> - Some come from **Latin, Greek or German** names — **Fe** (*ferrum*), **Hg** (*hydrargyros*), **W** (*wolfram*)
>
> **In 1813 Berzelius** suggested deriving symbols from Latin names; today **IUPAC** approves names and symbols.

---

Why does it matter whether the second letter is capital or small?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because case changes the meaning completely.**
>
> **Co** is the element **cobalt**. **CO** is **carbon monoxide** — a compound of two completely different elements, carbon and oxygen.
>
> Similarly **Ni** (nickel) vs **NI**, or **Ho** (holmium) vs **HO**. **Get the case wrong and you have written something else entirely** — which is why the rule is a rule and not a style preference.

---

What problems would arise if every scientist used their own symbols for the same element?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Results could not be compared or reproduced.** A paper from one country would be unreadable in another; formulas and equations would have to be translated, and translation invites error.
>
> **Symbols are used instead of full names precisely because they are INTERNATIONALLY RECOGNISED and let scientists worldwide communicate clearly, regardless of language.**
>
> **It is the same argument as for SI units** — and the aircraft fuel disaster in Chapter 1 shows what a mismatch costs.

---

Define atomic number and explain why it determines the identity of an element.

> [!ANSWER]
> **ATOMIC NUMBER Z = the number of PROTONS in the nucleus.**
>
> **Why it fixes the identity:** the number of protons fixes the number of electrons (since atoms are neutral), which fixes the **electronic configuration**, which fixes the **number of valence electrons** — and **chemical behaviour depends on the valence electrons**.
>
> So **two atoms with different Z are necessarily different elements**, and Z uniquely identifies an element. Change the neutrons and you still have the same element; change the protons and you do not.

---

Define mass number and nucleons, and explain why electrons are ignored.

> [!ANSWER]
> **MASS NUMBER A = the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.**
> ```
> A = number of protons + number of neutrons
> ```
> **Protons and neutrons together are called NUCLEONS.**
>
> **Electrons are ignored because their mass is almost negligible** compared with a proton or a neutron. Counting them would change the answer by far less than the precision of the count.

---

Write the standard notation for carbon-12 and explain each part.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>    12
>     6  C
> ```
> - **C** — the **symbol** of the element
> - **6** (below) — the **ATOMIC NUMBER Z**: 6 protons, and therefore 6 electrons
> - **12** (above) — the **MASS NUMBER A**: 6 protons + 6 neutrons
>
> **Neutrons = A − Z = 12 − 6 = 6.** That one subtraction answers most questions in this chapter.

---

An atom with atomic number 26 has 56 nucleons. Find its electrons, protons and neutrons.

> [!ANSWER]

> protons  = Z = 26
> electrons = 26   (the atom is neutral)
> neutrons = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30
> ```
> **26 protons, 26 electrons, 30 neutrons** — this is iron.

An atom's nucleus has 20 protons and its mass number is 41. Find the number of neutrons.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> neutrons = A − Z = 41 − 20 = 21
> ```

An atom has 18 neutrons and atomic number 17. Find its mass number.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> A = protons + neutrons = 17 + 18 = 35
> ```
> **Mass number 35** — this is chlorine-35.

---

State the Bohr–Bury rules for filling electrons into shells.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **The maximum number of electrons in a shell is 2n²**, where n is the shell number:
> ```
> K (n=1): 2 × 1² = 2      L (n=2): 2 × 2² = 8      M (n=3): 2 × 3² = 18
> ```
> 2. **The OUTERMOST shell can hold a maximum of 8 electrons** (the first shell holds at most 2).
> 3. **Shells fill STEPWISE from the nucleus outwards** — K, then L, then M. **The L-shell is filled only after the K-shell is complete.**

---

The M-shell can hold 18 electrons, yet argon's configuration is 2, 8, 8. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because rule 2 overrides rule 1 while the shell is OUTERMOST.**
>
> The M-shell's capacity of 18 (from 2n² with n = 3) applies only once it is an **inner** shell. **While it is the outermost shell it is capped at 8.**
>
> So argon fills to 2, 8, 8 and the **next** electron must begin a new shell (N) rather than continue filling M. Only later, once N has begun, can M go on towards 18.

---

Write the electronic configurations of the elements with atomic numbers 12, 16 and 18.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Z = 12 (magnesium):  2, 8, 2
> Z = 16 (sulfur):     2, 8, 6
> Z = 18 (argon):      2, 8, 8
> ```
> **Method:** fill K with 2, then L with 8, then put the rest in M — never exceeding 8 in the outermost shell.

---

How many electrons are in the outermost shell of ¹²₆C, ¹⁹₉F and ²⁸₁₄Si?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ¹²₆C   Z = 6   →  2, 4    →  4 valence electrons
> ¹⁹₉F   Z = 9   →  2, 7    →  7 valence electrons
> ²⁸₁₄Si Z = 14  →  2, 8, 4 →  4 valence electrons
> ```
> **Note the mass number plays no part** — only Z decides the configuration. And note that **carbon and silicon both have 4 valence electrons**, which is why they behave chemically alike.

---

Define valence shell, valence electrons, octet and valency.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **VALENCE SHELL** — the outermost shell containing electrons.
> - **VALENCE ELECTRONS** — the electrons present in that shell.
> - **OCTET** — 8 electrons in the outermost shell. Elements with a complete octet (or **2 electrons in the case of helium**) are **largely unreactive and more stable**.
> - **VALENCY** — the number of electrons **gained, lost or shared** to complete the octet.
>
> **Atoms with incomplete valence shells are usually more reactive**, because they have something to gain by reacting.

---

State the rule for finding valency from the electronic configuration, and explain why it works.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Fewer than 4 valence electrons → the atom LOSES them.** Sodium 2, 8, **1** → loses 1 → **valency 1**.
> - **More than 4 valence electrons → the atom GAINS electrons.** Oxygen 2, **6** → gains 2 → **valency 2**.
> - **Exactly 4 → it can neither easily gain nor lose, so it SHARES.** Carbon 2, **4** → shares 4 → **valency 4**.
>
> **Why: the atom takes the cheaper route to a complete octet.** For sodium, **removing 1 electron is far easier than acquiring 7**. For oxygen, **acquiring 2 is far easier than losing 6**. Carbon sits exactly in the middle, with 4 either way, so sharing wins.

---

What is combining capacity, and why are hydrogen and chlorine used as the yardstick?

> [!ANSWER]
> **COMBINING CAPACITY = the number of hydrogen or chlorine atoms with which one atom of an element can combine to form a compound.**
>
> **Hydrogen and chlorine are used because both have a combining capacity of ONE** — so counting how many of them attach to your atom reads off its capacity directly, with no arithmetic.
>
> | Compound | Reading |
> |---|---|
> | **H₂O** | oxygen holds 2 hydrogens → capacity **2** |
> | **NH₃** | nitrogen holds 3 hydrogens → capacity **3** |
> | **MgCl₂** | magnesium holds 2 chlorines → capacity **2** |

---

Will an atom that already has 8 electrons in its outermost shell try to gain or lose electrons?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> **It already has a complete octet, which is the stable arrangement every other atom is reacting in order to reach.** There is nothing to be gained by losing or acquiring electrons — so such atoms are **largely unreactive**.
>
> **This is exactly why the noble gases are inert**, and why the octet rule is worth stating at all: it names the arrangement that reactions are *aiming at*.

---

Magnesium has mass number 24 and atomic number 12. Find its protons, neutrons and electrons, and give its electron arrangement.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> protons   = Z = 12
> electrons = 12
> neutrons  = 24 − 12 = 12
>
> configuration:  K = 2,  L = 8,  M = 2    →   2, 8, 2
> ```
> **Valency 2** — it has 2 valence electrons, fewer than 4, so it **loses** both to reach the stable 2, 8 arrangement.

---

An element X has mass number 35 and 18 neutrons. Identify it, give its configuration and valence electrons, and say what happens if two neutrons are added.

> [!ANSWER]

> protons = 35 − 18 = 17,  so Z = 17 and electrons = 17
> ```

Define isotopes and give the three isotopes of hydrogen.

> [!ANSWER]
> **ISOTOPES are atoms of the SAME element with the SAME atomic number but DIFFERENT numbers of neutrons — and therefore different mass numbers.**
>
> | Isotope | Notation | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons | Abundance |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | **Protium** | ¹₁H | 1 | **0** | 1 | about 99.98% |
> | **Deuterium** | ²₁H | 1 | **1** | 1 | about 0.015% |
> | **Tritium** | ³₁H | 1 | **2** | 1 | traces |
>
> **All three have ONE electron**, because all three have one proton.

---

Why do isotopes have the same chemical properties but different physical properties?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because chemical properties depend mainly on the number of VALENCE ELECTRONS, and isotopes have the SAME number of electrons and the SAME electronic configuration.**
>
> Neutrons carry **no charge**, so adding or removing them changes nothing about how the atom bonds.
>
> **But neutrons do have MASS.** So the isotopes differ in **physical** properties such as **boiling and melting points**, density, and rate of diffusion.
>
> **Mass, not chemistry, is what neutrons control.**

---

Two atoms each have 11 protons; one has 12 neutrons, the other 13. Compare their atomic and mass numbers. Are they the same element?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> both:   Z = 11        (11 protons each)
> first:  A = 11 + 12 = 23
> second: A = 11 + 13 = 24
> ```
> **Same atomic number, different mass numbers — so they are ISOTOPES of the SAME element, sodium.**
>
> **The element is fixed by the PROTON count alone.** Neutrons change the mass, never the identity.

Name four uses of isotopes.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **²³⁵₉₂U** — an isotope of uranium, used as **fuel in nuclear reactors** to generate electricity
> - **⁶⁰₂₇Co** — a radioactive isotope of cobalt, used in **radiation treatment for cancer**
> - **¹³¹₅₃I** — an isotope of iodine, used to treat **goitre and thyroid cancer**
> - **¹⁴₆C** — an isotope of carbon, used in **archaeology and geology to determine the age of ancient fossils and artefacts**

---

Chlorine has isotopes of mass 35 u and 37 u in the ratio 3 : 1. Why is 36 u the wrong answer for its atomic mass, and what is the right one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **36 u is the SIMPLE arithmetic mean, `(35 + 37)/2`. It is wrong because it treats the two isotopes as equally common — and they are not.**
>
> **The WEIGHTED average takes the natural abundances into account:** ³⁵Cl is about **75%**, ³⁷Cl about **25%**.
> ```
> average atomic mass = (35 × 75/100) + (37 × 25/100)
>                     = 105/4 + 37/4
>                     = 142/4
>                     = 35.5 u
> ```
> **The answer is closer to 35 than to 37 precisely because ³⁵Cl is three times as common.**

---

Does 35.5 u mean some chlorine atom has a fractional mass?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No. No single chlorine atom has a mass of 35.5 u** — every one is either 35 u or 37 u.
>
> **What it means:** in, say, **1 million chlorine atoms there are 7.5 lakh ³⁵Cl atoms and 2.5 lakh ³⁷Cl atoms**, and their masses **average** 35.5 u.
>
> **The number describes the element as it occurs in nature, not any individual atom** — like an average family size of 4.2 people.

---

Bromine exists as ⁷⁹₃₅Br (49.7%) and ⁸¹₃₅Br (50.3%). Calculate its average atomic mass.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> average = (79 × 49.7/100) + (81 × 50.3/100)
>         = 39.263 + 40.743
>         = 80.006 ≈ 80 u
> ```
> **About 80 u.**
>
> **Sanity check:** the two isotopes are almost equally abundant, so the answer should sit almost exactly midway between 79 and 81 — and 80 is exactly midway. **When abundances are nearly equal, the weighted and simple averages nearly agree.**

---

Why do scientists use the unified atomic mass unit rather than kilograms?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a kilogram is far too large a unit for particles this small** — atomic masses in kilograms would be numbers like 0.000000000000000000000000002 kg, unusable in practice.
>
> **Just as it is easier to weigh a grain of wheat in milligrams rather than kilograms**, atoms are measured in the **unified atomic mass unit (u)**. (It was earlier called the **atomic mass unit, amu**.)

---

Define isobars and give an example. How do they differ from isotopes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **ISOBARS are atoms of DIFFERENT elements with the SAME mass number but DIFFERENT atomic numbers.**
>
> **Example:** **calcium (Z = 20), potassium (Z = 19) and argon (Z = 18)** all have **mass number 40**. Different numbers of protons, but the same total number of nucleons.
>
> | | Atomic number Z | Mass number A | Same element? |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **ISOTOPES** | **same** | **different** | **yes** |
> | **ISOBARS** | **different** | **same** | **no** |
>
> **Memory aid:** iso**top**es share the **top** of the periodic-table identity (the element); iso**bar**s share the **bar**e total (the mass number).

Three species X, Y and Z have: X 18 protons and 19 neutrons; Y 17 protons and 18 neutrons; Z 17 protons and 20 neutrons. How are Y and Z related, and Z and X?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> X:  Z = 18,  A = 18 + 19 = 37
> Y:  Z = 17,  A = 17 + 18 = 35
> Z:  Z = 17,  A = 17 + 20 = 37
> ```
> **(i) Y and Z are ISOTOPES** — same atomic number 17 (both are chlorine), different mass numbers 35 and 37.
>
> **(ii) Z and X are ISOBARS** — same mass number 37, different atomic numbers 17 and 18 (chlorine and argon), so different elements.

---

Electrons move around the nucleus. Why don't they fly away from the atom?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of the ELECTROSTATIC ATTRACTION between the negatively charged electrons and the positively charged nucleus.**
>
> Unlike charges attract, and that attraction supplies exactly the inward pull needed to keep an electron circling instead of flying off in a straight line — the same role the ring played for the marble in Chapter 4.
>
> **The opposite question — why don't they fall IN? — is the stability problem**, and its answer is Bohr's stationary states: **in an allowed shell an electron loses no energy**, so it neither escapes nor spirals in.

---

Which statements about the gold foil experiment are correct? (i) It showed the existence of neutrons. (ii) It disproved the plum pudding model and led to the nucleus. (iii) Large deflections showed most mass and positive charge are in a tiny centre. (iv) The deflections showed how electrons move.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Correct: (ii) and (iii).**
>
> - **(ii) Correct.** The results contradicted Thomson's prediction and forced the idea of a concentrated nucleus.
> - **(iii) Correct.** Only something very massive and very concentrated could turn back a fast alpha particle.
> - **(i) INCORRECT.** The **neutron was discovered by Chadwick in 1932**, twenty-one years later, and by a completely different route.
> - **(iv) INCORRECT.** The experiment revealed the **nucleus**, not the behaviour of electrons. Alpha particles are far too heavy to be noticeably affected by electrons, and the experiment gave no information about their motion.

---

Which of these are correct according to Bohr's model? (i) Electrons lose energy in fixed orbits and fall in. (ii) Electrons can be anywhere with no fixed energy. (iii) Electrons revolve in orbits of fixed energy without losing energy. (iv) Electrons can be found between energy levels.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only (iii) is correct.**
>
> - **(iii) CORRECT** — this is exactly Bohr's stationary-state postulate, and it is what explains atomic stability.
> - **(i) INCORRECT** — this is the failure of **Rutherford's** model, which Bohr's postulate was specifically designed to fix.
> - **(ii) INCORRECT** — Bohr's whole point is that energies are **fixed and discrete**, not arbitrary.
> - **(iv) INCORRECT** — electrons revolve **only in allowed shells and NEVER in between them**.

---

Arrange in chronological order and explain: Bohr's fixed orbits, Thomson's plum pudding, Rutherford's nucleus, Dalton's indivisible atoms.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. DALTON (1808)** — atoms as **indivisible particles**, the first model based on experiment.
> **2. THOMSON (1897–1904)** — the electron is discovered, so atoms are **not** indivisible; the **plum pudding** model explains neutrality.
> **3. RUTHERFORD (1911)** — the gold foil experiment disproves plum pudding; atoms have a **dense central nucleus** and are mostly empty space.
> **4. BOHR (1913)** — electrons move in **fixed orbits of definite energy**, explaining why atoms are stable.
>
> **Each step was forced by an experiment the previous model could not explain** — that is the shape of the whole story, and afterwards came the **quantum mechanical model**, with electrons as **"electron clouds"**.

---

Assertion: "Rutherford concluded that most of an atom's mass is in a small central nucleus." Reason: "In Thomson's model, electrons are embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge." Evaluate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both are TRUE, but the reason is NOT the correct explanation of the assertion.**
>
> The assertion is Rutherford's conclusion, and the reason is a correct description of Thomson's model. **But Thomson's model is not WHY Rutherford concluded what he did** — his conclusion came from the **large-angle deflections and bounce-backs in the gold foil experiment**.
>
> Thomson's model is what the results **contradicted**, not the evidence for the new picture.

---

Assertion: "The discovery of subatomic particles helped in understanding atomic structure." Reason: "The number of electrons equals the number of protons in an atom." Evaluate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both are TRUE, but the reason is NOT the correct explanation of the assertion.**
>
> The assertion is true — discovering electrons, protons and neutrons is exactly what turned "atom" from a featureless ball into a structure.
>
> The reason is also true — it follows from atoms being electrically neutral.
>
> **But equal electron and proton counts is one particular FACT that emerged AFTER the particles were discovered.** It is a consequence, not the reason the discoveries mattered.

An atom has 12 protons and 12 neutrons. Its electrons are replaced by particles with the same charge but 500 times heavier. What happens to its atomic number, atomic mass, mass number and overall charge?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) Atomic number: UNCHANGED at 12.** Z is defined by the number of **protons**, and no proton was touched.
>
> **(ii) Atomic mass: INCREASES noticeably.** Electrons are normally so light they are ignored — but 500 times heavier, the 12 of them together weigh roughly as much as **three extra protons**, which is no longer negligible.
>
> **(iii) Mass number: UNCHANGED at 24.** A counts only **nucleons** — protons and neutrons — and by definition takes no account of electrons whatever their mass.
>
> **(iv) Overall charge: still NEUTRAL.** The new particles carry the **same charge** as electrons, so the balance of +12 and −12 is untouched.
>
> **The instructive part is (ii) vs (iii):** "atomic mass" is a physical quantity you could weigh, while "mass number" is a **count** — so a change in mass need not change the mass number at all.
