Distinguish a homogeneous from a heterogeneous mixture, with two examples of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **HOMOGENEOUS (a solution):** **uniform composition throughout** — a well-stirred sugar solution is equally sweet in the first and the last sip. The particles are **not visible**. Examples: **vinegar** (acetic acid in water), **soda** (carbon dioxide in water), brass.
> - **HETEROGENEOUS:** **not uniform**. Sand in water stays visible and **settles with time**; oil and water form separate layers. Examples: **muddy water, oil and water**.
>
> **A solution always remains homogeneous** — that is what makes it a solution.

---

Salt water, chalk-powder water and dilute milk are each tested with a laser beam and with filter paper. Predict the results and say what they reveal.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **Salt + water** | **Chalk + water** | **Milk + water** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Particles visible? | no | **yes** | no |
> | Laser path visible from the side? | **no** | **yes** | **YES** |
> | Residue on filter paper? | no | **yes** | no |
> | Settles on standing? | no | **yes** | no |
>
> **Salt water is a SOLUTION, chalk water a SUSPENSION — and milk is the interesting one.**
>
> Milk **looks** homogeneous, passes through filter paper and does not settle, **yet it scatters the beam**. That single result rules out both a true solution and a suspension: **it is a COLLOID.**

---

Why must you never look directly into the laser beam in this experiment?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It can cause irreversible eye damage.** A laser concentrates a great deal of light into a very narrow beam, so all of that energy lands on a tiny patch of the retina.
>
> **View the beam only from the side**, perpendicular to it — which is exactly how the Tyndall effect is meant to be observed anyway.

---

Define concentration, and explain why the right proportion matters using ORS and pesticide spray.

> [!ANSWER]
> **CONCENTRATION = the amount of solute dissolved in a given amount of solvent or solution.**
>
> - **ORS** needs **specified** amounts of salt and sugar in a **fixed** volume of water. Change either, and you still have a salt-and-sugar solution — **but it is no longer ORS** and will not rehydrate a patient correctly.
> - **Pesticide:** **too little** may fail to protect the crop; **too much** can damage the crop, the soil and the environment.
>
> **The general point: having the right ingredients is not the same as having the right concentration.** That is also why not every sugary drink sold in the market is ORS.

---

Write the formulas for the three percentage concentrations, and say when each is used.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> % m/m = (mass of solute)   ÷ (mass of solution)   × 100
> % m/v = (mass of solute)   ÷ (volume of solution) × 100
> % v/v = (volume of solute) ÷ (volume of solution) × 100
> ```
> | Method | Used when | Example |
> |---|---|---|
> | **% m/m** | general; also for heterogeneous mixtures | milk powder, spice mixes, food labels |
> | **% m/v** | **volume is easier to measure than mass** | 5% glucose solution, medicines |
> | **% v/v** | **two miscible liquids** are mixed | vinegar, perfumes, cosmetics |
>
> **In all three the denominator is the SOLUTION, not the solvent.**

---

Why is the denominator the solution rather than the solvent?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two reasons, both practical.**
>
> 1. **You can measure the finished solution directly** — weigh it or read its volume. You usually cannot get the solvent back out to measure it.
> 2. **Volumes do not simply add when something dissolves.** Putting 5 g of glucose into 100 mL of water does **not** give 100 mL of solution, so "per 100 mL of water" would be ambiguous. "Per 100 mL of solution" is unambiguous.

---

10 g of salt is dissolved in 90 g of water. Find the mass by mass percentage.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> mass of solution = 10 g + 90 g = 100 g
>
> % m/m = 10/100 × 100 = 10 % m/m
> ```
> **Note the deliberate arithmetic:** 90 g of water was chosen so the solution comes to exactly 100 g. **Do not use 10/90** — that would be "per 100 g of solvent", a different quantity.

A talcum powder contains 4 % m/m zinc oxide as an antiseptic. How much zinc oxide is in 300 g of the powder?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 4 % m/m means 4 g of zinc oxide per 100 g of powder
>
> in 300 g:  4 × 3 = 12 g
> ```
> **12 g of zinc oxide.**

You mix two tablespoons (15 mL each) of orange juice concentrate with water to make 150 mL of juice. What is the % v/v of concentrate?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> volume of concentrate = 2 × 15 mL = 30 mL
> volume of solution    = 150 mL
>
> % v/v = 30/150 × 100 = 20 % v/v
> ```

---

Vinegar is 5 % v/v acetic acid. Glacial acetic acid is 100% acetic acid. How would you make vinegar from it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Take 5 mL of glacial acetic acid and add water until the total volume of the solution is 100 mL.** That gives `5/100 × 100 =` **5 % v/v**.
>
> **Safety: always add the ACID to the WATER, never water to concentrated acid**, and work under supervision. Mixing releases heat, and adding water to acid can make it spit.
>
> **Note the wording matters:** "add water to MAKE 100 mL", not "add 100 mL of water" — the second would give a different concentration.

---

A saline drip is 0.9 % m/v sodium chloride. What does that mean in grams and millilitres?

> [!ANSWER]
> **0.9 g of sodium chloride in every 100 mL of solution.**
>
> **Why that exact figure:** 0.9% is the strength that is **safe for blood** — it matches the body's own fluids closely enough that it can be put straight into a vein to replace lost fluid without harming blood cells.

A cake uses 75 g sugar, 420 g flour and 5 g sodium hydrogencarbonate. Express each as a percentage.

Brass contains 70% copper by mass. Find the copper and zinc in 120 g of brass.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> copper = 70% of 120 g = 0.70 × 120 = 84 g
> zinc   = 120 − 84     = 36 g          (or 30% of 120 = 36 g)
> ```

---

Define solubility and saturated solution, and explain why the temperature must always be stated.

> [!ANSWER]
> **SOLUBILITY = the maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a fixed quantity of solvent (100 g or 100 mL) AT A GIVEN TEMPERATURE.**
>
> **A SATURATED SOLUTION can dissolve no more solute at that temperature.**
>
> **Why temperature matters so much:**
> - the solubility of a **solid in a liquid generally INCREASES** with temperature
> - the solubility of a **gas in a liquid generally DECREASES** with temperature
>
> **A solubility quoted without a temperature is meaningless** — potassium nitrate dissolves 21 g per 100 g of water at 10 °C and 167 g at 80 °C, eight times as much.

---

Using the solubility table, explain why sodium chloride is obtained from seawater by evaporation rather than by cooling.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because sodium chloride's solubility barely changes with temperature: 36 g per 100 g of water at 10 °C and only 37 g at 80 °C.**
>
> **Cooling a saturated salt solution from 80 °C to 10 °C would deposit only about 1 g per 100 g of water** — almost nothing. There is no useful gap to exploit.
>
> **So the water must be removed instead**, by evaporation. Compare potassium nitrate: 167 g at 80 °C down to 21 g at 10 °C, so **cooling alone deposits 146 g** — which is why it crystallises beautifully.

---

What mass of potassium nitrate is needed to make a saturated solution in 50 g of water at 40 °C? (Solubility at 40 °C = 62 g per 100 g water.)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 62 g dissolves in 100 g of water
> so in 50 g of water:  62 ÷ 2 = 31 g
> ```
> **31 g.**

---

A student makes a saturated solution of potassium chloride at 80 °C and lets it cool to 25 °C. What will she observe? (Solubility: 54 g at 80 °C, about 36 g at 25 °C, per 100 g water.)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Crystals of potassium chloride will appear in the solution.**
>
> ```
> at 80 °C the water holds  54 g per 100 g water
> at 25 °C it can hold only about 36 g
> so about  54 − 36 = 18 g  per 100 g of water must come out as solid
> ```
> **Why:** the cooler water simply cannot hold as much solute. The excess **has nowhere to go but out of solution**, and it comes out as **crystals**.

---

Compare how much the solubility of potassium nitrate, sodium chloride, potassium chloride and ammonium chloride changes from 10 °C to 80 °C.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Salt | 10 °C | 80 °C | Increase |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Potassium nitrate** | 21 | 167 | **+146** — by far the largest |
> | **Ammonium chloride** | 24 | 66 | +42 |
> | **Potassium chloride** | 35 | 54 | +19 |
> | **Sodium chloride** | 36 | 37 | **+1** — almost none |
>
> **General rule: the solubility of these solids increases with temperature — but by very different amounts.**
>
> **Why it matters:** the bigger the change, the better the salt can be purified by **crystallisation on cooling**. Potassium nitrate is ideal; sodium chloride is hopeless by that route.

---

What is a crystal, what is crystallization, and what principle does it rest on?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A CRYSTAL is a solid made of particles arranged in a REGULAR GEOMETRIC PATTERN.**
>
> **CRYSTALLIZATION is the process of forming crystals from a saturated solution.**
>
> **The principle: the difference in the solubility of a substance at DIFFERENT TEMPERATURES.** Cool a hot saturated solution and the solvent can no longer hold all the solute, so the excess separates out as pure solid.
>
> **Uses:** separating two solids when one is present in **small quantity** and both dissolve in the same solvent, and **purifying** solids — newly made compounds usually carry unwanted impurities.

---

A saturated solution holds 287 g per 100 g of water at 60 °C. At 40 °C only 241 g can stay dissolved. How much crystallises out on cooling?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 287 g − 241 g = 46 g
> ```
> **46 g of the compound separates out as pure solid crystals** per 100 g of water.
>
> **Why it comes out PURE:** the impurities are present in small amounts and are still well below their own saturation point, so they **stay dissolved** while the main compound is forced out.

---

In making copper sulfate crystals, why is the hot solution filtered while still hot, and why is it then cooled slowly?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Filter while HOT** — to remove **insoluble impurities** while all the copper sulfate is still dissolved. **If you let it cool first, your product would crystallise out and be thrown away with the impurities** on the filter paper.
> - **Cool SLOWLY and undisturbed** — this gives the particles **enough time to come together in an orderly arrangement**, producing **larger, shiny, well-shaped** crystals. Rapid cooling gives many **small, poorly formed** ones.
>
> **Also:** a drop of dilute sulfuric acid helps make pure crystals by **preventing unwanted reactions** (needed for some salts only), and the whole activity needs adult supervision because **copper sulfate is toxic** and sulfuric acid must be handled with great care.

---

Design an experiment to test the claim "slow cooling gives larger crystals than rapid cooling".

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Prepare **one** hot saturated solution of copper sulfate.
> 2. **Divide it into two equal parts** in identical beakers.
> 3. Leave one to **cool slowly at room temperature**, undisturbed; place the other in **ice-cold water** to cool rapidly.
> 4. After the same time, filter, dry and **compare the size and shape** of the crystals — measure the largest crystal in each, and count them.
>
> **Why splitting ONE solution is the key step:** both halves then have exactly the same concentration, the same solute, the same starting temperature and the same purity. **The ONLY difference is the cooling rate**, so any difference in crystal size must be caused by it.
>
> **Expected result:** the slowly cooled half gives **fewer, larger, better-formed** crystals.

---

Will the size of common salt crystals change if the rate of evaporation is increased or decreased? Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes. Slower evaporation gives LARGER crystals; faster evaporation gives many SMALL ones.**
>
> **Why:** crystal size depends on **how much time the particles have to arrange themselves into the regular pattern**. Slow growth lets particles keep joining a few existing crystals, so those grow big. Rapid growth starts a great many crystals at once and none of them gets time to grow.
>
> **This is exactly the same principle as slow versus rapid cooling** — both are about the time available for orderly growth.

---

Describe how salt is obtained from seawater, and name the two traditional Indian methods.

> [!ANSWER]
> Seawater is let into shallow pans and allowed to **evaporate** in sun and wind until it becomes a **saturated solution**. Continued evaporation then deposits **salt crystals**, which are collected.
>
> **Traditional Indian methods used by coastal communities:**
> - **Panga salt** — obtained by **boiling** concentrated sea brine
> - **Karkatch salt** — obtained by **evaporating** seawater
>
> The two methods give **crystals of different sizes**, for the reason above: boiling is fast, sun-evaporation is slow.

---

What is distillation, and why must the boiling points differ by at least about 25 °C?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Heat the mixture until the liquid with the LOWER boiling point vaporises; pass the vapour through a CONDENSER where it is cooled back into liquid; collect that pure liquid separately, while the other liquid or the dissolved solid stays behind in the distillation flask.**
>
> **Why a 25 °C gap is needed:** a large difference lets one liquid **vaporise before significant vapour of the other has formed**. If the boiling points are close, **both boil off together** and the distillate is still a mixture — you have separated nothing.

---

Why is distillation an effective method for separating water and acetone?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because their boiling points differ by a lot: acetone boils at about 56 °C and water at 100 °C — a gap of 44 °C.**
>
> On heating, the **acetone vaporises well before the water does**. Its vapour is condensed and collected pure, while the water remains in the flask. They are miscible, so no other simple method would separate them.

---

Why would you use distillation instead of evaporation to separate salt from water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because distillation RECOVERS THE SOLVENT as well as the solute.**
>
> In **evaporation** the water simply escapes into the air and is lost — you get only the salt. In **distillation** the water vapour is **condensed and collected**, so you end up with **both** the salt (left in the flask) and the pure water (in the receiver).
>
> **So the choice depends on whether you want the solvent back.** For sea-salt production nobody wants the water, so evaporation is cheaper. For purifying drinking water, the water **is** the product.

---

Which pairs from this list can be separated by simple distillation, and why? Water (100 °C), acetone (56 °C), alcohol (78 °C), chloroform (61 °C), benzene (80 °C).

> [!ANSWER]
> | Pair | Gap | Simple distillation? |
> |---|---|---|
> | **water – acetone** | 44 °C | **YES** |
> | **water – salt** | (solid in liquid) | **YES** — distillation also recovers a liquid from a solution of a solid |
> | acetone – alcohol | 22 °C | **no** — under 25 °C |
> | alcohol – chloroform | 17 °C | **no** |
> | alcohol – benzene | **2 °C** | **no** — hopeless |
> | sand – salt | — | **no** — both solids, not miscible liquids; dissolve in water, filter off the sand, then evaporate |
>
> **The pairs that fail need FRACTIONAL distillation**, which handles differences of less than 25 °C.

---

What is fractional distillation, and where is it used on an industrial scale?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fractional distillation separates components whose boiling points differ by LESS than 25 °C.**
>
> **Its great industrial use is the petroleum refinery.** Crude oil extracted from the Earth's crust is separated into fractions, in rising order of boiling point:
> ```
> petroleum gas → petrol → aviation fuel (kerosene) → diesel
>              → lubricating oil → bitumen
> ```
> **The gaseous fraction is compressed into steel cylinders, where it liquefies — that is LPG**, our domestic fuel.

---

Describe paper chromatography and explain what actually causes the separation.

> [!ANSWER]
> Draw a **pencil** line 2 cm from the bottom of a paper strip, put a spot of ink on the line, and stand the strip in a shallow layer of solvent with the **solvent level BELOW the spot**. As the solvent rises through the paper, the ink separates into different coloured spots.
>
> **What causes it:** the components differ in **how strongly they interact with the SOLVENT and with the PAPER**. The solvent carries them up, and they separate according to **how fast each one moves** — a component that clings to the paper lags behind, one that prefers the solvent races ahead.

---

Why must the solvent level start below the ink spot, and why is the line drawn in pencil?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Solvent below the spot:** if the spot were submerged, the ink would simply **dissolve away into the liquid** in the container instead of being carried up the paper — nothing would separate.
> - **Pencil, not pen:** pen ink is itself a mixture of dyes and would **run up the paper too**, producing extra streaks and ruining the result. Pencil graphite does not dissolve in the solvent, so it stays put.

---

Will water always work as the chromatography solvent?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Some substances barely dissolve in water and would simply not move, giving no separation.
>
> **In those cases use a different solvent** — alcohol, a **2 % m/v salt solution**, or a mixture of solvents. The right solvent is the one in which the components dissolve **to different extents** — because it is exactly that difference that separates them.
>
> The method works for **spinach leaf pigments, flower petal colours, food colours and coloured mouth fresheners** as well as ink.

---

Where does the word "chromatography" come from?

> [!ANSWER]
> From the Greek ***chroma*, meaning colour, and *graphein*, meaning to write** — literally **"writing with colour"**.
>
> The name stuck because the technique was **first used to separate coloured substances** like dyes and inks, where the separation is directly visible.

---

Mustard oil and water are poured into a separating funnel. Which forms the upper layer, and how do you collect the two liquids?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The yellow mustard oil forms the UPPER layer** and water the lower one, because **oil is LESS DENSE than water** — the less dense liquid floats.
>
> **Method:**
> 1. Let it stand undisturbed until two clear layers form.
> 2. Open the stopcock slowly and run off the **lower layer (water)** into a container.
> 3. Close the stopcock when the water is almost fully drained.
> 4. **Collect and discard the small middle portion** that contains both liquids.
> 5. Reopen the stopcock and collect the **oil** separately.
>
> **Why discard the middle bit:** at the boundary the two are mixed. Sacrificing that small volume is the price of getting both fractions pure.

---

A cooking oil pack says "one litre (910 g)". Will it form a separate layer with water, and which will be on top?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> density of the oil   = 910 g ÷ 1000 mL = 0.91 g/mL
> density of water     = 1.00 g/mL
> ```
> **Yes, it forms a separate layer** — oil and water are **immiscible**. **The OIL will be on top**, because at **0.91 g/mL it is less dense than water**.
>
> **Separate them with a SEPARATING FUNNEL**: let the layers settle, run off the lower (water) layer through the stopcock, discard the small middle portion, then collect the oil.

---

What would happen in a separating funnel if two immiscible liquids had exactly the same density?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No layers would form** — neither liquid could float on the other, since floating depends entirely on a **density difference**.
>
> The mixture would stay as scattered droplets of one in the other, and **the separating funnel would be useless**. You would have to use some other difference between them — boiling point, or solubility in a third solvent.

---

Most gas mixtures are homogeneous. Give examples of heterogeneous mixtures that contain a gas.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Why gas mixtures are usually homogeneous:** gas particles are **free to move in all directions**, so they mix easily and completely — as in the **hydrogen–oxygen mixture used as rocket fuel**.
>
> **Heterogeneous mixtures with a gas as one component:**
> - **smoke** — solid particles suspended in air
> - **fog** — tiny liquid water droplets in air
> - **dust in air**
>
> **What makes these heterogeneous is that the OTHER component is not a gas** — the solid or liquid particles stay as separate lumps rather than mixing at the particle level.

---

What is sublimation, and how is it used to separate camphor from sand?

> [!ANSWER]
> **SUBLIMATION: a solid changes DIRECTLY into vapour, below its melting point, without passing through the liquid state.** **DEPOSITION** is the reverse — vapour to solid, again with no liquid stage.
>
> **The separation:** heat the camphor–sand mixture gently in a china dish under an **inverted funnel** whose nozzle is plugged with cotton. **White solid camphor deposits on the cool inner wall of the funnel**, while the **sand stays in the dish**.
>
> **Why it works: camphor sublimes and sand does not.** The cotton plug stops the vapour escaping, and the funnel gives a cool surface for deposition.
>
> **Other sublimable substances:** naphthalene, and **dry ice** (solid carbon dioxide) used for ice-cream storage.

---

Is sublimation the same as evaporation? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> | | **Evaporation** | **Sublimation** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Starting state | **liquid** | **solid** |
> | Passes through liquid? | already liquid | **NO — skips it entirely** |
> | Where | from the **surface** of a liquid | from the solid, below its melting point |
>
> **Both end in vapour, but they start from different states** — and the whole point of sublimation is what it **misses out**.

---

What is an alloy, why can't physical methods separate one, and why are alloys made?

> [!ANSWER]
> **An ALLOY is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or of a metal and a non-metal.** Metals do not dissolve in each other at room temperature, but **melted together and cooled, they solidify into what looks like a single metal**.
>
> **Why physical methods fail:** the components are mixed **at the level of individual particles**, uniformly throughout — there is nothing of one kind to pick out from something of another kind. No filtering, settling or magnet can find a boundary to work on.
>
> **Why we make them:** to get materials that are **stronger, more rigid or more corrosion-resistant** than the pure metals. **Brass** is about 80% copper and 20% zinc; **bronze** about 80% copper and 20% tin; **stainless steel** is iron with carbon, chromium, nickel and molybdenum.

---

What is a suspension, and why does filtering muddy water often leave it still cloudy?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A SUSPENSION is a heterogeneous mixture in which solid particles do not dissolve but remain suspended throughout the medium.** Its particles are **much larger than in a solution** and are **visible to the naked eye** — sand in water, sawdust in water, tea leaves in water.
>
> **Why filtering isn't enough:** filter paper catches the **larger** particles, but the **finest** ones pass straight through the pores, and they are also too small to settle under gravity in a reasonable time. **The water stays cloudy because those fine particles are still in it.**
>
> **For those, use centrifugation and/or coagulation.**

---

Explain centrifugation and why it beats simply letting a mixture stand.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The mixture is spun in a tube at high speed. The tubes swing horizontal, and the outward (centrifugal) force drives the heavier particles outwards, so they collect at the bottom of the tube; the lighter liquid stays on top.**
>
> **Why it beats waiting:** settling under gravity is **slow**, and the finest particles **never settle at all**. Spinning applies a **far larger effective force** than gravity, so what would take hours takes minutes — and particles too fine to settle on their own are separated.
>
> **Used to separate blood** into plasma, platelets, white cells and red cells, and widely in chemical industries. You can feel the effect by spinning while holding a partner's crossed hands — the game called **phugadi** in Marathi and **kikli** in Punjabi.

---

What is a paperfuge, and why does it matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A simple hand-powered centrifuge** — a cardboard disc on a loop of string with two handles, borrowed from a common spinning toy — that spins blood samples **fast enough to separate heavier components from lighter ones, without any electricity**.
>
> **Why it matters:** it is **low-cost and needs no power supply**, so it can be used to **detect diseases like malaria and anaemia in remote areas** where a laboratory centrifuge is impossible.
>
> **The lesson:** an ordinary toy mechanism, understood properly, solved a real medical-access problem.

---

How does adding alum clear muddy water, and what is the general principle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Powdered alum (fitkari) makes the fine suspended particles CLUMP TOGETHER.** The larger clumps then **settle under gravity (sedimentation)** and can be removed by **decantation or filtration**.
>
> **This is COAGULATION, and alum is the COAGULANT.**
>
> **The principle: you are not removing the particles, you are changing their SIZE.** Individual fine particles are too small to settle and too small to be caught by filter paper. Turn many small particles into one big clump and both problems disappear at once.

---

Give an everyday example of coagulation from the kitchen.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Making paneer (cheese) from milk.**
>
> An **acid — lemon juice or vinegar — acts as the coagulant**, causing the **milk proteins to clump together** into solid cheese, which can then be separated from the liquid whey.
>
> **Same principle as alum in muddy water:** dispersed particles too fine to collect are made to join into lumps big enough to handle.

---

Compare solutions, suspensions and colloids on particle size, visibility, filtration, settling and the Tyndall effect.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Property | **Solution** | **Suspension** | **Colloid** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Nature | **homogeneous** | **heterogeneous** | heterogeneous (**looks** homogeneous) |
> | Particle size | `<` **1 nm** | `>` **1000 nm** | **1 – 1000 nm** |
> | Particles visible? | no | **yes, to the naked eye** | no |
> | Separated by filtration? | no | **yes** | no |
> | Settles on standing? | no | **yes** | no |
> | Tyndall effect? | **no** | **yes** | **yes** |
> | Examples | salt solution, brass | sand in water, mud | milk, blood, smoke, butter |
>
> **Almost every row follows from PARTICLE SIZE alone** — which is why size is the real defining difference.

---

Why is blood a colloid rather than a solution or a suspension?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Not a suspension:** its cells **cannot be seen with the naked eye**, and the blood does not separate into layers as it stands in the body.
> - **Not a solution:** it **can be separated by centrifugation** and it **coagulates** — a true solution would do neither.
>
> **So it is a COLLOID:** particles in the 1–1000 nm range, dispersed evenly and not settling, but large enough to be spun out and to scatter light. **Milk, tomato sauce and ice cream are colloids too.**

---

What would happen if blood behaved like a true suspension inside the body?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The blood cells would SETTLE OUT** — suspension particles are large enough to sediment under gravity.
>
> The consequences would be immediate: the cells would collect in the lowest parts of the body and in narrow vessels, **blocking circulation**; the upper blood would be almost cell-free, so **oxygen could not be delivered**; and the blood could not stay uniform enough to be pumped.
>
> **Being a colloid — with particles small enough to stay dispersed indefinitely — is precisely what makes blood able to circulate.**

---

In a blood sample, what is the dispersed phase and what is the dispersion medium?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Dispersed phase:** the **blood cells and platelets** — the solute-like, suspended component.
> - **Dispersion medium:** the **plasma** — the liquid in which they are dispersed.
>
> **The general definition:** the **dispersed phase** is the dispersed particles; the **dispersion medium** is the component they are suspended in.

---

What is the Tyndall effect, and why is it the decisive test for a colloid?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The SCATTERING of light by particles**, which makes the path of a light beam visible from the side. Named after **John Tyndall**, who first explained it.
>
> **Light is scattered by COLLOIDS and SUSPENSIONS but not by true solutions** — solution particles (`<` 1 nm) are far too small to interfere with light waves.
>
> **Why it is decisive:** a colloid and a solution look identical — both clear and uniform, both pass through filter paper, neither settles. **Shine a beam through and the colloid lights up a visible path while the solution does not.** It is the one property that tells them apart.

Where do you see the Tyndall effect in everyday life?

> [!ANSWER]
> - a **fine beam of light entering a dark room** through a small hole, scattered by dust and smoke in the air
> - **sunbeams through gaps between the leaves** of a dense tree
> - **floodlights in a sports stadium**
> - the **haze over cities with a lot of smoke and dust** — the extra particles scatter light in all directions, so distant objects lose contrast and the air itself looks bright

Clouds are tiny water droplets or ice crystals floating in air. What kind of mixture are they?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A COLLOID** — with the **droplets or ice crystals as the dispersed phase** and **air as the dispersion medium**.
>
> **The evidence:**
> - the droplets **do not settle out** — clouds stay up for hours, so it is not a suspension
> - the droplets are **not individually visible**, so it is not a coarse mixture
> - clouds **scatter light strongly** — that is exactly why they are white and why the sun dims behind them. **Strong Tyndall scattering plus no settling is the colloid signature.**

Which of these show the Tyndall effect: (a) air and dust, (b) copper sulfate in water, (c) starch in water, (d) acetone and water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a) and (c).**
>
> - **(a) air and dust — YES.** The dust particles are large enough to scatter light; this is the classic sunbeam-in-a-dark-room case.
> - **(c) starch in water — YES.** Starch forms a **colloid**, with particles in the 1–1000 nm range.
> - **(b) copper sulfate in water — NO.** A **true solution**; its particles are `<` 1 nm, far too small to scatter light. Coloured is not the same as scattering.
> - **(d) acetone and water — NO.** Two **miscible liquids** forming a true solution.

---

Assertion: "Solutions do not exhibit the Tyndall effect." Reason: "The particles in solutions are larger than 100 nm, so they cannot scatter light." Evaluate both.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The assertion is TRUE; the reason is FALSE.**
>
> Solutions really do not show the Tyndall effect — but the reason given has it **exactly backwards**. Solution particles are **smaller than 1 nm**, not larger than 100 nm, and it is being **too SMALL** that prevents them scattering light.
>
> Anything **larger** than about 1 nm — colloids and suspensions — is what **does** scatter.

Classify each as homogeneous or heterogeneous: muddy water, milk, blood, brass.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Muddy water — heterogeneous** (a suspension; it settles and can be filtered)
> - **Milk — heterogeneous** (a colloid; it looks uniform but shows the Tyndall effect)
> - **Blood — heterogeneous** (a colloid, separable by centrifugation)
> - **Brass — homogeneous** (an alloy — a genuine solution of zinc in copper)
>
> **The trap is milk and blood:** they *look* uniform, but a colloid is classified as **heterogeneous** because its dispersed particles remain a distinct phase — which the Tyndall effect reveals.

You are given a mixture of sand, common salt and naphthalene. Give the sequence of techniques to separate all three.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. SUBLIMATION** — gently heat the mixture under an inverted funnel. **Naphthalene sublimes** and deposits on the cool inner wall; sand and salt stay behind.
>
> **2. Add water and FILTER** — the **salt dissolves**, the **sand does not**. Sand is left as residue on the filter paper; salt solution passes through.
>
> **3. EVAPORATION or CRYSTALLIZATION** of the filtrate — recovers the **salt** as solid.
>
> **Why this order:** each step uses a property that **only one component has** — naphthalene alone sublimes, salt alone dissolves. Taking the sublimable one out first keeps it from being contaminated by water in step 2.

Two miscible liquids A (b.p. 60 °C) and B (b.p. 90 °C) are mixed. How would you separate them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Simple distillation** — the boiling points differ by **30 °C**, comfortably more than the 25 °C minimum.
>
> **The set-up:** a **distillation flask** holding the mixture, heated over a burner on a wire gauze and tripod; a **thermometer** at the neck to watch the vapour temperature; a **water condenser** (water entering at the bottom, leaving at the top) sloping down to a **conical flask** for the distillate.
>
> **What happens:** on heating, **A vaporises first at 60 °C**; its vapour is cooled in the condenser and collected pure in the receiver. **B remains in the distillation flask.** The thermometer holds steady at about 60 °C while A distils over and then climbs — telling you when A is finished.

Compare evaporation, crystallization and distillation, and say when you would choose each.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Method | What you get | Choose it when |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Evaporation** | the **solute** only; the solvent is lost to the air | the solvent is worthless and the solute is stable on heating — e.g. **salt from seawater** |
> | **Crystallization** | the solute as **PURE, well-formed crystals** | you need **purity** — separating a compound from impurities, or from another solid present in small quantity |
> | **Distillation** | **BOTH** the solvent (as distillate) and whatever stays behind | you want the **solvent back**, or you are separating **two miscible liquids** (b.p. ≥ 25 °C apart) |
>
> **Evaporation and crystallization are not the same process.** Evaporation just drives off the solvent and can leave impurities behind mixed with the product; **crystallization exploits the change of solubility with temperature to produce a genuinely PURE solid.**

---

State whether each is true or false, correcting the false ones. (i) Salt can be separated from salt solution by evaporation or distillation. (ii) Distillation separates liquids with the same boiling point. (iii) In chromatography the solvent should start above the spot. (iv) Evaporation and crystallization are the same.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) TRUE.** Evaporation drives off the water leaving salt; distillation does the same but **also recovers the water**.
>
> **(ii) FALSE.** Distillation needs the boiling points to differ by **at least about 25 °C**. Liquids with the same boiling point vaporise together and are not separated at all.
>
> **(iii) FALSE.** The solvent must start **BELOW the spot** — otherwise the sample dissolves straight into the liquid instead of being carried up the paper.
>
> **(iv) FALSE.** Evaporation removes the solvent; **crystallization uses the change of solubility with temperature to produce pure crystals**. The products differ in purity and in form.

Three students make sugar solutions: A puts 20 g in 80 g water, B puts 20 g in 100 g water, C puts 30 g in 80 g water. Find each % m/m and say whose is most concentrated.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> A:  20 / (20 + 80)  × 100 = 20 / 100 × 100 = 20 %
> B:  20 / (20 + 100) × 100 = 20 / 120 × 100 = 16.7 %
> C:  30 / (30 + 80)  × 100 = 30 / 110 × 100 = 27.3 %
> ```
> **C's solution is the most concentrated.**
>
> **Why:** C used **more sugar in less water** than either of the others, so the sugar makes up the largest share of the total mass. B is the weakest — same sugar as A but spread through more water.
>
> **Note the denominator:** it is the **solution** (sugar + water), not the water alone.

Give three places where separation techniques matter outside the laboratory.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **In your own body:** the **kidneys** continuously remove waste from the blood.
> - **Sewage treatment:** **sedimentation, coagulation and filtration** clean waste water before it is released; the treated water can be reused for flushing toilets or watering plants.
> - **Waste segregation and recycling:** separating **dry waste** (plastic, paper, glass, metal — recyclable) from **wet waste** (food scraps, peels — compostable), and recovering valuable materials such as **lithium from old phone and laptop batteries**.
> - **Bigger challenges:** removing **plastic waste from oceans and rivers**.
