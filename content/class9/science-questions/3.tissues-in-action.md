Define a tissue, and explain why multicellular organisms have them but an amoeba does not.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A tissue is a group of cells, similar in structure, that work together to perform a specific function.**
>
> **In a unicellular organism like amoeba, ONE cell must do everything** — feed, move, respond, reproduce. There is nothing to divide the work between.
>
> **In a multicellular organism, different groups of cells specialise.** This is **division of labour**, and it **increases efficiency**, making complex life processes possible. Muscle tissue moves, nervous tissue signals, xylem carries water, phloem carries food — each doing one job well.

---

Almost every difference between plant and animal tissues traces back to one fact. What is it, and give two consequences.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Plants are fixed in one place; animals generally move.**
>
> - **Support:** a fixed organism must **stand up on its own**, so plant cells have a **rigid cell wall**. Animal cells have none, so they **change shape easily** — flexibility that suits locomotion.
> - **Nutrition:** a fixed organism cannot go and find food, so plants have tissues that **make food from solar energy**. Animals have tissues that **digest food obtained from outside**.
> - **Growth:** plant growth is concentrated in **specific zones (meristems)** that stay active for life; animal growth is more uniform and stops at maturity.

---

Onion bulbs are grown in two jars. On day 3 the root tips of Jar B are cut by 1 cm. Roots in A keep growing; roots in B stop. What does this prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That roots grow ONLY from their tips**, because the tips contain cells that **divide continuously** — the **apical meristem**.
>
> **Why cutting is the decisive test:** if growth happened all along the root, removing 1 cm from the end would barely change anything. **Growth stopping completely proves all the dividing cells were in that 1 cm.**
>
> It also matches the mitosis you saw in onion root tips in Chapter 2 — direct evidence of actively dividing cells there.

---

Name the three types of meristem, where each is located, and what kind of growth each produces.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Meristem | Location | Growth |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Apical** | tips of **roots and shoots** | **length** — taller stems, deeper roots |
> | **Lateral** | in a **ring** along the circumference of the stem | **girth** — thicker stems |
> | **Intercalary** | at the **nodes / base of internodes**, e.g. in grasses | **regrowth** after cutting or grazing |

---

What are annual rings, and what two things can be read from them?

> [!ANSWER]
> Rings visible on a cut tree trunk, produced by the **lateral meristem** dividing to add new cells inside and outside in a concentric manner.
>
> 1. **Counting the rings gives the AGE of the tree.**
> 2. **Wide rings mean a year of favourable growth conditions; narrow rings mean unfavourable** — so the rings record the **climate** the tree lived through.
>
> **Why the rings exist:** the meristem works fast in the good season and slowly in the harsh one, producing visibly different wood. **One good-then-poor cycle = one year = one ring.**

---

Why can grass survive being mowed and grazed repeatedly, while a young tree seedling dies if its tip is bitten off?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Grass keeps its growth zone at the BASE — the intercalary meristem, located at the base of the internode, just above the node.** A grazing animal bites off the **top**, leaving the growth machinery untouched, so the grass simply grows again.
>
> **A seedling grows from its APICAL meristem at the tip.** Bite off the tip and you have removed the **entire growth zone**.
>
> **It is a design that anticipates being eaten** — and it is also why a cut hedge grows back bushier, with new branches from the nodes.

---

List four features of meristematic cells and explain why vacuoles are absent.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Small cells, thin cell walls, a large prominent nucleus, dense cytoplasm with many organelles, tightly packed with little or no intercellular space — and generally no vacuoles.**
>
> **Why no vacuoles:** a large vacuole is mostly **water — storage, not machinery**. A cell whose job is **continuous, rapid division** needs its volume packed with **cytoplasm and organelles** instead.
>
> **Every item on the list serves the same purpose:** maximum machinery, minimum dead weight, thin walls that don't restrict, ready to divide again immediately.

---

What is differentiation?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The process by which meristematic tissue becomes specialised permanent tissue.**
>
> After a meristematic cell divides, **some daughter cells remain meristematic** while others **lose the ability to divide**. Those cells then **change in structure and function** and become specialised for **support, transport or storage**.

---

Distinguish simple from complex permanent tissue, and name examples of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **SIMPLE** — made of **only ONE type of cell**: **parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma**.
> - **COMPLEX** — made of **MORE THAN ONE type of cell working together**: **xylem and phloem**.
>
> Xylem, for instance, contains tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma and xylem fibres — four different cell types cooperating in one tissue.

Compare parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma on: living or dead, wall type, and function.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **Parenchyma** | **Collenchyma** | **Sclerenchyma** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Living? | **living** | **living** | **mostly dead** |
> | Walls | **thin**, cells loosely packed with intercellular spaces | **unevenly thickened at the CORNERS** with **pectin** | **thick**, with **LIGNIN** |
> | Function | **stores food**; **photosynthesis** in green parts; forms **air spaces** that help aquatic plants float | **support with FLEXIBILITY** — stems and tendrils bend without breaking | **hardness and strength** — the woody structure |

---

Why does a fresh twig bend but a dry twig snap?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **fresh** twig has plenty of **living collenchyma**, whose walls are thickened with **pectin — a chemical that gives flexibility, like rubber**. That lets the twig bend and spring back.
>
> **Drying kills those cells**, leaving only rigid lignified material with no give — so the twig **snaps instead of bending**.

---

Coconut husk fibres are hard and brittle; coriander leaf stalks are soft and flexible. Explain the difference in terms of tissue.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Coconut husk = SCLERENCHYMA**, with thick walls hardened by **LIGNIN**. Lignin makes cells **hard and strong but rigid** — good for a protective covering, but it does not bend.
> - **Coriander stalk = COLLENCHYMA**, with corners thickened by **PECTIN**, which gives **rubber-like flexibility**.
>
> **Two different thickening chemicals, two completely different mechanical properties.**

---

Coconut husk is used for tough, fibrous mats. Why could living parenchyma not serve the same purpose?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because parenchyma has THIN walls, loosely packed with intercellular spaces**, and is built for **storage and photosynthesis**, not strength.
>
> Husk works because **sclerenchyma has thick LIGNIFIED walls** that stay hard and intact even after the cells die — which is exactly what a mat needs: strength that lasts long after the plant is gone.
>
> A parenchyma mat would collapse as soon as its cells lost water.

---

Sclerenchyma cells are dead but still useful. How can dead cells do a job?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because their job is purely STRUCTURAL — and a thick, lignified box is just as strong empty as full.**
>
> Parenchyma and collenchyma stay alive because they still **do** things: store, photosynthesise, flex. Sclerenchyma only has to **hold something up**, and **a wall doesn't need to be alive to hold something up**.
>
> So the cell dies, the wall remains, and the plant saves the cost of keeping it alive. Xylem tracheids and vessels are dead for the same reason.

---

What is the epidermis, and name three of its specialised features with their functions.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The outermost layer of the plant body — a tightly packed, single layer of flat, rectangular cells** protecting against mechanical injury, water loss, harmful microorganisms and extreme conditions.
>
> | Feature | Function |
> |---|---|
> | **Cuticle** — a waxy layer of **cutin** | **reduces water loss**; also protects against injury and parasites |
> | **Root hair** — projections from root epidermal cells | **increase surface area** for absorbing water and minerals |
> | **Stomata** — pores in the leaf epidermis | **gaseous exchange** and **transpiration** |

---

Why is a thick cuticle an advantage in a desert plant but a disadvantage in an underwater plant?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the limiting resource is different in each place.**
>
> - **In a desert**, water is scarce and **water loss is the enemy** — a thick waxy cuticle **seals the surface** and saves the plant.
> - **Underwater**, there is no shortage of water at all, but **dissolved gases are scarce**. A thick waxy layer would **block gas exchange**, which the plant urgently needs.
>
> **The same feature, opposite verdicts** — which is why you cannot judge an adaptation without knowing the habitat.

---

What is transpiration, and what two useful things does it do for the plant?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The evaporation of water vapour through the stomata.**
>
> 1. **It creates a TRANSPIRATION PULL in the xylem**, which is what drags water up the plant against gravity.
> 2. It helps **eliminate wastes** from the plant body.
>
> **Why the pull works:** water evaporating at the leaves leaves a gap, and because water molecules cling to one another the whole column is pulled up behind it — like drinking through a straw, but powered by evaporation instead of your lungs.

---

Water absorbed by roots must travel upwards against gravity through DEAD xylem cells. How do dead cells and living leaves work together to move it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The dead cells are the PIPES; the living leaves are the PUMP.**
>
> - Xylem tracheids and vessels are **dead, hollow, thick-walled tubes** — an unobstructed pathway from root to leaf.
> - At the top, **living leaf cells lose water by evaporation through the stomata**. That creates the **transpiration pull**.
> - Water molecules **stick to one another**, so as the topmost molecules leave, the whole column is drawn up behind them.
>
> **No energy is spent by the xylem itself** — the sun's evaporation does the lifting, which is exactly why the transporting cells can afford to be dead.

---

What would happen if there were no stomata in the epidermis of leaves and stems?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **No gaseous exchange** — no carbon dioxide could enter for photosynthesis, and oxygen could not leave. **Photosynthesis would stop.**
> - **No transpiration**, so **no transpiration pull** — the plant could not draw water up from the roots to the leaves.
> - **No elimination of waste** by that route.
>
> The plant would starve and dehydrate at the same time. **Stomata are a compromise:** they must be open to let gas in, which necessarily lets water out — which is why they can close in dry conditions.

---

Why are xylem and phloem called "complex" tissues?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because each is made of SEVERAL DIFFERENT CELL TYPES working together**, unlike simple tissues made of one type.
>
> - **Xylem** = tracheids + vessels + xylem parenchyma + xylem fibres
> - **Phloem** = sieve tubes + companion cells + phloem parenchyma + phloem fibres

---

Name the four components of xylem, say which is living, and state what xylem does.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma, xylem fibres.**
>
> **Only the xylem parenchyma is LIVING.** Tracheids, vessels and fibres are primarily **sclerenchymatous** and dead — tracheids and vessels being **tubular and thick-walled**.
>
> **Function: transports WATER and MINERALS from the roots to the rest of the plant, and provides strength.**

---

Name the four components of phloem and state what each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Component | Job |
> |---|---|
> | **Sieve tubes** | long tubular cells **joined end to end by perforated walls** — they **carry the food** from leaves to the rest of the plant |
> | **Companion cells** | specialised **parenchyma** cells that **regulate the sieve tube's functions**, mainly by **monitoring the loading and unloading of sugars** |
> | **Phloem parenchyma** | **stores** food materials, and resin, tannins and latex |
> | **Phloem fibres** | primarily **sclerenchymatous** — provide **strength** to support the sieve tubes |

---

Xylem cells can be dead but phloem cells cannot. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of what each transport job requires.**
>
> **Xylem** is driven from OUTSIDE — by the **transpiration pull** at the leaves. It needs nothing but **open, strong, hollow pipes**, and a dead cell with a thick wall makes an excellent pipe.
>
> **Phloem** must **actively load and unload sugars** at particular places, and it moves food in **both directions** depending on the season. That needs **living machinery** — which is exactly why sieve tubes keep **companion cells** beside them to run the loading and unloading.

---

Name the three plant tissue systems and what each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> | System | Role |
> |---|---|
> | **Dermal** | forms the **outer covering** — protects inner parts and **reduces water loss** |
> | **Ground** | the **main body** between dermal and conducting tissues — parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma |
> | **Vascular** | the **conducting tissues** — **xylem and phloem** |

---

How does bark form, and why are cork cells useful even though they are dead?

> [!ANSWER]
> In young plants the outer protective layer is a **single-layered epidermis**. As the plant ages, some cells **below the stem epidermis gain the ability to divide**, acting as a lateral meristem called the **CORK CAMBIUM**. Its divisions produce **cork cells**, and this forms the **bark**.
>
> **Cork cells are dead, compactly arranged, and contain a substance that makes them impermeable to water and gases.**
>
> **Being dead is no drawback for a sealing layer** — indeed being dead and packed with waterproofing is exactly what makes it a good seal.

---

An elephant strips the bark off a tree. Which function is hampered, and what happens if the damage goes deeper?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) Debarking removes the protective outer layer**, exposing the trunk to **water loss, mechanical injury and infection by microorganisms**.
>
> **(ii) Just beneath the bark lie the PHLOEM and the lateral meristem (cambium).** Further damage destroys these.
>
> **(iii) If the phloem is severely damaged, FOOD MADE IN THE LEAVES CANNOT REACH THE ROOTS.** The roots starve — and since the roots supply water to the whole tree, the tree eventually dies even though its leaves are still green. Damage to the cambium also stops any increase in girth and prevents repair.
>
> **(iv) Assumptions:** that the damage is deep enough to reach phloem and cambium, that it goes all the way round the trunk, and that the xylem (deeper in) is intact. **If the strip is only partial, the remaining phloem can still carry food and the tree may survive and heal.**

---

If a plant cannot transport food from its leaves to its roots, which tissue is malfunctioning — xylem, phloem, epidermis or sclerenchyma?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Phloem.**
>
> **Phloem carries FOOD from the leaves to the rest of the plant.** Xylem carries **water and minerals upward from the roots** — the opposite cargo in the opposite direction. Epidermis protects; sclerenchyma supports.

---

A young mango sapling's stem bends in monsoon winds without breaking. Which tissue is responsible, and what would happen if it were replaced by sclerenchyma?

> [!ANSWER]
> **COLLENCHYMA** — living cells with corners unevenly thickened by **pectin**, giving flexibility like rubber.
>
> **If it were replaced by sclerenchyma:** the stem would be **thick-walled, lignified, hard and rigid**. It would resist a gentle push better, but in a strong monsoon wind it would have **no give — it would SNAP** instead of bending.
>
> **The lesson:** for a young stem in wind, **flexibility is stronger than stiffness**. A rigid stem fails suddenly; a flexible one bends and recovers.

---

Rohan says "a tissue is a group of similar cells performing similar functions". Rajiv says this is only true for simple tissues. Who is right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Rajiv's qualification is correct.**
>
> The definition fits **SIMPLE** tissues exactly — parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma are each made of **one cell type**.
>
> But **COMPLEX** tissues like **xylem and phloem contain SEVERAL different cell types** (tracheids, vessels, parenchyma, fibres) with **different structures and different individual jobs**.
>
> **The better general definition:** a tissue is a group of cells that **work together to perform a common function** — whether or not the cells are all alike. In xylem the shared function is transporting water; the cells simply divide that work between them.

---

A textbook states "each plant tissue performs only one specific function". What questions would you ask to test this, and which tissues would you examine?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Questions to ask:** Does any single tissue do more than one thing? Does the same tissue do different jobs in different parts of the plant? Does it change function with age or conditions?
>
> **Tissues to examine:**
> - **Parenchyma** — **stores food**, but also **performs photosynthesis** in green parts, and forms **air spaces for floating** in aquatic plants. Three different jobs.
> - **Xylem** — **transports water and minerals** AND **provides strength**.
> - **Epidermis** — **protects**, but also **absorbs** (root hair) and enables **gas exchange and transpiration** (stomata).
>
> **Conclusion: the statement is too strong.** Many tissues are multifunctional; the specialisation is real but not exclusive.

---

Vibha says "meristematic cells are located only at the root and shoot apices". Is she right, and what question would help her see the problem?

> [!ANSWER]
> **She is wrong** — she has named only the **apical** meristem.
>
> **Questions Neha could ask:**
> - *"Then how does a tree trunk get THICKER every year?"* → the **lateral meristem** in a ring inside the stem.
> - *"Why does grass grow back after mowing, when its tips have been cut off?"* → the **intercalary meristem** at the nodes.
> - *"What forms the bark of an old tree?"* → the **cork cambium**, a lateral meristem below the epidermis.
>
> **Each question points at growth that the apical meristem cannot explain**, which is the cleanest way to show a claim is incomplete.

---

Sohan plants two kinds of sugarcane cutting. Type B sprouts; type A does not. What was different, and what should he keep the same for a fair test?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) and (ii) Type B contained at least one NODE; type A was a piece of internode only.**
>
> New shoots and roots arise from **buds at the nodes**, and the **intercalary meristem** sits at the base of the internode just above the node. **A piece with no node has no growth zone at all** and cannot sprout, however healthy it looks.
>
> **(iii) What was measured:** whether shoots emerged, how many, and their length after a fixed number of weeks.
>
> **(iv) To keep the comparison fair:** same **length and thickness** of cutting, same **variety and age** of cane, same **soil, water, light and temperature**, planted at the same **depth** on the same **day**, and **several cuttings of each type** rather than one — so the only difference is the presence of a node.

---

What property of meristematic cells lets them divide repeatedly — thick walls, large vacuoles, thin walls with dense cytoplasm and a large nucleus, or being differentiated?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Thin walls, dense cytoplasm and a large prominent nucleus.**
>
> **Why the others are wrong:** thick walls would **restrict expansion**; large vacuoles would fill the cell with **water instead of machinery**; and **differentiated cells have LOST the ability to divide** — that is what differentiation means.

Name the four types of animal tissue and what each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Tissue | Role |
> |---|---|
> | **Epithelial** | forms the **outer covering** of the body and the **lining of internal organs** — protection, exchange, secretion, absorption, sensation |
> | **Connective** | **connects and supports** other tissues and organs |
> | **Muscular** | produces **voluntary and involuntary movement** |
> | **Nervous** | **receives and transmits impulses**; regulates body activities |

---

Why are the epithelial tissues lining internal organs usually only one or a few cells thick?

> [!ANSWER]
> **To allow quick exchange of materials across them.**
>
> Diffusion across a **thick** barrier is slow; a **single layer of thin, flat cells** in the lungs and blood vessels lets gases and liquids pass rapidly.
>
> **The trade-off:** thin means fragile. That is exactly why **skin, mouth and oesophagus** — which face friction and injury — use **many layers of tightly packed cells** instead. **You cannot be thin and protective at once.**

---

Match structure to function: thin flat cells / many layers / tall pillar-like cells with hair-like structures / cells with cilia.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Structure | Function | Where |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Single layer of thin, flat cells** | **exchange** — rapid diffusion of gases and liquids | lining of blood vessels and lungs |
> | **Many layers, outer cells flat and tightly packed** | **protection** from injury, friction and microbes | skin, mouth, oesophagus |
> | **Single layer of tall, pillar-like cells with hair-like structures** | **absorption** of nutrients and water | lining of the small intestine |
> | **Receptor cells with hair-like cilia** | **sensory** — smell, taste, sound, balance | nostrils, taste buds, inner ear |
>
> **Secretion** uses **cuboidal or columnar cells specialised to produce and release substances** — salivary glands, sweat glands, stomach lining.

---

Why are the absorbing cells of the small intestine tall and covered in hair-like projections?

> [!ANSWER]
> **To maximise SURFACE AREA.**
>
> Absorption happens **across a surface**, so the more surface packed into the same length of intestine, the more nutrients are taken up. **Tall pillar-like cells** stand more surface per unit of floor space, and the **hair-like projections** multiply it again.
>
> It is the same principle as the **cristae in mitochondria** and the **root hairs** on a plant root — whenever a job happens at a surface, evolution folds that surface.

---

Blood and bone are both connective tissues, yet one is liquid and one is rock-hard. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **All connective tissues share the same plan — cells scattered in a MATRIX. The difference is entirely in the matrix.**
>
> - **Blood:** the matrix (plasma) is **watery, soft and jelly-like** → the tissue is **fluid**.
> - **Bone:** the matrix is **hard, solid and rigid**, containing **calcium and phosphorus compounds** → **strength and rigidity**.
>
> The cells are suspended in a material; **whatever that material is like, the tissue is like**.

---

Name the components of blood and what each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Plasma is about 55% of the total volume; formed elements about 45%.**
>
> | Component | Function |
> |---|---|
> | **RBCs** | carry oxygen; red from **haemoglobin**, an **iron-rich protein**; live about **4 months** and are replaced regularly |
> | **WBCs** | defend against infection — they **collect at infected areas**, causing pus, inflammation, redness and swelling |
> | **Platelets** | **clot the blood** at the site of injury |
> | **Plasma** | the fluid matrix that carries everything |

---

Explain in terms of blood: why a cut clots, why an infected patch turns red and swollen, and why your face goes red when you run.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **A cut clots** because **platelets** act at the site of injury to form a clot, sealing the wound.
> - **An infected patch turns red and swollen** because **WBCs collect at the infected area**, producing inflammation and sometimes pus. Redness and swelling are the defence at work, not the infection itself.
> - **Your face goes red when you run** because working **muscles need more oxygen**, so **breathing becomes faster and blood flow increases** — more blood near the skin surface shows as redness.

---

Distinguish tendon from ligament, and explain why each is arranged the way it is.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **TENDON connects MUSCLE to BONE** — it **transmits the pulling force** of a contracting muscle to the bone, producing movement.
> - **LIGAMENT connects BONE to BONE** — it provides **stability, limits movement and helps prevent dislocation**.
>
> **Why each runs where it does:** a tendon must carry a **pull**, so it runs **from the pulling thing to the pulled thing**. A ligament must **stop** a joint going too far, so it runs **between the two bones it restrains** — which is why your raised leg stops at a definite limit.
>
> **Memory aid:** **Ten**don → **T**o the bone from muscle. **Liga**ment → **li**nks bone to bone.

---

Touch your ear, then your elbow. Which connective tissues are you feeling, and what does each do?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **The ear is soft, flexible and springs back into shape — CARTILAGE.** It provides **flexibility** and **cushions the ends of bones for shock absorption**, thanks to its **soft, jelly-like matrix**.
> - **The elbow is hard and rigid — BONE.** It gives **strength, support and protection**, thanks to a **rigid matrix containing calcium and phosphorus compounds**.
>
> **Same tissue class, opposite properties, because of the matrix.**

Compare skeletal, smooth and cardiac muscle on control, shape, nuclei, striations and location.

> [!ANSWER]

> |---|---|---|---|

---

Why can cardiac muscle contract continuously without fatigue?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because cardiac muscle cells have a **high number of mitochondria** and an **abundant blood supply**.
>
> **Why that matters:** many mitochondria means a large, continuous production of **ATP**; abundant blood supply means **oxygen and fuel never run short**. Skeletal muscle, worked hard, **outruns its oxygen supply** and fatigues.
>
> **The heart cannot be allowed to rest, so it is built never to need to.**

---

Why are cardiac muscle fibres branched?

> [!ANSWER]
> **So the contraction signal can spread SIDEWAYS through the whole heart wall at once.**
>
> Branching connects each fibre to several neighbours, so the chamber contracts **as one coordinated unit** rather than fibre by fibre. A heart whose fibres contracted at slightly different moments would **squirm instead of pumping**.

---

Name the three parts of a neuron and explain how the shape of the cell fits its job.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Part | Job |
> |---|---|
> | **Cell body** | contains the **nucleus** and **controls the cell's activities** |
> | **Dendrites** | **receive** signals from other neurons |
> | **Axon** | a **long fibre** that carries the message away, ending at **axon terminals** which pass it to other cells |
>
> **Why this shape:** **many short dendrites** collect inputs from many sources; **one long axon** delivers the message intact over a long distance. A neuron looks like nothing else in the body because **nothing else has to gather from many places and deliver far away**.

---

During exercise your heart beats faster without you deciding to. Which tissue is responsible, and what does this show about muscle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Nervous tissue.** The **brain** signals the heart to beat faster to meet the body's increased **oxygen demand**.
>
> **What it shows:** **muscles — both voluntary and involuntary — cannot function independently.** They **receive instructions from the nervous tissue**. The heart's cardiac muscle does the work, but the decision about when to speed up is made elsewhere.

---

Trace the chain of events from deciding to lift your arm to the arm actually moving.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> nerve signal from the brain
>    → SKELETAL MUSCLE contracts
>    → TENDON transmits the force to the bone
>    → BONE moves at the JOINT
> ```
> **Note what joints do NOT do: joints allow movement but cannot move bones on their own.** They are the hinge, not the motor. **Muscles do all the pulling** — and muscles can only **pull**, never push, which is why they come in opposing pairs.

---

Name the four types of joint with an example of each and the movement it allows.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Joint | Example | Movement |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Ball and socket** | **shoulder**, hip | **forward, backward, sideways and circular** — the freest |
> | **Hinge** | **elbow**, **knee** (protected by the kneecap) | **one direction only** — bend and straighten, like a door hinge |
> | **Pivot** | **skull on the backbone** | **side-to-side rotation** — shaking your head 'no', like a doorknob turning |
> | **Fixed** | bones of the **skull** | **none** |

---

The skull's joints allow no movement at all. Is that a design failure?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — it is the whole point.**
>
> The skull is a **hard case of flat bones joined together to protect the brain, eyes and ears**. Fixed joints mean the case **cannot shift or open**, so **the brain stays safe even when the body moves violently**.
>
> **The general principle: freedom of movement is traded against stability.** The shoulder moves in every direction and is the easiest joint to dislocate; the skull moves not at all and never fails.

---

Which type of joint is used when you bend your knees and ankles for a jump?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Hinge joints** — they bend and straighten in **one plane only**.
>
> **Why bending matters in a jump:** with knees and ankles **stiff**, you jump using almost no muscle range and land with the shock going straight up the skeleton. **Bending naturally** lets the leg muscles pull through a long range (more force for the take-off) and lets the joints **absorb the landing gradually**, cushioned by the **cartilage** at the bone ends.

---

Assertion: "Tendons connect bone to bone and allow joint movement." Reason: "Tendons are tough connective tissue that transmits force from muscle to bone." Evaluate both.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The assertion is FALSE; the reason is TRUE.**
>
> Tendons connect **muscle to bone**, not bone to bone — **ligaments** connect bone to bone. The reason correctly states what a tendon actually does, and in doing so **contradicts the assertion**.
>
> **A useful habit:** when a reason contradicts its assertion, at least one of them is wrong — check which.

---

Assertion: "In a hinge joint, movement occurs primarily in one plane." Reason: "The bone ends are shaped to allow sliding in all directions." Evaluate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The assertion is TRUE; the reason is FALSE.**
>
> A hinge joint does move in **one plane only** — but that is precisely **because the bone ends are shaped to PREVENT movement in other directions**, not to allow sliding in all of them. Sliding in all directions describes a **ball and socket** joint instead.

---

Describe the vertebral column and explain how it solves three conflicting requirements at once.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **flexible column of small bones called VERTEBRAE**, running from the base of the skull, with a **CARTILAGE DISC between each pair** acting as a cushion.
>
> **The three requirements:**
> 1. **Stiff enough to hold you upright** — the stacked bones do this.
> 2. **Flexible enough to bend and twist** — the many small joints between them do this.
> 3. **Protective of the delicate spinal cord** — the bones form a tunnel around it, and the discs stop bone grinding on bone.
>
> **A single solid rod would do only the first.** Many small bones with springy pads between them do all three.

---

Why must the rib cage — a protective cage — be able to move?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because breathing depends on it.**
>
> The **12 pairs of ribs** attach to the **spine at the back** and the **breastbone (sternum) in front**, joined by **flexible cartilage**. That flexibility lets the cage **expand and contract**, changing the space in the chest and so **moving air in and out of the lungs**.
>
> **This is why injured ribs make breathing painful and difficult** — you cannot take a breath without moving the broken part. A perfectly rigid chest would protect the heart and lungs and then suffocate you.

What did F. C. Steward demonstrate in 1958, and what steps did the carrot cells go through?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That single cells from the vascular PHLOEM of a carrot can regenerate a whole plant** — he was the first to do so.
>
> He grew carrot phloem cells in a **nutrient medium with simple sugars and hormones**:
> ```
> mature phloem cell
>    → DEDIFFERENTIATION   (the cell regains the ability to divide)
>    → an undifferentiated mass of unspecialised cells
>    → REDIFFERENTIATION   (given nutrients and growth chemicals)
>    → roots, shoot, and finally a complete plant
> ```

---

What is totipotency, and why is it a surprising property?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The ability of a mature cell to dedifferentiate, divide and redifferentiate into a complete new organism.**
>
> **Why it is surprising:** it means **differentiation is not a one-way door** in plants. A specialised phloem cell had apparently "become" a transport cell for good — yet it still carried **every instruction needed to build an entire plant**. It had simply **stopped using most of them**.
>
> **This is similar to the ability of a ZYGOTE** to divide and differentiate into an entire organism — except the zygote never specialised in the first place.

---

Steward found: solid medium → weight reduced; liquid medium with light and air → 20% increase; liquid medium without them → reduced. What does this tell you, and where would biomass be highest and lowest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Highest: liquid medium WITH light and air. Lowest: either of the reduced conditions.**
>
> **What the comparison shows:** the medium alone is not enough. **Liquid beats solid** because the cells are surrounded by nutrients on all sides and stirring can shear them apart into single cells. But the **same liquid medium fails without light and air**, so those are required too.
>
> **Why light and air matter:** the cells need **oxygen for respiration** and, once green tissue forms, **light for photosynthesis**.
>
> **The method is the lesson:** by changing **one factor at a time**, each result becomes readable. Compare only the first and second rows and you would wrongly credit everything to the liquid medium.

---

Would you get the same result culturing animal cells instead of carrot cells?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — not a whole organism.**
>
> Animal cells **can** be grown in culture (that is how vaccines and many medicines are produced), and they will **divide and multiply**. But an ordinary differentiated animal cell **does not regain the ability to rebuild the entire organism** the way a plant cell does.
>
> **Why plants differ:** plants stay rooted and are regularly damaged by grazing, wind and fire, so the ability to **regenerate from whatever survives** is enormously valuable. Animals move away from danger instead, and their highly specialised tissues cannot simply revert.

---

Name two commercial applications of the carrot totipotency work.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Plant tissue culture / micropropagation** — producing **thousands of genetically identical plants** from a small piece of tissue, quickly and in a small space, which is the basis of the nursery and horticulture industry.
> - **Crop improvement** — raising **disease-free planting material**, propagating rare or slow-growing species, and the **anther culture** route developed by Sipra Guha Mukherjee and S. C. Maheshwari.
> - **Production of valuable phytochemicals** — growing plant cells in vats to make medicines and other compounds without growing whole fields.

Crown gall disease is caused by *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*. How did a plant disease become a useful tool?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In crown gall disease, tumour-like swellings develop on stems due to rapid, uncontrolled cell division.**
>
> **Instead of only trying to CURE the disease, scientists studied HOW the bacterium transfers its genetic material into plant cells.** That mechanism turned out to be exactly what genetic engineers needed.
>
> **Today *Agrobacterium* is used as a tool to introduce useful genes into plants** — for valuable phytochemicals, improved crops and disease-resistant varieties.
>
> **The lesson:** a disease is a mechanism doing something unwanted. **Understand the mechanism and you can aim it somewhere useful.**

A plant cell and an animal cell are the same size. Which has the larger vacuole, and what are you assuming?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The plant cell**, by a large margin. A mature plant cell usually has **one large central vacuole** filled with cell sap, storing water, minerals, sugars and waste, and **maintaining the pressure that keeps the cell firm**. Animal cells have **small vacuoles, if any**, used only for temporary storage.
>
> **Assumptions:** that the plant cell is a **mature, differentiated** one — a **meristematic** plant cell has almost **no vacuole at all**, so the comparison would fail. Also that the animal cell is an ordinary body cell, not a specialised storage cell.
