Hydrogen burns and oxygen supports burning, yet water puts out fire. What does this show, and what is nevertheless preserved?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It shows that the properties of the elements are NOT retained when they form a compound.** Water is a completely new substance, not a blend of hydrogen and oxygen behaviour — it is a liquid at ordinary temperatures while both its elements are gases.
>
> **But the MASS is preserved:** the mass of the water formed equals the sum of the masses of the hydrogen and oxygen that combined.
>
> **That contrast is the whole chapter in one line** — everything about the substance can change except how much matter there is.

---

Salt is dissolved in water and the beaker is reweighed. What do you find, and what does it tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The mass of the solution equals the sum of the masses of the water and the salt taken.**
>
> **There is practically no change in mass during the formation of a solution** — and this is true for **all physical changes**. Tearing a sheet of paper into pieces gives the same result.
>
> **Dissolving rearranges where matter is, not how much there is.**

---

Baking soda is tipped into vinegar in an open flask and the total mass drops. In a second set-up with a balloon fixed over the flask, the mass is unchanged. Explain both results.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both reactions produce carbon dioxide gas, seen as a brisk effervescence.**
>
> - **Open flask:** the **carbon dioxide ESCAPES into the room**, so the balance no longer weighs it. The final reading is lower.
> - **Balloon fixed on:** the gas **inflates the balloon and stays in the system**, so nothing leaves and **the final reading matches the initial**.
>
> **The mass never actually changed.** The open set-up had a **leak**, not a broken law — and comparing the two set-ups is what proves it.

---

State the Law of Conservation of Mass and say who proposed it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.**
>
> Proposed by **Antoine Lavoisier in 1789**, known as the **Father of Modern Chemistry**. In his own words: *"in every operation an equal quantity of matter exists both before and after the operation."*

---

Why must both the flask and the balloon stay on the balance throughout, and why doesn't a tiny difference in readings disprove the law?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both on the balance:** small traces of baking soda may remain **stuck to the balloon**. Weighing the flask and balloon **together** before and after means nothing is accidentally left out of either measurement.
>
> **Small differences:** any measurement carries an **uncertainty of about ±1 in the last digit**. A variation within that range is **experimental error, not a change in mass**.
>
> **Knowing the size of your own uncertainty is what lets you tell a real effect from noise.**

---

A student burns 10 g of ethanol in an open beaker and no residue is left. Has the Law of Conservation of Mass been violated?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> The products of burning ethanol are **carbon dioxide and water vapour** — **both gases**, which **escape into the air**. Nothing has been destroyed; it has simply **left the beaker**.
>
> **Repeat it in a sealed container and the total mass would be exactly unchanged.** This is the vinegar-and-baking-soda lesson in another form: **an open system that loses gas only LOOKS like a violation.**

---

4.0 g of calcium carbonate reacts with 2.92 g of hydrochloric acid in a closed container, giving 1.76 g carbon dioxide, 0.72 g water and 4.44 g calcium chloride. Is the law obeyed?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total reactants = 4.0 + 2.92 = 6.92 g
> total products  = 1.76 + 0.72 + 4.44 = 6.92 g
> ```
> **Yes — mass of reactants = mass of products, so the Law of Conservation of Mass is obeyed.**
>
> **Note "closed container" is doing real work in this question.** Carbon dioxide is a gas; in an open vessel 1.76 g of it would have walked away.

---

5.3 g of sodium carbonate reacts with 6.0 g of acetic acid to give 2.2 g carbon dioxide, 0.9 g water and 8.2 g sodium acetate. Verify the law.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> reactants = 5.3 + 6.0 = 11.3 g
> products  = 2.2 + 0.9 + 8.2 = 11.3 g
> ```
> **The law is valid.**

---

20 g of hydrogen reacts completely with 160 g of oxygen. How much water is formed?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> by conservation of mass:  20 + 160 = 180 g of water
> ```
> **Note the check:** 20 : 160 simplifies to **1 : 8**, which is exactly the fixed mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen in water — so all of both really does react, with nothing left over.

---

12 g of carbon combines with 32 g of oxygen to give 44 g of carbon dioxide. How much carbon dioxide comes from 2.4 g of carbon?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 12 g of carbon gives 44 g of CO₂
> 1 g gives 44/12 g
> 2.4 g gives (44/12) × 2.4 = 8.8 g of carbon dioxide
> ```

Design an experiment to test whether mass is conserved when zinc reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Zinc + Hydrochloric acid (dilute) → Zinc chloride + Hydrogen
> ```
> **The reaction produces HYDROGEN GAS, so the system must be CLOSED — that is the whole design problem.**
>
> 1. Put the dilute hydrochloric acid in a conical flask and the zinc granules in a **balloon** tied over its mouth (as in the baking-soda experiment).
> 2. Weigh the whole assembly on a digital balance and record the reading.
> 3. Lift the balloon so the zinc falls into the acid; **the balloon inflates** with hydrogen.
> 4. Weigh the whole assembly again, **balloon still attached**.
>
> **Expected result:** the two readings agree within experimental error, showing mass is conserved.
>
> **Safety:** hydrogen is flammable — keep well away from any flame.

---

State the Law of Constant Proportions and give the evidence for it from water.

> [!ANSWER]
> **In any compound formed by two or more elements, the elements combine in a FIXED RATIO BY MASS, irrespective of the source of the compound.** (Also called the Law of Definite Proportions, or Proust's Law.)
>
> **The evidence:** water purified from **rivers, borewells or the ocean** and then analysed **always** contains hydrogen and oxygen in the mass ratio **1 : 8**. Decompose 9 g of pure water from any source and you get **1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen** every time.
>
> **Joseph Proust** showed the same for **copper carbonate** — always the same proportions of copper, carbon and oxygen, however it was prepared or wherever it was found.

---

Why does the Law of Definite Proportions hold for compounds but not for mixtures?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because in a COMPOUND the atoms are joined by CHEMICAL BONDS in a fixed whole-number ratio.** You cannot add a bit more oxygen to water and still have water — you would have a different substance, or leftover oxygen.
>
> **In a MIXTURE the components are simply put together in any proportion you like.** Salt water can be weak or strong; both are still salt water, and neither has a definite composition.
>
> **A fixed ratio is a consequence of BONDING** — and a mixture has none.

---

Ancient civilisations found cinnabar always gives 86.22% mercury and 13.78% sulfur. Why is this significant?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the SAME fixed ratio was found independently, in separate civilisations, at different times and places.**
>
> Cinnabar (**hingula** in India) is a red rock pigment. Heating it yields mercury and sulfur in that exact proportion — and grinding mercury and sulfur together in that ratio re-forms cinnabar.
>
> **That reproducibility across independent observers is exactly what a LAW OF NATURE looks like.** A result that only one group can get is a curiosity; one that everyone gets is a law. (Both elements are toxic, which is why the process never became widespread.)

---

Sodium chloride contains sodium and chlorine in the mass ratio 23 : 35.5. How much chlorine reacts completely with 46 g of sodium?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> chlorine = (35.5 / 23) × 46 = 35.5 × 2 = 71 g
> ```

---

A compound is 40% sulfur and 60% oxygen by mass. How much oxygen accompanies 20 g of sulfur?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> S : O = 40 : 60 = 2 : 3
>
> 20 g of sulfur → oxygen = (3/2) × 20 = 30 g
> ```
> **30 g of oxygen.** By the Law of Constant Proportions, that ratio holds for **any** sample of this compound, whatever its size or source.

---

Carbon monoxide contains carbon and oxygen in the mass ratio 3 : 4. How much oxygen combines with 9 g of carbon?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> oxygen = (4/3) × 9 = 12 g
> ```

---

Student X makes copper oxide by combining copper and oxygen in the ratio 4 : 1; student Y uses 8 : 2. Do their results justify the Law of Constant Proportions?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> ```
> 8 : 2  simplifies to  4 : 1
> ```
> **The two ratios are IDENTICAL.** Y simply used twice as much of everything, which changes the amount of product but not the proportion.
>
> **The law is about the RATIO, not the quantity** — which is exactly why it can be tested by two students working on different scales.

---

What is a postulate, and state Dalton's six postulates.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A POSTULATE is a fundamental assumption accepted as true without formal proof, from which further ideas are developed.**
>
> 1. **All matter is made of very tiny particles called ATOMS**, which take part in chemical reactions.
> 2. **Atoms are indivisible and cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.**
> 3. **Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties.**
> 4. **Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties.**
> 5. **Atoms combine in the ratio of simple whole numbers to form compounds.**
> 6. **The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound.**

---

Show how Dalton's postulates explain BOTH the Law of Conservation of Mass and the Law of Constant Proportions.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Conservation of mass ← postulate 2.** In a chemical reaction, **atoms are indivisible and merely REARRANGE** — none is created or destroyed. Since the same atoms are present before and after, the total mass cannot change.
>
> **Constant proportions ← postulates 3, 5 and 6.** If **every atom of an element has the same mass** (3), and atoms combine in a **fixed simple whole-number ratio** (5, 6), then the **ratio by MASS must be fixed too**.
>
> **This is what a good theory does:** it takes two separate experimental laws, found independently, and shows that both follow from a single picture of matter.

---

Assertion: "2 g of hydrogen combines with 16 g of oxygen to form 18 g of water." Reason: "According to Dalton's Atomic Theory, atoms combine in a simple whole number ratio to form compounds." Evaluate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both are TRUE, and the reason correctly explains the assertion.**
>
> The assertion actually illustrates **two** things at once:
> - `2 + 16 = 18` shows **conservation of mass** — nothing was lost;
> - `2 : 16 = 1 : 8` is exactly the **fixed mass ratio** of hydrogen to oxygen in water.
>
> **And both trace back to Dalton:** atoms combining in fixed whole-number ratios, and merely rearranging rather than being created or destroyed. The reason is the underlying explanation of what the numbers show.

---

Define a molecule, and explain why helium does not form one.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A MOLECULE is an electrically neutral entity consisting of MORE THAN ONE atom, capable of INDEPENDENT EXISTENCE, and showing all the properties of that substance.**
>
> - Atoms of the **same** element → a molecule of an **element** (H₂, O₂)
> - Atoms of **different** elements → a molecule of a **compound** (HCl, H₂O)
>
> **Helium exists only as single atoms** because its **atoms are already stable** — the K-shell holds 2 electrons, which is a full outermost shell. **It has nothing to gain by bonding**, so it never does.

---

What are the two ways atoms combine, and why do they bond at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **SHARING of electrons** — atoms share a few or all of their valence electrons → a **COVALENT** bond
> - **TRANSFER of electrons** — one atom gives one or more valence electrons and another accepts them → an **IONIC** bond
>
> **Why they bond: when atoms combine, the TOTAL ENERGY of the system becomes LOWER than the sum of the energies of the separate atoms** — and a lower-energy arrangement is more stable.
>
> **The force holding the atoms together is called a CHEMICAL BOND.**

---

Explain the formation of a hydrogen molecule and define a covalent bond.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Hydrogen (Z = 1)** has one electron in the K-shell, which can hold **two** — so each atom needs **one more**.
>
> **Two hydrogen atoms share one electron each**, giving both a filled K-shell → **H₂**.
>
> **The SHARED PAIR of electrons attracts BOTH nuclei, and that attraction holds the molecule together. That is a COVALENT BOND.**
>
> **Two atoms sharing one electron each are joined by a SINGLE BOND**, drawn as **H—H**.

---

Explain the bonding in a chlorine molecule and an oxygen molecule, and say why they differ.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Chlorine (2, 8, 7)** — each atom needs **ONE** electron. Two atoms share **one electron each** → one shared pair → a **SINGLE BOND**, **Cl—Cl**.
> - **Oxygen (2, 6)** — each atom needs **TWO** electrons. Two atoms share **two electrons each** → two shared pairs → a **DOUBLE BOND**, **O=O**.
>
> **The number of electrons each atom still needs decides the number of bonds.** Need 1 → single; need 2 → double; need 3 → triple.

---

Nitrogen has five valence electrons. Describe the nitrogen molecule.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Nitrogen is 2, 5** — with 5 valence electrons it needs **3 more** to complete its octet.
>
> **Two nitrogen atoms therefore share THREE electrons each**, giving **three shared pairs** — a **TRIPLE BOND**, drawn **N≡N**.
>
> **This is why nitrogen gas is so unreactive:** a triple bond is very strong, and a great deal of energy is needed to break it — which is why N₂ makes up most of the air yet takes part in almost nothing.

---

Fluorine has atomic number 9. Explain the formation of F₂.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fluorine is 2, 7** — it needs **one** electron to complete its octet.
>
> **Two fluorine atoms share one electron each**, giving both a complete octet → **F—F**, a **single covalent bond**.
>
> **Same pattern as chlorine (2, 8, 7)**, which is exactly why the two elements behave so similarly — what matters is the **7 valence electrons**, not the number of inner shells.

---

Explain the formation of a water molecule, and say why the formula is H₂O rather than HO.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Hydrogen** needs **1** electron to complete its duplet.
> - **Oxygen (2, 6)** needs **2** electrons to complete its octet.
>
> **One hydrogen cannot satisfy oxygen** — it has only one electron to offer. So **TWO hydrogen atoms each share one electron with one oxygen atom**, and now everyone is complete.
>
> **That is where the formula comes from: H₂O is not a convention, it is a direct consequence of oxygen needing two while each hydrogen supplies one.**

Name the following: CO₂, NO₂, SF₆, PCl₃, N₂O₅, CS₂.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Formula | Name |
> |---|---|
> | **CO₂** | carbon **dioxide** |
> | **NO₂** | nitrogen **dioxide** |
> | **SF₆** | sulfur **hexafluoride** |
> | **PCl₃** | phosphorus **trichloride** |
> | **N₂O₅** | **dinitrogen pentoxide** |
> | **CS₂** | carbon **disulfide** |
>
> **The rule:** first element keeps its name, second ends in **-ide**, and prefixes **mono- di- tri- tetra- penta- hexa-** give the counts.

---

Why is CO called carbon monoxide and not "monooxide"? And why is CO₂ not "monocarbon dioxide"?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **"Monoxide", not "monooxide":** when a prefix ends in **'o' or 'a'** and the element begins with a **vowel**, the **last vowel of the prefix is dropped**. Hence mon**oxide**, pent**oxide**, tetr**oxide**.
> - **No "monocarbon":** **mono- is usually OMITTED for the FIRST element** but kept for the second. So CO₂ is simply **carbon dioxide**.
> - **But the 'i' is kept** for pronunciation — d**i**oxide, tr**i**oxide, never "doxide".

---

Why is H₂S called hydrogen sulfide and not dihydrogen sulfide? And why are H₂O and NH₃ exceptions?

> [!ANSWER]
> **When hydrogen is the FIRST element in the formula, no prefix is added before it, however many hydrogen atoms there are.** So H₂S is **hydrogen sulfide**.
>
> **H₂O and NH₃ are kept by their common names.** Strictly, H₂O would be "hydrogen monoxide" and NH₃ "nitrogen trihydride" — but **water** and **ammonia** were named long before the system existed, and everyone knows them by those names.
>
> **Naming systems have to live alongside history**, which is why a few binary compounds are known only by their common names.

---

Explain how sodium chloride is formed, and define an ionic bond.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sodium (2, 8, 1)** loses its single valence electron:
> ```
> Na → Na⁺ + e⁻       11 protons, 10 electrons → charge +1 → SODIUM CATION
> ```
> **Chlorine (2, 8, 7)** gains one electron:
> ```
> Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻       17 protons, 18 electrons → charge −1 → CHLORIDE ANION
> ```
> **Both now have stable configurations.** The two oppositely charged ions are then held together by the **ELECTROSTATIC FORCE OF ATTRACTION** between them.
>
> **That force is the IONIC BOND**, and the compound is **NaCl**.

---

Why does sodium lose an electron while chlorine gains one, rather than the other way round?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Each takes the cheaper route to a complete octet.**
>
> - **Sodium has 1 valence electron.** Losing that one leaves the stable 2, 8. Gaining seven would be far harder.
> - **Chlorine has 7.** Gaining one completes the octet. Losing seven would be far harder.
>
> **The general rule: fewer than 4 valence electrons → DONATE; more than 4 → GAIN or SHARE.** Four exactly → **share**, since neither route is cheaper.

---

What ion does oxygen form, and what ion does sulfur form? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Oxygen is 2, 6** — it has **6 valence electrons and needs 2 more**, so it gains two and becomes **O²⁻**, an anion with two units of negative charge.
> - **Sulfur is 2, 8, 6** — same 6 valence electrons, same need, so it forms **S²⁻**.
>
> **They behave alike because what matters is the number of VALENCE electrons, not the number of shells.**

---

Magnesium can give two electrons to become Mg²⁺, but chlorine takes only one. How does magnesium chloride form?

> [!ANSWER]
> **One magnesium ion needs TWO chlorine atoms to absorb its two electrons.**
> ```
> Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
> two Cl atoms each take one:  2Cl + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻
>
> ONE Mg²⁺  +  TWO Cl⁻  →  MgCl₂
> ```
> **The compound's formula is fixed by the requirement that the total positive and negative charges must BALANCE** — which is exactly what the criss-cross method encodes.

---

Illustrate how sodium sulfide (Na₂S) is formed.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> sodium (2, 8, 1) loses 1 electron:   Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
> sulfur (2, 8, 6) needs 2 electrons:  S + 2e⁻ → S²⁻
>
> so TWO sodium atoms are needed for ONE sulfur atom
>
> 2Na⁺  +  S²⁻  →  Na₂S
> ```
> **Charge balance: 2 × (+1) + (−2) = 0.** The compound must be electrically neutral overall.

---

Show the formation of the cations of potassium and calcium, and of their chlorides.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> potassium (2, 8, 8, 1)  → K⁺ + e⁻       (loses 1)
> calcium   (2, 8, 8, 2)  → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻    (loses 2)
>
> K⁺  + Cl⁻          → KCl      (one chloride balances one K⁺)
> Ca²⁺ + 2Cl⁻        → CaCl₂    (two chlorides balance one Ca²⁺)
> ```
> **Potassium sits below sodium and behaves the same way; calcium sits below magnesium and behaves the same way** — because the valence electron count repeats.

---

Neon has atomic number 10 and neither shares nor transfers electrons. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neon's configuration is 2, 8** — its outermost shell already holds a **complete OCTET**.
>
> **There is nothing to gain by reacting.** Atoms bond in order to reach a stable octet, and neon already has one — so sharing or transferring electrons would take it **away** from stability, not towards it.
>
> **This is why the noble gases are inert**, and it is also why the octet rule is worth stating: it names the arrangement that all other reactions are aiming at.

---

Why do ionic compounds form crystals rather than molecules?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because an ion attracts EVERY oppositely charged ion around it, not just one partner.**
>
> In **sodium chloride**, each **Na⁺ is surrounded by SIX Cl⁻** and each **Cl⁻ by SIX Na⁺**, in a regular, repeating **three-dimensional pattern** — a **crystal structure**, represented as a **crystal lattice** of points.
>
> **So there is no such thing as an "NaCl molecule".** The formula NaCl only tells you the **ratio** in which the ions occur — which is why we speak of a **formula unit** rather than a molecule.

---

Write the chemical formulae of hydrogen chloride, hydrogen sulfide and carbon tetrachloride using the criss-cross method.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> H   Cl          H   S           C   Cl
> 1    1          1   2           4    1
>
>    HCl            H₂S             CCl₄
> ```
> **Method:** write the symbols, write the **valencies** below them, then **cross over** the valencies to become subscripts.
>
> **If a subscript comes out as 1 after crossing over, it is not written** — HCl, not H₁Cl₁.

---

Write the formulae of calcium chloride, aluminium oxide and magnesium oxide.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Ca   Cl          Al    O          Mg    O
> 2+   1−          3+   2−          2+   2−
>
>    CaCl₂            Al₂O₃        Mg₂O₂ → written MgO
> ```
> **Magnesium oxide is the important one:** the criss-cross gives Mg₂O₂, but a chemical formula must show the **SIMPLEST whole-number ratio**, so both subscripts are divided by 2 to give **MgO**.
>
> **Always check for a common factor after crossing over.**

---

Write the formulae of magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide and aluminium sulfate, and explain the brackets.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Mg   OH          Al   OH          Al   SO₄
> 2+   1−          3+   1−          3+   2−
>
>   Mg(OH)₂          Al(OH)₃         Al₂(SO₄)₃
> ```
> **Brackets are used when there are TWO OR MORE polyatomic ions of the same type.** The bracket says "this whole group, that many times".
>
> **Al(OH)₃, NOT AlOH₃** — without the bracket the 3 would attach to the **H alone**, describing something completely different.
>
> **Brackets are not needed when only ONE polyatomic ion is present** — CaCO₃, not Ca(CO₃).

Write the formulae for compounds of: Fe³⁺ and OH⁻; K⁺ and CO₃²⁻; Ca²⁺ and Br⁻; Al³⁺ and CO₃²⁻; NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻.

Write the formulae of sodium hydrogencarbonate, sulfur dioxide, ferric chloride and cuprous oxide.

Write the formulae of the compounds formed by magnesium + nitrogen, lithium + nitrogen, sodium + sulfur, and aluminium + oxygen.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```

Complete the grid: the compounds formed by NH₄⁺, Li⁺, Al³⁺ and Cu²⁺ with NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻ and PO₄³⁻.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **NO₃⁻** | **SO₄²⁻** | **PO₄³⁻** |

---

You want an ionic compound with total charge 6+ and 6−. Which works: 2Al³⁺ and 3Cl⁻; 3Mg²⁺ and 1PO₄³⁻; 2Fe³⁺ and 3O²⁻; 3Ca²⁺ and 2SO₄²⁻?

> [!ANSWER]
> **2Fe³⁺ and 3O²⁻.**
> ```
> 2 × (3+) = 6+      3 × (2−) = 6−      ✔ balanced  →  Fe₂O₃
> ```
> **Why the others fail:**
> - 2Al³⁺ and 3Cl⁻ → 6+ but only **3−**
> - 3Mg²⁺ and 1PO₄³⁻ → 6+ but only **3−**
> - 3Ca²⁺ and 2SO₄²⁻ → 6+ but only **4−**
>
> **A compound must be electrically NEUTRAL overall**, so the totals have to match exactly.

Which correctly represents the Cl⁻ ion, and what is its configuration?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The one showing 2, 8, 8 with an overall NEGATIVE charge.**
> ```
> chlorine atom:  17 protons, 17 electrons  →  2, 8, 7  →  neutral
> chloride ion:   17 protons, 18 electrons  →  2, 8, 8  →  charge −1
> ```
> **The proton count NEVER changes** — it is still chlorine. Only the electron count changed, by exactly one, which is where the charge comes from.

Camphor, sodium chloride, copper sulfate, sugar and naphthalene are tested for solubility and conductivity. Summarise the results.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Property | **IONIC** (NaCl, copper sulfate) | **COVALENT** (camphor, naphthalene, sugar) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Soluble in **water** | generally **yes** | mostly **no** (sugar is an exception) |
> | Soluble in **kerosene, petrol** | **no** | **yes** |
> | Conducts as a **solid** | **NO** | **no** |
> | Conducts **dissolved in water** | **YES** | **no** |
> | Melting/boiling points | **HIGH** | **LOW** |
>
> **Safety in this experiment:** do not touch the electrodes while connected, use a **low-voltage battery**, and remember that **petrol and kerosene are flammable**.

---

Why do ionic compounds not conduct electricity as solids but do when dissolved in water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Conduction needs charged particles that are FREE TO MOVE.**
>
> - **In the solid**, the ions are **held in fixed positions in the crystal lattice by strong forces**. The charges are all there, but **none of them can go anywhere**, so no current flows.
> - **Dissolving in water frees the ions**, and now they can move and carry the current.
>
> **The prediction this makes: melting should work too.** Melting also frees the ions from their fixed positions — so **molten ionic compounds DO conduct electricity**.

---

Sugar dissolves readily in water yet the solution does not conduct. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because sugar is a COVALENT compound. It dissolves as whole neutral MOLECULES and provides NO IONS in solution.**
>
> **No charged particles means no conduction**, no matter how well it dissolves.
>
> **Solubility and conductivity are different questions.** Dissolving asks whether the solvent can separate the particles; conducting asks whether those particles carry charge. **Sugar answers yes to the first and no to the second.**
>
> Camphor and naphthalene fail both tests in water — they are covalent and insoluble.

---

Why do ionic compounds generally have much higher melting points than covalent ones?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the INTER-IONIC attractions in an ionic crystal are very strong.**
>
> Every ion is held by the electrostatic attraction of **all** the oppositely charged ions around it — six of them in sodium chloride — and a great deal of energy is needed to break that lattice apart.
>
> **Covalent molecules attract each other only weakly** (the bonds *within* a molecule are strong, but the forces *between* molecules are not), so far less energy is needed to separate them. That is why camphor and naphthalene melt easily and even sublime.

---

A solid does not conduct electricity in the solid state but does when dissolved in water. What kind of bond does it have?

> [!ANSWER]
> **An IONIC bond.**
>
> **The two observations together are decisive:** conducting in solution proves it contains **ions**; not conducting as a solid proves those ions are **held fixed in a lattice**. Only an ionic compound behaves that way.
>
> A covalent compound would fail both tests, and a metal would conduct as a solid.

---

Metal M has two electrons in its M shell and reacts with oxygen. Predict the formula, the bond type and the conductivity of the solution.

> [!ANSWER]
> **M has configuration 2, 8, 2 — so M is MAGNESIUM (Z = 12).**
>
> **(i) Formula:** Mg²⁺ with O²⁻ crosses over to Mg₂O₂, which simplifies to **MgO**.
>
> **(ii) Bond:** **IONIC**. Magnesium has 2 valence electrons (fewer than 4) so it **donates** them; oxygen needs 2 so it **accepts** them. That is a transfer, not a sharing.
>
> **(iii) Conductivity:** its aqueous solution **CONDUCTS electricity**, because dissolving frees the Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions to move. (MgO is only slightly soluble, so the conduction will be weak — few ions in solution means a small current.)

Element A has one electron in its third shell; element B has six electrons in its second shell. Describe the compound they form.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> A: 2, 8, 1  →  SODIUM        B: 2, 6  →  OXYGEN
> ```
> **(i)** A **gives 1** electron to become stable (2, 8).
> **(ii)** It forms a **CATION, Na⁺**.
> **(iii)** B **takes 2** electrons to complete its octet.
> **(iv)** It forms an **ANION, O²⁻**.
> **(v)** A metal donating to a non-metal → an **IONIC bond**.
> **(vi)** Two Na⁺ are needed to supply oxygen's two electrons → **Na₂O**.

---

Element X has six electrons in its outer shell and forms a diatomic molecule. Element Y has two electrons in its second shell. Describe both molecules.

> [!ANSWER]
> **X is OXYGEN (2, 6); Y is BERYLLIUM (2, 2).**
>
> **(i) Why X is diatomic:** with 6 valence electrons it needs **2 more**. Two oxygen atoms can satisfy each other by **each sharing two electrons**, so the pair is stable on its own.
>
> **(ii) Bond in X₂:** **covalent** — and specifically a **DOUBLE bond**, since two pairs are shared.
>
> **(iii) Structure:** **O=O**.
>
> **(iv) X with Y:** beryllium has **2 valence electrons — fewer than 4 — so it DONATES both**, while oxygen **accepts both**. One of each balances:
> ```
> Be²⁺ + O²⁻  →  BeO
> ```
> **Note how the same oxygen atom takes different routes with different partners:** it *shares* with another oxygen (both need electrons) and *accepts* from beryllium (which has spare ones).

Which statements are correct? (i) Elements are made of molecules and compounds of atoms. (ii) A molecule of a compound is made of two or more atoms of the same kind. (iii) A nitrogen molecule has three atoms. (iv) Water is two hydrogen atoms covalently bonded to one oxygen.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only (iv) is correct.**
>
> - **(i) WRONG.** It is the wrong way round. **Elements are made up of ATOMS** (which may group into molecules like O₂); **compounds are made up of atoms of DIFFERENT elements**, joined as molecules or as ions.
> - **(ii) WRONG.** A molecule of a **compound** is made of atoms of **DIFFERENT kinds** — that is what makes it a compound. Atoms of the same kind give a molecule of an **element**.
> - **(iii) WRONG.** A nitrogen molecule is **N₂ — TWO atoms**, joined by a triple bond.
> - **(iv) CORRECT.** H₂O is exactly that.

Elements A (2, 8, 5) and B (2, 8, 7) combine. Which is more reactive, what kind of bond forms, and what is the formula?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) B is more reactive.** B needs only **ONE** electron to complete its octet, while A needs **THREE** — and the closer an atom is to a complete octet, the more readily it reacts.
>
> **(ii) COVALENT.** Both have **more than 4 valence electrons**, so **both want to gain** — neither will donate. The only way both can be satisfied is by **SHARING**.
>
> **(iii)** A needs 3 electrons and each B can share only 1, so **three B atoms are needed for one A**:
> ```
> AB₃      (A is phosphorus, B is chlorine → PCl₃, phosphorus trichloride)
> ```

Assertion: "Copper sulfate conducts electricity in the molten state but not in the solid state." Reason: "Copper and sulfate ions are fixed in the lattice in the molten state, while in the solid state they move freely." Evaluate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The assertion is TRUE; the reason is FALSE — it has the two states exactly swapped.**
>
> The truth is the reverse: **in the SOLID the ions are fixed in the lattice**, which is why it does not conduct; **MELTING frees them to move**, which is why it does.
>
> **A useful habit: when a reason contains the right ideas in the wrong order, check it against a case you are sure of** — a solid crystal is rigid, a melt is not.

Find the molecular masses of H₂O, CO₂, HNO₃ and CH₄. (H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16 u)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> H₂O  = (1 × 2) + (16 × 1)          = 18 u
> CO₂  = (12 × 1) + (16 × 2)         = 44 u
> HNO₃ = 1 + 14 + (16 × 3)           = 63 u
> CH₄  = 12 + (1 × 4)                = 16 u
> ```
> **Method: add the atomic masses of every atom present in the molecule.**

---

Why do we speak of "formula unit mass" for ionic compounds rather than "molecular mass"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because ionic compounds do NOT form molecules.** Their ions form **three-dimensional crystals**, with every ion surrounded by ions of the opposite charge.
>
> **A FORMULA UNIT is the collection representing the SIMPLEST WHOLE-NUMBER RATIO of ions**, and its mass is the **formula unit mass**.
>
> **"NaCl" therefore names a ratio, not a particle.** There is no such object as one NaCl molecule you could pick out of the crystal.

Find the formula unit masses of Na₂O, KCl, Ca(NO₃)₂ and Mg(OH)₂. (Na = 23, O = 16, K = 39, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40, N = 14, Mg = 24, H = 1 u)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Na₂O     = (23 × 2) + 16                      = 62 u
> KCl      = 39 + 35.5                          = 74.5 u
> Ca(NO₃)₂ = 40 + 2 × (14 + 16 × 3) = 40 + 124  = 164 u
> Mg(OH)₂  = 24 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 24 + 34        = 58 u
> ```
> **With brackets, work out the group inside FIRST, then multiply by the subscript.** In Ca(NO₃)₂ the NO₃ group is 62 u and there are **two** of them.

Find the formula unit masses of ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃), phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) and sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃). (P = 31 u)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> NH₄NO₃ = (14 × 2) + (1 × 4) + (16 × 3) = 28 + 4 + 48 = 80 u
> H₃PO₄  = (1 × 3) + 31 + (16 × 4)       = 3 + 31 + 64 = 98 u
> NaHCO₃ = 23 + 1 + 12 + (16 × 3)        = 23 + 1 + 12 + 48 = 84 u
> ```
> **In NH₄NO₃, count BOTH nitrogens** — there is one in the ammonium and one in the nitrate. Missing that is the commonest slip here.
>
> (Ammonium nitrate is used as a **nitrogen fertiliser**, phosphoric acid for **phosphate fertiliser and detergents**, and sodium hydrogencarbonate to **relieve acidity**.)

A species has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 10 electrons. Find its atomic and mass numbers, say whether it is neutral, and name it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i)** `Z = 11` (protons) and `A = 11 + 12 = 23`.
>
> **(ii) It is a CATION.** There are **11 positive protons but only 10 negative electrons**, giving a net charge of **+1**.
>
> **(iii) Configuration: 2, 8** — the 10 electrons fill K and L exactly.
>
> **(iv) It is the SODIUM ION, Na⁺.**
>
> **Note that it is still sodium** — the proton count is untouched. Losing an electron changes the charge, never the identity.

²⁷Al, ⁸⁰Br⁻ and ²⁰¹Hg²⁺ have 13, 35 and 80 protons. Find their electrons and neutrons.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Species | Protons | Electrons | Neutrons |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **²⁷Al** | 13 | **13** (neutral atom) | 27 − 13 = **14** |
> | **⁸⁰Br⁻** | 35 | **36** (one extra, giving the −1) | 80 − 35 = **45** |
> | **²⁰¹Hg²⁺** | 80 | **78** (two fewer, giving the +2) | 201 − 80 = **121** |
>
> **The two rules:** `neutrons = A − Z` always, and **the charge tells you how the electron count differs from the proton count** — negative means extra electrons, positive means missing ones.
