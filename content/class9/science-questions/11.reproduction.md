Define reproduction and explain why it is necessary.

> [!ANSWER]
> **REPRODUCTION is the biological process by which living beings produce new individuals of their own kind.**
>
> **Why it is necessary: every organism has a DEFINITE LIFE SPAN** — it is born, grows, matures, reproduces and eventually dies. **Reproduction is how life on Earth continues despite that.**
>
> A mango tree grows old and dies, but its **seeds continue to grow as new mango plants**. Without reproduction, every species would end with its current generation.

---

Compare asexual and sexual reproduction on three points.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **Asexual** | **Sexual** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Parents | **ONE** | **TWO** |
> | Offspring | **almost exact copies** of the parent | inherit a **mix** of characteristics from both |
> | Variation | **none** | **plenty** |
>
> **Why the variation matters:** the small differences between parents and offspring, **accumulated over many generations, help living beings ADAPT to changing environments** — and sometimes even give rise to new species.

---

What is vegetative propagation? Give four examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> **New plants arising from the VEGETATIVE (growing) parts of an existing plant, without seeds.**
>
> - **Potato and ginger** — fleshy underground stems sprout new plants
> - **Money plant** — stem cuttings grow into new plants
> - **Sugarcane** — stem cuttings, provided they carry a node
> - **Bryophyllum** — its **LEAVES** sprout tiny plantlets that grow into new plants
>
> **The key point: it involves only ONE parent and therefore produces GENETICALLY IDENTICAL individuals.**

---

Describe grafting, and explain why two different plants are used.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Make a slit in a twig of a healthy rooted plant (A), insert a stem cutting from the desired plant (B) into the slit, and wrap it with cloth or film until it heals.** Cut the other branches of A, and water regularly.
>
> **Why two plants: each contributes what it is good at.**
> - **Plant A supplies the ROOTS** — chosen for being hardy, disease-resistant, and well adapted to the local soil.
> - **Plant B supplies the DESIRED VARIETY** — chosen for its fruit or flowers.
>
> **The wrapping keeps pests out of the wound until it heals**, and cutting A's other branches makes sure its energy goes into the graft rather than its own shoots.

---

In layering, why is the twig left attached to the parent plant while roots develop?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the buried twig cannot yet feed or water itself.**
>
> While it is buried, **the parent plant continues to supply it with water and nutrients**, so it survives comfortably for the **10–15 days** it takes for **roots to develop** from the buried region.
>
> **Only once roots have formed is the twig cut free** — by then it can support itself. **Cutting it first would very likely kill it**, which is exactly the risk that plain cuttings run.

---

How has tissue culture changed banana farming?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Farmers are now supplied with mass-produced healthy plantlets grown from the SHOOT TIP (apical meristem) of selected plants.**
>
> **Why the shoot tip specifically:** the apical meristem is usually **virus-free**, even in a plant that is infected elsewhere. Starting from it therefore **eliminates virus-infected plants** and ensures **high yields**.
>
> It is also **asexual reproduction**, so every plantlet is **genetically identical** to a parent chosen for its quality.

---

Why do farmers often prefer asexual methods for propagating a crop?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because every plant produced is GENETICALLY IDENTICAL to a parent chosen for its desirable characters.**
>
> A farmer who finds **one outstanding mango tree** can reproduce **exactly that tree** thousands of times. Seeds could never guarantee this — sexual reproduction shuffles the characters, so seedlings from that tree would all differ from it and from each other.
>
> **It is also fast**, needs no partner plant, no pollinator and no waiting for seed to set — which lets desirable crops be cultivated on a large scale.

---

What is budding? Describe it in yeast and in hydra.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Repeated cell division at a specific site on the parent's body produces a small outgrowth called a BUD. The bud enlarges and SEPARATES from the parent to live independently.**
>
> - **Yeast:** put in warm sugar solution and examined under a microscope after an hour or two, the cells show **small round outgrowths** emerging from them.
> - **Hydra**, a multicellular animal: **many buds often grow on the parent's body at the same time**.
>
> **It is asexual — one parent, and the bud is genetically identical to it.**

---

Moist bread kept in a warm dark place grows mould in three days. Where did the mould come from?

> [!ANSWER]
> **From SPORES already present in the AIR**, which settled on the moist bread and began reproducing rapidly. There was no mould on the fresh bread.
>
> Under a microscope you see **thread-like HYPHAE with a round SAC at the tip, and tiny round SPORES inside the sac**.
>
> **This is why fungi appear "from nowhere" on rotten fruit or in manure heaps** — the spores were already everywhere, simply waiting for the right conditions.

---

Why are spores such an effective means of reproduction?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Produced in HUGE numbers** — millions from a single mould colony
> - **Lightweight and usually single-celled**, so they **float easily on air currents** and travel far
> - They can simply **wait** for moisture and nutrients, then **germinate quickly** into a new individual
>
> **The strategy is the same as wind pollination:** make enormous numbers of very cheap, very light units, scatter them everywhere, and let a tiny fraction succeed.

---

Why do we refrigerate perishable food?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because mould spores need WARMTH and MOISTURE to grow** — around **25–35 °C** is ideal — and **lower temperatures slow or stop their reproduction**.
>
> **The food is not sterile in the fridge; the microbes are simply slowed down.** That is why refrigerated food still spoils eventually, just far more slowly.
>
> **Before refrigerators became common about 100 years ago, fresh food lasted only 1–2 days.** Refrigeration, including deep freezing, transformed food habits by making fruits, vegetables and dairy available year-round.

---

What did Louis Pasteur prove, and what came of it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That NEW LIFE ALWAYS COMES FROM PRE-EXISTING LIFE.**
>
> For a long time people believed living things **arose spontaneously from non-living matter** — mould simply appearing on bread seemed to prove it. Pasteur's experiments showed the organisms had come from **spores already present**.
>
> **What followed:**
> - the **germ theory of disease**
> - support for the **cell theory** — all cells arise from pre-existing cells
> - practices such as **food and instrument sterilisation**

---

Give three ways in which fungi are useful, despite spoiling our food.

> [!ANSWER]
> - They **grow very fast by spore formation and DEGRADE organic wastes and pollutants** — nature's recyclers
> - They help **remove HEAVY METALS from industrial wastes**
> - Many **ANTIBIOTICS are derived from fungi** — **penicillin and amoxicillin** among them — saving countless lives from bacterial infections

---

What single process underlies all asexual reproduction, and why does it produce clones?

> [!ANSWER]
> **MITOSIS** — the type of cell division that produces **two daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes, identical to the parent cell**.
>
> **Because every division copies the genetic material exactly, the offspring are GENETICALLY IDENTICAL to the parent** — and such offspring are called **CLONES**.
>
> **The advantage: it is FAST.** No partner, no gametes, no pollination, no waiting — which lets an organism increase its population quickly when conditions are favourable.

---

If sexual reproduction combines genetic material from two parents, why doesn't the chromosome number double each generation?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of MEIOSIS.**
>
> If each generation received the **full set** from **both** parents, the number really would double every generation — an impossibility within a few generations.
>
> **Meiosis is a special cell division that HALVES the chromosome number when making gametes.** Human body cells have **46**; sperm and eggs have **23**. When two gametes combine at fertilisation, **the original 46 is exactly restored.**

---

How does meiosis create variation? Show the arithmetic for three pairs of characters.

> [!ANSWER]
> **During meiosis the chromosomes of each pair SEPARATE, so each gamete receives only ONE chromosome from each pair — and which one is a matter of chance.**
>
> **With three pairs of contrasting characters** (say hair colour, hair type, eye colour), picking one from each pair gives:
> ```
> 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 possible combinations
> ```
> **Now scale that to 23 pairs, each carrying information for many characters** — the number of possible combinations is astronomical.
>
> **This is why children are genetically different from their parents AND from their siblings.** Each of us receives a unique combination of chromosomes.

---

Why is variation important for a species?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because it helps some individuals adapt better to CHANGING environments**, and over time this contributes to **evolution**.
>
> If every individual were identical, a single change — a new disease, a shift in climate — could wipe out the entire population, since **what harms one harms all**. Variation means **some individuals may survive whatever comes**.
>
> **Examples in humans:** the ability to **tolerate low oxygen levels at high altitudes**, and the ability to **digest milk in adulthood**.

Name the four parts of a complete flower and give the function of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Part | Function |
> |---|---|
> | **SEPAL** — outermost whorl, green | **protects the flower in the BUD stage**, and the other parts when it blooms |
> | **PETAL** — the coloured projections | **attracts pollinators** — colour and fragrance are advertising |
> | **STAMEN** — the **MALE** part: **filament + anther** | the **anther produces POLLEN GRAINS containing male gametes** |
> | **PISTIL** — the **FEMALE** part: **stigma + style + ovary** | receives pollen; the **ovary contains OVULES, each with an egg cell** |
>
> In some flowers the sepals and petals are **fused**.

---

Describe the three parts of the pistil and how each suits its job.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **STIGMA** — at the **tip**, often **flat and/or sticky**, so it can **catch and hold** pollen that lands on it.
> - **STYLE** — a **thin long tube** connecting the stigma to the ovary; the pollen tube grows down through it.
> - **OVARY** — the swollen base, **containing OVULES, each holding an egg cell (female gamete)**.
>
> **The design is a landing pad on a stalk above a protected chamber** — pollen must be caught high up where it is exposed, while the eggs must be kept safe deep inside.

---

Five pea flowers are treated differently. Only the bud with its stamens removed fails to form fruit. What does this show, and why did the OPEN flower with stamens removed still form fruit?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It shows that the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma is NECESSARY for fruit formation. That transfer is POLLINATION.**
>
> **Why the open flower still formed fruit:** in pea, **pollination happens inside the BUD, before the flower opens**. By the time the flower is open, **pollination has already occurred** — so removing the stamens then is too late to prevent anything.
>
> **Removing them from a BUD prevents pollination entirely**, and that flower alone produces no fruit.
>
> **The muslin bags are the control:** they show that the failure was not caused by bagging, since three bagged flowers did form fruit.

---

Distinguish self-pollination from cross-pollination, and say which produces more variation.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **SELF-POLLINATION:** pollen is transferred to the stigma of the **same flower**, or another flower **on the same plant**.
> - **CROSS-POLLINATION:** pollen is transferred from a flower of **one plant** to the stigma of a flower on **another plant of the same kind**.
>
> **CROSS-pollination produces far more variation**, because it combines genetic material from **two different individuals**. Self-pollination mixes a plant's material only with its own.

---

A farmer plants two maize varieties side by side and finds seeds form only when pollen from one reaches the stigma of the other. What kind of pollination is this?

> [!ANSWER]
> **CROSS-POLLINATION** — pollen from a flower of one plant reaching the stigma of a flower on **a different plant**.
>
> **Why this is useful to the farmer:** the resulting seeds combine characters from **both varieties**, which is exactly the basis of **hybrid** crops — and often gives higher yield than either parent alone.

---

Compare wind-pollinated and insect-pollinated flowers, and explain why each is built the way it is.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **WIND** (wheat, maize, rice) | **INSECT** (sunflower, hibiscus, marigold) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Pollen | **light, small, HUGE numbers** | **large, sticky or spiny** |
> | Stigma | **long and feathery**, to trap pollen from the air | **sticky**, to hold pollen off an insect's body |
> | Flower | plain — no need for colour or scent | **brightly coloured**, with **nectar** and **fragrance** |
>
> **Every feature follows from the agent.** **Wind cannot aim**, so the plant scatters enormous amounts of very light pollen and spreads big feathery nets to catch it — and wastes nothing on petals, scent or nectar. **Insects can aim**, so the plant **pays them with nectar** and **advertises with colour and scent**, and needs far less pollen.

---

Wind-pollinated grasses release 500,000–1,000,000 pollen grains per flower but form only 50–200 seeds; insect-pollinated plants release 20,000–40,000 and form 800–1,000 seeds. Compare the strategies.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> wind:    ~1,000,000 grains ÷ ~100 seeds  ≈ 5000 grains per seed
> insect:  ~30,000 grains ÷ ~900 seeds     ≈ 30 grains per seed
> ```
> **Insect pollination is more than a hundred times more EFFICIENT** by this measure.
>
> **So why does wind pollination survive?** Because **producing vast quantities of cheap pollen is still effective** when the plant:
> - does not have to make **nectar, scent or showy petals** — all expensive
> - does not depend on any **animal being present** at the right time
>
> **Waste is cheap if the alternative is expensive.** Each strategy pays a different price for the same result.

---

Trace the events from pollen landing on a stigma to seed formation.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> pollen lands on a compatible stigma
>    → the pollen grain produces a POLLEN TUBE
>    → the tube grows down through the STYLE into the OVARY
>    → the male gamete travels down it to the OVULE
>    → it FUSES with the egg cell               ← FERTILISATION
>    → the fertilised egg is a ZYGOTE → develops into an EMBRYO
>
> OVULE → SEED          OVARY → FRUIT
> ```
> **Seeds are then dispersed by wind, water or animals**, and when water, air and temperature are favourable, the seed **germinates** into a new plant.

---

In a hibiscus flower, a pollen tube has grown down through the style. What happens next?

> [!ANSWER]
> **FERTILISATION.**
>
> The pollen tube has carried the **male gamete** down to the **ovule**, where it will **fuse with the egg cell**. That fusion **marks the beginning of a new life**, producing a **zygote** which develops into an embryo.
>
> Meanwhile the **ovary enlarges into a fruit** and the **ovules become seeds**.

---

Calotropis (madar) and dandelion seeds both carry fluffy hairs. What dispersal are they adapted for?

> [!ANSWER]
> **WIND dispersal.**
>
> The **light, fluffy hairs greatly increase the surface area** relative to the seed's tiny weight, so **the seed catches the air and drifts a long way** instead of dropping straight down.
>
> **Why dispersal matters at all:** a seed that falls beside its parent must compete with it for light, water and nutrients. Travelling even a short distance gives it **fresh ground of its own**.

---

Compare external and internal fertilisation, and explain why the number of eggs differs so much.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **EXTERNAL:** the female releases eggs into the water and the male releases sperm over them — **frogs, most fish**.
> - **INTERNAL:** fertilisation occurs **inside the female's body** — **reptiles, birds, mammals**. **Survival of the young is generally higher**, because the fertilised egg or embryo is **better protected**.
>
> | Animal | Fertilisation | Eggs at a time | Survival |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Fish | external | 100s–1000s | **low** |
> | Frog | external | **5,000–50,000** | **low** |
> | Lizard | internal | 2–20 | moderate |
> | Bird | internal | **1–15** | moderate to high |

---

Why do animals with external fertilisation generally produce far more eggs?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because so few of them survive.**
>
> In open water, **many eggs are destroyed by currents or eaten by other animals** before they can even be fertilised, and many more afterwards. There is no protection at any stage.
>
> **A very low survival rate is compensated by very large numbers** — 50,000 eggs with a tiny chance each may leave the same number of adults as 10 well-protected ones.
>
> **Internal fertilisation shifts the strategy from QUANTITY to CARE.** The embryo is protected, so far fewer eggs are needed.

---

Why do fish, amphibians and insects have a larval stage?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the mother's body cannot provide enough yolk for thousands of eggs.**
>
> Each egg carries **just enough yolk to produce a LARVA**, which hatches and then **feeds itself** — on organic wastes, rotten food, manure — accumulating the nutrition the yolk could not supply. Once enough has been gathered, **a transformation takes place and the adult body forms**, as in butterflies and frogs.
>
> **The larva is an intermediate FEEDING stage in development, not a small adult.**
>
> **Contrast: reptiles and birds lay few eggs, each with enough yolk to nourish the embryo all the way to hatching. In mammals the zygote develops inside the mother's body.**

Name the parts of the male reproductive system and their functions.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Part | Function |
> |---|---|
> | **TESTES** (two oval organs) | produce **SPERM**, and a **hormone** controlling sperm production and the physical changes of puberty |
> | **SCROTUM** (pouch of skin) | keeps the testes **slightly cooler than body temperature**, **necessary for sperm formation** |
> | **VAS DEFERENS** | a long tube carrying sperm from the testes to the urethra |
> | **URETHRA** | the **common passage** for urine and sperm |
> | **SEMINAL VESICLES and PROSTATE** | add **fluids to nourish the sperm** and keep them active and moving |
>
> **Each sperm has a HEAD with genetic material and a long TAIL to swim towards the egg.**

---

Why are the testes located outside the body cavity, in the scrotum?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because sperm formation requires a temperature SLIGHTLY LOWER than normal body temperature**, and the scrotum provides exactly that by holding the testes outside the main body.
>
> **This is a rare case where an organ is deliberately placed in a MORE exposed position** — the risk of injury is accepted because the temperature requirement is non-negotiable.

---

Name the parts of the female reproductive system and their functions.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Part | Function |
> |---|---|
> | **OVARIES** (a pair) | produce **EGGS** and release **hormones** that bring about the changes of puberty |
> | **OVIDUCTS (fallopian tubes)** | connect each ovary to the uterus; **fertilisation happens here** |
> | **UTERUS** | a bag-like structure where the **foetus develops** |
> | **CERVIX** | the narrow passage by which the uterus opens into the vagina |
> | **VAGINA** | the passage to the outside |

---

What is gametogenesis, and why must it use meiosis?

> [!ANSWER]
> **GAMETOGENESIS is the formation of gametes, taking place in the TESTES and OVARIES by MEIOSIS.**
>
> **Why meiosis is essential: human cells have 46 chromosomes but sperm and eggs have only 23.**
>
> **If gametes carried 46, the zygote would have 92** — and the number would double every generation. **Halving first is what allows fertilisation to restore exactly the parental number.**

---

Compare sperm and egg on four features, and explain why they differ so much.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Feature | **Sperm** | **Egg** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Size | **very small** | **large** |
> | Number produced | **millions** | **few** |
> | Stored nutrients | **absent** | **present** |
> | Motility | **actively motile** | **non-motile** |
>
> **The asymmetry makes sense of itself.** The sperm's whole job is **to TRAVEL and arrive first**, so it is stripped down to genetic material plus a tail — and since only one will succeed, millions are made cheaply. The egg's job is **to NOURISH the early embryo**, so it is large and stocked with nutrients — and there is no point making millions of something so expensive.
>
> **This asymmetry is seen across most animals.**

---

Trace what happens from ovulation to the beginning of pregnancy.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a girl's ovaries hold millions of immature eggs at birth
> from puberty, usually ONE mature egg is released each month  ← OVULATION
> before ovulation the uterus lining becomes THICK
> the egg travels from the ovary into the OVIDUCT
> millions of sperm enter through the vagina and swim up the tract
> if one fuses with the egg IN THE OVIDUCT → a ZYGOTE forms   ← FERTILISATION
> the uterus lining becomes thicker and richer in blood vessels
> the zygote divides by MITOSIS while travelling to the uterus
> it IMPLANTS into the uterus lining to receive nourishment
>                                     ← IMPLANTATION begins PREGNANCY
> ```
> **Note that fertilisation happens in the OVIDUCT, not the uterus** — and that pregnancy begins at **implantation**, not at fertilisation.

---

Assertion: "The zygote formed after fertilisation immediately attaches to the uterus wall." Reason: "The uterus wall is always prepared to receive the zygote." Evaluate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **BOTH are FALSE.**
>
> **The assertion is false:** fertilisation happens in the **oviduct**, and the zygote **divides by mitosis while TRAVELLING to the uterus** — implantation takes several days, not an instant.
>
> **The reason is false:** the uterus wall is **NOT always prepared**. Its lining **thickens in the second half of each cycle** in readiness, and if no fertilisation occurs it is **shed during menstruation** — so for part of every cycle it is not ready at all.

---

What happens if the egg is not fertilised?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The egg remains viable for about a day and then degenerates.**
>
> The uterine lining, which had become **thick and rich with blood vessels** to nourish a developing zygote, is **no longer needed — so it SHEDS**, leaving the body through the vagina along with some blood.
>
> **This is MENSTRUATION (a period), usually lasting 3 to 7 days.**

---

Describe the four stages of a typical 28-day menstrual cycle.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Days | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **1–5** | **MENSTRUATION** — the uterine lining is shed |
> | **6–14** | the lining **gradually rebuilds**; an egg matures in the ovary |
> | **14** | **OVULATION** — the ovary releases a mature egg |
> | **15–28** | the lining becomes **thicker and rich with blood vessels**; with no fertilisation it starts breaking down around day 28 and the cycle repeats |
>
> **The cycle repeats every 21–35 days (often about 28)**, beginning at puberty between about **10–14 years**, and continuing until **menopause around age 50**.

---

Rina's cycle is 28 days and her last period began on 5 March. When is her next one likely?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Around 2 April** — 28 days after 5 March (March has 31 days, so 5 March + 28 days = 2 April).
>
> **But "likely" is the right word.** Cycle length varies from person to person **and from month to month in the same person**, ranging from 21 to 35 days. A calculated date is an estimate, not a guarantee.

---

A student claims "ovulation always happens on day 14 of the cycle". Examine this critically.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The claim is NOT correct. Day 14 is a typical figure for a typical 28-day cycle, not a rule.**
>
> **Two reasons:**
> 1. **Cycle lengths vary from 21 to 35 days** between individuals. In a 21-day cycle ovulation would fall much earlier than day 14; in a 35-day cycle, much later.
> 2. **The same person's cycle varies from month to month**, and can be affected by illness, stress, weight change and other factors — so even a woman with an average 28-day cycle does not ovulate on day 14 every single month.
>
> **The general lesson: a textbook "typical" figure describes an average, not a law.**

---

Explain why the menstrual cycle stops during pregnancy.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because menstruation is the SHEDDING of a uterine lining that is no longer needed — and during pregnancy it IS needed.**
>
> Once the zygote **implants into the thickened lining**, that lining is **nourishing the developing embryo**. Shedding it would end the pregnancy.
>
> **So the whole cycle pauses:** no new egg is released, the lining is maintained rather than broken down, and menstruation does not occur until after the pregnancy ends.

---

Who determines the biological sex of a baby, and how?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The FATHER — through his gamete.**
>
> Everyone has two sex chromosomes: **females XX, males XY**.
> - **The mother always contributes an X** — she has nothing else to give.
> - **The father contributes either an X or a Y.**
> ```
> father's X + mother's X → XX → girl
> father's Y + mother's X → XY → boy
> ```
> **Since the mother's contribution is always the same, the outcome depends entirely on which of the father's two kinds of sperm reaches the egg** — and that is a matter of chance.

---

Describe the three trimesters of human pregnancy.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Pregnancy lasts about NINE MONTHS, divided into three TRIMESTERS.**
>
> | Trimester | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **First** | the fertilised egg develops into an **EMBRYO** over the first two months, and **major organs start forming**. From about the **ninth week** it is called a **FOETUS** |
> | **Second** | the foetus **grows bigger and stronger**; the mother can usually **feel its movements** |
> | **Third** | the baby **grows rapidly** and prepares for life outside the womb |
>
> **At childbirth, strong contractions of the uterine muscles push the foetus out through the birth canal.** If a normal vaginal birth is not possible or safe, doctors may use medical or surgical procedures to deliver the baby safely.

---

Why is breastfeeding important, and what other care does a newborn need?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A mother's milk provides COMPLETE NUTRITION and PROTECTS the infant from many diseases** — it carries not just food but protective factors the baby cannot yet make for itself.
>
> **Newborns must also be kept warm, vaccinated on time and handled gently.**
>
> **The mother's well-being matters just as much:** nutritious food, adequate rest, and avoiding **smoking, alcohol and any medicine without medical advice**. **The health of mother and baby are closely connected.**

---

What should a woman's care include during pregnancy?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **A balanced diet** rich in proteins, vitamins and minerals
> - **Regular medical check-ups**, following the doctor's advice on **light exercise and rest**
> - **Avoiding harmful habits** — smoking, alcohol, or any medicine not medically advised
> - **Emotional well-being** — support from family helps her remain healthy and stress-free
>
> **Her health during pregnancy plays a vital role in the baby's growth and safety** — the two cannot be separated.

---

What is post-partum depression, and what should be done about it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A form of anxiety and fatigue that some mothers experience AFTER the delivery of a baby.**
>
> **It is a RECOGNISED CONDITION THAT CAN BE TREATED** — not a failing or something to be hidden.
>
> **Mothers who feel low or overwhelmed should be encouraged to talk to a healthcare worker** — a doctor, nurse or **ASHA worker** — so they can get proper help and support.

---

Who are ASHA workers and what do they do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Accredited Social Health Activists — more than 10 lakh community health workers across India**, women trained under the **National Health Mission**, especially in rural areas.
>
> **They promote hygiene, immunisation and family planning**, and **advise on maternal care, safe deliveries and contraceptive methods**.
>
> **Why they matter: they bring health advice to places where doctors and hospitals are far away** — often the difference between a safe delivery and a dangerous one.

---

What is IVF, and what is India's place in its history?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In-Vitro Fertilisation: an egg and a sperm are combined OUTSIDE the female body, usually in a laboratory dish. The resulting fertilised egg is then implanted in the uterus to begin a pregnancy.**
>
> A baby born this way is commonly called a **test tube baby** — though the process actually occurs in a **culture dish**, not a test tube.
>
> **In 1978, Subhash Mukhopadhyay of Kolkata pioneered India's first test tube baby, Kanupriya Agarwal (nicknamed Durga)**, through experimental IVF work.

Distinguish sexual maturity from emotional maturity.

> [!ANSWER]
> **SEXUAL MATURITY** — the body becoming capable of reproduction: the **production of sperm in boys** and **menstrual cycles in girls**. It happens **gradually** during adolescence, alongside overall body growth.

A flower's anthers are removed before it matures, and pollen from another plant of the same species is then dusted on its stigma. Which process has been ensured?

> [!ANSWER]
> **CROSS-POLLINATION.**
>
> **Removing the anthers before the flower matures makes SELF-pollination impossible** — the flower has no pollen of its own left to transfer. **Dusting on pollen from another plant** then guarantees the transfer came from a different individual.
>
> **This is exactly the technique of ARTIFICIAL HYBRIDISATION** used in plant breeding: remove the stamens, bag the flower to prevent stray self-pollination, and manually transfer pollen with the desired characters.
>
> (Fertilisation follows afterwards, but the process *ensured* by these steps is cross-pollination.)

Arrange in order: fertilisation, pollen germination on stigma, formation of zygote, pollination.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1. POLLINATION                    — pollen reaches the stigma
> 2. POLLEN GERMINATION ON STIGMA   — the pollen grain grows a pollen tube
> 3. FERTILISATION                  — the male gamete fuses with the egg
> 4. FORMATION OF ZYGOTE            — the fertilised egg
> ```
> **The logic is a chain of prerequisites:** pollen must **arrive** before it can germinate, the tube must **grow** before the gamete can reach the egg, and the gametes must **fuse** before a zygote can exist.

Why are flowers that bloom at night usually white or pale, while day-blooming flowers are brightly coloured?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because they are advertising to different pollinators under different conditions.**
>
> **At night there is very little light, and colours cannot be distinguished** — a bright red flower would simply look dark. **White and pale flowers REFLECT what light there is** and stand out most against the darkness, so night-flying pollinators such as moths can find them.
>
> **Night-blooming flowers also tend to be strongly FRAGRANT**, since scent works in the dark where colour does not.
>
> **Day-blooming flowers can rely on colour**, because bees and butterflies see it well in sunlight.

Why are vegetatively propagated plants more vulnerable to disease than sexually reproduced ones?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because they are all GENETICALLY IDENTICAL clones — so they all share exactly the same weaknesses.**
>
> **If one plant is susceptible to a disease, EVERY plant in the field is susceptible in exactly the same way.** A single pathogen can therefore destroy an entire crop.
>
> **Sexually reproduced plants VARY.** Some individuals may happen to resist the disease, so an outbreak damages part of the population rather than all of it.
>
> **This is precisely why variation matters for survival** — and why relying on a single clone for a major crop is a real agricultural risk.

If a plant could only self-pollinate, how would its genetic diversity change over generations? Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The genetic diversity would DECLINE steadily, generation after generation.**
>
> **Why:** variation in sexual reproduction comes from **combining material from two DIFFERENT individuals**. With strict self-pollination, **each plant reproduces only with itself**, so no new combinations ever enter — the population can only reshuffle what a single line already carries.
>
> **The consequences:** the plants become increasingly uniform, and therefore **increasingly vulnerable to any new disease or environmental change**, with **less ability to adapt** over time.
>
> **This is why so many plants have evolved devices to AVOID self-pollination** — separate male and female flowers, or stamens and stigmas that mature at different times.

A farmer wants a large number of genetically identical plants quickly. Suggest suitable methods and explain why they work.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Any ASEXUAL method — cutting, grafting, layering, or best of all TISSUE CULTURE.**
>
> **Why they give identical plants:** asexual reproduction proceeds by **MITOSIS**, which copies the genetic material exactly, so every offspring is a **CLONE** of the parent.
>
> **Why they are quick:** no partner plant, no pollinator, no waiting for flowers, fertilisation or seed set. **Tissue culture is fastest of all** — it mass-produces plantlets from a shoot tip, and because the apical meristem is usually **virus-free**, the plantlets are healthy as well as identical.

Suresh germinates pollen grains in sugar solutions of 0%, 2.5%, 5%, 7.5% and 10%. What hypotheses can he test, and what must be kept the same?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Hypotheses he could test:**
> - **Pollen germination depends on the sugar concentration** of the medium.
> - **There is an OPTIMUM concentration** at which germination is highest — with both too little and too much being worse.
> - **Pollen will not germinate at all in plain water (0%).**
>
> **What must be kept the same:**
> - the **same plant species** and **same age of flower**, with pollen collected at the same time
> - the **same number of pollen grains** in each drop
> - the **same temperature, humidity and light**
> - the **same observation time** before counting
> - the **same method of counting** what counts as "germinated"
>
> **Why: only the sugar concentration should differ.** If anything else varies, a difference in germination cannot be attributed to the sugar. **The 0% is the control.**

Tomato flowers have stamens covering the stigma; wheat flowers open only after pollination; papaya has male and female flowers on different trees. What pollination does each show?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Tomato — SELF-pollination.** The stamens **surround the stigma**, so pollen falls directly onto it and rarely gets the chance to go anywhere else.
> - **Wheat — SELF-pollination.** The flower **opens only AFTER pollination has happened**, so pollen from other plants cannot get in beforehand.
> - **Papaya — CROSS-pollination, necessarily.** With **male and female flowers on DIFFERENT trees**, a female flower can only ever receive pollen from another plant.
>
> **Papaya's arrangement is the strongest guarantee of cross-pollination there is** — self-pollination is not merely discouraged but physically impossible.

Apple orchards at Place A rely on natural pollinators; at Place B beekeeping is added. B shows higher fruit setting and lower fruit drop. Analyse this investigation.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) The hypotheses:** that **declining natural pollinator populations are limiting apple yields**, and that **introducing managed bee colonies will increase yield** — measured as higher fruit setting and lower fruit drop.
>
> **(ii) The parameters:** the **presence of managed bee colonies** (the variable being changed); **fruit setting** = number of fruits ÷ total number of fruit-bearing branches; and **fruit drop** = premature falling of developing fruits. Everything else — variety, soil, irrigation, season — should ideally be matched.
>
> **(iii) Comparing them:** Place B shows **higher fruit setting and lower fruit drop** than Place A, so a **greater proportion of flowers become fruit and more of those fruits survive to harvest**.
>
> **(iv) Inference: pollination was the LIMITING FACTOR at Place A.** Adding pollinators raised the yield, which means the trees were healthy but under-pollinated. **Beekeeping is therefore a practical response to pollinator decline** — and it produces honey as a second income alongside the apples.
>
> **A caution on the conclusion:** the two orchards are at **different locations**, so soil, weather and altitude may also differ. A stronger design would compare **adjacent plots** at the same place.

A human zygote has just formed. How many chromosomes does it have?

Human babies are helpless for years, while many animals can walk and feed themselves within hours. What are the advantages and disadvantages for humans as a species?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Disadvantages:**
> - the baby is **completely dependent** and vulnerable for a long time
> - it demands an **enormous investment of time and energy** from the parents
> - it limits how many offspring can be raised at once
>
> **Advantages:**
> - a long dependent period is a long period of **LEARNING**. Behaviour that other animals must be born with, humans can **learn** — language, tool use, cooperation, culture
> - it allows the **brain to keep developing after birth**, growing far larger than it could if the child had to be ready to run immediately
> - **extended care** allows knowledge to be passed from one generation to the next
>
> **The trade-off is between being ready EARLY and being capable of far MORE later** — and it is the single biggest reason human societies can accumulate knowledge across generations.
