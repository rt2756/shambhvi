What must you specify to describe the position of an object, and when is the object said to be in motion?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You need a REFERENCE POINT first.** Then the position at any instant is the **DISTANCE and the DIRECTION of the object from that reference point**.
>
> - **In motion:** the position with respect to the reference point **changes with time**.
> - **At rest:** the position with respect to the reference point **does not change with time**.
>
> **Why direction is needed too:** "30 m from the school gate" could be anywhere on a circle around the gate. For straight-line motion there are only two possibilities, so a **+ or – sign** is enough.

---

Distinguish an instant of time from a time interval.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **An INSTANT of time is a single reading of a clock** at one point in time — "4 s".
> - **A TIME INTERVAL is the duration between two instants**, i.e. between two clock readings — "from 4 s to 10 s, so 6 s".
>
> **Why the distinction matters:** average velocity and average acceleration are defined **over an interval**, while position and instantaneous velocity are defined **at an instant**.

---

An athlete starts at O, runs 100 m to A, then comes back 60 m to B. Find the total distance travelled and the displacement.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total distance = OA + AB = 100 m + 60 m = 160 m
> displacement   = OB      = 40 m in the positive direction
> ```
> **Why they differ:** distance counts **every metre of path walked**; displacement is the **net change in position** — the shortcut from start to finish, with a direction attached.

---

Can the magnitude of displacement ever be greater than the distance travelled? When are the two equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never greater.** The magnitude of displacement is **always less than or equal to** the total distance travelled.
>
> **Why:** distance is the **whole route walked**; displacement is the **straight shortcut** between the two ends. **A shortcut can never be longer than the route.**
>
> **They are equal only when the object moves in ONE direction throughout** — i.e. never turns back. The moment it turns back, some of the path starts undoing earlier path, and distance runs ahead of displacement.

---

A ball is thrown straight up from O, rises 140 cm to B, and falls back to O. Complete the distance and displacement at B, at C (80 cm on the way down), and back at O.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Position | Distance travelled from O | Displacement from O |
> |---|---|---|
> | **B** (top) | **140 cm** | **140 cm upward** |
> | **C** (80 cm, coming down) | 140 + 60 = **200 cm** | **80 cm upward** |
> | **O** (back at start) | 140 + 140 = **280 cm** | **0 cm** |
>
> **Yes, this is motion in a straight line** — the ball goes up and comes down along the **same vertical line**. Straight-line motion does not mean one-way motion.

---

When is an athlete's displacement zero on a straight track, and what is the distance travelled then?

> [!ANSWER]
> **When the athlete returns to the starting point.** The **net change in position is then zero**, however far they ran.
>
> **The distance travelled is not zero at all** — for a 100 m out-and-back it is **200 m**. This is the clearest illustration that **the two quantities measure genuinely different things**.

---

Does the fuel used by a vehicle depend on the total distance travelled or on the displacement? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **On the TOTAL DISTANCE TRAVELLED.**
>
> **Why:** the engine has to do work for **every metre of road covered**. It has no idea where the journey started. Drive 50 km out and 50 km back and you burn fuel for 100 km — even though your **displacement is zero**.
>
> If fuel depended on displacement, a return trip would be free.

---

My father walks 250 m from home to a shop, comes home for a forgotten bag, goes to the shop again, and returns home. Find the total distance and the displacement.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> home → shop   250 m
> shop → home   250 m
> home → shop   250 m
> shop → home   250 m
> ----------------------
> total distance travelled = 1000 m = 1 km
> ```
> **Displacement = 0 m**, because he ends exactly where he started.

---

A student runs from the ground floor to the fourth floor and then down to the second floor. Each floor is 3 m high. Find the total vertical distance and the displacement.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ground → 4th floor : 4 × 3 m = 12 m upward
> 4th → 2nd floor    : 2 × 3 m =  6 m downward
>
> total vertical distance = 12 + 6 = 18 m
> displacement            = 2 × 3 m = 6 m UPWARD from the ground floor
> ```
> **Why displacement is 6 m:** only the **net** change in height counts — the student ended two floors above where they began.

---

Define average speed and average velocity, and state which one carries a direction and why.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> average speed    = (total distance travelled) ÷ (time interval)
> average velocity = (displacement)             ÷ (time interval) = s / t
> ```
> **Average speed has NO direction, average velocity DOES.**
>
> **Why:** speed is calculated from **distance**, which has only a numerical value. Velocity is calculated from **displacement**, which has a direction — and that direction is passed straight on to the velocity. For straight-line motion it is shown by a **+** or **–** sign.
>
> **Both are measured in m/s** (also km/h).

---

Sarang swims 25 m to the far end of a pool and back in 50 s. Find his average speed and average velocity.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total distance = 25 + 25 = 50 m
> displacement   = 0 m  (he is back where he started)
>
> average speed    = 50 m ÷ 50 s = 1 m/s
> average velocity = 0 m  ÷ 50 s = 0 m/s
> ```
> **He was swimming the whole 50 s and his average velocity is zero.** Average velocity asks only *where did you end up compared with where you started* — not how hard you worked.

---

You drive 200 km north in 3 hours, then 200 km south in 2 hours. Find the average speed and average velocity for the whole trip.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total distance = 200 + 200 = 400 km
> displacement   = 0 km      (back at the start)
> total time     = 3 + 2 = 5 h
>
> average speed    = 400 km ÷ 5 h = 80 km/h
> average velocity = 0 km   ÷ 5 h = 0 km/h
> ```
> **Note the trap:** average speed is **not** the average of 200/3 and 200/2. **You must divide TOTAL distance by TOTAL time**, because the two legs took different times and so should not be weighted equally.

---

Under what conditions is (i) the magnitude of average velocity equal to the average speed, and (ii) the average velocity zero while the average speed is not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) When the object moves in ONE direction throughout the interval** — never turning back. Then **distance = magnitude of displacement**, so the two ratios are identical.
>
> **(ii) When the object RETURNS to its starting point.** Displacement is then zero, so average velocity is zero — but the path length is not zero, so average speed is not zero.

---

Two postmen start 210 yojanas apart and walk towards each other, one covering 9 yojanas a day and the other 5. When do they meet, and why can you add their speeds?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> distance closed per day = 9 + 5 = 14 yojanas
> time to close 210       = 210 ÷ 14 = 15 days
> ```
> **They meet after 15 days** — the first having walked 135 yojanas and the second 75.
>
> **Why adding works:** what matters is how fast the **GAP BETWEEN THEM** shrinks, and **both of them shrink it at the same time**. So the gap closes at the sum of the two speeds. (This problem is from the **Ganitakaumudi**, 14th century CE; speed = distance ÷ time is already established in the **Aryabhatiya**, 5th century CE.)

---

Distinguish uniform from non-uniform motion in a straight line.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **UNIFORM:** the object covers **equal distances in equal intervals of time**, for **all possible choices of interval** → **constant speed**.
> - **NON-UNIFORM:** it covers **unequal distances in equal intervals** → the speed is increasing, decreasing, or both.
>
> **Why "for all possible choices of interval" matters:** an object could cover 20 m in each of two consecutive 1-second intervals by speeding up and slowing down within them. Uniform motion has to hold **whatever intervals you check**.

---

Define average acceleration and give its SI unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> average acceleration = (change in velocity) ÷ (time interval)
>                      = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ (time interval)
>                    a = (v − u) / (t2 − t1)
> ```
> **SI unit: m/s² (m s^-2).** Like velocity, it needs a **magnitude and a direction**.

---

Can an object be moving very fast and yet have zero acceleration? Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> **Acceleration depends not on how fast an object is moving, but on how QUICKLY ITS VELOCITY IS CHANGING.**
>
> A bus cruising down a straight highway at a steady 100 km/h is moving very fast indeed and has **zero acceleration**, because its velocity is not changing at all. Conversely a car pulling away from a red light is barely moving and has a large acceleration.

---

When is the acceleration in the direction of the velocity, and when is it opposite?

> [!ANSWER]
> | If the magnitude of velocity is | Acceleration is |
> |---|---|
> | **increasing** (speeding up) | **in the direction of velocity** — comes out **positive** |
> | **decreasing** (slowing down) | **opposite to the direction of velocity** — comes out **negative** |
>
> **You do not have to decide this by feel** — put the signed values into `a = (v − u)/t` and the sign of the answer tells you.

---

A bus goes from 36 km/h to 54 km/h in 10 s, and later stops from 54 km/h in 5 s. Find the average acceleration in each case.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (i) accelerating
>     u = 36 km/h = 36 × 5/18 = 10 m/s
>     v = 54 km/h = 15 m/s,  t = 10 s
>     a = (15 − 10)/10 = +0.5 m/s²        along the velocity
>
> (ii) braking
>     u = 15 m/s,  v = 0 m/s,  t = 5 s
>     a = (0 − 15)/5 = −3 m/s²            opposite to the velocity
> ```
> **The minus sign is the answer to "which way?"** — it says the acceleration points backwards along the motion, which is exactly what braking is.

---

How do you convert km/h to m/s, and what is a useful check value?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Multiply by 1000/3600 = 5/18.** To go from m/s to km/h, multiply by **18/5**.
>
> **Useful check: 36 km/h = 10 m/s.** If a conversion doesn't sit sensibly against that anchor, you have probably multiplied instead of divided.
>
> **Why 5/18:** 1 km = 1000 m and 1 h = 3600 s, so 1 km/h = 1000/3600 m/s.

---

An object dropped from a height has velocities 0, 9.8, 19.6, 29.4 and 39.2 m/s at t = 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 s. Find the average acceleration in each interval and comment.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Interval | Calculation | Average acceleration |
> |---|---|---|
> | 0–1 s | (9.8 − 0)/1 | **9.8 m/s²** |
> | 1–2 s | (19.6 − 9.8)/1 | **9.8 m/s²** |
> | 2–3 s | (29.4 − 19.6)/1 | **9.8 m/s²** |
> | 3–4 s | (39.2 − 29.4)/1 | **9.8 m/s²** |
>
> **The acceleration is CONSTANT at 9.8 m/s², directed downwards** — the same as the direction of motion, which is why the object speeds up.
>
> **This is the acceleration due to the gravitational force of the Earth, denoted g.**

---

In one worked example the origin was taken at the ground with "up" positive; in another it was taken at the release point with "down" positive. Is that allowed?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — you may choose the origin and the positive direction however you like.**
>
> **But once chosen, they must NOT be changed while solving that problem.** Every u, v, a and s in the working has to be measured in the same frame with the same sign convention.
>
> **Why the freedom exists:** nothing in nature marks a direction as positive; the signs are a bookkeeping device we impose. **Why the consistency is essential:** the moment you switch conventions mid-problem, quantities that should cancel start adding instead.

---

Why is a position–time graph not a route map?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because it does not show the PATH the object took — it shows how its POSITION changes with TIME.**
>
> A person walking due north at a steady pace produces a **straight sloping line** on a position–time graph. That line is not a picture of the road; the road might be dead straight while the graph rises, and the graph would be identical for someone walking straight south with "south" taken as positive.
>
> **The X-axis is time, not east–west.** Confusing the two is the commonest mistake with these graphs.

---

What does the SHAPE of a position–time graph tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> | Shape | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **straight sloping line** | equal displacements in equal times → **constant velocity** |
> | **curve** | unequal displacements in equal times → **velocity changing** → **accelerated motion** |
> | **straight line parallel to the time axis** | position not changing → **the object is AT REST** |
> | **steeper line** | **greater magnitude of velocity** |

---

A position–time graph is a horizontal line at 40 m. Describe the motion.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The object is AT REST, 40 m from the origin.**
>
> Its position is **40 m and is not changing with time** — which is precisely the definition of being at rest. A straight line **parallel to the time axis** on a position–time graph always represents a stationary object.
>
> (Note this is a **position**–time graph, not a distance–time graph: a stationary object's distance–time graph would also be flat, but at zero.)

---

How do you get velocity from a position–time graph, and why does that method work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Take the SLOPE.** Pick two points A and B on the line, complete the right-angled triangle:
> ```
> BC = change in position (s2 − s1)
> CA = change in time     (t2 − t1)
> v  = BC / CA = (s2 − s1)/(t2 − t1)
> ```
> **Why it works — it isn't a rule to memorise.** Average velocity is *defined* as change in position ÷ change in time, and **that is exactly what the slope computes**. The slope of any graph gives the **rate of change of the Y-quantity with respect to the X-quantity**.
>
> **Worked:** from 40 m at 2 s to 80 m at 4 s → `v = (80 − 40)/(4 − 2) = 40/2 =` **20 m/s**.

---

Two straight-line position–time graphs are drawn on the same axes; B's line is steeper than A's. Which object has the higher velocity?

> [!ANSWER]
> **B.**
>
> **Why:** over the **same time interval**, B's line rises further, so B has the **larger displacement in the same time** — and displacement ÷ time is exactly velocity. **Steeper slope = higher velocity**, always.

---

Two objects A and B move on parallel tracks in the same direction, and both position–time graphs are straight lines with different slopes. Do they ever have equal velocity?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> **Why:** a **straight** position–time line means **constant velocity**, and the velocity equals the **slope**. Two straight lines with **different slopes** therefore have two **different constant velocities**, at every instant.
>
> **What their crossing point WOULD mean:** if the lines intersect, the two objects are at the **same position at the same time** — they meet. That is a shared *position*, not a shared *velocity*.
>
> **For velocities to become equal, at least one graph would have to be curved** so that its slope could change to match the other's.

---

What does the SHAPE of a velocity–time graph tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> | Shape | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **straight line parallel to the time axis** | velocity constant → **acceleration is ZERO** |
> | **straight line sloping UP** | velocity increasing by equal amounts in equal times → **constant acceleration, in the direction of velocity** |
> | **straight line sloping DOWN** | velocity decreasing by equal amounts → **constant acceleration, opposite to the direction of velocity** |
>
> **Careful:** a *flat* line here means **constant velocity**, not "at rest" — the object may be racing along. On a *position*–time graph a flat line does mean at rest. **Always check which quantity is on the Y-axis.**

---

A car's velocity rises steadily from 5 m/s at t = 10 s to 10 m/s at t = 20 s. Find its acceleration from the graph.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Take the slope of the velocity–time line:**
> ```
> a = (v − u)/(t2 − t1) = (10 − 5)/(20 − 10) = 5/10 = 0.5 m/s²
> ```
> **Why slope gives acceleration here:** acceleration is *defined* as change in velocity ÷ change in time, and on this graph velocity is the Y-quantity and time the X-quantity — so the slope computes exactly that.

---

What does the AREA between a velocity–time line and the time axis represent, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The DISPLACEMENT in that time interval.**
>
> **Why:** for constant velocity, `displacement = velocity × time`. On the graph the **velocity is the height** and the **time interval is the width** — so their product **is the area of the rectangle**.
>
> When the velocity changes, the region becomes a rectangle plus a triangle, but the meaning is unchanged: **area still equals displacement**.

---

A car travels at a constant 20 m/s for 6 s. Find the displacement from the velocity–time graph.

> [!ANSWER]
> The graph is a horizontal line at height 20 m/s, so the enclosed region is a **rectangle**:
> ```
> displacement = area = 20 m/s × 6 s = 120 m
> ```

---

Between t = 10 s and t = 20 s a car's velocity increases uniformly from 5 m/s to 10 m/s. Find the displacement using the area.

> [!ANSWER]
> The region is a **rectangle plus a triangle**:
> ```
> rectangle = 5 m/s × 10 s              = 50 m
> triangle  = ½ × 10 s × (10 − 5) m/s   = 25 m
> ------------------------------------------------
> displacement                          = 75 m
> ```
> **Check by a second route:** with constant acceleration the average velocity is just the mean of the ends, `(5 + 10)/2 = 7.5 m/s`, and `7.5 × 10 =` **75 m**. Two methods, same answer.

---

Derive `s = u t + ½ a t²` from the velocity–time graph.

> [!ANSWER]
> The graph is a **straight line starting at height u and ending at height v** after time t, so the enclosed area is a rectangle plus a triangle:
> ```
> s = (u × t) + (½ × t × (v − u))
> ```
> From the first equation, `v − u = a t`. Substituting:
> ```
> s = u t + ½ × t × a t
> s = u t + ½ a t²
> ```
> **The whole derivation is just "area under the line", plus one substitution.**

---

Derive `v² = u² + 2 a s` by eliminating t.

> [!ANSWER]
> From `v = u + a t` we get `t = (v − u)/a`. Substituting into `s = u t + ½ a t²`:
> ```
> s = u(v − u)/a + ½ · a · ((v − u)/a)²
>   = (uv − u²)/a + (u² + v² − 2uv)/(2a)
>   = (2uv − 2u² + u² + v² − 2uv)/(2a)
>   = (v² − u²)/(2a)
>
> 2 a s = v² − u²
>    v² = u² + 2 a s
> ```
> **Why this equation is useful: it contains no t at all**, so it answers questions where time is neither given nor wanted — such as "how far before it stops?".

---

State the three kinematic equations and give a rule for choosing between them.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> v  = u + a t          (contains no s)
> s  = u t + ½ a t²     (contains no v)
> v² = u² + 2 a s       (contains no t)
> ```
> **The rule: look at what is MISSING from the problem.** Each equation omits exactly one of the five quantities, so pick the one that omits the quantity you are neither given nor asked for.
>
> **They are valid ONLY when the acceleration is constant.**

---

A car starts from rest and reaches 24 m/s in 6 s. Find its acceleration and the distance travelled.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = 0, v = 24 m/s, t = 6 s
>
> a = (v − u)/t = 24/6 = 4 m/s²
>
> s = u t + ½ a t² = 0 + ½ × 4 × 36 = 72 m
> ```
> **Check:** with constant acceleration from rest the average velocity is `(0 + 24)/2 = 12 m/s`, and `12 × 6 =` **72 m**. Agrees.

---

A motorbike moving at 28 m/s stops after travelling 98 m with constant acceleration. Find the acceleration and the time taken.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = 28 m/s, v = 0, s = 98 m
>
> v² = u² + 2 a s
> 0   = 784 + 2a(98)
> 196 a = −784
> a = −4 m/s²          (minus: opposite to the motion — it is braking)
>
> v = u + a t
> 0 = 28 − 4t
> t = 7 s
> ```

---

A truck at 54 km/h slows to 36 km/h in 36 s. How far does it travel in that time?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = 54 km/h = 15 m/s,  v = 36 km/h = 10 m/s,  t = 36 s
>
> a = (10 − 15)/36 = −5/36 m/s²
>
> s = u t + ½ a t² = 15(36) + ½(−5/36)(1296) = 540 − 90 = 450 m
> ```
> **Quicker route:** with constant acceleration, average velocity = `(15 + 10)/2 = 12.5 m/s`, so `s = 12.5 × 36 =` **450 m**. Same answer with far less arithmetic.

---

A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 20 m/s in 5 s, travels at 20 m/s for 10 s, then brakes uniformly to rest in 6 s. Find the total distance.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Treat it as three stages and use "average velocity × time" for each:**
> ```
> stage 1  (0 → 20 m/s in 5 s)    average 10 m/s × 5 s  =  50 m
> stage 2  (steady 20 m/s, 10 s)          20 m/s × 10 s = 200 m
> stage 3  (20 → 0 m/s in 6 s)     average 10 m/s × 6 s =  60 m
> ---------------------------------------------------------------
> total                                                 = 310 m
> ```
> **Why you must split it:** the acceleration is **not the same throughout**, and the kinematic equations only apply where a is constant. Each stage is fine on its own.

---

A bus travelling at 36 km/h sees an obstacle 30 m ahead. The driver reacts in 0.5 s, then brakes at 2.5 m/s². Does the bus stop in time?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = 36 km/h = 10 m/s
>
> reaction distance = 10 m/s × 0.5 s = 5 m      (full speed, brakes not yet on)
>
> braking distance:  v² = u² + 2as
>                     0 = 100 − 2(2.5)s
>                     s = 100/5 = 20 m
>
> total stopping distance = 5 + 20 = 25 m
> ```
> **YES — the bus stops with 5 m to spare.**
>
> **Note how much the reaction time cost:** 5 m of the 25 m total, a fifth of the whole distance, travelled before the brake was even touched.

---

Brakes give a car –4 m/s². Find its stopping distance from 54 km/h and from 108 km/h, and state the general lesson.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> v² = u² + 2as with v = 0  →  0 = u² − 8s  →  s = u²/8
>
> 54 km/h  = 15 m/s  →  s = 225/8  = 28.1 m
> 108 km/h = 30 m/s  →  s = 900/8  = 112.5 m
> ```
> **DOUBLE the speed and the stopping distance becomes FOUR times as long — not twice.**
>
> **Why:** `s = u²/8` depends on the **square** of the speed. This single fact is why speed limits matter so much, and why you must leave a much bigger gap at high speed than intuition suggests.

---

Besides the initial speed, what else determines how far a vehicle takes to stop?

> [!ANSWER]
> - the **road surface** — wet, icy or loose gravel gives much less grip
> - the **braking capacity of the vehicle** — how large a negative acceleration the brakes can produce; worn tyres or worn brakes reduce it
> - the **driver's REACTION TIME** — the vehicle travels at **full speed** during the fraction of a second before the brake is touched, and that distance is pure `speed × time`
> - the **mass** of the vehicle, and conditions such as **fog, rain or night** which lengthen reaction time
>
> **Which is why a safe following distance must grow with speed** — and why **V2V (vehicle-to-vehicle) communication**, under development in India and elsewhere, warns drivers of possible collisions before they can see the danger.

---

A car moves at a constant 6 m/s for 2 minutes, then accelerates at 1 m/s² for 6 s. Find the total displacement.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> stage 1:  s = 6 m/s × 120 s = 720 m
>
> stage 2:  u = 6 m/s, a = 1 m/s², t = 6 s
>           s = ut + ½at² = 36 + ½(1)(36) = 36 + 18 = 54 m
>
> total = 720 + 54 = 774 m
> ```
> **On a velocity–time graph** this is a **rectangle** (720 m) followed by a **rectangle plus triangle** (36 + 18 m) — the areas are the same calculation drawn instead of written.

---

Car A reaches 5 m/s in 5 s from rest; car B reaches 3 m/s in 10 s from rest. Both accelerate uniformly. Find each acceleration and each displacement.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Car A:  a = 5/5  = 1 m/s²      s = ½ × 1 × 5²   = 12.5 m in 5 s
> Car B:  a = 3/10 = 0.3 m/s²    s = ½ × 0.3 × 10² = 15 m in 10 s
> ```
> **A has more than three times B's acceleration, yet B covers MORE ground** — because B was given twice as long. **Acceleration and displacement answer different questions**, and a bigger a does not automatically mean a bigger s.

---

A girl notices her scooter's speedometer reading is constant. Can the scooter still be accelerating?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — if she is TURNING.**
>
> A speedometer shows only the **magnitude** of the velocity. **Velocity changes if EITHER its magnitude OR its direction changes** — so going round a bend at a steady 30 km/h means the direction of velocity is changing continuously, and therefore **the scooter is accelerating**.
>
> Only on a **straight** road does a constant speedometer reading mean zero acceleration.

---

A child on a merry-go-round completes one full revolution of a circle of radius R. Find the distance travelled, the displacement, and the average velocity.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> distance travelled = circumference = 2 π R
> displacement       = 0             (back to the starting point)
>
> average speed    = 2 π R / T       where T is the time for one revolution
> average velocity = 0 / T = 0
> ```
> **The most extreme distance-vs-displacement case there is:** the child can travel any distance you like and still have zero displacement, simply by going round enough times.

---

Define uniform circular motion, and explain why it counts as accelerated motion even though the speed never changes.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Uniform circular motion is motion in a circular path with CONSTANT SPEED.**
>
> **It is accelerated because VELOCITY changes if EITHER its magnitude OR its DIRECTION changes** — and in circular motion the **direction of velocity is changing continuously**, at every single point of the path.
>
> **In everyday speech we only call it "accelerating" when something speeds up**, which is why this feels wrong at first. But acceleration is defined as the rate of change of **velocity**, and velocity has a direction built into it.

---

Explain, using a square track then a hexagonal track, why a circular path means the direction changes continuously.

> [!ANSWER]
> - On a **rectangular** track the athlete runs straight, then **changes direction 4 times** per lap.
> - On a **hexagonal** track, **6 times** per lap — more turns, each one smaller, taken more often.
> - Keep **increasing the number of sides** and the turns become more frequent and each one smaller.
> - In the limit, **each side shrinks to a point and the track becomes a circle** — and the direction of the velocity now **changes continuously**, at every instant, rather than at a few corners.
>
> **A circle is a polygon with turning spread out evenly everywhere** — which is exactly why the acceleration in circular motion never stops.

A marble is spun round the inside of a ring on a smooth surface. The ring is lifted away. What happens, and what does it prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The marble moves off in a STRAIGHT LINE** — it does not keep curving.
>
> **Why:** once released, it **continues moving in the direction it had at the instant the ring was removed** — along the **tangent** to the circle at that point.
>
> **What this proves:** something had to be **continuously forcing** the marble to curve; the ring was pushing it inward the whole time. **Circular motion is not what an object does when left alone — straight-line motion is.**
>
> **Repeat the activity several times** before concluding, because one trial could be a fluke.

What is a tangent, and what is its connection to velocity in circular motion?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A tangent is a straight line that meets a circle at exactly ONE point.**
>
> **In circular motion, the velocity at any point is along the TANGENT at that point, in the direction of motion.**
>
> **That is why a released object flies off along a tangent**, and why mud flies off a spinning wheel in straight lines rather than continuing to curve.

Uniform circular motion is called an idealised model. Why is it still worth studying?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In the real world, the two conditions — perfectly constant speed and a perfectly circular path — are often not met.**
>
> **But it is still useful because it is the FOUNDATION for more complex real situations:** planets revolving around the Sun, a vehicle making a circular turn, a satellite in orbit.
>
> **This is exactly the Chapter 1 method** — build the simplest model that captures what matters, understand it fully, then add the complications back one at a time.

A disc has numbers written 7 cm from the centre and letters 4 cm from the centre, in the same font size. When the disc spins, the numbers blur first. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the numbers are moving FASTER.**
>
> Every point on the disc completes **one revolution in the same time T**. But speed is `2πR / T`, so a point further from the centre covers a **larger circumference in the same time**:
> ```
> numbers:  speed = 2π(7)/T
> letters:  speed = 2π(4)/T        →  the numbers move 7/4 times as fast
> ```
> **A faster-moving mark sweeps past your eye in less time**, so it smears into a blur while the slower letters can still be resolved.
>
> **The general point: on a rotating body, all points share the same rotation rate but NOT the same speed.**

A minute hand of length 7 cm runs from 6:00 PM to 7:30 PM. Find the distance travelled by its tip, its displacement, its speed and its velocity.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> time = 90 min = 1.5 revolutions = 5400 s
> circumference = 2πr = 2 × 22/7 × 7 = 44 cm
>
> (i)  distance = 1.5 × 44 = 66 cm
>
> (ii) after 1.5 revolutions the tip is DIAMETRICALLY OPPOSITE its start
>      (it began at 12, ends at 6)
>      displacement = diameter = 2 × 7 = 14 cm, directed straight DOWN
>
> (iii) speed    = 66 cm ÷ 5400 s ≈ 0.0122 cm/s
>
> (iv)  velocity = 14 cm ÷ 5400 s ≈ 0.0026 cm/s, directed downward
> ```
> **The half-revolution is what makes this interesting:** a whole number of revolutions would have given zero displacement, but the extra half leaves the tip on the far side.

A student says "the Earth moves around the Sun, so nothing on Earth is ever really at rest". Is this right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both statements can be true at once, because REST AND MOTION ARE ALWAYS RELATIVE TO A CHOSEN REFERENCE POINT.**
>
> - Take the **ground** as the reference point: a book on a table is **at rest** — its position with respect to the ground does not change with time. That is a perfectly correct description, and it is the one we use for everything in this chapter.
> - Take the **Sun** as the reference point: the same book is moving at about 30 km/s along with the Earth.
>
> **Neither answer is wrong — they answer different questions.** This is precisely why the definition of position starts with *"first choose a reference point"*. There is no such thing as being at rest full stop; only at rest **with respect to something**.
