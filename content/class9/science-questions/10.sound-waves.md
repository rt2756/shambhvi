A stretched rubber band is plucked and makes a sound. What happens when it stops moving, and what does that tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The sound stops too.**
>
> As long as the band is **stretched and vibrating**, sound is produced; **once the vibration stops, so does the sound.**
>
> **Conclusion: SOUND IS PRODUCED BY VIBRATIONS.** A **VIBRATION** is the **periodic to-and-fro motion (oscillation)** of an object, and the object producing the sound is called the **SOURCE**.

---

Name four different kinds of vibrating things that produce sound.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Strings** — a plucked veena or guitar string
> - **Membranes** — the stretched skin of a tabla or drum
> - **Air columns** — the air inside a **bansuri (flute)**, set vibrating when you blow across it
> - **Solid objects struck** — a metal taal or bell, which produces sound because of **sonority**
>
> **In most musical instruments MORE THAN ONE part vibrates** — a string plus the body of the instrument, for example.

---

How do humans produce sound, and how do grasshoppers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Humans:** by the vibration of the **VOCAL CORDS** — tightly stretched muscular flaps inside the **voice box or LARYNX** in the throat. (Touch your throat while speaking and you can feel it.) The **tongue, lips, mouth and nasal cavity** then shape that sound into speech or music.
>
> **Grasshoppers and crickets:** by **rubbing their wings or legs** together — striking or rubbing body parts rather than using vocal cords.

---

Describe a tuning fork and give two reasons why it is used in sound experiments.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A U-shaped metal bar with a stem**, usually of steel or aluminium. The two sides of the U are the **PRONGS (tines)**, struck against a **soft rubber pad** to set them vibrating.
>
> **Why it is used:**
> 1. **It produces very nearly a SINGLE frequency**, unlike everyday sounds which mix many frequencies — so results are clean and repeatable.
> 2. **It vibrates steadily and predictably** for a useful length of time.
>
> **Strike it on the soft pad, never on a hard surface** — that can damage the fork and changes the sound.

---

How would you show that the prongs of a sounding tuning fork are really vibrating?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Touch a vibrating prong gently to the surface of water. Waves form and spread out over the surface.**
>
> **Why this is good evidence:** you cannot see the prongs moving — the vibration is far too small and fast. **The water makes the invisible motion visible**, because only something moving could disturb a still surface.
>
> **And you can hear the fork whichever way you turn it near your ear**, showing that sound propagates in multiple directions from a source.

---

Describe two experiments showing that sound travels through solids and through liquids.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Through a SOLID:** stand across a desk from a friend who knocks or scratches it. You hear it through the air. Now **press your ear against the desk, close your other ear, and listen again** — **you hear it through the desk itself**.
>
> **Through a LIQUID:** tap two metal spoons together in air and listen. Then **submerge them in a bucket of water, without touching the sides or bottom, and tap them again** — you still hear the sound, which has reached you **through the water and then the air**.
>
> **Sound can propagate through SOLIDS, LIQUIDS and GASES.** The material it travels through is the **MEDIUM**.

---

Describe the bell jar experiment and explain what it proves.

> [!ANSWER]
> An **electric bell is switched on inside a sealed glass jar** and its loudness noted. As **air is sucked out with a vacuum pump, the sound becomes fainter**. At near vacuum, **almost NO sound is heard — even though the bell can still be SEEN ringing.** Let the air back in and the sound returns, gradually as loud as before.
>
> **It proves that SOUND CANNOT PROPAGATE IN A VACUUM — it needs a MEDIUM.**
>
> **Why the "still seen ringing" detail is the key to the whole experiment:** the bell is doing exactly the same thing throughout. **The only thing that changed is the medium** — so the medium must be what carries the sound.

---

Two astronauts on a spacewalk are repairing a space station together. Can they hear each other speak, or hear metal clanking?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> **In outer space there is a NEAR VACUUM**, and sound cannot propagate without a medium. Their voices and the clanking of metal produce vibrations, but there is nothing there to carry them across.
>
> **They communicate through radio devices fitted into their spacesuits** — radio waves, like light, need **no medium at all**.

---

Assertion: "We cannot hear a bell ringing in a jar after most of the air is pumped out." Reason: "Sound requires a medium to travel." Evaluate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both are TRUE, and the reason IS the correct explanation of the assertion.**
>
> The reason states exactly the principle at work: with the air removed there is no medium, so the compressions and rarefactions have nothing to travel through — which is precisely why the sound is not heard.

---

A mark is made on one turn of a slinky and the end is pushed and pulled. What travels along the slinky and what does not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **What TRAVELS:** regions where the turns are **closer together** and regions where they are **more spread out** — the **DISTURBANCE** moves the whole length of the slinky.
>
> **What does NOT travel:** the **MARK**. It only **oscillates about its rest position, parallel to the direction of the disturbance**, and ends up where it began.
>
> **Each turn oscillates about its own position while the DISTURBANCE travels from one end to the other.** This is the single most important idea about sound: **the wave moves, the medium does not.**

---

Using an oscillating piston in an air-filled tube, explain how compressions and rarefactions are formed.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Piston motion | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **not moving** | the air has a uniform **AVERAGE DENSITY** throughout the tube |
> | **moves FORWARD** | it pushes nearby air particles forward, **compressing** the air → a region of **HIGHER density: a COMPRESSION (C)** |
> | **moves BACKWARD** | the particles follow it back, thinning the air near the piston → a region of **LOWER density: a RAREFACTION (R)** |
> | **oscillates** | **alternate compressions and rarefactions** are produced and travel outward |

---

How does a compression travel forward through the air if the air particles do not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By COLLISIONS.**
>
> The compressed particles **collide with the particles just ahead of them**, passing the crowding forward. Those particles then collide with **their** neighbours, and so on down the tube.
>
> **So the high-density region moves forward while each particle merely oscillates about its own mean position** and stays roughly where it was. It is like a crowd passing a nudge along a queue — the nudge reaches the front, nobody walks there.

---

Define a sound wave.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A disturbance consisting of a series of alternating COMPRESSIONS and RAREFACTIONS propagating through a medium, WITHOUT the actual flow of the particles of the medium.**
>
> **The direction in which the wave travels is the direction of propagation.**

---

What is a longitudinal wave, and how does it differ from a transverse wave?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **LONGITUDINAL:** the particles of the medium vibrate **PARALLEL** to the direction of wave propagation. **Sound is a longitudinal wave.**
> - **TRANSVERSE:** the particles vibrate **PERPENDICULAR** to the direction of propagation. **Light is a transverse wave.**
>
> **Earthquakes produce SEISMIC waves of both kinds** — and the **longitudinal ones are the FIRST to be detected by seismographs**, because they travel faster.

---

What is a mechanical wave? Is light one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A MECHANICAL WAVE requires a MATERIAL MEDIUM to propagate. Sound is a mechanical wave.**
>
> **Light is NOT.** Light is a **transverse wave that can travel through a vacuum** — which is why **light from the Sun and distant stars reaches the Earth**, while their sound never could.
>
> **This one difference explains a great deal:** you always see distant lightning before you hear the thunder, and you can watch a bell ring in a vacuum without hearing it.

---

Assertion: "Compressions and rarefactions move through the medium." Reason: "Individual particles of the medium continuously move forward with the wave." Evaluate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The assertion is TRUE; the reason is FALSE.**
>
> Compressions and rarefactions really do travel through the medium — but the particles **do NOT move forward with the wave**. They only **vibrate about their mean positions**.
>
> **If the particles did travel with the wave, a shout across a room would produce a wind** — and it does not.

---

Grains on a stretched sheet jump when a loud sound is made nearby, without anything touching them. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The sound travels through the AIR, reaches the sheet, and MAKES IT VIBRATE. That vibration throws the grains up.**
>
> **What this proves: sound is a form of ENERGY.** When the source vibrates it **transfers energy to the surrounding medium**, and the medium's particles pass that energy on through their vibration and collisions.
>
> **Strike the plate harder and the grains jump higher** — more energy into the medium means larger displacements from the mean positions, so the sheet moves more.
>
> **In sound propagation it is the ENERGY that is transferred, not the particles of the medium.**

---

When sound travels from a tuning fork to your ear, what actually reaches your ear — air particles from near the fork, energy, the fork's material, or a stream of compressed air?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The ENERGY carried by the sound waves.**
>
> **Why the others are wrong:**
> - **Air particles from near the fork** never reach you — each particle only oscillates about its own mean position.
> - **The fork's material** obviously stays where it is.
> - **A continuous stream of compressed air** would be a **wind**, not a sound — and a shout does not blow anyone's hair about.

---

How do a microphone and a speaker work, and how are they related?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are exact opposites.**
>
> - **MICROPHONE:** sound waves make a thin membrane called the **DIAPHRAGM** vibrate, and those vibrations are converted into an **electrical signal**. **Sound energy → electrical energy.**
> - **SPEAKER:** an electrical signal makes a **cone or diaphragm** vibrate, which produces sound. **Electrical energy → sound energy.**
>
> If all the components work properly, the sound from the speaker closely matches the sound the microphone originally captured.

---

On a graph of density against distance, what are the crest and the trough, and why is the wavy curve slightly misleading?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **CREST — the highest point:** the **maximum density**, the middle of a **COMPRESSION**.
> - **TROUGH — the lowest point:** the **minimum density**, the middle of a **RAREFACTION**.
> - The horizontal dashed line is the **average density** of the undisturbed medium.
>
> **Why it can mislead: the curve is NOT a picture of the sound moving up and down.** Sound is **longitudinal** — nothing moves sideways at all. **The graph plots DENSITY against DISTANCE**, and the ups and downs are variations in **how crowded the particles are**.

---

Define wavelength, frequency and time period, with their units.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **WAVELENGTH (λ)** — the distance between **two consecutive crests, or two consecutive troughs**. SI unit: **metre (m)**.
> - **FREQUENCY (ν)** — the number of **density oscillations at a fixed point per unit time**. SI unit: **per second (s⁻¹), called the HERTZ (Hz)**.
> - **TIME PERIOD (T)** — the time taken for **ONE complete density oscillation at a fixed point**. SI unit: **second (s)**.
>
> **One complete oscillation** = the density going from maximum to minimum and back to maximum (or the reverse).

---

How are frequency and time period related, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ν = 1 / T
> ```
> **They are INVERSELY related** — a shorter time period means a higher frequency.
>
> **Why: they are two ways of saying the same thing.** Frequency asks *how many oscillations per second*; time period asks *how long each one takes*. If each takes 0.2 s, then 5 fit into a second — and 1/0.2 = 5.

---

There are 10 density oscillations in 2 seconds at a point. Find the frequency and the time period.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> frequency   = 10 / 2 s = 5 Hz
> time period = 2 s / 10 = 0.2 s
> ```
> **Check: T = 1/ν = 1/5 = 0.2 s ✔** — always worth doing, since the two answers must be reciprocals.

---

If 20 compressions pass a point in 4 seconds, what is the frequency?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ν = 20 / 4 s = 5 Hz
> ```
> **Each passing compression is one complete oscillation at that point** — the density rises to a maximum and falls again — so counting compressions per second gives the frequency directly.

---

A piston produces a 20 Hz sound wave. How many oscillations does it complete per minute?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 20 oscillations per second × 60 seconds = 1200 oscillations per minute
> ```

---

A thin rubber band and a thick one are plucked. Which produces the higher frequency, and how do their time periods compare?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The THIN band vibrates FASTER, so it produces a HIGHER FREQUENCY and a SHORTER TIME PERIOD.**
>
> (The thick band vibrates more slowly: lower frequency, longer time period.)
>
> **This is exactly why instruments are built the way they are** — the thick strings of a guitar or veena give the low notes and the thin ones the high notes, and tightening a string raises its pitch.

---

Define the amplitude of a sound wave and explain how it relates to energy.

> [!ANSWER]
> **AMPLITUDE = the MAXIMUM CHANGE in the density of the air in a compression (or rarefaction), compared with the average density.**
>
> **A wave with a larger amplitude carries MORE ENERGY.**
>
> **You can see this in the grains experiment:** strike the plate harder and more energy goes into the medium, so the particles are displaced further from their mean positions, the sheet vibrates more, and **the grains jump higher**.

---

Define intensity, and explain why sound gets quieter as you move away from the source.

> [!ANSWER]
> **INTENSITY = the amount of sound energy passing through a unit area, perpendicular to the direction of propagation, in unit time.**
>
> **Why it falls with distance:** as the wave travels outward it **spreads over a larger and larger area**. **Energy must be conserved**, so the **same amount of energy is now spread thinner** — less passes through each unit of area, and the intensity drops.
>
> **Sounds produced with a larger initial amplitude carry more energy and travel further** before their intensity fades away.

---

Derive the relation between speed, wavelength and frequency of a sound wave.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The speed of sound is the distance a point on the wave — a crest, say — travels in unit time.**
>
> **A crest covers exactly ONE WAVELENGTH in exactly ONE TIME PERIOD**, so:
> ```
> v = λ / T
>
> and since ν = 1/T,
>
> v = λ × ν            speed = wavelength × frequency
> ```

---

Rank the speed of sound in steel, water and air, and explain the order.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> steel  ≈ 5000 m/s     water ≈ 1500 m/s     air ≈ 340 m/s     (at 15 °C)
> ```
> **Fastest in SOLIDS, slower in LIQUIDS, slowest in GASES** — about **4–5 times faster in water** and **15–20 times faster in solids** than in air.
>
> **Why: sound is passed along by COLLISIONS between particles.** In a solid the particles are **packed close together and tightly bound**, so a disturbance is handed on almost immediately. In a gas the particles are **far apart** and must travel a long way before colliding.

---

Compare the speeds: water against air, and steel against water.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> water / air   = 1500 / 340  ≈ 4.4      sound travels about 4.4 times faster in water
> steel / water = 5000 / 1500 ≈ 3.3      about 3.3 times faster again in steel
> ```
> **And steel against air: 5000/340 ≈ 15 times faster** — which is what makes the fence experiment below so dramatic.

---

How do temperature and humidity affect the speed of sound in air?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The speed INCREASES as either the temperature or the humidity increases.**
>
> In dry air it is about **331 m/s at 0 °C** and nearly **344 m/s at 22 °C**.
>
> **Note what this implies:** any sound calculation that quotes a speed is quoting it **for particular conditions**, which is why textbook problems always tell you which value to use.

---

Does the speed of sound depend on its frequency? Why does this matter for music?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — in most media the speed depends only on the MEDIUM, not on the source or the frequency.** If the frequency changes, **the wavelength changes to match while the speed stays the same**.
>
> **Why this matters enormously for music:** if high notes travelled faster than low ones, the notes of a chord struck together would **arrive at different times**. A song would be scrambled by the time it reached the back of a hall, and a singer with an orchestra would sound hopelessly out of time.
>
> **Music is only possible because sound of every pitch travels at the same speed.**

---

Human hearing spans 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Find the corresponding wavelengths in air. (v = 344 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> λ = v / ν
>
> ν = 20 Hz    →  λ = 344 / 20    = 17.2 m
> ν = 20000 Hz →  λ = 344 / 20000 = 0.0172 m = 1.72 cm
> ```
> **A thousand-fold range of wavelengths, from longer than a classroom to shorter than your thumb.**

---

A source produces a sound wave of wavelength 3.44 m travelling at 344 m/s. Find its frequency and time period.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ν = v / λ = 344 / 3.44 = 100 Hz
> T = 1 / ν = 1 / 100     = 0.01 s
> ```

---

A sound wave in steel has a wavelength of 50 m, and the speed of sound in steel is 5000 m/s. Find the frequency and time period.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ν = v / λ = 5000 / 50 = 100 Hz
> T = 1 / ν = 1 / 100   = 0.01 s
> ```
> **Note the same 100 Hz as the previous wave in air — but with a wavelength 15 times longer**, because the speed in steel is about 15 times greater. **Same frequency, different medium, different wavelength.**

---

The distance between two consecutive compressions in a sound wave is 8 cm and the speed is 340 m/s. Find the wavelength and frequency.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the distance between consecutive compressions IS the wavelength:
> λ = 8 cm = 0.08 m
>
> ν = v / λ = 340 / 0.08 = 4250 Hz
> ```
> **Convert to metres before dividing** — mixing centimetres with m/s is the commonest error here.

---

Waves A and B travel at 345 m/s. A completes two full waves in 5 cm; B completes one. Find both wavelengths and frequencies.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> A:  λ = 5 cm ÷ 2 = 2.5 cm = 0.025 m
>     ν = 345 / 0.025 = 13,800 Hz
>
> B:  λ = 5 cm       = 0.05 m
>     ν = 345 / 0.05 = 6,900 Hz
> ```
> **A has half the wavelength and therefore twice the frequency of B** — as it must, since both travel at the same speed and `v = λν` with v fixed makes λ and ν inversely proportional.

---

Thunder is heard 5 s after the lightning flash. How far away was the strike? (v = 340 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> distance = v × t = 340 × 5 = 1700 m ≈ 1.7 km
> ```
> **Why the light's travel time is ignored:** light travels at **300,000 km/s**, so it crosses 1.7 km in about six millionths of a second — **essentially instantaneously**. The entire 5 s delay is the sound's journey.
>
> **This gives you a field rule: count the seconds and divide by 3 for the distance in kilometres.**

---

How much longer does thunder take to cross 1720 m if the air cools from 22 °C to 0 °C? (344 m/s and 331 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> at 22 °C:  t = 1720 / 344 = 5.00 s
> at  0 °C:  t = 1720 / 331 = 5.196 s
>
> extra time = 5.196 − 5.00 ≈ 0.20 s
> ```
> **About 0.2 s longer in the colder air** — small, but easily measurable, and a direct consequence of sound travelling more slowly when it is cold.

---

Two friends stand 340 m apart along a steel fence. One knocks it; the other listens with an ear on the fence. Find the time gap between the two sounds, and say whether she could tell them apart. (steel 5000 m/s, air 340 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> through steel:  t = 340 / 5000 = 0.068 s
> through air:    t = 340 / 340  = 1.000 s
>
> time difference = 1.000 − 0.068 = 0.932 s
> ```
> **YES, easily.** Two sounds can be heard separately if the gap is **at least 0.1 s**, and 0.93 s is almost ten times that.
>
> **She hears the knock through the STEEL first**, then again nearly a second later through the air — a striking way to feel how much faster sound moves in a solid.

---

Distinguish pitch from loudness, and say which physical quantity each corresponds to.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **PITCH is how FREQUENCY is perceived.** Shrill sounds — a whistle, a siren — are **high-pitched** and have **higher frequency**; deep sounds — thunder, an aircraft rumble — are **low-pitched** with **lower frequency**.
> - **LOUDNESS is how AMPLITUDE is perceived.** Larger amplitude sounds louder; loudness also **decreases as you move away** from the source.
>
> **Both are SUBJECTIVE.** The physical properties — frequency, amplitude, wavelength, speed — are well defined and measurable; **how we experience them is not**, and the exact mathematical relations are complicated.

---

Why are loudness and intensity not the same thing?

> [!ANSWER]
> **INTENSITY is a measurable physical quantity** — energy per unit area per unit time — and it is the same for everyone standing in the same place.
>
> **LOUDNESS depends on the LISTENER'S hearing ability.** Two people sitting side by side in identical intensity can find the sound differently loud, and someone with hearing loss may barely hear what another finds loud.
>
> **The words are used interchangeably in everyday speech**, but only one of them is a property of the sound itself.

---

State the human audible range, and name the two ranges outside it with animals that can hear them.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> INFRASONIC   below 20 Hz          humans cannot hear;  ELEPHANTS can
> AUDIBLE      20 Hz to 20,000 Hz   the human hearing range
> ULTRASONIC   above 20 kHz         humans cannot hear;  DOGS, CATS, BATS
>                                   and DOLPHINS can
> ```
> **The range varies from person to person and DECREASES WITH AGE** — which is why some high-frequency sounds are audible to children and not to adults.

---

Why do boys' voices deepen during adolescence?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Their VOCAL CORDS LENGTHEN AND THICKEN**, so they **vibrate less frequently** — and lower frequency is perceived as lower pitch.
>
> **It is the same physics as the rubber band:** a thicker, longer, looser vibrating object gives a lower note than a thin, short, tight one.
>
> **Voices also differ because of how the sound is SHAPED** by the throat, mouth and nasal cavities — which is why you can recognise a friend instantly even when two people sing the same note.

---

What are decibels, and why is noise pollution a health concern?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Loudness is commonly measured in DECIBELS (dB)** — rustling leaves a few dB, normal conversation about **60 dB**, firecrackers over **100 dB**. **Even a small increase in dB means a LARGE increase in sound intensity**, which is why 100 dB is not "not quite twice" 60 dB but very much more.
>
> **NOISE is unwanted or harmful sound.** Exposure above recommended limits, **especially for long durations**, can affect **health, sleep and hearing**, and prolonged exposure to loud sound can cause **hearing loss** — tested using **audiograms**.
>
> **Hearing aids** contain a **microphone, an amplifier and a speaker**, helping the wearer hear and communicate.

---

How does the ear turn sound into something the brain can use, and how do two ears help?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sound entering the ear makes the EARDRUM vibrate. Tiny bones amplify those vibrations, and the COCHLEA converts them into electrical signals** which travel to the brain, where they are perceived as sound.
>
> **Two ears let the brain work out the DIRECTION of a sound**, by comparing **which ear hears it first** — a time gap often **less than a thousandth of a second**.
>
> **Animals differ:** **snakes and fish sense vibrations through their bodies**, and some insects have ear-like organs on their body parts.

---

Explain tone, musical note, timbre and octave.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **TONE** — a sound of a **single frequency**, like a tuning fork or whistling.
> - **MUSICAL NOTE** — a combination of a lowest frequency called the **FUNDAMENTAL** with higher frequencies called **OVERTONES**, which together create a **rich and pleasant** sound. Indian string instruments like the **sarangi, sitar and veena** use extra strings to enrich this combination.
> - **TIMBRE** — the quality that makes a flute, an ektara and a tabla sound different **playing the same note at the same loudness**. It comes from their **shape, material and construction**, which set the **pattern and intensity of the overtones**.
> - **OCTAVE** — the interval between two notes where **one has double the frequency of the other** (200 Hz and 400 Hz), measured between their fundamentals.

---

What is the syaahi on a tabla, and what does it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The black patch at the centre of the drum head membrane** on Indian drums such as the **tabla and mridangam**.
>
> **It ALTERS the vibration of the membrane**, letting these instruments produce a **rich variety of sounds** and giving **a level of tonal control rarely found in other drums**.
>
> **Sir C. V. Raman** — who won India's first Nobel Prize in Science for the **Raman Effect** in light — also studied the **acoustics of the tabla and mridangam** to understand how they produce such nuanced sounds.

---

State the laws of reflection as they apply to sound.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sound obeys the SAME laws of reflection as light:**
>
> **The directions in which the sound is incident and reflected make EQUAL ANGLES with the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence, and all three lie in the SAME PLANE.**

---

What is an echo, and why can't you hear one in a small room?

> [!ANSWER]
> **An ECHO is a reflected sound heard again after some time** — your voice bouncing off the hard surface of a distant object, such as a mountain, a cliff or the far end of a long corridor.
>
> **In a small room the wall reflections arrive TOO QUICKLY** for the brain to separate them from the original sound.
>
> **Two sounds are heard as separate only if the gap between them is AT LEAST 0.1 s** — less than that and they merge.

---

Calculate the minimum distance of a reflecting surface for an echo to be heard. (v = 340 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> distance sound travels in 0.1 s = 340 × 0.1 = 34 m
>
> but that is the ROUND TRIP — source → surface → back
>
> minimum distance to the surface = 34 / 2 = 17 m
> ```
> **The factor of 2 is the commonest mistake in the whole chapter.** In every echo or sonar problem, **the sound covers the distance TWICE** — always ask whether the time given is for the round trip or one way.

---

Why do soft and rough surfaces give no clear echo?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Echoes are strongest from HARD, SMOOTH surfaces, which reflect sound well.**
>
> - **Soft surfaces like curtains ABSORB sound**, so little of it comes back.
> - **Rough surfaces SCATTER sound in different directions**, so what returns is spread out and weak rather than arriving together as a distinct reflection.
>
> **This is exactly why halls are furnished with curtains and upholstery** — to kill unwanted reflections.

---

You clap in an empty corridor and hear an echo after 0.5 s. How far away is the wall? (v = 340 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total distance travelled = v × t = 340 × 0.5 = 170 m
>
> that is there AND back, so
>
> distance from the wall = 170 / 2 = 85 m
> ```

---

An experiment needs echoes to arrive at least 0.2 s after the sound is made. How far away must the reflecting surface be? (v = 343 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total distance = 343 × 0.2 = 68.6 m
>
> distance to the surface = 68.6 / 2 = 34.3 m
> ```

---

What is reverberation, and how does it differ from an echo?

> [!ANSWER]
> **REVERBERATION is sound persisting after the source has stopped, caused by MULTIPLE REFLECTIONS from the walls of a large hall or auditorium.**
>
> | | **ECHO** | **REVERBERATION** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Cause | a **single** reflection from a distant surface | **multiple** reflections |
> | Time gap | **at least 0.1 s** | **less than 0.05 s** |
> | Heard as | a **separate, repeated** sound | the sound **smearing / persisting** |

---

Reflected sound reaches your ear 0.05 s after it was produced. Echo or reverberation?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Reverberation — NOT an echo.**
>
> **0.05 s is well under the 0.1 s needed** for the brain to hear two sounds as separate. Instead of a distinct repetition, the reflected sound **merges with the original and makes it persist**.

---

How are auditoriums designed to control reverberation?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Modern auditoriums and concert halls are architecturally designed to have DESIRABLE reverberation**, so the audience everywhere hears speech and music clearly without distortion.
>
> **Unwanted reverberation is reduced by SOUND-ABSORBING surfaces:** absorbing panels, **upholstered chairs**, **curtains** and other soft, porous materials. Without them the sound becomes **garbled**, as reflections from every wall pile onto each other.
>
> **Note that the aim is not to remove reverberation entirely** — some is desirable, which is why a hall sounds richer than an open field.

What is remarkable about the Whispering Gallery of the Gol Gumbaz?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Its design allows even a FAINT WHISPER to be heard MULTIPLE TIMES across the large dome.**
>
> The Gol Gumbaz is in **Bijapur, Karnataka**, and is one of many monuments built by medieval architects across the world, including in India, with **deep acoustic insight** — reflections shaped deliberately by the curve of the dome, centuries before the physics was written down.

List two uses of infrasonic waves and four of ultrasonic waves.

> [!ANSWER]
> **INFRASONIC (below 20 Hz):**
> - detecting natural events such as **earthquakes and volcanic eruptions**
> - detecting **severe storms** — these waves travel **long distances** through air and the Earth
>
> **ULTRASONIC (above 20 kHz):**
> - **ULTRASONOGRAPHY** — imaging internal organs **without surgery**
> - **breaking kidney stones** into pieces small enough to pass out of the body
> - **ultrasonic welding** and **cleaning delicate machine parts**
> - **detecting defects inside metal blocks** during construction and industrial testing
> - **locating objects** by reflection

What is echolocation, and how does a bat use it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **ECHOLOCATION is locating objects using REFLECTED SOUND WAVES.**
>
> **Bats are nocturnal and fly and hunt in the dark without colliding with anything.** They **emit short bursts of ULTRASONIC waves**, which **reflect from nearby objects**; by **sensing the echoes** the bat determines the **position of obstacles and prey**.
>
> **Dolphins, whales and some birds** also use echolocation for navigation and hunting.
>
> **Why ultrasound rather than audible sound:** short wavelengths reflect well from small objects like insects, which longer waves would simply flow around.

---

How does sonar work, and what is it used for?

> [!ANSWER]
> **SONAR — SOund Navigation And Ranging — applies the same principle underwater.**
>
> **Ultrasonic waves are sent into the water, and the reflected waves are analysed** to determine the **distance, direction and speed** of underwater objects such as **submarines or shipwrecks**.
>
> **Why sound rather than light underwater:** sound travels **much farther in water than light does**, which is why every underwater ranging system uses it.

---

A sonar signal returns after 0.90 s in seawater. How far away is the object? (v = 1530 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the 0.90 s is a ROUND TRIP, so time to reach the object = 0.90 / 2 = 0.45 s
>
> distance = v × t = 1530 × 0.45 = 688.5 m
> ```

---

A sonar signal sent to find the ocean depth returns after 4 s. Find the depth. (v = 1500 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> time down = 4 / 2 = 2 s
>
> depth = 1500 × 2 = 3000 m
> ```

A ship searching for a wreck detects an echo after 5 s. How deep is the wreck? (v = 1525 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> time down = 5 / 2 = 2.5 s
>

A parking sensor emits 40 kHz ultrasound and beeps at 1.2 m from an obstacle. How long does the wave take to travel there and back? (v = 345 m/s)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total distance = 2 × 1.2 = 2.4 m
>
> t = distance / speed = 2.4 / 345 = 0.00696 s ≈ 7 milliseconds
> ```
> **About 7 thousandths of a second** — which is why the system can update the warning many times a second as you reverse.
>
> **Note the 40 kHz frequency plays no part in the calculation** — the speed of sound does not depend on frequency.

Two identical sources are at A (in air) and B (underwater), the same distance from a cliff. The sound returns to A in 4.5 times the time it takes for B. Find the ratio of the two speeds.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> both cover the same round-trip distance 2d
>
> t_A = 2d / v_air      t_B = 2d / v_water
>
> given  t_A = 4.5 × t_B :
>
>       2d / v_air = 4.5 × 2d / v_water
>
>       v_water / v_air = 4.5
>
> so  v_air : v_water = 1 : 4.5 = 2 : 9
> ```
> **Sound travels 4.5 times faster in water than in air** — which matches the textbook figure that it is about 4–5 times faster in liquids.

Which observation best supports the idea that sound is a mechanical wave — that it reflects, that it needs a medium, that it has frequency, or that it carries energy?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That it NEEDS A MEDIUM to propagate.**
>
> **That is precisely the definition of a mechanical wave.**
>
> **Why the others don't settle it:** **light** also reflects, has frequency and carries energy — yet light is **not** mechanical, since it travels through a vacuum. Only the medium requirement separates the two.

---

Increasing the frequency of a sound wave in a given medium increases which of these — wavelength, speed, compressions per second, or time period?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The number of COMPRESSIONS PER SECOND.**
>
> **That is what frequency IS** — the number of density oscillations passing a point each second.
>
> **Why the others are wrong:**
> - **Wavelength DECREASES.** With `v = λν` and v fixed by the medium, raising ν must lower λ.
> - **Speed is UNCHANGED** — it depends on the medium, not the frequency.
> - **Time period DECREASES**, since `T = 1/ν`.

A film shows a spacecraft exploding in space with a flash of light and a bang at the same moment. What is wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two errors:**
>
> 1. **There should be NO SOUND AT ALL.** Space is a near vacuum, and **sound cannot propagate without a medium**. The explosion would be completely silent to an observer.
>
> 2. **Even in air, the light and sound would NOT arrive together.** Light travels at **300,000 km/s** and sound at about **340 m/s** — nearly a million times slower. You would see the flash long before you heard anything, exactly as with lightning and thunder.

Sketch what a sound wave graph would look like with a density amplitude of 3 units and a wavelength of 4 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Plot DENSITY on the y-axis against DISTANCE on the x-axis**, with the **average density as a horizontal dashed line at 0**.
>
> - **Crests reach +3 units** above the average line and **troughs reach −3 units** below it — that is what "amplitude 3 units" means: the **maximum change** from average, in both directions.
> - **Consecutive crests are 4 cm apart** — so crests at 0, 4, 8 cm and troughs midway between them at 2, 6, 10 cm.
>
> **Amplitude sets the HEIGHT of the curve; wavelength sets how far apart the humps are.** The two are completely independent — you can change either without touching the other.

---

Three sound waves are drawn on identical axes. How would you identify which has the highest frequency?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The one with the SHORTEST WAVELENGTH — the most crests packed into the same distance.**
>
> **Why:** all three travel in the same medium, so they share the same speed. With `v = λν` and v fixed, **the shortest wavelength must have the highest frequency**.
>
> **And the longest wavelength — the most stretched-out curve — has the lowest frequency.**
>
> **Do not confuse this with amplitude:** the **height** of the curve is amplitude (loudness); the **spacing** of the humps is wavelength (pitch).

---

Two sound wave graphs are drawn to the same scale. How would you tell which has the greater wavelength and which the smaller amplitude?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Greater WAVELENGTH:** the one whose **crests are further apart** horizontally — the more stretched-out curve.
> - **Smaller AMPLITUDE:** the one whose **crests rise less far above the average-density line** — the flatter curve.
>
> **The two are read off different axes and mean different things:** wavelength (horizontal) relates to **frequency and pitch**; amplitude (vertical) relates to **energy and loudness**.
