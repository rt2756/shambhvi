List four things a force can do to an object, with an example of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Make an object move from rest** — a ball at rest starts moving when you kick it
> - **Change the speed** of a moving object — pushing a moving trolley harder
> - **Change the direction** of motion — a cricket bat striking a moving ball
> - **Change the shape** of an object — squeezing a lemon between your fingers

---

Why must the direction of a force always be stated, and what is the SI unit of force?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because force is a quantity that needs a DIRECTION as well as a magnitude and unit** — like position, displacement, velocity and acceleration.
>
> **Every force you have met came with a direction attached:** friction acts **opposite** to the motion, the Earth pulls objects **towards itself**, buoyant force acts **upwards**, like poles **repel**, unlike poles **attract**.
>
> **If either the magnitude OR the direction of a force changes, its effect changes** — so a description without a direction is incomplete.
>
> **SI unit: the newton (N).**

---

Why is "newton" written with a small n but its symbol with a capital N?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because that is the convention for every unit named after a person:** the **full name begins with a small letter** (newton, not Newton) while the **symbol is capitalised** (N, not n).
>
> The same rule gives us **kelvin (K)**, **pascal (Pa)** and **watt (W)**.

---

How does a spring balance measure a force, and what does "weight" actually mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Pull on the free end of a spring balance and it reads the force with which you are pulling** on the spring inside — the stretch of the spring is proportional to the force.
>
> **Weight is not a special quantity at all: it is the GRAVITATIONAL FORCE with which the Earth pulls an object.** That is why the same instrument measures both — measuring "weight" was only ever measuring a force.

---

Give an example of an object with two forces acting on it, and say what the forces are.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **A box being pushed along the floor:** your **applied force** forwards, and the **force of friction** backwards. (Also the **weight** downwards and the **normal force** from the floor upwards, which balance each other.)
> - **A ball floating on water:** the **gravitational force** by the Earth downwards, and the **buoyant force** by the liquid upwards.
>
> **In real life, situations where only ONE force acts are rare.**

---

Define balanced and unbalanced forces using a tug of war.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **BALANCED FORCES are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.** If both teams pull with equal force, **the rope does not move** — the net force is zero.
> - **UNBALANCED FORCES leave a non-zero NET FORCE.** If one team pulls harder, **the rope moves in the direction of the larger force**.
>
> **The rule that follows: the motion of an object changes only when the forces on it are unbalanced.**

---

State the rule for finding the net force of two forces along a line, and apply it to 10 N and 6 N in each possible arrangement.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Same direction:** net force = **SUM**, in that direction.
> - **Opposite directions:** net force = **DIFFERENCE**, in the direction of the **larger** force.
>
> ```
> both to the right:          10 + 6 = 16 N to the right
> 10 N right, 6 N left:       10 − 6 =  4 N to the right
> 6 N right, 10 N left:       10 − 6 =  4 N to the LEFT
> ```

---

A weightlifter holds a barbell steady above her head. Name the forces on the barbell and say whether they are balanced.

> [!ANSWER]
> - the **gravitational force (weight)** of the barbell, acting **downwards**
> - the **upward force** applied by the weightlifter
>
> **They ARE balanced**, and you can tell without any calculation: **the barbell is not moving**, so its velocity is not changing, so the net force on it must be zero.
>
> **This is the first law used backwards** — instead of predicting motion from forces, we deduce the forces from the motion.

---

In an arm-wrestling match the arms tilt towards one player. Are the forces balanced, and who pushed harder?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No, they are NOT balanced** — the arms are moving, so the velocity is changing, so a **non-zero net force** must be acting.
>
> **The player pushing in the direction the arms are moving exerted the LARGER force.** The net force is the difference between the two pushes and points along the larger one.
>
> (Note that by the **third law** the two hands push each other equally at every instant — but the arms are also pushed by each player's **muscles**, and it is those that differ.)

---

Why do you sometimes have to push harder before a box will move at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of FRICTION**, which arises between the bottom of the box and the floor and acts **opposite** to the force you apply.
>
> **The box only starts moving when your push EXCEEDS the friction**, so that a **net force** acts in the direction of motion. Push with less than that, and the two forces are **balanced** and nothing happens.

---

Besides the applied force and friction, what other two forces act on a box being pushed along the floor?

> [!ANSWER]
> - the **gravitational force (weight)**, acting **downwards**
> - the **NORMAL FORCE** exerted by the surface, acting **upwards, perpendicular to it**
>
> **These two are balanced**, which is why the box slides along the floor rather than sinking into it or lifting off.
>
> Air also exerts friction on the moving box, but its magnitude is usually **so small it can be neglected**.

---

You stop pedalling and the bicycle comes to rest. Does this prove that a force is needed to keep something moving?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** It proves that **friction** is acting.
>
> **What the pedalling was really doing was CANCELLING friction, not sustaining the motion.** Take friction away and no force at all would be needed to keep the bicycle moving.
>
> **This exact observation misled people for centuries.** Because friction is everywhere on Earth, it looked as though motion itself needed a cause. **Galileo argued in the 17th century that a body moving on a horizontal plane, with all impediments removed, would move indefinitely.**

---

A stack of coins is launched by a rubber band across a wooden table, a laminated top, and a polished marble floor. Predict and explain the results.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Surface | Distance before stopping | Friction |
> |---|---|---|
> | Wooden table top | shortest | **largest** |
> | Laminated top | longer | smaller |
> | Polished marble / tile | **longest**, velocity falls most slowly | **smallest** |
>
> **The conclusion: the force of friction depends on the nature of the surfaces in contact. Less friction means the velocity decreases more slowly and the object travels further.**

---

Why must the rubber band be stretched by exactly the same amount in every trial of that experiment?

> [!ANSWER]
> **So that the launching force is IDENTICAL in every run.**
>
> Then the **only** thing that differs between trials is the **surface**, so any difference in the distance travelled must be caused by the **friction of that surface** and nothing else.
>
> **This is the same principle as splitting one crystal solution into two equal halves:** control everything except the one variable you are studying. (The marks A, B and C must also be at the same distances every time, and each run repeated twice.)

---

Trace the forces on the stack of coins from before release to when it stops.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Before release:** the stack is stationary, so the forces on it are **balanced**.
> 2. **On release:** the **stretched rubber band's force exceeds friction**, so a **net forward force** acts → the stack **accelerates** from rest to some velocity.
> 3. **After it loses contact with the band:** the band's force **disappears**, and **only friction acts**, opposite to the motion → the stack **decelerates** and finally stops.
>
> **Note that the deceleration in stage 3 needs no new force** — the friction was there all along; it simply has nothing left to oppose it.

---

A wooden block on a spring balance is pulled with gradually increasing force. Why does the reading, taken just as the block starts to move, give the force of friction?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because at that moment the block's velocity is neither increasing nor decreasing, so the NET FORCE on it is zero.**
>
> Only two horizontal forces act — the spring's pull and friction — and if the net is zero, **they must be equal in magnitude**. So the spring balance reading **is** the friction.
>
> **A smaller reading means smaller friction; a larger reading means larger friction** — and the surface with the smallest reading is the very one on which the stack of coins travelled the furthest. **Two independent methods, same ranking.**

---

Carry out a thought experiment: what would happen to the coins if the friction were exactly zero?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Their velocity would never decrease. They would never come to rest — they would keep moving forever.**
>
> **Why we reason instead of experimenting:** a truly frictionless surface **cannot be created in the real world**, so we extrapolate from the trend. Every surface we tested pointed the same way — **less friction, less slowing** — and zero friction is simply the limit of that trend.
>
> **That conclusion is Newton's first law**, arrived at from evidence we can actually collect.

---

State Newton's first law of motion and explain what "inertia" means.

> [!ANSWER]
> **An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with CONSTANT VELOCITY, unless a NET FORCE acts upon it.**
>
> Equivalently: **if the net force is zero, the object cannot start moving or change its velocity — its acceleration is zero.**
>
> **INERTIA** is Newton's word for **the tendency of objects to resist a change in their state of rest or uniform motion**. He built the first law on that idea and published all three laws in **1687**.

---

Why is "moving in a circle at a steady speed" not covered by "constant velocity" in the first law?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because constant velocity means neither the MAGNITUDE nor the DIRECTION changes.**
>
> Going round a bend at a steady speed keeps the magnitude fixed but **changes the direction continuously** — so the velocity is changing, and by the first law **a net force must be acting**.
>
> A non-zero constant velocity means motion **in a straight line, in the same direction, at the same speed**.

---

A person pushes a moving box forward with a force exactly equal to the friction on it. Does the box stop?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — it continues moving with CONSTANT VELOCITY.**
>
> The applied force forwards and the friction backwards are **equal and opposite, so they balance**. The **net force is zero**, and by Newton's first law an object in motion with no net force **keeps moving at constant velocity**.
>
> **The common wrong answer is "it slows down"** — but that would need a net backward force, and there isn't one.

---

Sketch the position–time and velocity–time graphs for an object on which no net force acts.

> [!ANSWER]
> **There are exactly TWO possibilities:**
>
> | Case | Position–time | Velocity–time |
> |---|---|---|
> | **At rest** | horizontal line — position unchanged | horizontal line **at zero** |
> | **Constant velocity** | **straight sloping line** | horizontal line **at a non-zero height** |
>
> **In both cases the velocity–time graph is horizontal** — that is the visual signature of zero acceleration, and therefore of zero net force.

---

An object is moving with constant velocity. Is a net force acting on it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — the net force is zero.**
>
> Constant velocity means **zero acceleration**, and by `F = ma`, zero acceleration means zero net force.
>
> **This does NOT mean no forces act.** Several may act — an engine's push, friction, weight, normal force — but they **add up to zero**. It is the **net** force that is zero.

---

Give an example of a real situation where extra forces are applied to make the net force zero.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A car cruising at a steady speed on a straight road.** The engine drives it forwards while friction and air resistance oppose it. The driver keeps the engine force **exactly equal** to the resistance, so the net force is zero and the car holds a constant velocity.
>
> **A weightlifter holding a barbell steady:** her upward force exactly cancels the weight.
>
> **A book resting on a table:** its weight acts down, the normal force acts up, and they cancel.
>
> **Why this matters:** in the real world you can almost never remove all forces, but you can very often arrange for them to **cancel** — which is just as good, because motion depends only on the **net** force.

---

State the two hypotheses that lead to Newton's second law, and how each was tested.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Hypothesis 1: for the same object, a larger force gives a larger acceleration.** (A gentle push starts a ball slowly; a strong push starts it fast.)
> **Tested** with a cart pulled by a thread over a pulley by a **cup of coins**, changing the **weight in the cup** while keeping the cart the same. **Doubling the weight increased the acceleration.**
>
> **Hypothesis 2: for the same force, a larger mass gives a smaller acceleration.** (With the same push, lighter objects are easier to get moving.)
> **Tested** by keeping the **cup fixed** and **doubling the mass of the cart**. **The acceleration decreased.**
>
> **Each test changes ONE thing at a time** — which is the only way to attribute the result to that thing.

---

In the cart experiment, both runs start from rest and cover the same distance. Show how the ratio of accelerations is found from the two times.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> both runs:  u = 0 and the same distance s,  so  s = ½ a t²
>
> s = ½ a1 T1²        and        s = ½ a2 T2²
>
> equating:      ½ a1 T1² = ½ a2 T2²
>
>                a1 / a2  =  T2² / T1²
> ```
> **A shorter time therefore means a larger acceleration** — and you never need to measure the acceleration directly, only the **times**, which a slow-motion video can give you.

---

Why are weights a convenient way to apply forces of known, different magnitudes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the WEIGHT of an object is the gravitational force with which the Earth pulls it**, and different objects have different weights.
>
> So a **cup with coins in it** is a ready-made adjustable force: change the coins and you change the force. And crucially, **the same cup released the same way gives the SAME force every trial**, which is what a fair comparison needs.

---

State Newton's second law and write it mathematically.

> [!ANSWER]
> **When a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates IN THE DIRECTION of the net force. The magnitude of the acceleration is PROPORTIONAL to the net force and INVERSELY PROPORTIONAL to the mass of the object.**
> ```
> a = F / m        or        F = m a
> ```
> **The direction of the acceleration is the direction of the net force.**

---

Define one newton from F = ma.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> put m = 1 kg and a = 1 m/s²:
> F = 1 kg × 1 m/s² = 1 kg m/s² = 1 N
> ```
> **One newton is the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² on an object of mass 1 kg.**
>
> **Note it is a DERIVED unit** — defined from kilogram, metre and second, not measured independently.

---

Why does g not depend on the mass of the falling object?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because both the force and the mass grow together, and they cancel.**
> ```
> gravitational force on a mass m:   F = m g
> its acceleration:                  a = F / m = m g / m = g
> ```
> A heavier object does feel a **larger** force — but that larger force has to accelerate a **proportionally larger mass**, so the acceleration comes out the same for everything.
>
> **g = 9.8 m/s²** near the Earth's surface (about 10 m/s² for quick estimates), and it is **nearly constant** there.

---

In the cart experiment, doubling the force did not quite double the measured acceleration. Why not?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Measurement errors** — reading times off a video, judging the exact moment of release.
> - **Friction between the cart's wheels and the surface**, which is not really zero. Part of the applied force is used up against friction rather than accelerating the cart, so the acceleration falls short of the ideal.
>
> **Real data rarely matches the ideal exactly, and knowing WHY is part of the experiment** — an unexplained discrepancy is a loose end; an explained one is evidence that you understand the set-up.

---

Why does a fielder pull their hands backwards while catching a fast cricket ball?

> [!ANSWER]
> **To increase the TIME over which the ball's velocity falls to zero.**
> ```
> a = change in velocity ÷ time    →   longer time = smaller acceleration
> F = m a                          →   smaller acceleration = SMALLER FORCE
> ```
> The ball has to be stopped either way — the velocity change is fixed. **What the fielder controls is how long it takes**, and stretching it out reduces the force on the hands, **minimising injury**.

---

Explain how airbags and high-jump landing mats work, using the same idea.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both lengthen the stopping time, so the force is smaller.**
>
> - **Airbag:** in a collision the vehicle halts abruptly. The bag inflates into a **soft, compressible cushion**, so the passenger's head and chest push into it instead of striking the hard steering wheel or dashboard. **The stopping takes longer → smaller acceleration → smaller force → lower risk of serious injury**, especially combined with a seat belt.
> - **Landing mat or sand bed:** the athlete sinks in and **decelerates gradually** instead of stopping dead on a hard floor, so the force on the body is far smaller.
>
> **Bubble wrap and hay round glass items work the same way** — soft packing spreads each jolt over a longer time.

---

Why is a fresh coconut cracked by bringing it down hard on a stone?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the same principle works in REVERSE — a very short stopping time means a very large force.**
>
> The coconut arrives at high velocity and **stops in a very short time** on the hard surface. To change its velocity that quickly, **the ground must exert a very large force on it** (`F = ma` with a huge a), and **that large force breaks the shell**.
>
> **Airbag: lengthen the time to protect. Coconut: shorten the time to break.** One idea, two opposite uses.

---

A barbell has a 10 kg bar with 10 kg on each side. What force must the lifter apply to hold it steady?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total mass = 10 + 10 + 10 = 30 kg
>
> gravitational force = m g = 30 × 9.8 = 294 N downwards
> ```
> **She must apply 294 N upwards.**
>
> **Why exactly that:** the barbell is steady, so its acceleration is zero, so the net force must be zero — her force has to cancel the weight exactly.

---

A 25 kg block faces a maximum friction of 50 N. Find its displacement in 2 s if it is pushed with (i) 50 N and (ii) 55 N.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) 50 N:** the push exactly equals the friction, so the forces are **balanced**, the **net force is zero**, and **the block stays stationary. Displacement = 0.**
>
> **(ii) 55 N:**
> ```
> net force = 55 − 50 = 5 N
> a = F/m = 5/25 = 0.2 m/s²
> s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 0.2 × 2² = 0.4 m forward
> ```
> **Note how small the difference in push is (5 N in 55 N) and how completely it changes the outcome** — because only the NET force matters, not the size of the push.

---

A 1500 kg car's velocity–time graph rises from 0 to 10 m/s over 0–5 s, stays flat from 5–10 s, then falls back to 0 by 15 s. Find the force in each interval.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 0–5 s    a = (10 − 0)/5  =  2 m/s²    F = 1500 × 2  =  3000 N  (east, along the motion)
> 5–10 s   flat line → constant velocity → a = 0 → NO net force at all
> 10–15 s  a = (0 − 10)/5 = −2 m/s²     F = 1500 × (−2) = −3000 N, i.e. 3000 N WEST
> ```
> **The minus sign is the direction** — the force opposes the motion, which is what braking is.

---

A toy car of mass 100 g moves with a constant velocity of 0.5 m/s. What is the net force on it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Zero.**
>
> **Constant velocity means zero acceleration**, and `F = ma = 0.1 × 0 = 0`.
>
> **The mass and the speed given are both red herrings** — neither affects the answer. It is the *constancy* of the velocity that settles it.

---

Two children of different masses sit on identical swings. To give them the same initial acceleration, for which child do you push harder?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The heavier child.**
>
> From `F = ma`, with **a the same for both**, the force needed is **proportional to the mass**. Double the mass and you need double the force for the same acceleration.
>
> **This is Hypothesis 2 of the second law, felt directly:** for a fixed force, a larger mass accelerates less — so to undo that, you must supply more force.

---

State Newton's third law, and explain why the two forces do not cancel.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object SIMULTANEOUSLY exerts an EQUAL and OPPOSITE force on the first.**
>
> **They do not cancel because they act on TWO DIFFERENT OBJECTS.**
>
> Cancelling requires two equal and opposite forces on the **SAME** object — that is what *balanced* forces are. A third-law pair is split between two bodies, so each body feels only one of them.
>
> **If action and reaction cancelled, nothing in the universe could ever accelerate.**

---

Two identical spring balances are hooked together and pulled. What do they read, and what does that show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both read exactly the SAME, every time, whatever the size of the pull.**
>
> **This shows that the forces the two balances exert on each other, in opposite directions, are EQUAL IN MAGNITUDE** — a direct measurement of Newton's third law.
>
> **Repeat it several times with different pulls** before concluding — a single agreement could be luck.

---

Explain how you walk, in terms of the third law, and say what role friction plays.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Your feet push the ground BACKWARDS; by the third law the ground pushes your feet FORWARDS**, and that forward push moves you.
>
> **The force from the ground is FRICTION.** So here **friction HELPS you move rather than opposing you** — the exception to the usual story.
>
> **Without friction your foot would simply slip backwards and you would fall.** That is why:
> - **grooves** are cut into the soles of footwear and **treads** into tyres, to increase friction
> - it is difficult to walk on **wet polished floors or ice**, and risky to drive on roads covered with **water or snow**

---

How does a canoe move forward, and why does pushing harder make it faster?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The canoeist pushes the WATER BACKWARDS with the paddle; by the third law the water pushes the PADDLE FORWARDS with an equal force**, and that carries the paddle and canoe forward.
>
> **The two forces do not cancel because they act on different things** — one on the water, one on the paddle.
>
> **Pushing harder makes the forward force on the paddle larger**, so by `a = F/m` the acceleration is larger and the canoe's velocity increases.
>
> (Real canoe speed also depends on drag, water currents, the mass of the canoe and the style of rowing.)

---

The same canoeist paddles with the same force in an empty canoe and in one carrying a passenger. Which goes faster?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The EMPTY canoe.**
>
> The **force is the same**, but the loaded canoe has a **larger mass**. From `a = F/m`, a larger mass gives a **smaller acceleration**, so it builds up speed more slowly and reaches a lower speed for the same effort.

---

Why is it harder to climb a smooth tree trunk than a rough one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because climbing depends on FRICTION.**
>
> The climber's legs **push down against the trunk**; by the third law **the friction between the trunk and the legs pushes the person UPWARDS** with an equal force.
>
> **A smoother trunk offers less friction**, so the upward push is smaller and the climber slips. **The grip is not a separate thing from the pushing — the harder you press, the more friction you get.**

---

A balloon is inflated, held on a straw threaded on a taut string, and released. Explain what happens.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The stretched material of the balloon squeezes the air inside and EXPELS IT BACKWARDS. By the third law, the air rushing out exerts an EQUAL FORCE ON THE BALLOON in the opposite direction**, so the balloon (and the straw) shoot forward along the string.
>
> **Note what the balloon is NOT doing:** it is not "pushing against the air outside". It works just as well in a vacuum, because the reaction comes from **the air being thrown out**, not from anything the balloon pushes against.

---

Explain a rocket launch using the third law, and say why the rocket actually rises.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The engine produces gas and expels it downwards; by the third law the gas exerts an equal and opposite force on the rocket, upwards.**
>
> **Why it rises:** that upward force is **larger than the weight of the rocket**, so the **NET force is upwards** and the rocket accelerates off the pad. Equal and opposite forces on **two different objects** (rocket and exhaust gas) do not cancel — but the rocket's own weight does have to be beaten.

---

How did Chandrayaan-3's Vikram lander slow itself down near the Moon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By firing its engine IN THE DIRECTION OF ITS MOTION.**
>
> The exhaust gases then push on the spacecraft in the direction **opposite to its motion**, producing a **backward force that slows it down**.
>
> **This is how the Vikram lander reached the velocity needed for a soft landing near the Moon's south pole** — the third law working as a brake instead of an accelerator.

---

A spacecraft is in a region where gravity is negligible. How can it change its velocity?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By firing its engine and expelling gas.**
>
> The craft **pushes gas out in one direction**; by the third law **the gas pushes the craft in the opposite direction**, giving it a net force and therefore an acceleration.
>
> **There is nothing outside to push against — and nothing needs to be.** The reaction partner is the **expelled gas itself**, which is exactly why rockets work in space.

---

Does Newton's third law apply only to contact forces?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — it applies to ALL forces, contact and non-contact.**
>
> - **Two bar magnets** exert equal and opposite **magnetic** forces on each other.
> - **Two similarly charged balloons** exert equal and opposite **electrostatic** forces.
> - **The Earth and a falling fruit** exert equal and opposite **gravitational** forces on each other.
>
> No touching is required — the law is about the **interaction**, not the contact.

---

The Earth and a falling fruit pull each other with equal forces. Why does only the fruit visibly move?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the accelerations are not equal — the masses are.**
> ```
> a = F / m
> ```
> The forces are equal in magnitude, but **the mass of the Earth is enormous compared with the fruit**, so the Earth's acceleration is **extraordinarily small** — far too small ever to be noticed. The fruit's is not.
>
> **The Earth does move towards the fruit** — just immeasurably little.

---

A 0.1 kg bullet is fired from a 5 kg gun with a force of 2 N. Find the initial acceleration of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> by the third law, the recoil force on the gun is also 2 N
>
> gun:    a = F/m = 2 / 5   = 0.4 m/s²
> bullet: a = F/m = 2 / 0.1 = 20 m/s²
> ```
> **Equal forces, wildly unequal accelerations — because the masses differ by a factor of 50.**
>
> This is why the gun recoils gently into the shoulder while the bullet leaves at high speed.

---

A sailor jumps forwards from a small boat to the shore. Does the boat move, and which way?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — the boat moves BACKWARDS.**
>
> To jump forwards, the sailor **pushes backwards on the boat**; by the third law the boat pushes the sailor forwards. The backward push on the boat gives it a backward acceleration.
>
> **Why it matters in practice:** if the boat slips back as you jump, you launch from a receding platform and may fall short — which is why sailors tie up first.

---

A loaded hand cart collides with an identical but empty one. Which exerts the larger force?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neither — they exert forces of EQUAL magnitude on each other**, in opposite directions.
>
> **Newton's third law makes no exception for mass.** Whatever force the loaded cart exerts on the empty one, the empty one exerts exactly the same back.
>
> **What DOES differ is the acceleration.** From `a = F/m`, the **lighter empty cart is accelerated much more** by the same force — which is why it visibly flies off while the loaded cart barely changes speed. **That difference in effect is what makes people wrongly think the forces differ.**

---

When a bar magnet is brought near a compass, the needle swings but the magnet does not move. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The forces on the two ARE equal and opposite, by the third law. The responses differ because of mass and mounting.**
>
> - The **compass needle is very light** and **pivoted so it can turn almost freely**, so even a tiny force produces a large, visible movement (`a = F/m` with a small m).
> - The **bar magnet is much heavier** and is resting on a surface, held in place by **friction**, so the same size of force produces an imperceptible acceleration.
>
> **Same force, very different effect** — exactly as with the Earth and the fruit.

Why does a fireperson sometimes struggle to hold a hose that is issuing water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of the third law.** The hose **pushes the water forwards** at high speed; by the third law the **water pushes the hose BACKWARDS** with an equal force.
>
> That backward force is large because a lot of water is being accelerated to a high speed every second, and the fireperson must supply an equal forward force just to hold the hose steady.
>
> **It is exactly the same effect as a rocket or a gun's recoil**, only continuous.

Two blocks on a smooth surface: block P has 5 N and 4 N acting on it in opposite directions, and block Q is moving with constant velocity. Which experiences a net force?

> [!ANSWER]
> **P only.**
>
> - **P:** the forces are opposite and unequal, so the net force is `5 − 4 =` **1 N**, in the direction of the 5 N force. **Non-zero → P accelerates.**
> - **Q:** moving with **constant velocity** means **zero acceleration**, so by `F = ma` the **net force on Q is zero** — whatever individual forces may act on it.

---

For a ball on a smooth frictionless surface, complete: (i) with no net force the velocity will …, (ii) with a net force along the motion …, (iii) with a net force opposite the motion …

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) remain the SAME** — no net force means no acceleration, so the velocity is unchanged (Newton's first law).
>
> **(ii) INCREASE** — the acceleration is in the direction of the net force, which is the direction of motion, so the object speeds up.
>
> **(iii) DECREASE** — the acceleration is opposite to the motion, so the object slows down.

When a net force acts, does the object accelerate opposite to the force, or along it? Proportional to what?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Along the direction of the force, with acceleration PROPORTIONAL to the force** (and inversely proportional to the mass).
> ```
> a = F / m
> ```
> **The common wrong options are worth naming:** "opposite to the force" contradicts the second law; "proportional to the mass" has the relationship upside down (it is **inversely** proportional); "inversely proportional to the force" is backwards too.

Four objects have position–time graphs: a horizontal line, two straight sloping lines, and a curve. On which does a net force act?

> [!ANSWER]
> **On the object with the CURVED graph.**
>
> **Why:** the slope of a position–time graph is the **velocity**. A **straight** line (sloping or horizontal) has a **constant slope**, hence constant velocity, hence **zero acceleration and zero net force**. A **curve** has a **changing slope** → changing velocity → **acceleration** → a net force must be acting.
>
> **A horizontal line means at rest — also zero net force.**

A table is moved across the floor at constant velocity by a horizontal force F. What is the frictional force on it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Exactly F**, acting backwards.
>
> **Why:** constant velocity → zero acceleration → **zero net force**. The only horizontal forces are the applied F and friction, so **friction must equal F in magnitude and be opposite in direction**.
>
> **You do not need to know anything about the floor or the table** to answer this — the motion tells you.

In a snake-boat race 100 oarsmen each apply 200 N, but 5 of them row the wrong way. Find the net force on the boat.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 95 rowing correctly:  95 × 200 = 19,000 N  forwards
>  5 rowing the wrong way: 5 × 200 = 1,000 N backwards
>
> net force = 19,000 − 1,000 = 18,000 N forwards
> ```
> **Note the cost of the mistake: 2000 N, not 1000 N.** Each wrong rower not only fails to add 200 N, they **subtract** 200 N — so every misdirected oar costs the boat twice their share.

A 50 g bullet moving at 100 m/s stops after penetrating 50 cm into a wooden block. Estimate the stopping force.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> m = 50 g = 0.05 kg,  u = 100 m/s,  v = 0,  s = 50 cm = 0.5 m
>
> v² = u² + 2as
> 0  = 10,000 + 2a(0.5)
> a  = −10,000 m/s²
>
> F = ma = 0.05 × (−10,000) = −500 N
> ```
> **The stopping force is 500 N**, acting opposite to the bullet's motion.
>
> **Why it is so large from such a light bullet:** the velocity change is huge and happens over a tiny distance, so the acceleration is enormous — 10,000 m/s², about a thousand times g.

A footballer kicks a 0.4 kg ball to a speed of 108 km/h with a force of 800 N. Find the contact time between foot and ball.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = 0,  v = 108 km/h = 108 × 5/18 = 30 m/s,  m = 0.4 kg,  F = 800 N
>
> a = F/m = 800 / 0.4 = 2000 m/s²
>
> v = u + at
> 30 = 0 + 2000 t
> t = 0.015 s
> ```
> **The foot is in contact with the ball for only 15 milliseconds** — which is exactly why such a large force is needed to produce that speed.

A 2 kg object moving at 10 m/s meets a rough patch where friction is 7 N, and an extra 3 N opposing force is applied. How far does it travel before stopping?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total opposing force = 7 + 3 = 10 N   (both act backwards, so they ADD)
>
> a = F/m = −10/2 = −5 m/s²
>
> v² = u² + 2as
> 0  = 100 + 2(−5)s
> s  = 100/10 = 10 m
> ```
> **It travels 10 m.**

An acceleration–mass graph shows a = 10 m/s² at 1 kg, 5 m/s² at 2 kg and 2.5 m/s² at 4 kg. What does the force–mass graph look like?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A HORIZONTAL STRAIGHT LINE at F = 10 N.**
> ```
> F = m a:   1 × 10 = 10 N    2 × 5 = 10 N    4 × 2.5 = 10 N
> ```
> **The force is the same in every case** — the whole point of the graph is that a single **fixed force** was applied to objects of different masses.
>
> **The acceleration–mass graph curves downwards** (a is **inversely** proportional to m), but the product m × a is constant, so the force–mass graph is flat.

Two boxes of masses m1 and m2 on a frictionless surface are joined by a string and pulled by a force F. Find the acceleration.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Treat the two boxes and the string as a SINGLE SYSTEM.**
> ```
> a = F / (mass of system) = F / (m1 + m2)
> ```
> **Why the tension can be ignored:** Box 1 pulls Box 2 through the string, and by the third law Box 2 pulls back equally. That pair is **INTERNAL** to the system, so it contributes nothing to the system's acceleration. **Only EXTERNAL forces matter** — here just F.
>
> **The system accelerates exactly like a single object of mass m1 + m2.** Analysing the two boxes separately gives the same answer with far more work.

A tractor pulls a harrow of mass m1 with force F giving acceleration a1, and a trolley of mass m2 with the same force giving a2. Find the acceleration when it pulls both together.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> from F = ma:     m1 = F/a1        m2 = F/a2
>
> together:  a = F / (m1 + m2)
>              = F / (F/a1 + F/a2)
>              = 1 / (1/a1 + 1/a2)
>
>          a = a1 a2 / (a1 + a2)
> ```
> **Sanity check:** if a1 = a2 = 6, the combined acceleration is `36/12 =` **3** — exactly half, which is right, since the mass has doubled for the same force.
>
> **Note the combined acceleration is always LESS than either of them** — more mass, same force.

Why is treating a walking person as a single object a useful simplification?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the arms and legs move in a very complicated way, yet the OVERALL motion of the body can be worked out by ignoring all of that.**
>
> All those internal movements involve **internal forces**, which cancel in third-law pairs and do not affect the motion of the whole. **Only external forces — friction from the ground, gravity — determine where the person goes.**
>
> **Science often becomes simpler when we stop looking at parts and start looking at the whole.** Choosing what counts as "the system" is itself a modelling decision, exactly as in Chapter 1.

Over what range do Newton's laws work, and where do they need modification?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They describe motion across an enormous range of scales — from everyday objects to planets and stars.**
>
> **They need modification only:**
> - **very close to extremely massive objects**
> - at **speeds near the speed of light**
> - at **very small (atomic) scales**
>
> **This is exactly the Chapter 1 point about theories having limits.** That Newton's laws break down in those regimes is not a failure — it is how we discovered there was more physics to find.
