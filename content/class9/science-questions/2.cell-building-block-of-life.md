Why do scientists study hot springs like those in the Puga Valley, Ladakh, when investigating the origin of life?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because their conditions — **near-boiling water in a cold climate, with changing environmental conditions** — appear **similar to those on early Earth about 3.5 billion years ago**.
>
> The organisms living there are **thermophiles**, heat-loving **unicellular** bacteria. Scientists from the **Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences, Lucknow** found that **calcium carbonate** forms rapidly around them, and such deposits may have **protected early organic molecules from radiation** and even helped form **the first protective membrane** — the barrier that defines a cell.

---

Arrange in order: organ, cell, organ system, tissue. Give an example of the full chain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **CELL → TISSUE → ORGAN → ORGAN SYSTEM**
>
> A group of **similar cells performing similar functions** forms a **tissue**; different tissues form an **organ**; several organs working together form an **organ system**.
>
> **Example:** nasal pores, nasal cavity, trachea and lungs together form the **respiratory system**.

---

Even in a large multicellular organism with organs and organ systems, why is the cell still called the fundamental unit?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it is the **basic level at which life exists** — the smallest thing that is alive.
>
> **Test it:** break a cell into pieces and no piece is alive. A single cell on its own (a bacterium, a yeast) **is** alive. Everything above the cell is organisation of living units; nothing below the cell is living at all.

---

What is meant by the "limit of resolution" of the human eye, and what is its value?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Resolution is the ability to see two very close objects as SEPARATE and DISTINCT.**
>
> Viewed from about **25 cm** (the near point of the eye), two points about **0.1 mm** apart can just be told apart. Closer than that and they look like a single point.
>
> **The limit of resolution of the human eye is 0.1 mm.** Most cells are far smaller, which is why they stayed invisible until microscopes were invented.

---

A microscope maker doubles the magnification but does not improve the resolution. Has the instrument become more useful?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> **Magnification** makes the image bigger; **resolution** decides whether two close points stay distinct. **Blowing up a blurred image just gives a bigger blur** — the extra size adds no new information.
>
> That is why the three features microscope makers work on are **resolution (clarity), contrast (brightness difference between parts) and magnification** — and why **electron microscopes**, using a beam of electrons instead of light, opened up the **nanometre scale**.

---

Who first observed cells, when, and what was he actually looking at?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Robert Hooke, in 1665**, using a **self-designed microscope of about 200–300X**.
>
> He was examining a **thin slice of cork** and saw small **box-like compartments**, which he named **'cells'**.
>
> (What he saw were actually the **empty cell walls** of dead plant tissue — the boxes, not living contents.)

A microscope has a 10X eyepiece and a 40X objective. What is the total magnification, and what does that number mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Total magnification = eyepiece × objective = 10 × 40 = 400X.**
>
> It means the object **appears 400 times larger** than its real size. A 200 µm onion cell would appear the size of an 80 mm object.

---

The field of view of a microscope measures 4 mm across, and you count 20 onion peel cells lying in a straight line across it. Estimate the size of one cell.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 4 mm = 4 × 1000 = 4000 µm
> 4000 µm ÷ 20 cells = 200 µm per cell
> ```
> **About 200 µm.**
>
> **Why this works:** the cells sit shoulder to shoulder across a **known** distance, so distance ÷ count gives the width of one — the same trick as finding the thickness of one sheet by measuring a stack of 500.

---

Define the cell membrane and state its two defining properties.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A thin boundary that surrounds a cell and protects its contents. It defines the INDIVIDUALITY of the cell, and is also called the plasma membrane.**
>
> 1. **Universal** — every cell has one, plant, animal or bacterial.
> 2. **Selectively permeable** — it lets some substances pass while blocking others.

---

Two equal potato pieces are weighed, then one is put in plain water and the other in 20% salt solution for an hour. Predict and explain the results.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Plain water:** the piece **swells** and its weight **increases**.
> - **Salt solution:** the piece **shrinks** and its weight **decreases**.
>
> **Why:** the cell membrane allows **water** to move in and out but **not** the salt or sugar molecules. Water moves from where there is **more water and less solute** to where there is **less water and more solute**, until the concentrations equalise.
>
> In plain water, more water is outside than inside → water flows **in**. In salt solution, less water is outside → water flows **out**.

---

Why is it important to cut the two potato pieces to roughly equal size and to record their initial weights?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **result is the CHANGE in weight, not the final weight.**
>
> - **Equal size** makes the two pieces comparable — otherwise a difference could come from the potato rather than the liquid.
> - **Initial weight recorded** is the only way to calculate the change at all.
>
> **The principle:** in a fair test, everything except the variable being studied (here, the liquid) must start the same.

---

Distinguish diffusion from osmosis. Is osmosis a kind of diffusion?

> [!ANSWER]
> | | Diffusion | Osmosis |
> |---|---|---|
> | What moves | **any particles** | **water only** |
> | Direction | higher → lower concentration | same |
> | Membrane needed? | **no** | **yes — selectively permeable** |
>
> **Yes — osmosis IS a kind of diffusion:** the diffusion of **water** across a **selectively permeable membrane**. Dye spreading in water and fragrance spreading in air are plain diffusion; water entering root cells from the soil is osmosis.

---

Define isotonic, hypotonic and hypertonic solutions, and state what each does to a cell.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Comparison (solute outside vs inside) | Name | Effect on cell |
> |---|---|---|
> | outside `=` inside | **isotonic** | no net water movement — **unchanged** |
> | outside `<` inside | **hypotonic** | water enters — cell **swells** |
> | outside `>` inside | **hypertonic** | water leaves — cell **shrinks** |
>
> **The trick to remembering:** track the **water**, not the solute. Water always moves **towards the more concentrated solution**, because that's the side with less water.

---

Mung bean seeds are soaked in water for 12 hours and then placed in a concentrated solution. Predict what happens and why.

> [!ANSWER]
> **They will shrink / lose the swelling they had gained.**
>
> After soaking, the seeds are **full of water** — inside is dilute compared with the concentrated solution outside. That makes the outside **hypertonic**, so **water leaves the seed cells by osmosis** and the seeds shrivel.

---

Describe the fluid-mosaic model of the cell membrane and explain both parts of the name.

> [!ANSWER]
> The membrane is about **7–10 nm thick**, made of **lipids (fats) and proteins**:
> - a **lipid bilayer** — two layers of fat molecules with **water-attracting heads facing outwards** and **water-repelling tails facing inwards**, with **proteins embedded** in it
> - **"FLUID"** because the molecules can **move sideways, flip and rotate** within the membrane
> - **"MOSAIC"** because the molecules are arranged like **tiles in a mosaic**
> - the **proteins act as gatekeepers**, helping selected substances through

---

Why do the lipid molecules arrange themselves with heads outward and tails inward?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because there is **water on BOTH sides** — outside the cell and inside it, in the cytoplasm.
>
> The **water-attracting heads** face both watery sides; the **water-repelling tails** hide together in the middle, away from water. **This is the only stable arrangement**, which is why the bilayer forms by itself rather than having to be built.

---

Why do plant cells need a cell wall while animal cells do not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Plants generally cannot move from place to place**, so they need a **rigid structure to withstand environmental stresses like wind and rain**. The wall keeps leaves and flowers firm, maintains shape, and helps the plant **stay upright**.
>
> **Animals move.** Without a rigid wall, animal cells can **change shape easily**, and this **flexibility supports the movement and functioning of animal tissues**. A rigid wall would make animal tissue unable to bend, stretch or flow.

---

The cell wall is rigid but permeable, while the cell membrane is selectively permeable. Why does a root need both?

> [!ANSWER]
> It's a **two-stage design**.
>
> The **wall** is fully permeable — water and dissolved minerals pass straight through it, so it never blocks anything the root needs. It contributes only **rigidity**.
>
> The **membrane** then does the **selecting** — deciding what actually enters the cell.
>
> **Together:** the wall gives strength without getting in the way, and the membrane gives control. That's how plant roots absorb water and nutrients from soil while still keeping unwanted substances out.

---

Onion peel cells and cheek cells are both placed in 20% sugar solution. Describe and explain the different results.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Onion (plant) cells:** the **outer boundary stays the same**, but the **inner content shrinks** and the space between the inner and outer boundary increases.
> - **Cheek (animal) cells:** the **whole cell shrinks considerably**.
>
> **Why:** both lose water by osmosis. The plant cell's **rigid cell wall holds the outer shape**, so only the membrane and contents pull inward, leaving a visible gap. The animal cell has **nothing rigid**, so the entire cell collapses inward.

---

Onion peel cells look box-shaped and regularly arranged; cheek cells look irregular. What does this tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The regular box shape is direct evidence of the rigid cell wall.**
>
> A wall forces each cell into a fixed shape and makes neighbouring cells stack neatly, like bricks. Cheek cells have **only a flexible membrane**, so they take whatever shape they are squashed into and lie irregularly.

---

What is the cell wall made of, and what does the same substance do in your diet?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Cellulose** — a carbohydrate formed by **many glucose units linked together**.
>
> In our diet, cellulose acts as **roughage**, helping digestion. (We cannot digest it, which is precisely why it works as roughage — it passes through and keeps things moving.)
>
> **Fungi and bacteria also have cell walls**, for protection and structural support.

---

Predict what would happen to a plant cell if its cell wall suddenly became as flexible as a cell membrane.

> [!ANSWER]
> The cell would **lose its fixed shape**, and:
> - in **pure water** it would keep swelling with nothing rigid to resist the pressure, and could **burst**
> - in a **concentrated solution** the whole cell would shrink like an animal cell
> - the plant would **no longer stay upright** — stems and leaves would flop, since a plant's stiffness comes from firm cells pressing against their walls

---

Distinguish prokaryotic from eukaryotic cells on four points.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Characteristic | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic |
> |---|---|---|
> | Nucleus | **no membrane-bound nucleus** — genetic material in a **nucleoid** | **well-defined, membrane-bound nucleus** |
> | Membrane-bound organelles | **absent** | **present** |
> | Typical diameter | **1 to 10 µm** | **10 to 100 µm** |
> | Organism | usually **unicellular** | unicellular **or** multicellular |
>
> **The names say it:** *pro* = primitive + *karyon* = nucleus; *eu* = true + *karyon* = nucleus.

---

In a prokaryotic cell, where do the cellular activities take place, and where is the DNA?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Most cellular activities take place directly in the CYTOPLASM**, because there are no membrane-bound organelles to compartmentalise them.
>
> The DNA is a **single circular molecule associated with specific proteins**, and the region containing it is called the **NUCLEOID** — a nucleus-like region with **no membrane around it**.

---

Why do eukaryotic cells need organelles at all, when prokaryotes manage without them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **So that many life processes can run INDEPENDENTLY and AT THE SAME TIME**, each in its own compartment.
>
> **A cell is like a tiny living factory** — separate departments for building materials, removing waste and producing energy. Separate rooms let incompatible processes (making things, destroying things) go on simultaneously without interfering.
>
> Prokaryotes manage because they are **much smaller and simpler** — 1–10 µm against 10–100 µm — with far less going on at once.

---

Viruses, viroids and prions are not cells. What is each made of?

> [!ANSWER]
> | Agent | Composition |
> |---|---|
> | **Virus** | **genetic material + a protein coat** |
> | **Viroid** | **genetic material only** — no protein coat |
> | **Prion** | **misfolded protein only** — no genetic material at all |
>
> All three are **acellular infectious agents**, too small to be seen under a light microscope. Being non-cellular, they fall outside the cell theory.

---

Describe the structure of the nucleus and explain why the nuclear membrane has pores.

> [!ANSWER]
> The nucleus has a **double-layered nuclear membrane**, a dense round **nucleolus**, and **chromatin/chromosomes**.
>
> **The pores allow the transfer of material between the nucleus and the cytoplasm.**
>
> **Why that is essential:** the instructions live inside the nucleus, but the work — protein synthesis on ribosomes — happens in the cytoplasm. **Ribosomal subunits are synthesised in the nucleolus and must EXIT to the cytoplasm**, where one large and one small subunit assemble into a ribosome. A sealed nucleus would be a library nobody could borrow from.

---

Explain the chain: chromatin → chromosome → DNA → gene.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Chromatin** — in a **non-dividing** cell, the DNA is present as an **entangled mass of thread-like material**.
> - **Chromosome** — when the cell is **about to divide**, chromatin **gets organised into rod-shaped chromosomes**, which is the only time they're visible. Chromosomes are made of **DNA plus specific proteins**.
> - **DNA** (Deoxyribonucleic acid) — the molecule that **contains the genetic information**, carrying characters from parents to the next generation.
> - **Gene** — a **functional segment of DNA**.

---

Why does chromatin condense into chromosomes only when the cell is about to divide?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **loose thread is easy to read from but impossible to share out without tangling.**
>
> During normal life the cell needs its DNA **spread out and accessible** so genes can be used. But at division the DNA must be **moved and divided exactly** between two daughter cells — and for that it has to be **packed into compact, separable rods**.
>
> It's exactly why you coil a long cable before carrying it, then uncoil it to use it.

---

Mature human red blood cells have no nucleus. What is the benefit, and what is the cost?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Benefit:** the absence of a nucleus **provides more space for haemoglobin**, so each cell can **transport more oxygen**.
>
> **Cost:** without a nucleus they **cannot repair themselves or divide**. As a result their **lifespan is short — about 120 days**.
>
> **The trade-off is the lesson:** specialisation buys performance by giving something up.

---

Distinguish rough ER from smooth ER, and explain where each name comes from.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **RER** | **SER** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Appearance | **rough** under an electron microscope | **smooth** |
> | Why | **ribosomes attached** to its surface | **no ribosomes** |
> | Function | **protein synthesis and secretion** (e.g. pancreatic gland cells) | **synthesis and storage of fats and hormones** |
>
> **The names are literally what you see** — the bumps are the ribosomes. So **rough = ribosomes = proteins**, and the memory aid is built in.

---

Why is the Golgi apparatus called the cell's post office?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it **modifies, sorts and packages** proteins and lipids into **vesicles** for **transport, secretion, or lysosome formation** — exactly what a post office does with parcels.
>
> It is a set of **stacks of flattened, sac-like structures**, functionally linked to the ER, the cell membrane and other organelles, and it sits in the pathway **ER → Golgi → vesicles → destination**.

---

Camillo Golgi's discovery was doubted for decades. What settled the argument, and what general lesson does it carry?

> [!ANSWER]
> He observed a thread-like network in **barn owl nerve cells in 1898** using special staining, but **early microscopes could not resolve it clearly**, so many doubted its existence. **Electron microscope observations confirmed it decades later**, and it was named in his honour.
>
> **The lesson:** the structure was real the whole time — **the instruments, not the claim, were the limiting factor**. It is a neat illustration of why resolution matters, and of how scientific disputes are settled by better evidence rather than by argument.

---

What do lysosomes contain, what do they do, and what happens to the products?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Single membrane-bound sacs filled with ENZYMES** that break down unwanted **proteins, carbohydrates, fats — and even damaged, worn-out parts of the cell**, keeping it clean and healthy.
>
> **The breakdown products are released into the cytoplasm, where they may be REUSED** in other cellular processes.
>
> **Why the membrane matters:** those enzymes would digest the cell itself if they were loose in the cytoplasm. Keeping them in a sealed sac is what makes them safe.

---

Human sperm cells carry lysosomal enzymes. What are they for?

> [!ANSWER]
> When a sperm meets an egg, these enzymes **break down the outer layer of the egg**, allowing **fertilisation** to take place.
>
> It's the same "break things down" function as in an ordinary cell, put to a completely different use.

---

Describe the structure of a mitochondrion and explain why the inner membrane is folded.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each mitochondrion has **two membranes**:
> - the **outer membrane** is **smooth and porous**
> - the **inner membrane** is folded into finger-like projections called **CRISTAE**
>
> **Why folded:** the cristae **increase the SURFACE AREA** available for the chemical reactions of energy production — so much more energy output fits inside the same volume. A flat inner membrane would offer only a fraction of the working area.

---

Why does a cell have many small mitochondria rather than one giant one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of the surface-area-to-volume relationship.**
>
> Energy production happens **on membrane surfaces**. When you make an object bigger, its **volume grows much faster than its surface area** — so one giant mitochondrion would have far **less total membrane area** than many small ones adding up to the same volume.
>
> **Many small units = far more working surface**, plus energy is produced **close to wherever it is needed** in the cell rather than all in one spot.

---

What is ATP, and why is it called the cell's energy currency?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Adenosine Triphosphate.** In mitochondria, glucose and other molecules are broken down during **cellular respiration**, and the **energy released is stored as ATP**.
>
> **"Currency"** because it is the **single common form of energy that almost every cellular activity accepts** — just as money lets you convert work in one place into goods in another. Food energy is deposited as ATP, and any process that needs energy spends ATP.

---

Name the three kinds of plastid and what each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Plastid | Pigment | Job |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Chloroplast** | **chlorophyll** (green) | absorbs sunlight for **photosynthesis** |
> | **Chromoplast** (*chroma* = colour) | yellow, orange, red | gives **bright colour** to flowers and fruits |
> | **Leucoplast** (*leukos* = white) | **none — colourless** | **stores** starch, oils or proteins |
>
> Leucoplasts are classified by what they store — those in **potato and taro (Colocasia)** store starch.

---

Rohit says roots have no plastids because they are underground and don't photosynthesise. Renu says all parts have plastids. Who is right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Renu is right.**
>
> **Rohit's mistake is assuming plastid = chloroplast.** Roots have few or no **chloroplasts** — correct, since there's no light. But they are full of **LEUCOPLASTS**, the colourless plastids that **store starch, oils and proteins**. A potato tuber is essentially a warehouse of starch-storing leucoplasts.
>
> **Photosynthesis is only one plastid job; storage is another.**

---

Do white flowers contain any pigment? Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Generally no pigment is what makes them white** — the petals contain **leucoplasts (colourless plastids)** rather than pigmented chromoplasts, and the white appearance comes from **light being reflected and scattered** by the tissue, not from a white pigment.
>
> **Why colour exists at all:** pigments in chromoplasts advertise to **pollinators**. White works as its own signal — it is highly visible at dusk and to night-flying pollinators like moths.

---

How are mitochondria and chloroplasts similar, and how do they differ?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Similar:**
> - both are **double-membrane-bound** organelles
> - both have their **own DNA and ribosomes**, so both can make some of their own proteins
> - both are involved in **energy** handling
>
> **Different:**
> - **mitochondria BREAK DOWN glucose to release energy** (cellular respiration → ATP); **chloroplasts BUILD glucose using light energy** (photosynthesis)
> - mitochondria are in **plant and animal cells**; chloroplasts only in **plant cells** (and other photosynthesisers)
> - mitochondria have **cristae**; chloroplasts have **stroma with disc-shaped membrane structures** holding chlorophyll

---

Mitochondria and plastids both have their own DNA and ribosomes. What does this suggest about their history?

> [!ANSWER]
> That they **share an evolutionary history with single-celled organisms** — those are features found in **certain bacteria**, not in other organelles.
>
> **The reasoning:** an organelle built by the cell would have no need of its own genetic machinery. The fact that these two carry a complete private set of DNA plus ribosomes is exactly what you would expect if they were **once free-living cells that came to live inside another cell**.

---

Why does a plant wilt when it is not watered? Answer at the level of the cell.

> [!ANSWER]
> A mature plant cell has **one large central vacuole** filled with **cell sap**, storing water, minerals, sugars and waste.
>
> By storing large amounts of water, **the vacuole maintains PRESSURE inside the cell, which keeps the cell firm.**
>
> Without enough water: **the vacuole loses water → cells become less firm → the plant wilts.** The plant's stiffness was never in the wood — in soft tissue it comes from firm cells pressing outward against their walls.

---

When your body grows, is it mainly because cells get bigger or because they divide? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because they DIVIDE.**
>
> **Cells can grow only up to a certain size.** Beyond that, the **surface area cannot keep up with the volume** it must supply with materials and clear of waste — the membrane simply cannot service the interior.
>
> So growth happens through **cell division forming new cells**, not by inflating existing ones. Every day an estimated **hundreds of billions of our cells** — about **1% of the total** — are replaced.

---

In the onion root tip experiment, why use the root TIP, and why squash the material under the coverslip?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The tip** is where the root is actively growing, so its cells **divide continuously** — it is where you are guaranteed to find cells in the middle of division. Cells further up have stopped dividing.
>
> **Squashing** spreads the cells out into a **single thin layer** you can see through. In a thick clump the cells overlap and nothing is distinguishable.
>
> **What you see:** cells at **many different stages of division at once**, which is exactly what lets you work out the sequence.

---

In the onion root tip slide, the cells look structurally different from one another. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because they are **caught at different stages of cell division**.
>
> The root tip divides continuously, so at any moment some cells are just beginning, some are in the middle of separating their chromosomes, and some are almost finished. **A single slide is a snapshot of the whole process.**

---

Compare mitosis and meiosis on number of daughter cells, chromosome number, genetic identity and purpose.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **Mitosis** | **Meiosis** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Daughter cells | **2** | **4** |
> | Chromosome number | **same** as parent | **half** the parent's |
> | Genetically | **identical** to parent | **varied** |
> | Where | body cells generally | **only reproductive organs** |
> | Purpose | **growth, repair, maintenance, asexual reproduction** | **sexual reproduction, genetic diversity** |
>
> **Meiosis is a two-step process:** the first division halves the chromosome number giving two cells; the second is **similar to mitosis**, splitting each into two — four cells in all.

---

Why must gametes have half the number of chromosomes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because fertilisation combines two gametes.**
>
> If each gamete carried the **full** number, the offspring would have **double**, and the count would **double again every generation** — an impossibility.
>
> Halving in meiosis means that when two gametes combine, **the original chromosome number is exactly restored.**

---

Why do children resemble their parents but are not identical to them?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **gametes are produced by MEIOSIS**, which **creates variation**.
>
> Each gamete receives a **different half** of the parent's genetic material, and a child is formed from **one gamete from each of two different individuals**. So the child's combination has never existed before.
>
> **Resemblance** comes from the material being inherited from the parents; **difference** comes from it being a fresh combination.

---

Where does meiosis occur in humans and in flowering plants?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Humans (and other animals):** only in the **testes of males** (producing sperm) and the **ovaries of females** (producing eggs).
> - **Plants:** in the **anthers** (male parts), forming pollen grains that later produce sperm cells, and in the **ovaries** (female parts), producing egg cells.
>
> **The pattern is the same in both:** meiosis is confined to the **reproductive organs**, because only gametes need halved chromosomes.

---

If skin cells started dividing by meiosis instead of mitosis, what would happen to a cut?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It would not heal properly.**
>
> Meiosis would produce cells with **half the chromosome number** and **not genetically identical** to the originals. Such cells could not function as normal skin cells, and having lost half their genetic instructions, many would not survive or work at all.
>
> **Healing needs exact replacements — which is precisely what mitosis produces** and meiosis does not.

---

What would happen if gametes were formed by mitosis instead of meiosis?

> [!ANSWER]
> Gametes would carry the **full** chromosome number. At fertilisation the offspring would have **twice** the normal number, and the count would **double in every generation** — quickly becoming unviable.
>
> There would also be **no genetic variation** from the halving and recombining, so offspring would be far more uniform.

---

State the three points of the classical cell theory and name who contributed each.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **All living organisms are made up of one or more cells.** — **Matthias Schleiden (1838)** showed all **plants** are made of cells; **Theodor Schwann (1839)** showed all **animals** are too.
> 2. **The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living beings.**
> 3. **All cells arise from pre-existing cells.** — **Rudolf Virchow (1855)**.
>
> **Why it unifies biology:** one statement covers **everything from bacteria to humans**, and point 3 explains **life's continuity** — every cell alive today traces back through an unbroken chain of divisions.

---

What is contact inhibition, and how is it related to cancer?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In many animal cells, cell division stops when cells come into contact with neighbouring cells.** That is **contact inhibition** — the body's natural brake on excess growth.
>
> **Cancer cells lose this control** and keep dividing uncontrollably, leading to **tumours**.
>
> **Plant cells do not show contact inhibition** at all — their **rigid cell walls** mean they follow a different pattern of growth, so plants can develop growths but not cancer in the animal sense.

---

Normal cells eventually die. Why is that a good thing?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it keeps the body **in balance**. Cells grow and divide in a controlled way, stay in the right place, do their job and **die when no longer needed**, and dead cells are replaced by new ones with the same function.
>
> **If cells do NOT die when they should → uncontrolled growth and tumours. If they die TOO EARLY → tissues are lost faster than they can be replaced.** Both cause problems.

Programmed Cell Death shapes your fingers. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **PCD is a genetically regulated, organised process of selective cell destruction**, essential for normal development, cellular quality control and immune function.
>
> **When an embryo develops, the hand first forms as a paddle. PCD then eliminates the cells BETWEEN the digits — and that is what separates the fingers. Without it we would have webbed hands.**
>
> **The point:** here cell death is not damage at all — it is **sculpture**. The shape is made by removing material, like carving.

What is totipotency, who proposed it, and what technology grew out of the idea?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Totipotency: the ability of any living plant cell — even a fully mature cell from a permanent tissue — to develop into a COMPLETE PLANT, given suitable nutrients and favourable conditions.**
>
> Proposed by the Austrian botanist **Gottlieb Haberlandt in 1902**, who suggested plant cells can form different types of cells and change them.
>
> His idea laid the foundation for **Plant Tissue Culture Technology**.

What is cell culture, what conditions must be maintained, and why does it matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Growing plant or animal cells OUTSIDE the body**, by placing cells from an organism in a **nutrient-rich medium** that lets them grow and multiply.
>
> **Conditions to control:** the right **temperature**, the right **acidic or alkaline conditions**, **moisture**, and **sterile conditions** throughout.
>
> **Why sterility matters most:** a nutrient-rich medium is just as good for bacteria and fungi as for your cells, and they grow faster — one contaminant ruins the culture.
>
> **Uses:** studying how cells work, and producing **biochemicals, food, medicines and vaccines**.

In 2010 Craig Venter's team inserted chemically synthesised DNA into a bacterium and it grew. What did this prove, and what did it NOT prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **What it proved: DNA controls the structure and activities of a cell.** They studied the complete DNA sequence of *Mycoplasma mycoides*, synthesised an exact chemical copy, removed the DNA from a closely related bacterium while keeping the cytoplasm and membrane intact, and inserted the synthetic DNA — and **the cell grew and divided following the new instructions**.
>
> **What it did NOT prove:** that a cell can be created from scratch. **Only the DNA was synthetic**; every other part of the cell came from an **already existing living cell**.

A student says "salt preserves pickles because it kills bacteria with its taste". Give the correct scientific explanation.

> [!ANSWER]
> **It's osmosis, not taste.**
>
> High concentrations of **salt or sugar** make the surrounding liquid strongly **HYPERTONIC** to the cells of spoilage-causing bacteria and fungi. **Water is drawn OUT of those microbial cells by osmosis**, and they shrink and cannot grow or multiply.
>
> **That is why pickles, murabbas and sharbat keep** — a farmer adding salt, sugar or jaggery to amla and lemons is applying the same principle you saw in the potato experiment, to extend shelf life and prevent post-harvest waste.

Why does a carrot left in plain water stay stiff and crunchy, while one in concentrated salt solution becomes rubbery and limp?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **In plain water** the surroundings are **hypotonic**, so water moves **into** the carrot cells by osmosis. Their **vacuoles fill and press against the rigid cell walls**, keeping the cells firm — and the carrot **stiff and crunchy**.
> - **In salt solution** the surroundings are **hypertonic**, so water moves **out**. The vacuoles shrink, the cells lose their internal pressure, and the carrot goes **limp and rubbery**.
>
> **Crunchiness IS cell pressure** — that is why the same vegetable can be crisp or floppy without any of its material being lost or gained except water.

Two potato cups are made, one from a raw potato and one from a boiled potato. Sugar is put in both and both stand in water. Water gathers in the raw one but not the boiled one. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because boiling kills the cells and destroys their membranes.**
>
> In the **raw** potato cup, the living cells have **intact selectively permeable membranes**. The sugar makes the inside of the cup hypertonic, so **water is drawn from the beaker through the potato tissue into the hollow** — it collects.
>
> In the **boiled** potato, the membranes are **no longer selectively permeable**, so there is **no osmosis** — nothing drives water in a particular direction, and none gathers.
>
> **The boiled cup is the control that proves the effect needs LIVING cells.** An empty cup (no sugar) is the control that proves it needs the **concentration difference**.

Predict the outcome if all the mitochondria were removed from a eukaryotic cell.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The cell would lose its main source of usable energy and would die.**
>
> Mitochondria are where **glucose and other molecules are broken down during cellular respiration to release energy stored as ATP**. Without them there is no ATP supply for **most cellular activities** — transport across membranes, synthesis of new molecules, division, movement.
>
> The cell might survive briefly on stored ATP, but nothing would replace it.

Which cell organelles help synthesise the cell membrane, and by what route do the materials reach it?

> [!ANSWER]
> The membrane is made of **proteins and lipids**:
> - **proteins** — synthesised on **ribosomes**, mostly those attached to the **Rough ER**
> - **lipids** — synthesised by the **Smooth ER**
>
> **The route:**
> ```
> RER (proteins) and SER (lipids)  →  Golgi apparatus (modify, sort, package)
>                                  →  vesicles  →  cell membrane
> ```
> **Why the Golgi is in the middle:** raw proteins and lipids need **modifying, sorting and packaging** before they are sent to the right destination — that is exactly the Golgi's job.

Say whether each is present or absent in a bacterial cell and in an animal cell: chromosome, nucleus, mitochondria, Golgi complex, chromoplast.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Structure | Bacterial cell | Animal cell |
> |---|---|---|
> | Chromosome (genetic material) | **present** — a single circular DNA molecule in the nucleoid | **present** |
> | Nucleus (membrane-bound) | **absent** | **present** |
> | Mitochondria | **absent** | **present** |
> | Golgi complex | **absent** | **present** |
> | Chromoplasts | **absent** | **absent** — plastids are found in plant cells |
>
> **The pattern:** bacteria lack **all membrane-bound** structures; animals lack only the **plant-specific** ones (cell wall, plastids, large central vacuole).

Which of these pairs both contain DNA — (a) chloroplasts and ribosomes, (b) mitochondria and nucleus, (c) Golgi bodies and ribosomes, (d) nucleus and lysosomes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(b) mitochondria and nucleus.**
>
> The **nucleus** holds the cell's chromosomes; **mitochondria** have their own DNA (as do **chloroplasts**) — which is the evidence for their bacterial ancestry.
>
> **Ribosomes, Golgi bodies and lysosomes contain no DNA** — ribosomes are made of RNA and protein, and the other two are membrane sacs.

---

Which is wrongly matched: (a) ribosome — protein synthesis, (b) SER — lipid and cellulose synthesis, (c) lysosome — digestion of foreign agents?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(b) is wrong.**
>
> The **SER does synthesise and store fats (lipids) and hormones** — that part is right. But **cellulose is not made by the SER**; it is the material of the **plant cell wall**, made at the cell surface.
>
> (a) and (c) are both correct: ribosomes are the sites of **protein synthesis**, and lysosomes contain enzymes that **break down unwanted material and damaged parts**.

A researcher puts one carrot in plain water and one in concentrated salt solution for 24 hours. What hypothesis is she testing, and how could the experiment be improved?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Hypothesis:** that **water moves in or out of plant cells by osmosis depending on the concentration of the surrounding solution**, and this changes the carrot's firmness and weight.
>
> **Improvements:**
> - **weigh both carrots before and after** — turn "looks limp" into a **number**
> - use carrots of **equal size and mass** to start, so the comparison is fair
> - keep both at the **same temperature** for the **same time**
> - **repeat with several carrots**, since one of anything could be unusual
> - add **more concentrations** to see how the effect varies, not just two extremes
