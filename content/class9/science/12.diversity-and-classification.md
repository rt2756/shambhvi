## Biodiversity

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **BIODIVERSITY is the immense variety of living organisms** — from microscopic organisms invisible to the naked eye to giant trees, from glowing jellyfish to soaring eagles, from the snow-clad Himalayas to the coral reefs of the Andaman Sea.

**Every organism plays a role in keeping nature stable and functioning:**
- **microscopic algae in the oceans release most of the oxygen we breathe**
- **fungi and bacteria decompose fallen leaves**, converting waste into manure and making soil fertile
- **birds, bees and bats pollinate flowers**
- **plants capture sunlight** to prepare food supporting nearly all life

**Humans depend on biodiversity for food, shelter, medicines and livelihoods.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why farmers have always conserved diverse crop varieties:** varieties with **drought tolerance, pest resistance, and the ability to grow in nutrient-poor soils** mean that **diversity REDUCES THE RISK OF CROP FAILURE and strengthens food security.**
>
> **A field of one variety is a field with one weakness.** If that variety fails, everything fails together.

## 12.1 India as a biodiversity hotspot

**India's natural landscape is diverse** — mountains in the north, desert in the west, rainforests in the North East, plateaus in the south, and long coastlines along the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal — **each region with distinct soils, temperature and rainfall.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ENDEMIC SPECIES are restricted to a particular region and found naturally nowhere else.**
>
> Indian examples: **Nilgiri tahr, Lion-tailed macaque, the Indian pitcher plant *Nepenthes khasiana*, and Neelakurinji.**
>
> **BIODIVERSITY HOTSPOTS are regions that support a large number of ENDEMIC species AND have undergone significant HABITAT LOSS.**

**Global hotspots including parts of India: the Western Ghats, Indo-Burma (including North East India), the Himalayas, and Sundaland (including the Nicobar Islands).**

> [!TIP]
> **Note the two conditions in the definition.** Many endemic species alone is not enough, and habitat loss alone is not enough. **A hotspot is where irreplaceable species are also in danger** — which is exactly why these areas are priorities for conservation.

## 12.2 How biodiversity evolved

**The biodiversity we see today was not always the same.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Small differences among individuals affected their chances of SURVIVAL and REPRODUCTION**, helping them adapt to changed conditions. **These differences accumulated over many generations and gave rise to new forms of life.**
>
> **Today's diversity is the outcome of continuous change over a vast span of time**, shaped by interactions between organisms and their surroundings.

> [!NOTE]
> **Ancient Indian traditions** such as the **Sangam Tinai classification of landscapes** and the **protection of sacred groves** show a sophisticated understanding of landscapes and their living communities. **These customs effectively preserved locally diverse habitats**, aligning with modern ecological principles even though they were never stated as a formal theory.

## 12.3 How to classify organisms

**Try grouping the animals of an ecosystem by different criteria — where they live, when they are active, what they eat — and something important appears:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The SAME organism can fit into DIFFERENT groups depending on the criterion you choose.**
>
> **So which features should be used?** That question is what leads to classification as a **systematic** discipline rather than a matter of convenience.

### 12.3.1 Criteria used

**Scientists look at broad, easily visible features first, then at more detailed ones:**

| Criterion | What is examined |
|---|---|
| **External features** | shape, size, body organisation |
| **Mode of nutrition** | **autotrophic** or **heterotrophic** |
| **Internal structures** | skeletal patterns, presence or absence of organs and tissue types |
| **Cell structure** | unicellular or multicellular; **prokaryote or eukaryote**; cell wall present or absent |
| **Ecological role** | producer, consumer or decomposer |
| **Reproduction** | asexual and/or sexual methods |
| **Genetic similarity** | similarities in inherited features, studied in detail using **DNA** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SIMILAR FEATURES IN ORGANISMS SUGGEST THAT THEY EVOLVED FROM COMMON ANCESTORS.**
>
> **This is why classification is more than filing.** A good classification does not just sort organisms — it reveals **how they are RELATED**, which is a claim about history, not just appearance.

## 12.4 The need for classification

> [!TIP]
> **Imagine a huge library with thousands of books scattered on the floor.** Finding *On the Origin of Species* would be nearly impossible. **Without arranging them by subject, author or section, no book can be found.**
>
> **The Earth holds millions of organisms.** Classifying them systematically is what makes them possible to study at all.

**What classification does for us:**
1. Makes the study of living organisms **organised and systematic**
2. Helps us understand the **similarities and differences** among living beings
3. Helps us understand **how organisms are RELATED** and how they interact
4. Helps in **identifying and naming newly discovered organisms**
5. Supports **biodiversity conservation** by identifying species under threat of extinction
6. Lets scientists **worldwide discuss organisms using a common system**

> [!NOTE]
> **Pakke Tiger Reserve, Arunachal Pradesh** has nearly **300 bird species** — striking, since India as a whole has about **1300**. It supports **four species of hornbill**: Rufous-necked, Oriental Pied, Great and Wreathed.
>
> **These birds nest only in large, OLD trees with suitable cavities** and feed on specific fruits, so different species occupy different parts of the forest depending on tree size and fruit availability.
>
> **Which is why the loss of large old trees would be catastrophic for them** — no other tree will do, and a large old tree cannot be replaced quickly.

## 12.5 Classification systems over time

| Who | When | System |
|---|---|---|
| **Aristotle** | 4th century BCE | grouped animals by **habitat — land, water, air** — and by external appearance |
| **Carolus Linnaeus** | 1758 | **TWO kingdoms: Plantae and Animalia** |
| **Ernst Haeckel** | 1866 | **THREE kingdoms**: added **Protista** for microscopic life |
| **Herbert F. Copeland** | 1938 | **FOUR kingdoms**: added **Monera** for bacteria |
| **Robert H. Whittaker** | 1969 | **FIVE kingdoms**: added **Fungi** |

**The two-kingdom system:**
- **Plantae** — do not move from place to place, **synthesise their own food**
- **Animalia** — move from place to place, **depend on other organisms for food**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Each new system was forced by organisms the previous one could not place.**
>
> - **Amoeba and Paramecium** move like animals but are **unicellular and heterotrophic**, while plants and animals are **multicellular** → **Protista** was added.
> - **Amoeba has a true (membrane-bound) nucleus; bacteria do not.** Both are unicellular but fundamentally different → **Monera** was separated out.
> - **Fungi such as mushrooms do not move like plants, but are HETEROTROPHIC**, obtaining nutrients by **absorption** — many from dead and decaying matter, some symbiotic, some parasitic → **Fungi** became a kingdom.
>
> **This is the same pattern as the atomic models in Chapter 8:** every revision was driven by evidence the earlier picture could not accommodate. **Aristotle's system worked for his time; microscopes and staining techniques revealed a world it could not describe.**

## 12.6 The five kingdom classification

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The four criteria that separate the five kingdoms:**
> 1. **CELL TYPE** — prokaryote or eukaryote
> 2. **LEVEL OF ORGANISATION** — unicellular or multicellular
> 3. **CELL STRUCTURE** — cell wall present or absent, and what it is made of
> 4. **MODE OF NUTRITION** — autotrophic or heterotrophic

```
                        CELL TYPE
   prokaryote ─────────────────────────────── eukaryote
       │                                          │
    MONERA                             LEVEL OF ORGANISATION
  bacteria, archaea,           unicellular ──────────── multicellular
   cyanobacteria                    │                        │
                                 PROTISTA              CELL STRUCTURE
                          amoeba, paramecium,   with cell wall ─── without
                          chlamydomonas, euglena       │              │
                                              chitin ── cellulose   ANIMALIA
                                                │          │
                                              FUNGI     PLANTAE
                                          heterotrophic  autotrophic
                                          DECOMPOSERS    PRODUCERS
```

### 12.6.1 Monera — unicellular prokaryotes

**Bacteria, archaea and cyanobacteria — single-celled PROKARYOTES.**

**Bacteria are found everywhere** — soil, water, air, **hot springs and other extreme environments where most organisms cannot survive** — and even inside human bodies. They live in the **gut of ruminants** and are responsible for **producing biogas from their dung**.

**Some are harmful (pathogens) and cause disease, but many are useful — Lactobacillus and Rhizobium.** Some **break down pollutants** like oil, pesticides and sewage. **Cyanobacteria are autotrophs and decomposers.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) were among the FIRST organisms to produce oxygen by photosynthesis.** About **2.5 billion years ago oxygen accumulated in the atmosphere, making the Earth suitable for other forms of life.**
>
> Fossils of ancient cyanobacteria are found in structures called **STROMATOLITES** — some found in **Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh** — providing **some of the earliest evidence of life on Earth**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Ram Bux Singh, the "Father of Modern Biogas"**, established India's first scientifically designed **biogas plant at Ramnagar, Sitapur, Uttar Pradesh, in 1957**. He developed **low-cost, efficient biogas plants for rural areas** and helped set them up in several countries — promoting renewable energy, waste management and sustainable development.

### 12.6.2 Protista — unicellular eukaryotes

**All single-celled EUKARYOTES, either without a cell wall or with one made of cellulose.** Microscopic and highly diverse, living in water or moist places. **Amoeba, Paramecium, Chlamydomonas, Euglena.**

**Some are autotrophic, others heterotrophic.** They are **an important link in aquatic food chains** — some produce oxygen, others serve as food for small animals, and some act as **decomposers** helping in nutrient cycling.

> [!TIP]
> **The hay infusion** is the standard way to see them: pack a bottle one-quarter full with mown grass or straw, fill with stagnant or pond water, cover with muslin, and leave **undisturbed for a week**. A drop under a microscope will be full of moving organisms.
>
> **Safety: the infusion smells bad.** Wear a lab coat, mask and gloves, and **discard it after autoclaving**.

### 12.6.3 Fungi — heterotrophic eukaryotes with a chitin cell wall

**Mostly multicellular eukaryotes with cell walls made of CHITIN. They do NOT make their own food.**

**They absorb nutrients through fine filaments, which together form a network called the MYCELIUM.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Most fungi are SAPROPHYTES — they feed on DEAD organic matter** (fallen leaves, twigs, dead organisms), **breaking down complex organic matter into simpler substances** and releasing them into the soil as minerals.
>
> **This makes them crucial DECOMPOSERS. Without them, the decay of dead plants and animals would be greatly reduced, damaging soil fertility and ecological balance.**

**Some fungi are MUTUALISTIC (symbiotic) with other organisms; others are PARASITES causing disease in plants and animals.** They **reproduce both sexually and asexually, often by forming spores**, and grow best in **warm, moist conditions**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Yeast is UNICELLULAR — so why is it in Fungi and not Protista?**
>
> **Because its cell wall is made of CHITIN.** Cell wall composition outranks the unicellular/multicellular criterion here.
>
> **A useful reminder that classification criteria are applied in a definite order, not all at once.**

**Mushrooms are macroscopic fungi reproducing by spores. Aspergillus and Penicillium are used to make ENZYMES and ANTIBIOTICS.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Wild edible mushrooms** are valuable food with high nutritional and medicinal value, used and conserved by many communities in India, including tribal communities with **traditional knowledge (folk taxonomy) of which are edible and which poisonous**.
>
> **Mushroom farming** is becoming a promising livelihood — **minimal space, low investment, and a fast life cycle of 30–45 days**.

### 12.6.4 Plantae — multicellular autotrophic eukaryotes with a cellulose cell wall

**Multicellular autotrophs performing photosynthesis, with a rigid cell wall of CELLULOSE for support and protection.** They **form the base of most food chains and release the oxygen essential for life.**

**Five classes:**

#### Thallophyta (algae) — the simplest plants

***thallos* = undifferentiated body, *phyton* = plant.** Mostly in **water or moist environments**. They form a **THALLUS** — a simple body allowing **direct exchange of gases, nutrients and water with the surroundings**. Example: **Spirogyra**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why a thallus works only in water:** with no roots, no transport tissue and no waterproof covering, every cell must be **in contact with its supply**. Surrounded by water, that is automatic. On land it would be impossible.

#### Bryophyta — the first steps onto land

***bryon* = moss, *phyton* = plant.** **Mosses and liverworts** — the green mats on damp rocks, old walls and soil, especially during the monsoon. Examples: **Marchantia and moss**.

**More differentiated than thallophytes:** they have **root-like RHIZOIDS**, and may have **stem-like and leaf-like simple structures** — but **no true roots, stems or leaves, and NO vascular tissue**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Bryophytes REQUIRE WATER FOR REPRODUCTION** — the male reproductive cells must **swim** to reach the female cells.
>
> **That is why they are called the "AMPHIBIANS of the plant kingdom":** they live on land but cannot reproduce away from moisture. They show how plants began to colonise land **while remaining dependent on water**.

#### Pteridophyta — vascular tissue arrives

***pteron* = feather, *phyton* = plant.** **Ferns** possess **TRUE roots, stems and leaves**.

**Cut a fern stem and look at the cross section: you see VASCULAR TISSUES — XYLEM and PHLOEM — transporting water and food throughout the plant.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why vascular tissue is the breakthrough:** it means a plant **no longer needs every cell to touch its water supply**. Water can be lifted from roots to the top, and food carried down. **Only now can a plant grow TALL.**
>
> **But they still depend on water for REPRODUCTION, and they do NOT produce seeds.**

#### Gymnosperms — reproduction without water

***gymnos* = naked, *spermos* = seed.** **Pines and cycads**, well adapted to **cold and dry** regions.

- **Needle-like or scale-like leaves REDUCE WATER LOSS** and withstand harsh conditions
- **They produce SEEDS**, which **protect the developing embryo and contain stored food**
- **They do NOT require water for fertilisation**
- **Their seeds are NOT enclosed in fruits** and are often **exposed on cones**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The seed is the second great breakthrough.** It carries **protection and a packed lunch**, so the embryo can survive a journey and a wait. **Together with water-free fertilisation, this finally frees plants from dependence on moisture.**

#### Angiosperms — flowers and fruits

***angeion* = vessel, *spermos* = seed.** **Flowering plants — the most complex body organisation and the MOST DIVERSE plant group on Earth.**

- **FLOWERS attract pollinators, increasing the EFFICIENCY of reproduction**
- **FRUITS help spread seeds to new locations**

**These features allow angiosperms to occupy a very wide range of environments.**

> [!TIP]
> **Read the five classes as a sequence of solutions to one problem — living on land:**
> ```
> thallophyta    no support, no transport, no protection   → water only
> bryophyta      slight differentiation, rhizoids           → damp land, water to reproduce
> pteridophyta   TRUE organs + VASCULAR TISSUE             → can grow tall, still needs water
> gymnosperms    SEEDS + no water needed for fertilisation → dry and cold regions
> angiosperms    FLOWERS + FRUITS                          → almost everywhere
> ```
> **Each group solves a problem the last one could not — but each new solution brings new costs.** Angiosperm reproduction depends on pollinating agents, and needs a complex tissue system to support it.

> [!NOTE]
> **Lichens** — the white-green patches on tree trunks, damp walls and stones — are **SYMBIOTIC**: an **autotrophic ALGA** and a **heterotrophic FUNGUS** living together. **The fungus provides protection; the alga photosynthesises and provides food.**
>
> **They are natural BIOINDICATORS of air quality** — they change colour with air pollutants, so their colour reveals pollutant concentration. Villagers collect some as forest produce: certain kinds, called **patthar ke phool**, are used as a spice, others as medicine, and some for **dyes** giving maroon, violet or burgundy to wool and silk. **Some lichens are poisonous, so correct identification matters.**

> [!NOTE]
> ***Hortus Malabaricus***, one of the earliest scientific books on Indian plants, was compiled in the **17th century by Hendrik van Rheede with the help of Itty Achudan** — an Indian herbalist, botanist and physician — and other local experts. It describes hundreds of species and their medicinal uses, **showing how traditional knowledge and science can work together**.

### 12.6.5 Animalia — multicellular heterotrophic eukaryotes

**Multicellular heterotrophs depending on other organisms for food. Most show LOCOMOTION, RAPID RESPONSE TO STIMULI and COORDINATED BEHAVIOUR** — allowing them to actively search for food, avoid predators, and interact dynamically with their surroundings.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The major criterion for classifying animals is the presence or absence of a NOTOCHORD** — a flexible rod-shaped structure.
> ```
> NON-CHORDATA (invertebrates)  — no notochord
> CHORDATA                       — notochord present
>      PROTOCHORDATA  — notochord at least once in life
>      VERTEBRATA     — the notochord's place taken by a VERTEBRAL COLUMN
> ```

### The invertebrate phyla

| Phylum | Habitat | Level of organisation | Skeleton | Key advance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PORIFERA** (sponges) | marine water | **CELLULAR** | none | multicellularity, but **no tissues** |
| **CNIDARIA** (hydra, jellyfish, corals) | fresh and marine water | **TISSUE** | none | **true tissues**, tentacles to capture prey |
| **PLATYHELMINTHES** (flatworms) | water / inside host | **ORGAN** | none | **BILATERAL SYMMETRY** |
| **NEMATODA** (roundworms) | soil, water, inside host | **ORGAN SYSTEM** | none | **TWO openings** — mouth and anus |
| **ANNELIDA** (earthworms) | moist soil, water | organ system | none | **SEGMENTATION** and a **body cavity** |
| **ARTHROPODA** (insects, crabs, spiders) | land, water | organ system | **EXOSKELETON** | **jointed appendages** + hard external skeleton |
| **MOLLUSCA** (snails, squids, octopuses) | water, moist land | organ system | **exoskeleton** (shell) | soft body with head, muscular foot and hump |
| **ECHINODERMATA** (starfish, sea urchins) | marine water | organ system | **ENDOSKELETON** | **internal** skeleton of calcium carbonate |

**Reading the sequence:**

- **PORIFERA** — **numerous pores** let water flow continuously through the body, bringing **food particles and oxygen directly to individual cells** and carrying waste away. **Fixed in one place.** They cannot survive on land, because the whole design depends on water flowing through them.
- **CNIDARIA** — **tissue-level organisation** lets specialised cells do specific jobs. **Tentacles CAPTURE prey**, instead of waiting for currents to bring food. **But there is a SINGLE opening for both food intake and waste removal** — you cannot eat and excrete efficiently at the same time.
- **PLATYHELMINTHES** — **BILATERAL SYMMETRY** means the body can be divided into two halves along one plane, with **distinct head–tail and front–back regions**. This allows **better coordination of movement — the beginning of directional activity**. The **flattened body permits gas diffusion without respiratory organs**, but there is **still only one opening**. Parasitic forms have **hooks and suckers** to attach to host tissues.
- **NEMATODA** — **elongated cylindrical bodies** for efficient movement through soil, water or host tissues, and **TWO openings**, allowing food to pass through in one direction.
- **ANNELIDA** — **cylindrical bodies divided into SEGMENTS**, with **muscles for locomotion** and a **nerve cord** for control and coordination, plus a **body cavity**. **Segmentation allows greater flexibility and more precise control of movement.**
- **ARTHROPODA** — **segmented bodies with different segments specialised for different functions**, and a **hard EXTERNAL SKELETON**. That covering **protects, REDUCES WATER LOSS, and supports powerful muscles** — which is exactly what lets arthropods survive in dry, exposed environments.
- **MOLLUSCA** — soft bodies, often protected by a **shell**; shows how one basic body plan can be modified in many directions depending on environmental demands.
- **ECHINODERMATA** — a **hard INTERNAL skeleton of calcium carbonate**. Although they lack a notochord, this **internal support** gives protection and controlled movement, **showing a gradual shift towards internal skeletal support**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The pattern across the invertebrates: increasing complexity of body organisation, from CELLULAR → TISSUE → ORGAN → ORGAN SYSTEM.**
>
> **New structural features improve feeding, movement and protection — but often introduce new challenges.** An exoskeleton protects, but must be shed to grow. A single opening is simple, but limits digestion.

> [!NOTE]
> **One kilogram of sponge can filter up to 24,000 litres of sea water per day** — an extraordinary output for an animal with no tissues, no organs and no movement.

> [!NOTE]
> **Parasitic worms** cause infectious diseases, entering the body through **contaminated water and food** and living in the **alimentary canal**, taking nutrients from the **host**.
>
> **Which is why:** wash your hands properly, maintain personal hygiene and good sanitary habits, eat properly cooked food, and drink **boiled or filtered water**.

### Protochordates and vertebrates

**PROTOCHORDATES** (such as **Amphioxus**) possess a **notochord at least once during their life** — a structure providing **internal support without restricting movement**. They help us understand **how animals with a notochord may have arisen from simpler forms**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **VERTEBRATES possess a VERTEBRAL COLUMN (backbone)** — an internal skeleton that **supports the body and protects vital organs** such as the brain and spinal cord.
>
> **What that framework makes possible: LARGER body size, efficient movement, complex organ systems, advanced sensory abilities and coordinated behaviour.**

**The five vertebrate groups — fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals — are separated by their HABITAT USE, BODY COVERING and REPRODUCTION.**

## 12.7 Adaptations as outcomes of structural change

| Feature | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Fins and gills** in fish | move and breathe **in water** |
| **Feathers and hollow bones** in birds | make **flight** possible |
| **Fat storage** in camels | survive where food and water are scarce |
| **Thick fur** in polar bears | survive extreme cold |
| **Mammary glands** in mammals | **improve the survival of the young** |

**Animal diversity reflects the wide range of body forms found in organisms living under different environmental conditions.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Biodiverse forests act as barriers against disaster:**
> - **Mangroves and the 1999 Odisha super cyclone** — villages with **more mangroves experienced less destruction**
> - In the **Western Ghats**, forest diversity acts as a **biological barrier against Monkey Fever (Kyasanur Forest Disease)**, because many host animals are ones **inside which the virus cannot replicate**
> - **Diverse soil and root microorganisms absorb, transform or break down pollutants**, improving water quality; **mangrove soils trap sediments and heavy metals**, keeping pollution out of oceans and rivers

## 12.7.1 The hierarchy of classification

```
KINGDOM → PHYLUM → CLASS → ORDER → FAMILY → GENUS → SPECIES
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Classification moves from very BROAD groups towards smaller, more SPECIFIC ones. At each lower level, organisms share MORE features in common, and every lower group is PART of the group above it.**
>
> **It works like an address.** Just as a house address locates a place exactly, classification lets scientists identify, compare and study organisms accurately and understand how they are related.

| Level | **Tiger** | **Pea** |
|---|---|---|
| Kingdom | Animalia | Plantae |
| Phylum | Chordata | Magnoliophyta |
| Sub-phylum | Vertebrata | — |
| Class | Mammalia | Magnoliopsida |
| Order | Carnivora | Fabales |
| Family | Felidae | Fabaceae |
| Genus | **Panthera** | **Pisum** |
| Species | ***P. tigris*** | ***P. sativum*** |

## 12.8 Binomial nomenclature

**A tiger is *bagh* in Hindi, *puli* in Tamil, *tiger* in English, *tigre* in French.** If people from different regions discuss it, **confusion arises**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **BINOMIAL NOMENCLATURE — a universal system introduced by Carolus Linnaeus in the 18th century.** Every organism gets a **two-part scientific name in Latin or a Latinised form**.
>
> - The **FIRST word is the GENUS** — grouping closely related species that share common features
> - The **SECOND word is the SPECIES** — a group of similar individuals **capable of interbreeding and producing offspring**

| Common name | Scientific name |
|---|---|
| Tiger | ***Panthera tigris*** |
| Lion | ***Panthera leo*** |
| Mango | ***Mangifera indica*** |

**Tiger and lion share the genus *Panthera* — the roaring cats, with the ability to roar and a similar skull structure.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE RULES**
> 1. Two parts — **genus** then **species**.
> 2. **The genus name begins with a CAPITAL letter; the species name is entirely in SMALL letters.**
> 3. **Printed in ITALICS; UNDERLINED when handwritten.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Where species names come from.** Sometimes the **place of discovery**, sometimes a **scientist's name**. The **Purple Frog, *Nasikabatrachus sahyadrensis*, from Kerala, is named after the Sahyadri Hills.**
>
> It lives underground most of the year and emerges only in the monsoon to breed, and belongs to an **ancient family of frogs** known from fossils. **Its discovery in 2003** helped scientists understand ancient amphibian groups and highlighted the need for conservation in the Western Ghats.

## Beyond five kingdoms

> [!NOTE]
> **The five kingdom system was more comprehensive than earlier ones, but still could not fully explain the diversity of life.**
>
> **With better microscopes and genetic studies, scientists began comparing organisms at the DNA level. Organisms with similar DNA are considered to have a common ancestry.**
>
> **Based on genetic data, Carl Woese (1977) proposed the THREE DOMAIN system:**
> ```
> BACTERIA    ·    ARCHAEA    ·    EUKARYA
> ```
> **It showed that microscopic life forms are far more diverse than previously believed.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why do classification systems keep changing? Because that is how science works.**
>
> Aristotle's system worked for his time. **New tools — microscopes, staining techniques, DNA sequencing — revealed things his system had no way of describing**, and the classification changed to fit the evidence.
>
> **Biological classification, like science itself, is an ongoing process of reasoning and change** — not a fixed answer.

## 12.9 Fossils as evidence

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **FOSSILS are preserved remains of plants and animals found in layers of rock, sand and mud.**
>
> **Generally, OLDER layers contain SIMPLER organisms, while NEWER layers show MORE COMPLEX forms.**
>
> **They are natural records** showing how life has changed over millions of years — from giant dinosaurs to early humans and ancient plants, many important fossils have been found in India.

> [!NOTE]
> **Birbal Sahni** studied fossil plants and founded the **Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences (BSIP) in Lucknow**, which continues his work on ancient plants and past environments. **His studies linked present-day plants with their ancestors and showed that life on Earth has a long, connected history.**

## 12.10 Biodiversity under threat

**Each species, large or small, plays an important role:** plants produce food and oxygen, animals pollinate flowers and disperse seeds, microorganisms recycle nutrients.

> [!WARNING]
> **Human activities — pollution, deforestation, overuse of resources and climate change — are reducing biodiversity.**
>
> **When one species disappears, others that depend on it may also decline and eventually disappear too.** Losses do not stay isolated; they propagate through the web of dependence.

> [!NOTE]
> **Phumdis — floating grasslands** in **Loktak Lake, Keibul Lamjao National Park, Manipur** — are among the world's unique habitats. These floating beds of soil, **a few centimetres to two metres thick**, carry rooted vegetation rich in organic matter.
>
> They are home to the **Sangai, the dancing deer, endemic to Manipur**. **Declared extinct in 1951, it was rediscovered in 1953** by naturalists working from its distinctive **hooves and elongated patterns** — an identification made possible by careful taxonomy.
>
> **The phumdis are now degenerating and the Sangai is on the IUCN Red List.** Habitat loss will significantly affect its population, and conservation efforts are in progress.

FIVE KINGDOMS
  MONERA    prokaryote, unicellular — bacteria, archaea, cyanobacteria
  PROTISTA  eukaryote, unicellular — amoeba, paramecium, euglena
  FUNGI     eukaryote, cell wall of CHITIN, heterotrophic DECOMPOSERS
  PLANTAE   eukaryote, cell wall of CELLULOSE, autotrophic PRODUCERS
  ANIMALIA  eukaryote, NO cell wall, heterotrophic CONSUMERS

PLANTAE classes — successive solutions to living on land
  thallophyta (thallus, water) → bryophyta (rhizoids, needs water to reproduce)
  → pteridophyta (true organs + XYLEM/PHLOEM) → gymnosperms (SEEDS, no water
  needed) → angiosperms (FLOWERS and FRUITS, most diverse)

ANIMALIA  notochord absent = invertebrate; present = chordata
  porifera (cellular) · cnidaria (tissue) · platyhelminthes (organ, bilateral)
  · nematoda (organ system, two openings) · annelida (segments) ·
  arthropoda (jointed legs, exoskeleton) · mollusca (soft, shell) ·
  echinodermata (endoskeleton)
  protochordata (notochord once) → vertebrata (backbone)
  fish · amphibians · reptiles · birds · mammals
