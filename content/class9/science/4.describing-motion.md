## Why we start with the simplest motions

**Everything in nature is in motion** — from astronomical objects to subatomic particles: flitting butterflies, slithering snakes, tendrils twining around a support, dust dancing in a sunbeam, ocean tides, gathering clouds.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To explore a complex phenomenon, scientists first study it in its IDEALISED, SIMPLIFIED forms** — exactly the modelling idea from Chapter 1.
>
> So we study **linear motion** (in a straight line) and **uniform circular motion**, and build up from there.

## 4.1 Motion in a straight line

**LINEAR MOTION = motion in a straight line — the simplest kind.** Children in a swimming race, a vertically falling ball, a car on a straight highway, a train on a straight track.

### 4.1.1 Describing position

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To describe position you first need a REFERENCE POINT.**
>
> **POSITION = the DISTANCE and the DIRECTION of the object from the reference point, at a given instant of time.**
>
> - **In MOTION:** position with respect to the reference point **changes** with time.
> - **At REST:** position with respect to the reference point **does not change** with time.

**For straight-line motion there are only two directions — forward and backward — so we use signs.** Mark the reference point as the **origin O**; positions to the **right of O are positive (+)** and to the **left negative (–)**.

> [!WARNING]
> **An INSTANT of time and a TIME INTERVAL are different things.**
>
> - **Instant** = a **single clock reading** at one point in time.
> - **Interval** = the **duration between two instants**, i.e. between two clock readings.

### 4.1.2 Distance travelled and displacement

**An athlete on a straight track:** starts at O (t = 0 s), reaches B at 40 m (t = 4 s), continues to A at 100 m (t = 10 s), then runs back to B, arriving at t = 16 s.

```
total distance travelled = OA + AB = 100 m + 60 m = 160 m
displacement             = OB      = 40 m in the positive direction
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **DISPLACEMENT = the NET CHANGE in position between two instants.**
>
> - Its **magnitude** = the **straight-line distance between the two positions**.
> - Its **direction** = from the first position **towards** the second.
>
> **Total distance travelled needs only a numerical value; displacement needs a value AND a direction.** The **SI unit of both is the metre (m)**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The magnitude of displacement is ALWAYS less than or equal to the total distance travelled — never more.**
>
> **Why:** the distance travelled is the whole path walked; the displacement is the shortcut from start to finish. **The shortcut can never be longer than the route.** The two are **equal only when the object never turns back** — i.e. moves in one direction throughout.

**Ball thrown vertically up from O, rising 140 cm to B and falling back to O:**

| Position | Distance travelled from O | Displacement from O |
|---|---|---|
| O (start) | 0 cm | 0 cm |
| A (40 cm up) | 40 cm | 40 cm upward |
| B (top, 140 cm) | 140 cm | 140 cm upward |
| C (80 cm, coming down) | 140 + 60 = **200 cm** | **80 cm upward** |
| O (back at start) | 140 + 140 = **280 cm** | **0 cm** |

> [!TIP]
> **Displacement CAN be zero while distance is large.** Return to where you started and the net change in position is nothing at all — however far you walked.
>
> **This is why fuel used depends on TOTAL DISTANCE, not displacement.** The engine works for every metre of the path; it has no idea where you started.

### 4.1.3 Average speed and average velocity

```
average speed = (total distance travelled) ÷ (time interval)
```

**Speed is calculated from distance, which has no direction — so speed has no direction either, only a numerical value.**

| Motion | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **UNIFORM** motion in a straight line | **equal distances in equal intervals of time**, for all choices of interval → **constant speed** |
| **NON-UNIFORM** motion | **unequal distances in equal intervals** → speed increasing, decreasing, or both |

> [!NOTE]
> **Speed = distance ÷ time is an ancient idea in India**, well established in the **Aryabhatiya (5th century CE)**. This problem is from the **Ganitakaumudi (14th century CE)**:
>
> *Two postmen start walking towards each other from 210 yojanas apart. One covers 9 yojanas a day, the other 5. When do they meet?*
>
> ```
> together they close 9 + 5 = 14 yojanas each day
> 210 ÷ 14 = 15 days
> ```
> **They meet after 15 days** — the first having walked 135 yojanas, the second 75.
>
> **Why adding the speeds works:** what matters is how fast the **gap** shrinks, and both walkers shrink it at once.

**Speed tells you how fast, but nothing about direction. For that we need VELOCITY.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> average velocity = (change in position) ÷ (time interval) = displacement ÷ time interval
>
>            v_av = s / t
> ```
> **The direction of velocity is the same as the direction of displacement**, shown by a **+** or **–** sign.
>
> **SI unit of both average speed and average velocity: metre per second (m/s or m s^-1).** Also commonly km/h.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **RATE OF CHANGE = the ratio of a change in one quantity to the corresponding change in time.**
>
> So **average velocity is the average rate of change of POSITION with time**, and (below) **average acceleration is the average rate of change of VELOCITY with time**.

**Worked — Sarang swims 25 m to the far end of the pool and back in 50 s:**

```
total distance    = 50 m         average speed    = 50 m ÷ 50 s = 1 m/s
displacement      = 0 m          average velocity = 0 m ÷ 50 s  = 0 m/s
```

> [!TIP]
> **He was moving the whole time, yet his average velocity is zero.** Average velocity asks only *"where did you end up compared with where you started, and how long did it take?"* — not how hard you worked.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Speed and the magnitude of velocity are equal only when the object moves in ONE direction** throughout the interval — the same condition that makes distance and displacement equal.

> [!NOTE]
> **Velocity at an instant** (properly, **instantaneous velocity**) is what you get as the time interval around an instant is made smaller and smaller. **A speedometer reads nearly the magnitude of instantaneous velocity**, and the direction the tyres point gives its direction.

### 4.1.4 Average acceleration

**Sit in a vehicle that suddenly starts, or suddenly stops, and you feel a jolt. That jolt is a change of velocity.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> average acceleration = (change in velocity) ÷ (time interval)
>                      = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ (time interval)
>
>                    a = (v − u) / (t2 − t1)
> ```
> **SI unit: m/s² (m s^-2).** Acceleration needs **magnitude AND direction**.

| If the magnitude of velocity is | Acceleration is |
|---|---|
| **increasing** | **in the direction of velocity** (positive sign) |
| **decreasing** | **opposite to the direction of velocity** (negative sign) |

> [!WARNING]
> **An object can be moving very fast and still have ZERO acceleration.**
>
> **Acceleration depends not on how fast you are moving, but on how QUICKLY YOUR VELOCITY IS CHANGING.** A bus cruising down a straight highway at constant 100 km/h has **zero** acceleration.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Acceleration can come from a change in the MAGNITUDE of velocity, a change in its DIRECTION, or both.** Section 4.4 shows a case where the speed never changes and yet the object is accelerating.

**Worked — a bus on a straight highway:**

```
(i) accelerator pressed
    u = 36 km/h = 36 × 1000/3600 = 10 m/s
    v = 54 km/h = 15 m/s,  t = 10 s
    a = (15 − 10) / 10 = 5/10 = +0.5 m/s²      (velocity increasing → a along v)

(ii) brakes pressed
    u = 54 km/h = 15 m/s,  v = 0 m/s,  t = 5 s
    a = (0 − 15) / 5 = −15/5 = −3 m/s²         (minus sign → a opposite to v)
```

> [!TIP]
> **To convert km/h to m/s, multiply by 1000/3600 = 5/18.** To go the other way, multiply by 18/5. **36 km/h = 10 m/s** is worth memorising as a check.

**Worked — an object dropped from a height** (taking downward as positive):

| Interval | Change in velocity | Average acceleration |
|---|---|---|
| 0 s to 1 s | 9.8 − 0 | **9.8 m/s²** |
| 1 s to 2 s | 19.6 − 9.8 | **9.8 m/s²** |
| 2 s to 3 s | 29.4 − 19.6 | **9.8 m/s²** |
| 3 s to 4 s | 39.2 − 29.4 | **9.8 m/s²** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The acceleration is CONSTANT at 9.8 m/s², directed downwards. This is the acceleration due to the gravitational force of the Earth, denoted g.**

> [!WARNING]
> **You may choose the origin and the positive direction however you like — but once chosen, DO NOT change them mid-problem.**
>
> In the thrown-ball table the origin was the ground and up was positive; in the dropped-object table the origin was the release point and **down** was positive. Both are legitimate; mixing them inside one calculation is not.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Constant acceleration in a straight line means the magnitude of velocity increases (or decreases) by EQUAL AMOUNTS in EQUAL intervals of time — for all choices of interval.** This chapter deals only with constant acceleration.

## 4.2 Graphical representation of motion

**A graph gives a VISUAL picture of how position, velocity and acceleration change with time** — useful for comparing two objects, calculating quantities, and telling uniform from non-uniform motion.

> [!WARNING]
> **A graph is NOT a route map.** It does not show the path taken; it shows **how the position changes with time**, measured from the origin. A position–time graph of a person walking north is a straight line — it is not a picture of a road.

### 4.2.1 Plotting

1. Draw two perpendicular lines meeting at the **origin O**: the horizontal **X-axis** and vertical **Y-axis**.
2. Decide what goes on each axis — **time along X**, position (or velocity) along **Y**.
3. Choose a **scale** that uses the available space well, e.g. `5 divisions = 1 s` and `5 divisions = 20 m`.
4. Mark the values, plot each (time, position) pair, and **join the points**.

**Data for a vehicle on a straight road:**

| Time | 0 s | 1 s | 2 s | 3 s | 4 s | 5 s | 6 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Position | 0 m | 20 m | 40 m | 60 m | 80 m | 100 m | 120 m |

**Equal position steps in equal time steps → the points fall on a STRAIGHT LINE.**

**A vehicle starting from rest and speeding up:**

| Time | 0 s | 2 s | 4 s | 6 s | 8 s | 10 s | 12 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Position | 0 m | 1 m | 4 m | 9 m | 16 m | 25 m | 36 m |

**Unequal, growing position steps → the points fall on a CURVE.**

### 4.2.2 Position–time graphs

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Shape of position–time graph | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Straight line, sloping** | equal displacements in equal times → **CONSTANT velocity** |
> | **Curved** | unequal displacements in equal times → **velocity is changing** → **accelerated motion** |
> | **Straight line parallel to the time axis** | position not changing at all → **the object is AT REST** |
> | **Steeper line** | **greater** magnitude of velocity |

**Reading velocity off the graph — the SLOPE:**

```
take two points A and B on the line
     BC = change in position (s2 − s1)
     CA = change in time     (t2 − t1)

     v = (s2 − s1) / (t2 − t1) = BC / CA = the SLOPE of AB
```

**Worked:** from 40 m at 2 s to 80 m at 4 s → `v = (80 − 40)/(4 − 2) = 40/2 =` **20 m/s**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The SLOPE of a graph gives the RATE OF CHANGE of the Y-quantity with respect to the X-quantity.**
>
> **On a position–time graph, slope = VELOCITY.** That is not a rule to memorise — it falls straight out of `velocity = change in position ÷ change in time`, which is exactly what the slope computes.

**Comparing two objects:** for the same time interval, the object whose line is **steeper** has the **larger displacement**, so the **higher velocity**.

### 4.2.3 Velocity–time graphs

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Shape of velocity–time graph | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Straight line parallel to the time axis** | velocity constant → **acceleration ZERO** |
> | **Straight line sloping UP** | velocity increasing by equal amounts in equal times → **constant acceleration, in the direction of velocity** |
> | **Straight line sloping DOWN** | velocity decreasing by equal amounts → **constant acceleration, opposite to the direction of velocity** |

**Two things can be read from a velocity–time graph:**

#### Slope → ACCELERATION

```
a = (v − u) / (t2 − t1) = BC / CA = the SLOPE of the line
```

**Worked:** velocity rises from 5 m/s at 10 s to 10 m/s at 20 s.
`a = (10 − 5)/(20 − 10) = 5/10 =` **0.5 m/s²**

For the decreasing case (15 m/s falling to 0 over 30 s) the same method gives **–0.5 m/s²** — the minus sign meaning the acceleration is **opposite to the velocity**.

#### Area under the line → DISPLACEMENT

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The area enclosed between the velocity–time line and the time axis, over a chosen interval, equals the DISPLACEMENT in that interval.**
>
> **Why:** for constant velocity, `displacement = velocity × time` — and on the graph, velocity is the **height** and time the **width**, so their product **is** the area of the rectangle. When velocity changes, the shape becomes a rectangle plus a triangle, but the meaning is unchanged.

**Worked (constant velocity):** 20 m/s for 6 s → area of rectangle `= 20 × 6 =` **120 m**.

**Worked (constant acceleration):** between 10 s and 20 s, velocity goes from 5 m/s to 10 m/s.

```
displacement = area of rectangle + area of triangle
             = (5 m/s × 10 s) + (½ × 10 s × 5 m/s)
             = 50 m + 25 m
             = 75 m
```

## 4.3 The kinematic equations

**For motion in a straight line with CONSTANT acceleration**, five quantities are involved:

| Symbol | Quantity |
|---|---|
| **u** | initial velocity |
| **v** | final velocity |
| **a** | acceleration |
| **t** | time interval |
| **s** | displacement |

### Deriving them

**First equation — straight from the definition of acceleration:**

```
a = (v − u) / t
a t = v − u
        v = u + a t                                    ... (1)
```

**Second equation — from the AREA under the velocity–time graph.** The graph is a straight line starting at height u and ending at height v, so the area is a rectangle plus a triangle:

```
s = area of rectangle + area of triangle
  = (u × t) + (½ × t × (v − u))

substitute (v − u) = a t  from equation (1):

  = u t + ½ × t × a t

        s = u t + ½ a t²                                ... (2)
```

**Third equation — eliminate t between (1) and (2).** From (1), `t = (v − u)/a`. Substituting:

```
s = u(v − u)/a + ½ · a · ((v − u)/a)²
  = (uv − u²)/a + (u² + v² − 2uv)/(2a)
  = (2uv − 2u² + u² + v² − 2uv) / (2a)
  = (v² − u²) / (2a)

2 a s = v² − u²
        v² = u² + 2 a s                                 ... (3)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE THREE KINEMATIC EQUATIONS**
> ```
> v  = u + a t
> s  = u t + ½ a t²
> v² = u² + 2 a s
> ```
> **They let you predict the position or velocity of an object at a future time.**

> [!WARNING]
> **These equations are valid ONLY when the acceleration is constant.** If a changes during the interval, none of them applies.
>
> **Signs carry the directions.** For motion back and forth along a line, the signs of u, v, a and s tell you which way each quantity points — so put them in with their signs and let the algebra keep track.

> [!TIP]
> **Which equation to reach for?** Look at what is missing.
> - No **s** in the problem → use `v = u + a t`
> - No **v** → use `s = u t + ½ a t²`
> - No **t** → use `v² = u² + 2 a s`
>
> Each equation is exactly the one that omits one of the five quantities.

**Worked — braking distance.** Brakes give `a = –4 m/s²`, and the car stops, so `v = 0`.

Using `v² = u² + 2as` with v = 0: `0 = u² − 8s`, so **s = u²/8**.

| Initial speed | u in m/s | Stopping distance |
|---|---|---|
| 54 km/h | 15 | `225/8 =` **28.1 m** |
| 108 km/h | 30 | `900/8 =` **112.5 m** |

> [!WARNING]
> **DOUBLE the speed and the stopping distance becomes FOUR times as long — not twice.**
>
> **Why:** `s = u²/8` — the distance depends on the **square** of the speed. This single fact is the reason speed limits matter so much, and why you must **leave more gap the faster you go**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Real stopping distance depends on more than the brakes:**
> - the **velocity** when brakes are applied (squared, as above)
> - the **road surface** — wet or dry
> - the **braking capacity** of the vehicle (how large a negative acceleration it can produce)
> - the **driver's REACTION TIME** — the vehicle travels at full speed during the fraction of a second before the brake is even touched
>
> **That reaction distance is pure `speed × time` and is often forgotten.** At 10 m/s, a 0.5 s reaction adds **5 m** before braking begins.
>
> **Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication technology**, under development in India and elsewhere, lets vehicles exchange signals and warn drivers of possible collisions.

## 4.4 Motion in a plane

**Motion in two dimensions** — a vehicle overtaking another, the path of a kicked ball, a satellite in a circular path.

### 4.4.1 Uniform circular motion

**A child on a merry-go-round moving from A to B to C:** the **distance travelled** is the **curved path ABC**, but the **displacement** is the **straight line AC**. They are not equal.

**In one complete revolution of a circle of radius R:**

```
distance travelled = circumference = 2 π R
displacement       = 0             (back to the starting point)

average speed    v_av = 2 π R / T      where T = time for one revolution
average velocity      = 0
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **UNIFORM CIRCULAR MOTION = motion in a circular path with CONSTANT (uniform) SPEED.**

### Why a circle means constantly turning

**Imagine an athlete running a closed track at uniform speed:**

| Track | Direction changes per lap |
|---|---|
| **Rectangle** | **4** |
| **Hexagon** | **6** |
| **more and more sides** | more and more turns, taken more often |
| **circle** (sides shrink to points) | **the direction changes CONTINUOUSLY** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In uniform circular motion the SPEED is constant at every point; only the DIRECTION of velocity changes.**
>
> **And that alone is enough to make the motion ACCELERATED** — because velocity changes if **either** its magnitude **or** its direction changes.

> [!WARNING]
> **In everyday speech we say a vehicle is "accelerating" only when it speeds up.** We routinely fail to notice that **turning at constant speed is also acceleration**. A car going round a roundabout at a steady 30 km/h is accelerating the whole way.

> [!NOTE]
> **The velocity at any point is along the TANGENT to the circle at that point**, in the direction of motion. **A tangent is a straight line meeting the circle at exactly one point.**

**The marble-and-ring activity:** spin a marble round the inside of an adhesive-tape ring on a smooth surface, then **lift the ring away**.

**The marble flies off in a STRAIGHT LINE — it does not keep curving.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why:** once released, the marble **continues moving in the direction it had at the instant the ring was removed** — i.e. along the **tangent**.
>
> **What this proves:** something had to be continuously **forcing** the marble to curve. Circular motion is not what an object does when left alone; **straight-line motion is.** (The reason is taken up in a later chapter.)

> [!TIP]
> **Uniform circular motion is an IDEALISED MODEL** — in the real world speeds vary and paths are rarely perfect circles. It is useful anyway, as the foundation for planets orbiting the Sun and vehicles taking a circular turn. **Model first, complications later** — exactly the Chapter 1 method.

> [!NOTE]
> **Motion in three dimensions** — a car climbing a mountain road, a bird flying, an aircraft moving through air.

## At a glance

```
POSITION      distance AND direction from a chosen reference point
IN MOTION     position changes with time      AT REST  position doesn't

distance travelled   whole path length, no direction, always ≥ |displacement|
displacement         net change in position, with direction; zero on returning

average speed        = total distance ÷ time interval          (no direction)
average velocity     = displacement    ÷ time interval  = s/t  (with direction)
average acceleration = (v − u) ÷ time interval                 (with direction)

SI units   m · m/s · m/s²            km/h → m/s: multiply by 5/18

GRAPHS
  position–time   straight sloping line = constant velocity
                  curve                 = accelerated
                  line parallel to t    = at rest
                  SLOPE                 = velocity
  velocity–time   line parallel to t    = zero acceleration
                  sloping line          = constant acceleration
                  SLOPE                 = acceleration
                  AREA under the line   = displacement

KINEMATIC EQUATIONS (constant acceleration only)
      v  = u + a t                (no s)
      s  = u t + ½ a t²           (no v)
      v² = u² + 2 a s             (no t)

stopping distance ∝ u²   →  double the speed, FOUR times the distance

UNIFORM CIRCULAR MOTION   one revolution: distance = 2πR, displacement = 0
                          speed constant, DIRECTION always changing
                          → therefore ACCELERATED motion
                          velocity is along the TANGENT; release it and it
                          flies off straight
```
