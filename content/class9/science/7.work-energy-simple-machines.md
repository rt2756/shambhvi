## Why a new set of ideas

**Chapters 4 and 6 gave you kinematic equations and Newton's laws.** But **when forces change with time or act in complicated ways, applying those laws directly becomes difficult.**

**Work, energy and power often let us analyse motion and interactions far more easily** — you can find a final speed without following every intermediate step.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In everyday speech "work", "energy" and "power" are loose words. In science each has a PRECISE meaning** — exactly the Chapter 1 point about scientific language.

## 7.1 Work done by a constant force

**Build the definition from lifting bags.** A 5 kg wheat bag on the floor; lifting it slowly needs an upward force equal to **mg**.

| What you do | Compared with lifting one bag by 1 m |
|---|---|
| Lift **1 bag** by 1 m | **W** |
| Lift **3 bags one after another** by 1 m | **3W** — three times the task, three times the fuel |
| Lift **3 bags together** by 1 m | **3W** — **3 times the force** over the same distance |
| Lift **1 bag** by **3 m** | **3W** — same force over **3 times the distance** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Both experiments point the same way:**
> - a **larger force** over the **same** distance does proportionally more work
> - the **same force** over a **larger** distance does proportionally more work
>
> **WORK DONE BY A CONSTANT FORCE = force applied × displacement IN THE DIRECTION OF THE FORCE**
> ```
> W = F × s
> ```

**The SI unit of work is the JOULE (J).**

```
1 J = 1 N × 1 m
    = 1 kg m/s² × 1 m
    = 1 kg m² s⁻²
```

**One joule of work is done when a constant force of 1 newton displaces an object by 1 metre in the direction of the force.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When describing work, always say WHICH FORCE (or agency) does the work and ON WHICH OBJECT.** "30 J of work was done" is incomplete; "the goalkeeper did −30 J of work on the ball" is not.

> [!TIP]
> **On a force–displacement graph, the WORK DONE is the AREA under the line.** For a constant 10 N over 1 m, the area of the rectangle is `10 × 1 =` **10 J**.
>
> **And this still works when the force is NOT constant** — just find the area under the curve between the initial and final positions. That is why the graphical view is worth having.

> [!NOTE]
> **Force and displacement both have direction, but WORK DOES NOT.** Work is described by a **number with a positive or negative sign**, nothing more.

### 7.1.1 When is the work zero?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Work is zero when:**
> 1. **F = 0** — no force, no work
> 2. **s = 0** — no displacement, no work, **however hard you push**
> 3. the force is **PERPENDICULAR to the displacement** — there is no displacement *in the direction of the force*

**Push a rigid wall as hard as you like: the wall does not move, so YOU DO NO WORK ON THE WALL.**

> [!WARNING]
> **But you feel tired — so why is the work zero?**
>
> **Because to apply a force at all, the muscles in your body repeatedly expand and contract, using up your body's internal energy.** That tiredness is real energy being spent inside **you**. It is simply not work done **on the wall**, because the wall never moved.
>
> **The scientific definition is about the OBJECT, not about your effort.**

**Carrying a box while walking:** you apply an **upward** force to balance its weight while the box moves **horizontally**. **Force and displacement are perpendicular → no work is done by that force on the box.**

### 7.1.2 Positive and negative work

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Displacement in the SAME direction as the force → POSITIVE work.** Pushing a wheelchair forward.
> - **Displacement OPPOSITE to the force → NEGATIVE work.** A goalkeeper stopping a ball applies a force opposite to the ball's motion.

**Worked — a girl lifting and lowering a dumbbell.**
- **Lifting:** her force is **upward**, the displacement is **upward** → **positive work**.
- **Lowering:** her force is still **upward** (holding it back), the displacement is **downward** → **negative work**.

**Worked — a goalkeeper's hand moves back 15 cm while applying 200 N to stop a ball.**
```
the ball moves opposite to the applied force, so take s as negative
W = 200 N × (−0.15 m) = −30 J
```
**She does −30 J of work on the ball.**

> [!TIP]
> **The ball and the goalkeeper each do work on the other** — equal and opposite forces, by Newton's third law. **The ball does POSITIVE work on the goalkeeper** (pushing her hands back) while **the goalkeeper does NEGATIVE work on the ball** (slowing it). Same interaction, opposite signs.

## 7.2 The work–energy theorem

**A thrown cricket ball knocks over a wicket. A raised flowerpot can damage what it falls on.** Each has acquired a **capacity to do work**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An object having the CAPACITY TO DO WORK is said to possess ENERGY.**
>
> The ball got its energy **from the work the fielder did in throwing it**; the pot got its energy **from the work done in raising it**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE WORK–ENERGY THEOREM:**
> ```
> work done on an object = change in its energy
> ```
> **It also holds for a SYSTEM of objects, and even when the forces are NOT constant** — which is exactly what makes it more powerful than F = ma in awkward situations.
>
> **The SI unit of energy is the same as that of work: the JOULE (J).**

**When positive work is done on an object it GAINS energy; that energy can then be used to apply a force on something else and move it — transferring the energy on.**

**Worked — a carrom shot.** The striker hits the white coin, which hits the black coin.

| Interaction | Positive work on | Negative work on |
|---|---|---|
| striker → white coin | **white coin gains energy** | **striker loses energy** |
| white coin → black coin | **black coin gains energy** | **white coin loses energy** |

**Why the negative half is there: by Newton's third law the white coin pushes back on the striker, opposite to the striker's motion — so it does negative work on it.** Energy is handed on, not created.

> [!NOTE]
> **The joule is named after James Prescott Joule**, who studied how **mechanical energy and thermal energy** are related and can be converted into one another — which gave us a **unified** way to understand energy.

> [!NOTE]
> **Doing mechanical work is only ONE way of transferring energy.** Energy also flows **as heat** from a hotter object to a colder one in contact; **as radiation** without contact, which is how the Sun's energy reaches Earth; through **electric circuits**; via **sound waves**; and in the **nuclear reactions** that power the Sun.

## 7.3 Forms of energy

| Form | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Mechanical** | energy due to the **motion or position** of objects |
| **Thermal** | energy that makes things **warm or hot** |
| **Light** | energy that allows us to **see** |
| **Sound** | energy of **vibrations** of air or other molecules |
| **Electrical** | energy related to the **position or motion of charges** |
| **Chemical** | energy stored in **fuels and food**, in the **bonds between atoms** |
| **Nuclear** | energy stored in the **nuclei of atoms** |

**Energy changes form constantly:** electrical → light and thermal in a bulb; chemical (food) → mechanical in muscles; mechanical → sound in a ringing bell.

## 7.4 Mechanical energy

**MECHANICAL ENERGY = the energy an object possesses due to its MOTION or its POSITION.**

### 7.4.1 Kinetic energy

**KINETIC ENERGY is the energy possessed by an object due to its MOTION.** A stationary object is defined to have **zero** kinetic energy.

**Deriving the formula.** Take an object of mass m, initial velocity u, final velocity v, displacement s, under a constant force F (so a is constant too).

```
from  v² = u² + 2as :        s = (v² − u²) / (2a)

work done:   W = F × s = m a × (v² − u²)/(2a)

             W = ½ m (v² − u²)
```

**By the work–energy theorem this is the CHANGE in the object's energy. Setting u = 0 makes it the final kinetic energy:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> K = ½ m v²
> ```
> **SI unit: joule (J). Kinetic energy has NO direction.**
>
> - positive work → velocity increases → **kinetic energy increases**
> - negative work → velocity decreases → **kinetic energy decreases**
> - no work → velocity unchanged → **kinetic energy constant**

> [!WARNING]
> **Kinetic energy depends on the SQUARE of the velocity.**
>
> **Double the velocity and the kinetic energy becomes FOUR times as large:**
> ```
> ½ m (2v)² = 4 × ½ m v²
> ```
> **This is the same v² that made stopping distance quadruple in Chapter 4** — and it is the same fact seen from the energy side, because the brakes must dispose of four times as much energy.

**Worked — a cricket ball, mass 0.2 kg, bowled at 154.8 km/h = 43 m/s:**
```
K = ½ × 0.2 × 43² = 0.1 × 1849 = 184.9 J
```

**Worked — a 15,000 kg jet stopped by an arrester wire.** The wire applies 367,500 N backwards and stops the jet in 100 m. Find the landing speed.
```
change in kinetic energy = 0 − ½ × 15000 × v²
work done by the wire    = 367500 × (−100) = −36,750,000 J   (force opposite to displacement)

by the work-energy theorem:
      −½ × 15000 × v² = −36,750,000
                   v² = 2 × 36,750,000 / 15,000 = 4900
                    v = 70 m/s = 252 km/h
```

### 7.4.2 Potential energy

**A released slingshot band shoots a stone; a released bow launches an arrow; a released compressed spring pushes an object away.** In each case the object gains kinetic energy it did not have.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **That energy came from the stretched band, the bent bow or the compressed spring — which had the capacity to do work BECAUSE OF THEIR SHAPE.**
>
> **The work done in deforming them was STORED in them.**

**Energy can also be stored by changing the ARRANGEMENT of objects in a system:**
- **Two magnets** with unlike poles pulled apart, released, rush together and gain kinetic energy — enough to crush a pea placed between them.
- **Two separated electric charges** likewise possess stored energy that can do work when released.
- **A ball raised above the Earth:** work was done against the gravitational force to lift it; released, the ball and the Earth rush towards each other and gain kinetic energy.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **POTENTIAL ENERGY = the energy stored by an object because of its DEFORMATION, or by a system of objects because of their RELATIVE POSITIONS.**
>
> **Whenever a system of objects interacts through forces — gravitational, electric or magnetic — it can store energy in the arrangement of those objects.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Why deformation stores energy:** you apply an external force to overcome the **internal forces** in the spring. Remove your force and those **internal forces undo the deformation**, doing work in the process.

### Gravitational potential energy

**The Earth is so much more massive than the ball that it hardly moves, so we simply call the stored energy of the Earth–ball system "the potential energy of the ball".**

**Activity — a heavy ball dropped into loose sand from 1 m, then 2 m.** **The depression is DEEPEST for the greatest height.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why: raising the ball higher requires MORE WORK, so the ball possesses MORE ENERGY at a greater height — and that extra energy digs a deeper hole.**
>
> **The depth of the depression is a visible measure of the stored energy.**

**Deriving the formula.** Define the potential energy as zero on the ground. To raise an object of mass m to a height h slowly, you apply a force **mg** upward through a displacement **h**:

```
W = force × displacement = mg × h
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> U = m g h
> ```
> **SI unit: joule (J)** — the same as work and kinetic energy.

**Worked — a 200 g ball thrown 10 m up (g = 10 m/s²):**
```
U = mgh = 0.2 × 10 × 10 = 20 J
```

> [!WARNING]
> **U = mgh is valid only NEAR the Earth's surface**, where g is nearly constant. Further away, **g decreases** and the formula fails.

> [!TIP]
> **Moving horizontally at constant velocity does NOT change the potential energy** — h is unchanged. Raising the object vertically **does**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Work done against SOME internal forces stores potential energy — but not all of them.** Work done against **friction** does **not** get stored anywhere; it is gone from the mechanical account.

### 7.4.3 Conservation of mechanical energy

**MECHANICAL ENERGY = kinetic energy + potential energy.**

**Follow an object of mass m dropped from height h at point A:**

```
At A (t = 0, u = 0):
    potential energy  = mgh
    kinetic energy    = 0
    MECHANICAL ENERGY = mgh

After falling for time t, at B:
    v  = u + gt = gt
    h' = h − ½ g t²

    potential energy  = mgh' = mgh − ½ m g² t²
    kinetic energy    = ½ m v² = ½ m g² t²
    MECHANICAL ENERGY = mgh − ½ m g² t² + ½ m g² t² = mgh
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The kinetic energy GAINED (½ m g² t²) is EXACTLY the potential energy LOST.**
>
> **CONSERVATION OF MECHANICAL ENERGY: as an object moves under the gravitational force, its mechanical energy stays constant, provided no other external forces act on it.**

**The pendulum test.** Draw a horizontal line at the level from which you release the bob.

| Point | Potential energy | Kinetic energy |
|---|---|---|
| **P** (release, height h) | **mgh — maximum** | **zero** |
| **Q** (lowest point) | **zero** | **maximum** |
| **R** (far side, where it stops) | **mgh again — it almost reaches the line** | **zero** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The bob returning to nearly the same height is the evidence** — it shows the mechanical energy is (almost) unchanged.
>
> **Why "almost":** in real life the pendulum slowly stops because of **friction at the support and air resistance**, which take energy out of the mechanical account.

> [!NOTE]
> **The bigger picture:** the **total energy** of an object or system not acted on by external forces stays constant — mechanical energy is only one part of that.

**Worked — a child sliding down a slide of height h (ignoring friction):**
```
potential energy at the top = kinetic energy at the bottom

           m g h = ½ m v²
               v = √(2 g h)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The mass CANCELS. So the speed at the bottom depends ONLY on the height h — not on the child's mass, and not on the shape of the slide.**
>
> Two children of different masses reach the bottom of the same slide at the **same speed**; and of several slides, the **tallest** gives the largest speed, however curved it is.
>
> **This is why energy methods are worth learning: Newton's laws would need you to track the changing slope of the slide at every instant. Energy needs only the start and the end.**

**Worked — a truck escape ramp.** A 10,000 kg truck at 72 km/h = 20 m/s runs onto a 30° sand ramp where sand exerts 50,000 N. On a 30° incline the truck rises **1 m vertically for every 2 m along the ramp**, so height = d/2. Take g = 10 m/s².

```
initial energy = ½ × 10000 × 20² + 0        = 2,000,000 J
final energy   = 0 + mg(d/2) = 10000 × 10 × d/2 = 50,000 d

work done by sand = −50,000 d      (force opposite to the motion)

work-energy theorem:  −50,000 d = 50,000 d − 2,000,000
                       2,000,000 = 100,000 d
                               d = 20 m
```
**The ramp must be at least 20 m long.**

> [!TIP]
> **Notice the ramp does the job in two independent ways at once:** the sand's friction removes energy, **and** climbing converts kinetic energy into potential energy. That is why an escape ramp is both **uphill** and **full of gravel**.

## 7.5 Power

**Carrying your bag up the stairs in one minute feels very different from taking five minutes — even though the WORK DONE IS THE SAME.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **POWER = the RATE at which work is done.**
> ```
> P = W / t
> ```
> **SI unit: the WATT (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s.**
>
> More work in the same time → more power. **The same work in a shorter time → more power.**

**Worked — a weightlifter raises 75 kg by 2 m in 5 s (g = 10 m/s²):**
```
W = mgh = 75 × 10 × 2 = 1500 J
P = 1500 / 5 = 300 W
```

**Worked — a 1000 kg car from rest to 72 km/h = 20 m/s in 10 s:**
```
work = final KE − initial KE = ½ × 1000 × 20² − 0 = 200,000 J
P    = 200,000 / 10 = 20,000 W
```

> [!NOTE]
> **The watt is named after James Watt**, who invented an efficient **steam engine** producing rotational motion to turn wheels.
>
> **HORSEPOWER (hp)**, still used for car engines and water pumps, dates from the early days of engines, when their power was compared with that of **actual horses** pulling carriages. **1 hp = 746 W.**

## 7.6 Simple machines

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The TOTAL WORK required for a task cannot be reduced. A machine only makes the task EASIER, by changing the MAGNITUDE or the DIRECTION of the force you must apply.**
>
> - **EFFORT** = the force you apply
> - **LOAD** = the force to be overcome
> ```
> mechanical advantage = load / effort
> ```

### 7.6.1 Pulley

**A pulley is a wheel with a groove that guides a rope.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A FIXED pulley does NOT reduce the force needed — it only changes its DIRECTION.**
>
> **Its mechanical advantage is 1**, since load and effort are equal.
>
> **So why use it?** Because **pulling downwards is far easier for us than lifting upwards** — you can use your own weight. **Convenience, not force reduction.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Movable pulleys, or systems of pulleys, DO have a mechanical advantage greater than 1** and can lift much heavier loads with much smaller effort. In a movable-pulley system the load hangs from the movable pulley, one rope end is fixed, and effort is applied to the other. **Used in elevators and cranes.**

### 7.6.2 Inclined plane

**Activity:** pull a cart up a plank with a spring balance, first lifting it vertically, then along a steep plank, then along a shallower one.

**The force needed DECREASES as the plank becomes less steep — but you must apply it over a LARGER distance.**

**Deriving the mechanical advantage** (moving at constant speed, ignoring friction):

```
work you do on the object          = F' × L
potential energy the object gains  = m g h

by the work-energy theorem:   F' × L = m g h

mechanical advantage = load / effort = mg / F' = L / h
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> mechanical advantage of an inclined plane = L / h
> ```
> **Since L is always larger than h, the effort F' is always LESS than mg — the mechanical advantage is greater than 1.**
>
> **Make L longer (a shallower angle) and the effort falls further.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The work done is the SAME in every case.** `F × s` stays constant: **when the force decreases, the displacement increases in exactly the same proportion.**
>
> **That is the fundamental bargain of every simple machine — and why "hill roads wind around gently" is the same idea as "the ramp is longer than the step".**

**Worked — a ramp over a 30 cm step, 40 cm wide:**
```
by the right-angled triangle:  L = √(30² + 40²) = 50 cm

mechanical advantage = L / h = 50 / 30 = 1.67
```

### 7.6.3 Lever

**Balance a ruler on a pencil, put a heavy stapler on the short side and a light eraser on the long side — the eraser lifts the stapler.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A LEVER is a rigid bar that can rotate about a fixed point.** Three parts:
> - **FULCRUM** — the fixed point it rotates about
> - **LOAD** — the force to be overcome; its distance from the fulcrum is the **LOAD ARM**
> - **EFFORT** — the force applied; its distance from the fulcrum is the **EFFORT ARM**

**Why a small force can lift a large one:** the end where the **smaller** force is applied moves a **larger** distance, while the end applying the **larger** force moves a **smaller** distance.

```
F1 × d1 = F2 × d2

effort × effort arm = load × load arm

mechanical advantage = load / effort = effort arm / load arm
```

**Beam-balance activity:** with 1 coin each side the beam is level. Add coins to one pan and it tips; **move the heavier pan closer to the centre** to level it again.

```
n1 × L1 = n2 × L2     (coins × distance, on each side)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Increase the EFFORT ARM and the effort needed falls** — but that end must **move through a larger distance**, so **the total work done stays the same.**
>
> **A lever reduces the FORCE required, not the WORK done.**

**Worked — the seesaw.** Seats A, B, D, E with fulcrum C; AC = EC = 2 m, BC = DC = 1 m. A 15 kg child sits at A. Where should a 30 kg child sit?
```
15 × 2 = 30 × L
     L = 1 m   →  seat D
```

> [!NOTE]
> **Three classes of lever**, by what lies in the middle:
>
> | Class | In the middle | Examples |
> |---|---|---|
> | **I** | **FULCRUM** | tongs, scissors, crowbar, pliers, balance scale, seesaw |
> | **II** | **LOAD** | lemon squeezer, wheelbarrow, bottle opener |
> | **III** | **EFFORT** | tongs, tweezers, broom, hammer, oar |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Machines do not create energy. They only help us use it more effectively.** Ignoring friction, the work you put in equals the useful work done on the load — which is why **no perpetual motion machine can ever work**: friction always removes some mechanical energy, and nothing puts it back.

> [!NOTE]
> **The gharat / panchakki** — a traditional Himalayan watermill still found in hilly regions. Water starts high with **potential energy**, converts it to **kinetic energy** coming down the pipe, and that drives a **wheel** connected to a **grinding stone**.
>
> **Modern dams do exactly the same thing** — the stored potential energy of water becomes kinetic energy, which generates electricity.

## At a glance

```
WORK       W = F × s   (displacement IN THE DIRECTION of the force)
           SI unit joule; 1 J = 1 N × 1 m = 1 kg m² s⁻²
           area under a force–displacement graph = work (even if F varies)
           ZERO work when F = 0, or s = 0, or F is perpendicular to s
           positive when s is along F; negative when s is opposite to F

WORK–ENERGY THEOREM      work done on an object = change in its energy
                         holds for systems and for non-constant forces

ENERGY forms   mechanical · thermal · light · sound · electrical · chemical · nuclear

KINETIC ENERGY     K = ½ m v²      no direction; doubles v → FOUR times K
POTENTIAL ENERGY   U = m g h       stored by deformation or by relative position
                                   valid only near the Earth's surface

CONSERVATION OF MECHANICAL ENERGY   KE + PE stays constant with no other
                                    external forces
   sliding down a height h:  v = √(2gh)  — independent of MASS and of SHAPE

POWER      P = W / t      SI unit watt; 1 W = 1 J/s;  1 hp = 746 W

SIMPLE MACHINES     mechanical advantage = load / effort
   fixed pulley      MA = 1 — changes DIRECTION only, for convenience
   inclined plane    MA = L / h — longer, shallower ramp needs less effort
   lever             effort × effort arm = load × load arm; MA = effort arm/load arm
   IN EVERY CASE the total WORK is unchanged: less force, more distance
```
