## 8.1 The roots of atomic theory

**More than 2000 years ago, thinkers in ancient India and ancient Greece asked the same question — what is everything made of?**

**Acharya Kanada** (recorded in the Sanskrit **Vaisesika Sutras**): if matter (**dravya**) is divided again and again, you reach a stage where the particles **can no longer be divided**. He called them **PARMANUS** — infinitely small and **not perceivable by the senses**. They combine as **dyads** (two) and **triads** (three), and out of such combinations the whole material universe is built.

**Leucippus and Democritus** in Greece proposed a similar idea, calling the indivisible particles **ATOMOS** — Greek for *indivisible*.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Kanada's account does not specify the PROPORTIONS in which parmanus combine to form different substances** — and that is the crucial gap.
>
> **The concept of the atom originated as an IMAGINARY IDEA, not from experimental observation.** It was a brilliant guess that could not be tested.

**In 1808 John Dalton proposed his atomic theory — based on the SCIENTIFIC EXPERIMENTS of his time.** All matter is composed of indivisible particles called **atoms**, the fundamental building blocks that cannot be broken into smaller parts.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Dalton's was the FIRST SCIENTIFIC description of how matter is made**, and the starting point for everything that followed. **The difference from Kanada is not the idea but the EVIDENCE.**

**Dalton's theory left three questions:** what are atoms made of? what would they look like if we could see them? what makes the atoms of one element different from another's?

## 8.2 A journey through atomic models

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **As new experiments were performed and new evidence appeared, the models were changed and improved.**
>
> **We now know the early models were not fully correct — and they are still important**, because they show how science moves forward: **one step at a time, driven by curiosity, questioning and experimentation.** This is Chapter 1's point about models and revision, told as a history.

**The first crack:** atoms were thought indivisible until scientists discovered **RADIOACTIVITY** — certain elements emitting invisible energy and particles. **If something comes OUT of an atom, the atom must be made of smaller parts.**

### The discovery of the electron

**In 1897 J. J. Thomson studied the conduction of electric current through gases at very low pressure**, in a glass tube with two electrodes and a high voltage. He saw rays travelling from the **cathode (negative)** to the **anode (positive)** — **CATHODE RAYS**.

**Studying them in electric and magnetic fields, he concluded they are streams of NEGATIVELY CHARGED PARTICLES with a mass much smaller than that of atoms.** These were later named **ELECTRONS**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The decisive detail: the nature of the cathode rays did NOT depend on the material of the cathode or the gas in the tube.**
>
> **Why that proves so much:** if you get the identical particle out of every material you try, that particle cannot belong to any one material — **electrons must be a fundamental component of ALL atoms, present in every element.**

**The charge of an electron is −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, taken as −1 by convention and convenience.**

> [!NOTE]
> **J. J. Thomson** received the **1906 Nobel Prize in Physics** for his studies of the electrical conductivity of gases, which led him to the electron — **the first subatomic particle ever identified**. As head of the **Cavendish Laboratory** in Cambridge he guided many scientists, including **Ernest Rutherford**.

### 8.2.1 Thomson's model

**Thomson's puzzle: electrons are negative, but atoms are NEUTRAL. Where is the positive charge?**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THOMSON'S MODEL: the atom is a SPHERE OF POSITIVE CHARGE with electrons distributed throughout it.**
>
> Compared to a **pudding with plums in it** — the **plum pudding model**. A **watermelon** is a closer picture: red pulp = positive charge, seeds = electrons spread through it.
>
> **It was the first genuine attempt to describe how an atom's positive and negative charges stay balanced.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Atoms have no colour.** The colours in every diagram in this chapter are purely for illustration.

### 8.2.2 The gold foil experiment

**In 1911 Geiger and Marsden, working under Rutherford, tested Thomson's model.** They aimed a narrow beam of **alpha (α) particles** — tiny, positively charged particles emitted by certain radioactive elements — at an **extremely thin sheet of gold foil**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **What Thomson's model PREDICTED:** with the positive charge spread out evenly, the α-particles should pass straight through or be deflected only **slightly**.
>
> **What actually happened:**
> - **most passed through undeflected**
> - **some were sharply deflected**
> - **a few even bounced straight back**
>
> This deflection from the straight path is called **SCATTERING**, which is why the experiment is also called the **α-ray scattering experiment**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Thomson's model FAILED** — it could explain neither the large-angle deflections nor why most particles went through untouched. **A prediction that fails is exactly how a model gets replaced.**

### A. Rutherford's model

**Reading the results backwards, Rutherford concluded:**

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Most α-particles passed **undeflected** | **Most of an atom is EMPTY SPACE** |
| Some were **sharply deflected**, a few **bounced back** | The positive charge is **not spread out** but **concentrated in an extremely small region — the NUCLEUS**, which is **dense** and holds **most of the atom's mass** |

**And the electrons revolve around the nucleus, somewhat like planets orbiting the Sun — hence the PLANETARY MODEL.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How small is the nucleus?**
> ```
> diameter of an atom    ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m
> diameter of a nucleus  ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m      — about 100,000 times smaller
> ```
> **If an atom were the size of a cricket ground (about 100 m across), the nucleus would be a single black pepper grain a few mm wide at the centre.**

> [!TIP]
> **How many atoms thick is a sheet of paper?**
> ```
> sheet thickness 0.1 mm = 10⁻⁴ m,   atom diameter ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m
> 10⁻⁴ ÷ 10⁻¹⁰ = 10⁶
> ```
> **About one million atoms stacked together.**

### B. The limitation of Rutherford's model

> [!WARNING]
> **Rutherford's model could not explain WHY ATOMS ARE STABLE.**
>
> **The argument, using Chapter 4:** a particle moving in a **circular path is constantly changing direction, which means it is ACCELERATING**. An accelerating charged particle should **lose energy**. Losing energy, the electron would **spiral inward and fall into the nucleus**.
>
> **If that really happened, atoms would collapse and matter would not exist.** But atoms are stable — so the model had to be incomplete.

### C. The proton

**Rutherford showed the nucleus carries positive charge, coming from particles called PROTONS.** Protons are **much heavier than electrons** and carry a charge **equal and opposite** to an electron's.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For an atom to be electrically neutral, the number of protons must EQUAL the number of electrons.**
>
> Helium: 2 protons, 2 electrons. Sodium: 11 protons, 11 electrons. **All atoms are electrically neutral.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Ernest Rutherford**, born in New Zealand, moved to Cambridge to work with J. J. Thomson and became known as the **Father of Nuclear Physics**. He won the **1908 Nobel Prize in Chemistry** for explaining how some elements naturally break down, and proposed the **nuclear model** in 1911. His portrait appears on New Zealand's $100 banknote.

### 8.2.3 Bohr's model

**In 1913 Niels Bohr proposed a model that could explain stability.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **BOHR'S POSTULATES**
> - Electrons do not move randomly but follow **FIXED CIRCULAR PATHS** called **stationary states, orbits or SHELLS**. In each shell an electron has a **definite amount of energy**, so shells are also called **ENERGY LEVELS**.
> - The shells are labelled **K, L, M, N, …** or **n = 1, 2, 3, 4, …**
> - Electrons can revolve **only in these allowed shells, never in between**, and **while in a fixed shell an electron does NOT lose energy**.
> - **K (n = 1) is closest to the nucleus and has the LEAST energy.** Energy increases as you move outwards.
> - An electron moves between shells **only by absorbing or releasing a FIXED amount of energy** — exactly the difference between the two levels.
> - **Each shell can hold only a certain number of electrons.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How this explains stability:** Bohr introduced **stationary states as a POSTULATE** — in a stationary state, **the energy of an electron stays constant even though it is moving** around the nucleus.
>
> **So the spiral-in problem simply cannot arise:** if the electron loses no energy in its shell, it has no way to spiral inward.
>
> **Notice this is asserted, not derived.** Bohr's model explained many experimental observations and was a major step forward — but *why* stationary states exist had to wait for the **quantum mechanical model**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why K, L, M, N and not A, B, C, D?** From early X-ray experiments by **Charles Barkla**, who called the first observed X-ray line **K**. He deliberately did not start at A, **leaving room for an earlier series that might be discovered** — none ever was. Bohr adopted the same notation.

## 8.3 What gives an atom its mass?

**Most of an atom's mass is in the nucleus; electrons are so light their mass can be ignored.**

**But there was a puzzle: hydrogen has 1 proton, helium has 2 protons — yet helium's mass is about FOUR times hydrogen's, not double.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **So something else must be in the nucleus, adding MASS without adding CHARGE.**

### 8.3.1 The neutron

**In 1932 James Chadwick (a student of Rutherford) discovered a subatomic particle with a mass nearly equal to a proton's but NO electrical charge — the NEUTRON, symbol n.**

**Neutrons are found in the nucleus of all atoms EXCEPT hydrogen.**

| Subatomic particle | Symbol | Relative charge |
|---|---|---|
| **Electron** | e⁻ | **−1** |
| **Proton** | p⁺ | **+1** |
| **Neutron** | n⁰ | **0** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the nucleus doesn't blow apart.** Every proton **repels** every other proton, since they all carry positive charge. **Neutrons help in two ways:**
> 1. by **intervening between protons and increasing the distance** between them, reducing the repulsion
> 2. by **strengthening the NUCLEAR FORCE** that binds all the nuclear particles together
>
> **This is why heavier atoms need proportionally MORE neutrons.** Light atoms often have equal numbers — carbon 6 and 6, oxygen 8 and 8. But **iron has 26 protons and 30 neutrons**, and **uranium has 92 protons and 146 neutrons**.

> [!NOTE]
> **The neutron opened a new era.** Being **uncharged**, neutrons are not repelled by nuclei and can **penetrate them easily** — leading to artificial radioactive elements and the splitting of uranium atoms. This gave birth to the **"atomic age"**, and to both nuclear power and nuclear weapons. **Chadwick won the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics.**

> [!NOTE]
> **The Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC), Mumbai** leads advanced **neutron-scattering** experiments using reactors such as **Dhruva**, revealing the structure of **superconductors, battery electrodes and drug molecules** — helping develop better medicines, energy storage and industrial alloys in India.

**By 1869 scientists knew about 69 elements. Today we know 118, some of them made artificially, and the search continues.**

## 8.4 Symbols of elements

**Dalton introduced the first PICTORIAL symbols in 1803.** In **1813 Berzelius** suggested symbols derived from the **Latin names**, giving us alphabetic symbols. Today **IUPAC** approves names and symbols.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The rules:**
> - Many symbols are the **first letter**, or the **first two letters**, of the name.
> - **The first letter is ALWAYS capital; the second (if any) is ALWAYS small.** H, **Al** not AL, **Co** not CO.
> - Some use the first letter and a **later** letter, not the second — **Cl** (chlorine), **Zn** (zinc).
> - Some come from **Latin, Greek or German** names — **Fe** (ferrum), **Hg** (hydrargyros), **W** (wolfram).

> [!WARNING]
> **The capital/small rule is not decoration.** **Co** is cobalt but **CO** is carbon monoxide — a compound of two different elements. Get the case wrong and you have written something else entirely.

## 8.5 Atomic number (Z)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ATOMIC NUMBER Z = the number of PROTONS in the nucleus of an atom.**
>
> **It determines the IDENTITY of the element and its chemical behaviour.**
>
> Since an atom is neutral, **Z is also the number of electrons.** Hydrogen: 1 proton, 1 electron, Z = 1. Helium: Z = 2.

**Elements with different atomic numbers are different elements — Z uniquely identifies an element.**

## 8.6 Mass number (A)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **MASS NUMBER A = the total number of PROTONS AND NEUTRONS in the nucleus.**
> ```
> A = number of protons + number of neutrons
> ```
> **Protons and neutrons together are called NUCLEONS.**
>
> **Electrons have almost negligible mass and are ignored in this calculation.**

| Element | Protons | Neutrons | Mass number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | 1 | **0** | 1 |
| Helium | 2 | 2 | 4 |
| Lithium | 3 | 4 | 7 |

**Standard notation:**
```
      A
       Z  X          mass number above, atomic number below

     12
      6  C           carbon: Z = 6, A = 12, so 6 protons and 6 neutrons
```

> [!TIP]
> **Number of neutrons = A − Z.** That single subtraction answers most questions in this chapter.

## 8.7 How electrons are distributed

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE BOHR–BURY RULES**
> 1. **The maximum number of electrons in a shell is 2n²**, where n is the shell number:
> ```
> K (n=1): 2 × 1² = 2      L (n=2): 2 × 2² = 8      M (n=3): 2 × 3² = 18
> ```
> 2. **The OUTERMOST shell can hold a maximum of 8 electrons** (the first shell holds a maximum of 2).
> 3. **Shells are filled STEPWISE from the nucleus outwards** — K, then L, then M… **The L-shell is filled only after the K-shell is complete.**

**The distribution of electrons among the shells is the ELECTRONIC CONFIGURATION.**

| Element | Symbol | Z | p⁺ | n⁰ | e⁻ | K | L | M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | – | – |
| Helium | He | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | – | – |
| Lithium | Li | 3 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | – |
| Beryllium | Be | 4 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 2 | 2 | – |
| Boron | B | 5 | 5 | 6 | 5 | 2 | 3 | – |
| Carbon | C | 6 | 6 | 6 | 6 | 2 | 4 | – |
| Nitrogen | N | 7 | 7 | 7 | 7 | 2 | 5 | – |
| Oxygen | O | 8 | 8 | 8 | 8 | 2 | 6 | – |
| Fluorine | F | 9 | 9 | 10 | 9 | 2 | 7 | – |
| Neon | Ne | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 | 2 | 8 | – |
| Sodium | Na | 11 | 11 | 12 | 11 | 2 | 8 | 1 |
| Magnesium | Mg | 12 | 12 | 12 | 12 | 2 | 8 | 2 |
| Aluminium | Al | 13 | 13 | 14 | 13 | 2 | 8 | 3 |
| Silicon | Si | 14 | 14 | 14 | 14 | 2 | 8 | 4 |
| Phosphorus | P | 15 | 15 | 16 | 15 | 2 | 8 | 5 |
| Sulfur | S | 16 | 16 | 16 | 16 | 2 | 8 | 6 |
| Chlorine | Cl | 17 | 17 | 18 | 17 | 2 | 8 | 7 |
| Argon | Ar | 18 | 18 | 22 | 18 | 2 | 8 | 8 |

> [!WARNING]
> **The M-shell can hold 18 — but not while it is the OUTERMOST shell**, where rule 2 caps it at 8. That is why argon stops at 2, 8, 8 and the next electron starts a new shell.

## 8.8 Valency — the combining capacity

**COMBINING CAPACITY = the number of hydrogen or chlorine atoms with which one atom of an element can combine.** Hydrogen and chlorine are used as the yardstick **because both have a combining capacity of one**.

- **H₂O** — oxygen combines with 2 hydrogens → combining capacity of oxygen is **2**
- **NH₃** — nitrogen combines with 3 hydrogens → **3**
- **MgCl₂** — magnesium combines with 2 chlorines → **2**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **VALENCE SHELL** = the outermost shell containing electrons.
> - **VALENCE ELECTRONS** = the electrons in it.
> - **OCTET** = 8 electrons in the outermost shell.
>
> **Elements with a complete octet (or 2 electrons, for helium) are largely UNREACTIVE and more STABLE.** Atoms with incomplete valence shells are **more reactive** — they lose, gain or share electrons to complete the octet.
>
> **VALENCY = the number of electrons gained, lost or shared to complete the octet.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How to work out the valency from the configuration:**
> - **Fewer than 4 valence electrons → the atom LOSES them.** Sodium is 2, 8, **1** → loses 1 → **valency 1**.
> - **More than 4 valence electrons → the atom GAINS electrons.** Oxygen is 2, **6** → gains 2 → **valency 2**.
> - **Exactly 4 → it can neither easily gain nor lose, so it SHARES.** Carbon is 2, **4** → shares 4 → **valency 4**.
>
> **Why "fewer than four → lose":** it is simply the cheaper route. Removing 1 electron is far easier than acquiring 7.

**Atoms that already have 8 in the outermost shell have nothing to gain by reacting — which is exactly why the noble gases are inert.**

## 8.9 A deeper look

### 8.9.1 Isotopes

**Dalton said all atoms of an element are identical with the same mass. Scientists later found otherwise.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ISOTOPES are atoms of the SAME element with the SAME atomic number (Z) but DIFFERENT numbers of neutrons — and therefore different mass numbers (A).**

**Hydrogen's three isotopes** — all with **1 proton and 1 electron**:

| Isotope | Notation | Neutrons | Abundance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protium** | ¹₁H | **0** | about 99.98% |
| **Deuterium** | ²₁H | **1** | about 0.015% |
| **Tritium** | ³₁H | **2** | traces |

**Carbon's three isotopes:** ¹²₆C (most abundant), ¹³₆C, ¹⁴₆C — each with **6 protons and 6 electrons**, differing only in neutrons.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Isotopes have SIMILAR CHEMICAL properties but DIFFERENT PHYSICAL properties** (such as boiling and melting points).
>
> **Why:** chemical behaviour depends on the **number of valence electrons**, and isotopes have the **same number of electrons and the same electronic configuration**. Neutrons change the **mass** but not the **chemistry**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Uses of isotopes:**
> - **²³⁵₉₂U** — fuel in a **nuclear reactor** to generate electricity
> - **⁶⁰₂₇Co** — radioactive cobalt, used in **radiation treatment for cancer**
> - **¹³¹₅₃I** — used to treat **goitre and thyroid cancer**
> - **¹⁴₆C** — used in **archaeology and geology** to date ancient fossils and artefacts

### A. Average atomic mass

**Chlorine occurs as two isotopes: mass 35 u and mass 37 u, in the ratio 3 : 1. Is the mass of a chlorine atom 35 or 37?**

**A SIMPLE average ignores how common each isotope is:**
```
(35 + 37) / 2 = 36 u        — but this does not reflect nature
```

**The isotopes do NOT occur in equal amounts — ³⁵Cl is about 75%, ³⁷Cl about 25%. So we take a WEIGHTED AVERAGE:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> average atomic mass = Σ (mass of each isotope × its fractional abundance)
>
> = (35 × 75/100) + (37 × 25/100)
> = 105/4 + 37/4
> = 142/4
> = 35.5 u
> ```

> [!WARNING]
> **35.5 u does NOT mean any single chlorine atom has a fractional mass.**
>
> **It means that in, say, 1 million chlorine atoms there are 7.5 lakh ³⁵Cl and 2.5 lakh ³⁷Cl atoms, averaging 35.5 u.** The number describes the **element as it occurs in nature**, not any individual atom.

> [!NOTE]
> **Atoms are far too small to weigh in kilograms or grams.** Just as a grain of wheat is more sensibly weighed in milligrams, atomic masses use the **unified atomic mass unit (u)**. (It was earlier called the **atomic mass unit, amu**.)

> [!NOTE]
> **Homi Jehangir Bhabha**, an Indian physicist known as the **father of the Indian nuclear programme**, made pioneering contributions to atomic energy in India and established the **Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR)** and the **Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC)** — for **peaceful uses** of atomic energy: generating electricity, supporting agriculture, and advanced medical treatments.

### 8.9.2 Isobars

**Calcium (Z = 20), potassium (Z = 19) and argon (Z = 18) all have MASS NUMBER 40.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ISOBARS are atoms of DIFFERENT elements with the SAME mass number but DIFFERENT atomic numbers.**
>
> **The contrast to hold on to:**
> - **ISOTOPES: same Z, different A** — same element, different mass
> - **ISOBARS: same A, different Z** — different elements, same total nucleons

## The story does not end

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Even Bohr's model was later found to be not entirely correct.** Electrons do **not** follow well-defined paths like fixed Bohr orbits. Today we understand them as **"electron clouds"** around the nucleus — **we can predict the regions where they are MOST LIKELY to be, not exactly where they are.**

```
Dalton → Thomson → Rutherford → Bohr → Quantum mechanical → still being discovered
indivisible  plum      nuclear     energy      electron
 particle   pudding     model      levels       clouds
```
