## Where life may have begun

**Life is widely accepted to have originated in water** — and some researchers think in **small water pools with changing conditions** rather than in the oceans.

**Hot springs are living examples.** The **Puga Valley springs in Ladakh** stay near the **boiling point of water** even in a cold climate — conditions similar to early Earth about **3.5 billion years ago**. The organisms there are **thermophiles** — heat-loving, **unicellular** bacteria.

> [!NOTE]
> Scientists at the **Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences, Lucknow** found **calcium carbonate** forming rapidly around these springs. Those deposits may have **shielded early organic molecules from radiation and extreme conditions**, and may even have helped form **the first protective membrane — the barrier that defines a cell**.

## Levels of organisation

```
CELL → TISSUE → ORGAN → ORGAN SYSTEM → ORGANISM
```

- **Unicellular** — bacteria, yeast: **one cell** does everything
- **Multicellular** — plants, fish, birds, humans: **millions of cells** working together

**A group of similar cells doing similar work = a TISSUE.** Different tissues together = an **ORGAN**. Several organs = an **ORGAN SYSTEM** (nasal pores + nasal cavity + trachea + lungs = the respiratory system).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Even inside tissues and organs, the CELL remains the fundamental unit of structure and function.**
>
> **Why "unit"?** Because it is the **smallest thing that is alive**. Cut a cell apart and no piece of it lives; a cell on its own can.

## 2.1 How do we study cells?

### Limit of resolution

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **RESOLUTION = the ability to see two very close objects as separate and distinct.**
>
> Viewed from about **25 cm** (the near point of the human eye), two points about **0.1 mm** apart can just be told apart. Closer than that and they merge into one dot.
>
> **The limit of resolution of the human eye is 0.1 mm.**

**A cell is usually far smaller than that** — so cells were invisible until lenses arrived.

| Object | Size | Seen with |
|---|---|---|
| Chicken egg | about 10 cm | unaided eye |
| Fish egg, Amoeba | about 1 mm | unaided eye (just) |
| Most plant and animal cells | 10–100 µm | **light microscope** |
| Nucleus, most bacteria, mitochondrion | about 1–10 µm | light microscope |
| Smallest bacteria, viruses, ribosomes | 10–100 nm | **electron microscope** |
| Proteins, lipids, small molecules, atoms | 0.1–10 nm | electron microscope |

> [!NOTE]
> **1 mm = 1000 µm** and **1 µm = 1000 nm**. A **nanometre is one-billionth of a metre.**

### The microscope

**A convex lens, or a combination of lenses** — an **objective lens** near the specimen and an **eyepiece** near your eye — **magnifies** the object so it appears larger.

**Robert Hooke, 1665**, was the first to observe a cell, with a self-designed microscope of about **200–300X**. Examining a **thin slice of cork**, he saw small box-like compartments and named them **'cells'**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Total magnification = magnification of eyepiece × magnification of objective.**
>
> A 10X eyepiece with a 10X objective gives **100X** — the cell appears **100 times larger** than it is.

**Estimating a cell's real size:**

```
Estimated cell size = (diameter of field of view in µm) ÷ (number of cells across that diameter)
```

**Worked:** field of view is 5 mm = **5000 µm**; you count **25 onion peel cells** across it.
`5000 ÷ 25 =` **200 µm per cell.**

> [!TIP]
> **Why this works:** the cells sit shoulder to shoulder across a known distance, so the distance divided by the count is the width of one. It's exactly how you'd find the thickness of one sheet of paper by measuring a stack of 500.

### Three things microscope-makers improved

| Feature | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Resolution** | measure of **clarity** — how close two points can be and still look separate |
| **Contrast** | difference in **brightness** between parts of the object |
| **Magnification** | how much **larger** the image appears |

> [!WARNING]
> **Magnification without resolution is useless.** Blowing up a blurred image just gives you a bigger blur. That's why **resolution** is the real limit — and why **electron microscopes**, which use a **beam of electrons instead of light**, can reach the **nanometre scale**.

## 2.2 Structure of a cell

**Cells must interact with each other and with their surroundings. Those interactions happen at the CELL BOUNDARY.**

### 2.2.1 Cell membrane — the universal feature

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The CELL MEMBRANE (plasma membrane) is a thin boundary that surrounds a cell, protects its contents and DEFINES ITS INDIVIDUALITY.**
>
> It is **SELECTIVELY PERMEABLE** — it lets some substances through while blocking others.

**Every cell has one.** It is the one structure common to all cells everywhere.

#### The potato experiment

**Two roughly equal potato pieces, weighed first, then left an hour:**

| Beaker | Contents | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | plain water | potato piece **swells**, weight **increases** |
| **B** | 20% salt or sugar solution | potato piece **shrinks**, weight **decreases** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why:** the cell membrane lets **water** move in and out but **not** the salt or sugar molecules.
>
> **Water moves from where there is MORE water (dilute) to where there is LESS water (concentrated)** — until the two concentrations become equal.

> [!TIP]
> **Why weigh them first and cut them equal?** Because the *change* is the result, not the final weight. Equal starting sizes and recorded starting weights make the two pieces a **fair comparison** — otherwise you could not tell whether a difference came from the liquid or from the potato.

#### Diffusion vs osmosis

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **DIFFUSION** = net movement of particles from **higher to lower concentration**. Happens **even without a membrane** (dye spreading in water, fragrance spreading in air).
>
> **OSMOSIS** = the **diffusion of WATER across a SELECTIVELY PERMEABLE membrane**.
>
> **Osmosis is a special case of diffusion** — of water, through a membrane.

**The driving force in both is the CONCENTRATION GRADIENT** — a difference in concentration between two places.

**In plants, water from the soil enters root cells by osmosis.**

#### Three kinds of surrounding solution

| Outside vs inside | Name | What happens to the cell |
|---|---|---|
| solute outside `=` solute inside | **ISOTONIC** | no net water movement — cell unchanged |
| solute outside `<` solute inside | **HYPOTONIC** | water moves **in** — cell **swells** |
| solute outside `>` solute inside | **HYPERTONIC** | water moves **out** — cell **shrinks** |

> [!TIP]
> **Remember by the water, not the solute.** Water always goes **towards the more concentrated solution** — because that's where there's less water.

#### The fluid-mosaic model

**Structurally the membrane is extremely thin — about 7 to 10 nanometres** — and made of **lipids (fats) and proteins**.

- **LIPID BILAYER** — two layers of special fat molecules, with **water-attracting heads facing outwards** and **water-repelling tails facing inwards**, with **proteins embedded** in them
- **FLUID** — the molecules can **move sideways, flip and rotate** within the membrane
- **MOSAIC** — the molecules are arranged like **tiles in a mosaic**
- **Proteins act like gatekeepers**, helping selected substances pass through

> [!TIP]
> **Why heads out and tails in?** The cell has water outside *and* inside. The water-loving heads face both watery sides; the water-hating tails hide in the middle. **The bilayer assembles itself** — that geometry is the only stable arrangement.

### 2.2.2 Cell wall — the outer covering

**Plants, fungi and bacteria have an EXTRA layer outside the cell membrane: the CELL WALL.** Animal cells do not.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why plants need one:** plants generally **cannot move from place to place**, so they need a **rigid structure** to withstand wind and rain, keep leaves and flowers firm, hold their shape, and **stay upright**.
>
> **Why animals don't:** without a rigid wall, animal cells **change shape easily** — and that flexibility supports the **movement and functioning of animal tissues**.

**The cell wall is rigid but PERMEABLE** — water and dissolved minerals pass straight through.

> [!TIP]
> **Permeable wall + selectively permeable membrane = a working root.** The wall lets everything through to the membrane; the **membrane** does the choosing. That two-stage design is how roots absorb water and nutrients from soil.

**Made of CELLULOSE** — a carbohydrate built from many **glucose units linked together**. In our diet cellulose acts as **roughage**, helping digestion.

#### The Rhoeo / cheek cell comparison

| | Plant cell (onion peel, Rhoeo) | Animal cell (cheek) |
|---|---|---|
| In water | **box-shaped, regularly arranged** | **irregularly arranged** |
| In 20% sugar solution | **outer boundary stays the same**; inner content shrinks and pulls away from the wall | **shrinks considerably** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the difference?** Both lose water by osmosis. But the plant cell's **rigid cell wall holds the outer shape**, so only the membrane and contents pull inward, leaving a gap. **The animal cell has nothing rigid to hold, so the whole cell shrinks.**

> [!TIP]
> **The regular box shape of plant cells is itself evidence of the wall.** Cheek cells, with only a flexible membrane, take whatever shape they are squashed into.

## 2.3 The cell interior — a coordinated working system

**Most cells have three basic parts:**
- a **selectively permeable plasma membrane**
- a **semi-fluid, jelly-like cytoplasm**
- a **prominent nucleus**

**In the cytoplasm sit ORGANELLES** — sub-cellular components, most visible only under an **electron microscope**.

### Prokaryotic vs eukaryotic

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **PROKARYOTIC** (*pro* = primitive, *karyon* = nucleus) — **no well-defined nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles.** Most cellular activity happens **directly in the cytoplasm**. The genetic material sits in a region called the **NUCLEOID**. Example: **bacteria**.
>
> **EUKARYOTIC** (*eu* = true) — a **well-defined nucleus** and **several membrane-bound organelles**. Examples: **plant and animal cells**.

| Characteristic | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic |
|---|---|---|
| Primitive nucleus (nucleoid) | **present** | absent |
| Membrane-bound nucleus | absent | **present** |
| Membrane-bound organelles | absent | **present** |
| Diameter of a typical cell | **1 to 10 µm** | **10 to 100 µm** |
| Number of cells in the organism | usually **unicellular** | unicellular **or** multicellular |

| Structure | Bacterial cell | Plant cell | Animal cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell membrane | ✔ | ✔ | ✔ |
| Cell wall | ✔ | ✔ | ✘ |
| Cytoplasm | ✔ | ✔ | ✔ |
| Well-defined nucleus | ✘ | ✔ | ✔ |
| Nucleoid | ✔ | ✘ | ✘ |
| Membrane-bound organelles | ✘ | ✔ | ✔ |

> [!NOTE]
> **Acellular infectious agents** — too small even for a light microscope:
> - **Viruses** — genetic material **plus a protein coat**
> - **Viroids** — genetic material with **no protein coat**
> - **Prions** — **misfolded proteins** with **no genetic material at all**
>
> None of them is a cell, which is why they sit outside the cell theory.

> [!NOTE]
> **Cytoskeleton** — a network of fine fibres in eukaryotic cells that gives **structural support**, maintains **cell shape**, and enables **cell movement and internal transport**. Visible only under an electron microscope. The cytoplasm may also hold **cell inclusions** — stored starch, or crystals of calcium oxalate or silica in some plant cells.

### 2.3.1 The organelles

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why have organelles at all?** So the cell can carry out **many life processes independently AT THE SAME TIME**, each in its own compartment.
>
> **A cell is like a tiny living factory** — each part doing a specific job: building new materials, removing waste, providing energy.

#### Nucleus — house of coded instructions

- **Double-layered nuclear membrane** with **PORES** that allow material to move between nucleus and cytoplasm
- **NUCLEOLUS** — the dense round body inside, where **ribosomal subunits are synthesised**. The subunits leave for the cytoplasm, where **one large and one small subunit assemble into a ribosome**
- **CHROMOSOMES** — rod-shaped structures, visible **only when the cell is about to divide**. Made of **DNA and specific proteins**, they carry information for **inheritance of characters from parents to the next generation**
- **GENES** are the **functional segments of DNA**
- **CHROMATIN** — in a non-dividing cell the DNA sits as an **entangled mass of thread-like material**. When the cell is about to divide, **chromatin gets organised into chromosomes**

> [!TIP]
> **Why condense into chromosomes only at division?** Loose thread is easy to *read from* but impossible to *share out* without tangling. Winding it into compact rods is exactly how you'd pack a long cable before moving it.

> [!NOTE]
> **Mature human RBCs have NO nucleus (enucleate).** The space saved holds **more haemoglobin**, so each cell carries more oxygen. **The cost:** without a nucleus they **cannot repair or divide**, so they live only about **120 days**.

#### Ribosomes — the protein factories

**Tiny structures, either free in the cytoplasm or attached to the ER. They are the SITES OF PROTEIN SYNTHESIS.**

#### Endoplasmic reticulum — the manufacturing network

**A large organelle spreading like a network through the cytoplasm, CONTINUOUS with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope.** It synthesises and transports **proteins, fats (lipids) and some hormones**.

| Type | Look | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Rough ER (RER)** | **rough** — **ribosomes attached** to its surface | **protein synthesis and secretion** (e.g. pancreatic gland cells) |
| **Smooth ER (SER)** | **smooth** — no ribosomes | **synthesis and storage of fats and hormones** |

> [!TIP]
> **The names are literally what you see.** "Rough" is the bumpy texture the attached ribosomes give it under an electron microscope. So **rough = ribosomes = protein**.

#### Golgi apparatus — packaging and shipping

**Stacks of flattened, sac-like structures**, functionally linked to the ER, the cell membrane and other organelles.

**It acts like the cell's POST OFFICE — it MODIFIES, SORTS and PACKAGES proteins and lipids into VESICLES** for transport, secretion, or lysosome formation.

> [!NOTE]
> **Camillo Golgi** first observed it in **1898**, in the nerve cells of a **barn owl**, using special staining. Early microscopes could not resolve it clearly, so **many doubted it existed** — until **electron microscopes confirmed it decades later**. It was then named in his honour.
>
> **A nice illustration of the resolution point:** the structure was real all along; the instruments simply weren't good enough to settle the argument.

#### Lysosomes — the clean-up system

**Single membrane-bound sacs filled with ENZYMES** that break down unwanted **proteins, carbohydrates, fats — and even damaged parts of the cell**, keeping it clean and healthy.

**The breakdown products are released into the cytoplasm, where they may be REUSED** in other processes.

> [!NOTE]
> **Human sperm cells carry lysosomal enzymes.** When a sperm meets an egg, these enzymes **break down the egg's outer layer**, allowing fertilisation.

#### Mitochondria — the powerhouse

**Two membranes:**
- **outer membrane** — smooth and porous
- **inner membrane** — folded into finger-like projections called **CRISTAE**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the folds?** Cristae **increase the SURFACE AREA** available for the chemical reactions, so more energy production fits inside the same volume.
>
> **This is why a cell has many small mitochondria rather than one giant one** — many small units have far more total membrane area than one big one of the same volume.

**Inside mitochondria, glucose and other molecules are broken down to release energy during CELLULAR RESPIRATION. The energy is stored as ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) — the cell's ENERGY CURRENCY.**

#### Plastids — food synthesis and storage in plants

**Animals get food from their surroundings; plants make theirs.** Plants use **PLASTIDS**.

| Plastid | Pigment | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **CHLOROPLAST** | **chlorophyll** (green) | absorbs sunlight for **photosynthesis** |
| **CHROMOPLAST** (*chroma* = colour) | yellow, orange, red | **bright colours** in flowers and fruits |
| **LEUCOPLAST** (*leukos* = white) | **none — colourless** | **stores** starch, oils or proteins |

**Chloroplast structure:** double-membrane-bound (like mitochondria). Inside is a semi-fluid **STROMA**, containing **disc-shaped membrane structures that hold the chlorophyll**. The sugars made are stored in the stroma, along with **starch granules**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why chromoplasts matter to the plant:** bright colours **attract pollinators** for pollination, and **fruit-eating animals** that disperse seeds. The colour is not decoration — it is advertising.

> [!NOTE]
> **Mitochondria and plastids both have their OWN DNA and ribosomes**, so they can make some of their own proteins — features shared with certain **bacteria**.
>
> **What this suggests:** both **share an evolutionary history with single-celled organisms**. Long ago they were probably free-living cells that came to live inside another cell.

#### Vacuoles — storage and support

**In a mature plant cell there is usually ONE LARGE CENTRAL VACUOLE**, surrounded by a single selectively permeable membrane and filled with a watery fluid called **CELL SAP**. It stores **water, minerals, sugars and waste**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why plants wilt.** By storing large amounts of water, the vacuole **maintains pressure inside the cell**, keeping it firm. Without enough water, **the vacuole loses water → cells become less firm → the plant wilts.**
>
> **In animal cells** vacuoles are sometimes present, much smaller, used for **temporary storage**.

## 2.4 How cells grow and divide

**A cut heals; fallen hair grows back. Cells grow and divide to replace old, dead or damaged cells.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When your body grows, it is NOT mainly because cells get bigger.**
>
> **Cells can grow only up to a certain size** — beyond that, the surface area cannot keep up with the volume it must supply. So **growth happens because cells DIVIDE to form new cells.**

**Every day an estimated hundreds of billions of cells in our body are replaced — about 1% of all our cells.**

**Onion root tip experiment:** grow onion roots in water for 5–6 days, fix the freshly grown tips in **aceto-alcohol (glacial acetic acid : ethanol :: 1 : 3)** for 24 hours, preserve in **70% ethanol**, soften with **dilute HCl**, stain with **aceto-carmine**, warm gently, then **squash under a coverslip** and observe.

**What you see:** cells at **different stages of cell division**, all in one field — because the root tip divides **continuously**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why the root TIP?** That's where growth is happening, so it's where you're guaranteed to find dividing cells. **Why squash?** To spread the cells into a single layer you can see through.

### 2.4.1 Two types of cell division

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **CELL DIVISION is the process by which new cells are formed from PRE-EXISTING cells.** It allows organisms to **grow, repair damaged tissues and reproduce**.

| | **MITOSIS** | **MEIOSIS** |
|---|---|---|
| Produces | **2** daughter cells | **4** daughter cells |
| Chromosome number | **same** as parent | **half** the parent's |
| Daughter cells are | **genetically identical** to the parent | **varied** |
| Where | body cells everywhere | **only reproductive organs** |
| For | growth, repair, maintenance, asexual reproduction | **sexual reproduction and genetic diversity** |

**Meiosis is a TWO-STEP process.** The **first division** halves the chromosome number, giving two cells. The **second division is similar to mitosis**, splitting each into two — giving **four cells, each with half the chromosomes**.

**Where meiosis happens:**
- **Animals (including humans)** — **testes** (sperm) and **ovaries** (eggs)
- **Plants** — **anthers** (pollen grains, which later produce sperm cells) and **ovaries** (egg cells)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why gametes must be halved.** At fertilisation two gametes combine. **If each carried the full number, every generation would DOUBLE the chromosome count.** Halving first means fertilisation **restores** the original number exactly.
>
> **And why this creates variation:** each gamete gets a different half, so children **resemble their parents but are not exactly the same**.

### When division goes wrong

| Error in | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Mitosis** | **uncontrolled cell division** → **tumours**, abnormal chromosome numbers in body cells |
| **Meiosis** | **genetic disorders**, developmental problems, distinctive physical features; also **early pregnancy loss or reduced fertility** |

## 2.5 Cell theory — the unifying principle of biology

| Year | Scientist | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **1838** | **Matthias Schleiden** (German botanist) | **all plants** are made of cells |
| **1839** | **Theodor Schwann** (German zoologist) | **all animals** are made of cells |
| **1855** | **Rudolf Virchow** (German scientist) | **new cells form only from pre-existing cells** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE CLASSICAL CELL THEORY**
> 1. **All living organisms are made up of one or more cells.**
> 2. **The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living beings.**
> 3. **All cells arise from pre-existing cells.**
>
> **Why it is called unifying:** it covers **everything from bacteria to humans** with one statement, and point 3 explains **life's continuity** — every cell alive today traces back through an unbroken chain of divisions.

### 2.5.1 Do cells live forever?

**No. Every cell has a definite life span.** Cells grow and divide in a controlled way, stay in the right place, do their job, and **die when no longer needed**. Dead cells are replaced by new ones doing the same work.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **CONTACT INHIBITION:** in many animal cells, **division stops when cells touch neighbouring cells.**
>
> **This is the body's natural brake on tumour formation.** **Cancer cells LOSE this control** and keep dividing uncontrollably, forming tumours.
>
> **Plant cells do NOT show contact inhibition** — their rigid cell walls mean they follow a different growth pattern.

> [!NOTE]
> **How cancer spreads.** Normal cells grow, age and die in a controlled manner. When that system breaks down, abnormal cells divide uncontrollably, forming **tumours — benign or malignant**. **Cancerous (malignant) tumours can invade nearby tissues and spread to other parts of the body to form new tumours.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Programmed Cell Death (PCD)** — a genetically regulated, organised process of **selective cell destruction**, essential for normal development, cellular quality control and immune function.
>
> **In a developing embryo, PCD forms the FINGERS by eliminating the cells between them — without it we would have webbed hands.** Cell death here is not damage; it is sculpture.

> [!NOTE]
> **Totipotency.** In **1902 Gottlieb Haberlandt** proposed that **any living plant cell — even a fully mature one from a permanent tissue — can develop into a complete plant** given suitable nutrients and conditions. That ability is **totipotency**, and his idea founded **Plant Tissue Culture Technology**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Arun Kumar Sharma**, an Indian botanist, was known for his work on **chromosomes**, plant taxonomy, evolution and development, and invented many lab methods for studying plant chromosomes. He received the **Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar award** and the **Padma Bhushan**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Cell culture** — growing plant or animal cells outside the body in a **nutrient-rich medium**, under controlled **temperature, acidity/alkalinity, moisture and sterile conditions**. Crucial for studying how cells work and for producing **biochemicals, food, medicines and vaccines**.

> [!NOTE]
> **The synthetic-DNA experiment (J. Craig Venter, 2010).** The team studied the complete DNA sequence of the bacterium *Mycoplasma mycoides*, **chemically synthesised an exact copy**, removed the DNA from a closely related bacterium while keeping the rest of the cell intact, and **inserted the synthetic DNA**. The cell grew and divided following the new instructions.
>
> **What it showed: DNA controls the structure and activities of a cell.**
> **What it did NOT show:** they did **not** create a cell from scratch — **only the DNA was synthetic**; every other part came from an existing living cell.
