## What a tissue is, and why it exists

**Life begins when a single cell divides many times.** Those cells gradually form skin (protection), muscles (movement), bones (support), nerves (control and coordination) and every other organ.

```
CELL → TISSUE → ORGAN → ORGAN SYSTEM → ORGANISM
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A TISSUE is a group of cells, SIMILAR IN STRUCTURE, that work together to perform a SPECIFIC FUNCTION.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why tissues exist: DIVISION OF LABOUR.**
>
> In a **unicellular** organism like amoeba, **one cell must do everything**. In a **multicellular** organism, different groups of cells each do one job well.
>
> **Specialising increases EFFICIENCY** and makes complex life processes possible — muscle tissue for movement, nervous tissue for messages, xylem for water, phloem for food.

## 3.1 Why plant and animal tissues differ

| | **Plants** | **Animals** |
|---|---|---|
| Movement | mostly **fixed in one place** | generally **move** (though sponges don't) |
| Support | **cell wall** gives rigidity and strength | no wall — cells **change shape easily**, suiting locomotion |
| Nutrition | tissues that **use solar energy** to make food (photosynthesis) | tissues that **digest food** obtained from outside |
| Growth | growth tissues located in **specific zones**, active throughout life | growth is more uniform and stops at maturity |

> [!TIP]
> **Every difference traces back to one fact: plants can't move.** A fixed organism must be **strong enough to stand up** and must **make its own food where it stands**. A moving organism must be **flexible** and can **go and find food**.

> [!NOTE]
> **B. G. L. Swamy**, a renowned Indian botanist, contributed to **plant morphology and anatomy**. His Kannada book **Hasuru Honnu** — a blend of science, satire and culture describing botanical excursions in the **Western Ghats** — won the **Kendra Sahitya Akademi Award in 1978**.

## 3.2 Tissues for growth in plants

**Plants grow in three ways:**
- **increase in LENGTH** — height of stem, depth of roots
- **increase in GIRTH** — thickness of stem
- **REGROWTH** after cutting or grazing

**All three need actively dividing cells — a MERISTEMATIC TISSUE.**

### 3.2.1 Apical meristem — growth in length

**The onion root experiment:**

| Jar | Treatment | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | roots left alone | roots **keep growing** in length |
| **B** | root tips **cut by 1 cm** on day 3 | roots **stop growing** after the cut |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Conclusion: roots grow ONLY from their tips**, because the tips contain cells that **divide continuously**.
>
> This matches what you saw in Chapter 2 — **mitosis in onion root tips**. Shoot tips work the same way.

**APICAL MERISTEM = the growth zone at the tips of roots and shoots. It makes the plant grow in LENGTH.**

> [!TIP]
> **Why cutting the tip is the decisive test.** If growth happened all along the root, removing 1 cm from the end would barely matter. Growth stopping completely proves that **all the dividing cells were in that 1 cm**.

### 3.2.2 Lateral meristem — growth in girth

**Dicot stems get thicker over time.** Cut a trunk and you see **ANNUAL GROWTH RINGS**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **LATERAL MERISTEM = actively dividing cells arranged IN A RING inside the stem.** They divide and produce new cells **inside and outside in a concentric manner**, increasing the diameter.

**Reading the rings:**
- **Counting the rings** estimates the **age of the tree**
- **Wide rings** = a year of favourable growth conditions; **narrow rings** = unfavourable
- So rings also record the **climate** the tree grew through

> [!TIP]
> **Why the rings exist at all:** the lateral meristem works fast in the good season and slowly in the harsh one, and the two kinds of wood look different. **One ring = one cycle of good-then-poor growth = one year.**

### 3.2.3 Intercalary meristem — regrowth after cutting

**Cut the tip of a young stem** and it stops growing in length — but **new branches arise from the NODES**.

- **NODE** = the point on a stem where **branches or leaves arise**
- **INTERNODE** = the part of stem **between two nodes**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **INTERCALARY MERISTEM sits at the BASE OF THE INTERNODE, just above the node.**
>
> **This is why a cut hedge grows back bushier**, and why **grass grows again after mowing or grazing**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why grass survives grazing but a tree seedling does not.** Grass keeps its growth zone at the **base**, below where an animal bites. A plant that grows only from its **tip** loses its entire growth machinery the moment the tip is eaten. **It's a design that anticipates being eaten.**

### The three meristems

| Meristem | Where | Gives |
|---|---|---|
| **APICAL** | tips of roots and shoots | **length** |
| **LATERAL** | in a ring along the circumference of stems | **girth** |
| **INTERCALARY** | at the nodes / base of internodes (grasses) | **regeneration** after cutting |

### What meristematic cells look like

- **small** cells, **thin** cell walls
- **large, prominent nucleus**
- **dense cytoplasm** with many organelles
- **vacuoles generally absent**
- **tightly packed**, little or no intercellular space

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why no vacuoles?** A big vacuole is mostly **water** — storage, not machinery. A cell whose job is **continuous, rapid division** needs its volume filled with **cytoplasm and organelles** instead. Every feature on that list serves the same purpose: **maximum machinery, minimum dead weight, ready to divide again.**

### Differentiation

**After division, some new cells stay meristematic; others LOSE the ability to divide.** Those cells change in structure and function and become **PERMANENT TISSUES**, specialised for support, transport or storage.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **DIFFERENTIATION = the process by which meristematic tissue becomes specialised permanent tissue.**

### 3.2.4 Permanent tissues

**SIMPLE** = made of only **one type of cell**. **COMPLEX** = made of **more than one type**.

#### (i) Protective tissue — EPIDERMIS

**The outermost layer of the plant body** — a **tightly packed, single layer of flat, rectangular cells**. It protects against **mechanical injury, water loss, harmful microorganisms and extreme conditions**.

- **CUTICLE** — a waxy layer of **cutin** covering epidermal cells. In **very dry habitats** it is **thick**, to reduce water loss through transpiration. It also guards against injury and parasites.
- **ROOT HAIR** — hair-like projections from root epidermal cells that **increase the surface area** for absorbing water and minerals from soil
- **STOMATA** — pores in the leaf epidermis for **gaseous exchange** and **transpiration**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **What transpiration does for the plant:**
> 1. **Creates a TRANSPIRATION PULL in the xylem**, which is what drags water up the plant
> 2. Helps **eliminate wastes** from the plant body
>
> **Why the pull works:** water evaporating at the top leaves a gap, and because water molecules stick to each other, the whole column is pulled up behind it — like sucking through a straw, but powered by evaporation instead of lungs.

> [!TIP]
> **A thick cuticle is an advantage in a desert but a disadvantage underwater.** In a desert, water loss is the enemy — sealing the surface saves the plant. Underwater, there is no shortage of water, but there **is** a shortage of dissolved gases, and a thick waxy layer would **block the gas exchange** the plant needs. **The same feature, opposite verdicts, because the limiting resource is different.**

#### (ii) Supporting tissue — SIMPLE permanent tissues

| Tissue | Cells | Walls | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PARENCHYMA** | **living**, loosely packed with **intercellular spaces** | **thin** | **stores food**; **photosynthesis** in green parts; in aquatic plants, specialised parenchyma forms **air spaces that help them float** |
| **COLLENCHYMA** | **living** | **unevenly thickened at the CORNERS**, from **pectin** deposits | **support WITH FLEXIBILITY** — stems and tendrils bend without breaking |
| **SCLERENCHYMA** | **mostly DEAD** | **thick**, from **LIGNIN** deposits | **hardness and strength** — the woody structure |

**Where sclerenchyma is found:** stems, leaf veins, and hard coverings of seeds and nuts — **coconut husk, walnut shell**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why a fresh twig bends but a dry twig breaks.** A fresh twig has plenty of **living collenchyma**, thickened with **pectin** — a chemical that gives flexibility **like rubber**. Drying kills those cells and leaves only rigid, lignified material, which **snaps instead of bending**.
>
> **Same reason coconut-husk fibres are hard and brittle while coriander stalks are soft and flexible:** husk is **sclerenchyma (lignin)**, coriander stalk is **collenchyma (pectin)**.

> [!TIP]
> **Living vs dead is the giveaway.** Parenchyma and collenchyma stay **alive** because they still do jobs — storing, photosynthesising, bending. Sclerenchyma's job is purely **structural**, and a thick lignified box is just as strong empty. So the cell dies and the wall stays. **A wall doesn't need to be alive to hold something up.**

#### (iii) Conducting tissue — COMPLEX permanent tissues

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **XYLEM and PHLOEM are called COMPLEX because they are made of SEVERAL DIFFERENT CELL TYPES working together.**

**XYLEM — transports WATER and MINERALS from roots to the rest of the plant, and provides strength.**

| Component | Living? | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Tracheids** | dead | **tubular, thick-walled** |
| **Vessels** | dead | **tubular, thick-walled** |
| **Xylem parenchyma** | **LIVING** — the only living part of xylem | storage |
| **Xylem fibres** | dead | strength |

**Tracheids, vessels and xylem fibres are primarily sclerenchymatous.**

**PHLOEM — transports FOOD from leaves to the rest of the plant. Mostly made of LIVING cells.**

| Component | Job |
|---|---|
| **Sieve tubes** | long tubular cells **joined end to end by PERFORATED walls** — they carry the food |
| **Companion cells** | specialised **parenchyma** cells that **regulate the sieve tube's activity**, mainly by **monitoring the loading and unloading of sugars** |
| **Phloem parenchyma** | **stores** food, resin, tannins and latex |
| **Phloem fibres** | primarily **sclerenchymatous** — provide **strength** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why xylem cells can be dead but phloem cells cannot.**
>
> **Xylem** is driven from outside — by the **transpiration pull** at the leaves. It needs only **open, strong, hollow pipes**, and a dead cell with a thick wall makes a perfect pipe.
>
> **Phloem** must **actively load and unload sugars** at particular places, in **both directions** depending on the season. That needs living machinery — which is exactly why sieve tubes keep **companion cells** alongside them.

### Tissue systems

| System | What it is |
|---|---|
| **DERMAL** | the **outer covering** — protects inner parts and reduces water loss |
| **GROUND** | the **main body** between dermal and conducting tissue: parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma |
| **VASCULAR** | the **conducting tissues** — xylem and phloem |

> [!NOTE]
> **Cork and bark.** In young plants the outer protective layer is a **single-layered epidermis**. As the plant ages, some cells **below the stem epidermis** gain the ability to divide, acting as lateral meristem — the **CORK CAMBIUM**. Its division produces **cork cells**: **dead, compactly arranged, and impermeable to water and gases**. This forms the **bark** of the tree.

> [!NOTE]
> **Sipra Guha Mukherjee**, with **S. C. Maheshwari**, made a breakthrough in **plant tissue culture** — developing a **complete plant through anther culture** on an artificial nutrient medium under controlled conditions. Their work contributed greatly to **crop improvement**.

## 3.3 Animal tissues

**Four main types: EPITHELIAL, CONNECTIVE, MUSCULAR, NERVOUS.**

### 3.3.1 Epithelial tissue

**Forms the outer covering of the body (skin) and lines internal organs** — mouth, lungs, blood vessels, intestine.

**Structure: closely packed cells with very little space between them.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why "closely packed" matters:** it **prevents germs entering** and **reduces water loss**. A barrier with gaps is not a barrier.

| Function | Structure | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **EXCHANGE** — rapid diffusion of liquids and gases | **single layer of thin, FLAT cells** | lining of blood vessels and lungs |
| **PROTECTION** — from mechanical injury, friction, microbes | **MANY layers**; outer cells flat and tightly packed | skin, mouth, oesophagus |
| **SECRETION** — mucus, enzymes, hormones, sweat, saliva | cells specialised for producing and releasing substances; **cuboidal or columnar** | salivary glands, sweat glands, stomach lining |
| **SENSORY** — smell, taste, sound, balance | specialised **receptor cells with hair-like CILIA** | nostrils, taste buds, inner ear |
| **ABSORPTION** — uptake of nutrients and water | **single layer of TALL, PILLAR-LIKE cells**, often with hair-like structures | lining of the small intestine |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The structure–function link is exact, every time:**
> - **Exchange needs THIN** — diffusion across a thick barrier is slow, so lung and blood-vessel lining is one flat cell deep.
> - **Protection needs THICK** — many layers means the outer ones can be worn away and replaced.
> - **Absorption needs SURFACE AREA** — tall pillar cells with hair-like projections pack far more absorbing surface into the same length of intestine.
>
> **You cannot have thin and protective at once** — which is why skin and lung lining look nothing alike.

### 3.3.2 Connective tissue

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **CONNECTIVE TISSUE connects and supports other tissues.**
>
> **All connective tissues share a plan: cells scattered in a MATRIX.** What makes blood and bone so different is the **matrix**:
> - **blood** — matrix is **watery, soft, jelly-like** (plasma) → **fluid**
> - **bone** — matrix is **hard, solid, rigid**, containing **calcium and phosphorus compounds** → **strength and rigidity**

**Blood:**
- **Plasma** = **55%** of total volume; **formed elements** = **45%**
- **RBCs** — red colour from **haemoglobin**, an **iron-rich protein**; live about **4 months** and are replaced regularly
- **WBCs** — collect at infected areas, causing **pus, inflammation, redness and swelling**
- **Platelets** — help in **blood clotting** at the site of injury

**Everyday evidence:**

| Experience | What is happening |
|---|---|
| A cut bleeds red, then clots | **haemoglobin** gives the colour; **platelets** form the clot |
| Skin infection turns red and swollen | **WBCs** gathering at the site |
| Running makes you breathe faster and go red | muscles need **more oxygen**, so breathing quickens and **blood flow increases** |

**Other connective tissues:**

| Tissue | Feel | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **BONE** | hard, rigid | **strength, support, protection** |
| **CARTILAGE** | soft, flexible, springs back | **flexibility**; **cushions the ends of bones** for shock absorption |
| **TENDON** | — | connects **MUSCLE to BONE** → brings about movement |
| **LIGAMENT** | — | connects **BONE to BONE** → **stability**, limits movement, helps **prevent dislocation** |

> [!WARNING]
> **Tendon vs ligament is the classic mix-up.** **Ten**don → **T**o the bone from **musc**le. **Liga**ment → **li**nks bone to bone.
>
> **Why the difference matters:** a tendon must **transmit a pull**, so it runs from the pulling thing to the pulled thing. A ligament must **stop** a joint going too far, so it runs between the two bones it restrains — which is why your leg stops at a limit when you raise it.

> [!NOTE]
> **Stem cells in the bone marrow** can divide and make new cells. In a **bone marrow transplant**, stem cells from a healthy person are given to patients with blood cancers like **leukaemia** or disorders such as **thalassaemia**.

### 3.3.3 Muscular tissue

| | **SKELETAL** | **SMOOTH** | **CARDIAC** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control | **VOLUNTARY** | **INVOLUNTARY** | **INVOLUNTARY** |
| Cell shape | **long, cylindrical**, unbranched | **spindle-shaped** | **cylindrical and BRANCHED** |
| Nuclei | **MULTInucleate** | **single** | **single** |
| Striations | **striated** (light and dark bands) | **none** | **faint** |
| Where | attached to the **skeleton** | stomach, intestines | **only in the heart** |
| Job | running, writing, lifting | **slow, continuous** movements like digestion | beats **rhythmically without fatigue**, lifelong |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why cardiac muscle never tires.** It has a **very high number of mitochondria** and an **abundant blood supply** — so ATP is produced continuously and oxygen never runs short. Skeletal muscle, worked hard, outruns its oxygen supply and fatigues.
>
> **Why cardiac fibres are BRANCHED:** branching lets the contraction signal spread sideways through the whole heart wall at once, so the chamber squeezes **as one unit**, not fibre by fibre.

### 3.3.4 Nervous tissue

**Forms the body's CONTROL AND COORDINATION network.** The **brain** is the control centre, coordinating activities, memory and responses.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Muscles cannot function independently — they receive instructions from nervous tissue.** During exercise the brain signals the heart to beat faster to meet increased oxygen demand.

**NEURONS (nerve cells) are specialised to RECEIVE, PROCESS and TRANSMIT messages.** Three main parts:

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| **CELL BODY** | contains the **nucleus**; controls the cell's activities |
| **DENDRITES** | **receive** signals from other neurons |
| **AXON** | a **long fibre** carrying the message away, ending at **axon terminals** which pass it to other cells |

> [!TIP]
> **The shape is the function.** **Many short dendrites** = many inputs collected. **One long axon** = the message delivered far away, intact. That is why a neuron looks like nothing else in the body.

## 3.4 The musculoskeletal system

**Made of bones, muscles, joints, cartilage, tendons and ligaments.** It lets us **stand upright, move, maintain posture and protect delicate organs** — and it works **under the control of the nervous system**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How a movement actually happens:**
> ```
> nerve signal → MUSCLE contracts → TENDON transmits the force → BONE moves at a JOINT
> ```
> **Joints allow movement but cannot move bones themselves** — muscles do the pulling.

**On average the adult human skeleton is about 12–15% of body weight**, varying with age, gender and body composition. Adult males average about **40–50% muscle**, adult females about **30–40%**.

## 3.5 Types of joints

**A JOINT is a junction between two or more bones.**

| Joint | How it works | Movement allowed | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BALL AND SOCKET** | rounded head of one bone fits into a **shallow hollow** of another | **forward, backward, sideways and circular** — freest of all | **shoulder** (with the collarbone it forms the **shoulder girdle**), hip |
| **HINGE** | like a **door hinge** | **one direction only** — bend and straighten | **elbow**; **knee** (protected by the **kneecap**) |
| **PIVOT** | like a **doorknob turning in its socket** | **side-to-side rotation** | **skull on the backbone** — shaking your head 'no' |
| **FIXED** | bones joined immovably | **none** | bones of the **skull** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Freedom is traded against stability.** The shoulder moves in every direction — and is the joint most easily dislocated. The skull's fixed joints allow nothing at all — which is exactly what keeps the **brain safe even when the body moves**.
>
> **Ask of any joint: what does it have to do?** A knee must bear weight in one plane, so it's a hinge. A head must scan side to side, so it's a pivot.

## 3.6 Skeletal system

**A framework of bones providing strength and protecting delicate internal organs** — skull, vertebral column, rib cage.

**VERTEBRAL COLUMN (backbone/spine):** a flexible column of small bones called **VERTEBRAE**, running from the base of the skull. It supports the body and lets us stand upright.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Between each vertebra is a CARTILAGE DISC**, acting as a **cushion** and allowing **flexibility** — so we can bend and twist **without injuring the spinal cord** inside.
>
> **The design problem it solves:** the spine must be **stiff enough to hold you up** but **flexible enough to bend**, while **protecting a delicate cord**. Many small bones with springy pads between them does all three; one solid rod would do only the first.

**RIB CAGE: 12 pairs of ribs**, attached to the **spine at the back** and the **breastbone (sternum) in front**, joined by **flexible cartilage**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why a protective cage must be able to move.** The cartilage lets the rib cage **expand and contract during breathing**, changing the space in the chest and so **moving air in and out of the lungs**.
>
> **That's why rib injury makes breathing painful and difficult** — you cannot breathe without moving the broken part.

> [!NOTE]
> **Yoga**, described in ancient Indian texts, includes physical postures, breathing and meditation. Research shows it improves **flexibility, posture and breathing**, reduces stress and helps prevent lifestyle diseases. **21 June is International Yoga Day.**
>
> **Correct posture, proper nutrition, regular exercise and yoga** keep bones strong, muscles fit and joints flexible.

## Totipotency — one cell to a whole plant

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In 1958, F. C. Steward showed that single cells from the vascular PHLOEM of a carrot can regenerate a whole plant** — the first person to do so.

**What he did:** grew carrot phloem cells in a **nutrient medium containing simple sugars and hormones**. The cells **divided into a mass of unspecialised cells**, which then divided and **differentiated into roots, shoot and finally a complete plant**.

```
mature phloem cell
   → DEDIFFERENTIATION  (the cell regains the ability to divide)
   → an undifferentiated mass of unspecialised cells
   → REDIFFERENTIATION  (with nutrients and growth chemicals)
   → roots, shoot, complete plant
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **TOTIPOTENCY = the ability of a mature cell to dedifferentiate, divide and redifferentiate into a whole new organism.**
>
> **Why this is remarkable:** it means **differentiation is not a one-way door** in plants. The phloem cell still carried **every instruction** for building a whole plant — it had simply stopped using most of them. This is **similar to the ability of a zygote** to divide and differentiate into an entire organism.

**His conditions experiment:**

| Light | Air | Medium | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| — | — | **solid** medium + nutrients | fresh weight **reduced** |
| yes | yes | **liquid** medium + nutrients | **20% increase** |
| — | — | **liquid** medium + nutrients | **reduced** |

> [!TIP]
> **What the table tells you:** the medium alone isn't enough — the **liquid** medium worked only when the other conditions were right. **Liquid beats solid** because cells are surrounded by nutrients on all sides and can be stirred apart into single cells; but without **light and air** even the best medium gives no growth. **Change one thing at a time and the reason for each result becomes readable.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Crown gall disease** — tumour-like swellings on plant stems from **rapid, uncontrolled cell division**, caused by the bacterium ***Agrobacterium tumefaciens***.
>
> **Instead of only trying to cure it, scientists studied HOW the bacterium transfers its genetic material into plant cells.** Today *Agrobacterium* is used as a **tool** to introduce useful genes into plants — for valuable phytochemicals, improved crops and disease-resistant varieties.
>
> **The lesson: a disease mechanism, once understood, became a technique.**

```

WHY      division of labour → efficiency

PLANT TISSUES
  meristematic   apical (length) · lateral (girth, annual rings) · intercalary (regrowth)
                 small cells, thin walls, big nucleus, dense cytoplasm, NO vacuoles
  differentiation  meristematic → permanent

  permanent, simple    parenchyma (living, thin wall, storage + photosynthesis + floating)
                       collenchyma (living, pectin at corners, FLEXIBLE support)
                       sclerenchyma (dead, LIGNIN, hard strength — husk, shells)
  permanent, complex   XYLEM  water + minerals up; tracheids, vessels, fibres (dead),
                              xylem parenchyma (only living part)
                       PHLOEM food both ways; sieve tubes + companion cells,
                              phloem parenchyma + fibres; mostly LIVING
  epidermis      cuticle (water loss) · root hair (surface area) · stomata (gas + transpiration)
  tissue systems dermal · ground · vascular

ANIMAL TISSUES
  epithelial   thin+flat = exchange · many layers = protection · cuboidal/columnar =
               secretion · ciliated = sensory · tall pillar = absorption
  connective   cells in a MATRIX — blood (fluid) · bone (rigid, Ca + P) ·
               cartilage (soft, cushions) · tendon (muscle→bone) · ligament (bone→bone)

```
