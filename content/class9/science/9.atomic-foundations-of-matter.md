## The puzzle to start with

**Hydrogen gas is combustible; oxygen supports combustion. Water neither burns nor helps burning — it puts out fire.** Properties of elements are not retained in the compound.

**And yet — the MASS of the water formed equals the sum of the masses of the hydrogen and oxygen that combined.** Something is conserved even when everything else changes.

## 9.1 The Law of Conservation of Mass

**A physical change.** Weigh a beaker of water, add a spatula of salt, weigh again, then stir until it dissolves.

**The mass of the solution equals the sum of the masses of water and salt.** **Dissolving changes nothing about the total mass** — and the same is true of tearing paper into pieces.

**A chemical change — vinegar and baking soda.**

```
Vinegar + Baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate) → Carbon dioxide + other substances
```

| Set-up | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — OPEN** | tip the baking soda straight into the flask | brisk effervescence; **the final reading is LOWER than the initial** |
| **2 — CLOSED** | tie a balloon of baking soda over the flask's mouth, then lift it so the soda falls in | brisk effervescence **inflates the balloon**; **final reading = initial reading** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why set-up 1 seems to lose mass: the CARBON DIOXIDE GAS produced escapes into the room.** It has not vanished — it has simply left the balance.
>
> **Set-up 2 keeps it, so nothing leaves and the mass is unchanged.** The apparent violation was a **leak, not a law being broken.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE LAW OF CONSERVATION OF MASS: matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.**
>
> Proposed by **Antoine Lavoisier in 1789**, the **Father of Modern Chemistry**: *"in every operation an equal quantity of matter exists both before and after the operation."*

> [!TIP]
> **Why the flask AND balloon must both stay on the balance:** small traces of baking soda may remain stuck to the balloon. Weighing both together means nothing is missed either before or after.
>
> **And why small differences don't disprove the law:** any measurement carries an uncertainty of about ±1 in the last digit. **A variation within experimental error is not evidence of a mass change.**

**A second verification — sodium sulfate + barium chloride:**

```
Sodium sulfate + Barium chloride → Barium sulfate + Sodium chloride
```

**A white precipitate of barium sulfate forms — and the total mass is unchanged.** No gas is produced here, so an open system is fine.

**Worked:** 4.0 g calcium carbonate + 2.92 g hydrochloric acid, in a closed container, give 1.76 g carbon dioxide + 0.72 g water + 4.44 g calcium chloride.
```
reactants: 4.0 + 2.92                = 6.92 g
products:  1.76 + 0.72 + 4.44        = 6.92 g       ✔ the law is obeyed
```

**Worked:** 12 g carbon + 32 g oxygen → 44 g carbon dioxide. How much CO₂ from 2.4 g of carbon?
```
1 g of carbon gives 44/12 g of CO₂
2.4 g gives (44/12) × 2.4 = 8.8 g of carbon dioxide
```

> [!WARNING]
> **Burning 10 g of ethanol in an OPEN beaker leaves no residue — the law is NOT violated.**
>
> The products are **carbon dioxide and water vapour**, both gases, which **escape into the air**. Weigh them and the total is unchanged; let them leave and it only looks as though matter has disappeared. **This is exactly the vinegar-and-baking-soda lesson in another form.**

## 9.2 The Law of Constant Proportions

**Purify water from a river, a borewell or the ocean, analyse it, and you always find hydrogen and oxygen in a mass ratio of 1 : 8.** Decompose 9 g of pure water from any source and you get **1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen** — every time.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE LAW OF CONSTANT PROPORTIONS (Law of Definite Proportions, Proust's Law): in any compound formed by two or more elements, the elements combine in a FIXED RATIO BY MASS, irrespective of the source of the compound.**
>
> Proposed by **Joseph Proust**, soon after Lavoisier. He showed that **copper carbonate always contains copper, carbon and oxygen in the same proportion by mass, however it was prepared or wherever it was found**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Cinnabar** — a red pigment from rocks, known in India as **hingula**, used for painting and colouring since ancient times. Many civilisations found that heating it yields **mercury and sulfur** in mass percentages of about **86.22% and 13.78%** — and that grinding mercury and sulfur together in that ratio re-forms cinnabar. (**Both are toxic**, so the process never became widespread.)
>
> **The same fixed ratio, found independently by separate civilisations** — which is what a law of nature looks like.

**Worked:** NaCl contains sodium and chlorine in the mass ratio 23 : 35.5. How much chlorine reacts completely with 46 g of sodium?
```
chlorine = (35.5 / 23) × 46 = 71 g
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the law holds for COMPOUNDS but not for MIXTURES.**
>
> In a **compound** the atoms are joined by chemical bonds in a **fixed whole-number ratio** — you cannot add a little extra oxygen to water and still have water.
>
> In a **mixture** the components are merely **put together in any proportion you like**. Salt water can be weak or strong; both are still salt water.
>
> **Fixed ratio is a consequence of BONDING**, and mixtures have none.

## 9.3 Dalton's Atomic Theory

**The two laws above needed an explanation — and Dalton's 1808 theory supplied one.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A POSTULATE is a fundamental assumption accepted as true without formal proof, from which further ideas are developed.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **DALTON'S POSTULATES**
> 1. **All matter is made of very tiny particles called ATOMS**, which participate in chemical reactions.
> 2. **Atoms are indivisible and cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.**
> 3. **Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties.**
> 4. **Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties.**
> 5. **Atoms combine in the ratio of simple whole numbers to form compounds.**
> 6. **The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How the postulates explain the two laws:**
> - **Conservation of mass** ← postulate 2. During a reaction, **atoms merely REARRANGE**; none is created or destroyed, so the total mass cannot change.
> - **Constant proportions** ← postulates 3, 5 and 6. If every atom of an element has the **same mass** and they combine in a **fixed whole-number ratio**, then the **mass ratio must be fixed too**.
>
> **This is what a good theory does: it takes separate experimental laws and shows they follow from one picture.**

**Hydrogen and oxygen atoms combine to form water, but the atoms themselves are not destroyed or changed into anything else.** Magnesium burning in air gives a white powder of magnesium oxide — magnesium atoms have **combined with** oxygen atoms.

## 9.4 How atoms combine

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A MOLECULE is an electrically neutral entity consisting of more than one atom, capable of INDEPENDENT EXISTENCE, showing all the properties of that substance.**
>
> - Atoms of the **same** element → a molecule of an **element** (H₂)
> - Atoms of **different** elements → a molecule of a **compound** (HCl)
>
> **Some elements, such as helium, exist only as single atoms — because their atoms are already stable.**

**Atoms with 8 electrons in the valence shell (2 if K is the outermost) are stable. Otherwise they combine, in two ways:**

| Way | What happens | Bond |
|---|---|---|
| **SHARING** electrons | atoms share a few or all of their valence electrons | **COVALENT** |
| **TRANSFER** of electrons | one atom gives electrons, another accepts them | **IONIC** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why atoms bond at all: when atoms combine, the TOTAL ENERGY of the system becomes LOWER than the sum of the energies of the separate atoms** — and a lower-energy arrangement is more stable.
>
> **The force that holds atoms together is called a CHEMICAL BOND.**

### 9.4.1 Covalent bonds — sharing

#### Molecules of elements

**Hydrogen (Z = 1):** one electron in K, which can hold 2 — so it needs **one more**.
**Two hydrogen atoms share one electron each → H₂.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The SHARED PAIR of electrons attracts BOTH nuclei, and that is what holds the molecule together.** That interaction is a **COVALENT BOND**.
>
> **Two atoms sharing ONE electron each are joined by a SINGLE BOND**, drawn as a single line: **H—H**.

**Chlorine (2, 8, 7):** each atom needs **one** electron. Two chlorine atoms share one each → **Cl—Cl**, a single bond.

**Oxygen (2, 6):** each atom needs **two** electrons. Two oxygen atoms **share two electrons each** → two shared pairs → a **DOUBLE BOND**, drawn **O=O**.

> [!TIP]
> **Count the electrons needed, and you have the bond.** Need 1 → single bond. Need 2 → double bond. Need 3 → triple bond (nitrogen, 2,5, needs 3 → **N≡N**).

#### Molecules of compounds

**Hydrogen chloride:** hydrogen needs 1 electron to complete its **duplet**; chlorine needs 1 to complete its **octet**. **Both share one electron each → H—Cl**, a single covalent bond.

**Water:** hydrogen needs **1**, oxygen needs **2**. **So TWO hydrogen atoms each share one electron with one oxygen atom → H₂O.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **This is where formulae come from.** H₂O is not a convention — it is a direct consequence of **oxygen needing two electrons while each hydrogen supplies one**.

#### C. Naming covalent compounds

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The first element keeps its regular name; the second ends in -ide. Prefixes give the number of atoms:**
> ```
> mono- 1   di- 2   tri- 3   tetra- 4   penta- 5   hexa- 6
> ```
> - **mono-** is usually **omitted for the first element** but **used for the second**
> - if a prefix ends in **'o' or 'a'** and the element starts with a **vowel**, **drop the last vowel** — mon**oxide**, pent**oxide**
> - if the prefix ends in **'i'**, **keep it** for pronunciation — d**i**oxide, tr**i**oxide

| Formula | Name |
|---|---|
| **CO** | carbon **monoxide** (not monooxide) |
| **CO₂** | carbon **dioxide** (not monocarbon dioxide) |
| **CS₂** | carbon **disulfide** |
| **PCl₃** | phosphorus **trichloride** |
| **SF₆** | sulfur **hexafluoride** |
| **N₂O₄** | **dinitrogen tetroxide** (not tetraoxide) |
| **N₂O₅** | **dinitrogen pentoxide** |

**When hydrogen is the first element, NO prefix is used before it however many atoms there are** — **H₂S is hydrogen sulfide**, not dihydrogen sulfide.

**A few binary compounds keep only their common names:** **H₂O** would be "hydrogen monoxide" but is **water**; **NH₃** would be "nitrogen trihydride" but is **ammonia**.

### 9.4.2 Ionic bonds — transfer

**Sodium (Z = 11, configuration 2, 8, 1):** losing its single valence electron gives the stable 2, 8.

```
after losing one electron: 11 protons, 10 electrons  →  net charge +1
Na  →  Na⁺  +  e⁻          a SODIUM CATION
```

**Chlorine (2, 8, 7):** gaining one electron gives the stable 2, 8, 8.

```
after gaining one electron: 17 protons, 18 electrons  →  net charge −1
Cl  +  e⁻  →  Cl⁻          a CHLORIDE ANION
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Cations and anions together are called IONS.**
>
> **Once formed, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are held together by the ELECTROSTATIC FORCE OF ATTRACTION between their opposite charges. That force is the IONIC BOND.**
> ```
> Na  +  Cl   →   Na⁺  +  Cl⁻   →   NaCl
> ```

> [!TIP]
> **Which route an atom takes is the cheapest one.** Sodium with 1 valence electron **gives it away**; chlorine with 7 **takes one**. Sulfur, with **6**, gains **two** and becomes **S²⁻**.
>
> **General rule: fewer than 4 valence electrons → donate; more than 4 → gain or share.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Ionic compounds do not stay as single units.** They form **three-dimensional crystals** with ions in a repeating pattern. In **NaCl** each Na⁺ is surrounded by **six** Cl⁻ and each Cl⁻ by **six** Na⁺ — a **crystal structure**, represented as a **crystal lattice** of points.

#### A. Naming ionic compounds

**The CATION is named first, then the ANION. Simple anion names end in -ide.**

**Generally METALS form cations and NON-METALS form anions**, so ionic compounds typically form when a **metal combines with a non-metal** — sodium chloride, calcium oxide, magnesium sulfide.

**POLYATOMIC IONS are formed from atoms of two or more elements, and their names generally do NOT end in -ide.**

## 9.5 Writing chemical formulae

### 9.5.1 Covalent compounds

1. Write the **symbols** of the elements.
2. Write their **valencies** underneath.
3. **CROSS OVER** the valencies and write them as **subscripts**.

```
H   Cl          H   S           C   Cl
1    1          1   2           4    1
   HCl            H₂S             CCl₄
```
**If a subscript comes out as 1 after crossing over, it is not written.**

### 9.5.2 Ionic compounds

1. Write the **CATION first**, then the **anion**.
2. Write the **charges** underneath (as numbers).
3. **CROSS OVER** the numbers to get the subscripts.
4. **Divide the subscripts by a common factor if any** — the formula gives the **SIMPLEST RATIO**.

```
Ca   Cl         Al    O          Mg    O
2+   1−         3+   2−          2+   2−
   CaCl₂           Al₂O₃        Mg₂O₂ → written MgO
```

> [!WARNING]
> **The charges are NOT shown in the finished formula** — write CaCl₂, not Ca²⁺Cl₂⁻.
>
> **And always simplify.** Mg²⁺ with O²⁻ crosses over to Mg₂O₂, which must be reduced to **MgO**. Likewise Ca²⁺ with CO₃²⁻ gives Ca₂(CO₃)₂ → **CaCO₃**.

**With polyatomic ions, use BRACKETS when there are two or more of the same type:**

```
Mg   OH          Al   OH          Al   SO₄
2+   1−          3+   1−          3+   2−
  Mg(OH)₂          Al(OH)₃         Al₂(SO₄)₃
```

> [!WARNING]
> **Al(OH)₃, NOT AlOH₃.** The bracket says "three whole hydroxide ions". Without it, the 3 would attach to the H alone, which means something else entirely.
>
> **Brackets are not needed when only ONE polyatomic ion is present** — CaCO₃, not Ca(CO₃).

## 9.6 Properties of ionic and covalent compounds

**Test camphor, sodium chloride, copper sulfate, sugar and naphthalene for solubility in water, kerosene and petrol, and for electrical conductivity as solids and in solution.**

> [!WARNING]
> **Do not touch the electrodes while they are connected**, and use a **low-voltage battery**. **Petrol and kerosene are flammable** — handle with care.

| Property | **IONIC** (NaCl, copper sulfate) | **COVALENT** (camphor, naphthalene, sugar) |
|---|---|---|
| Solubility in **water** | generally **soluble** | mostly **insoluble** (sugar is an exception) |
| Solubility in **kerosene, petrol** | **insoluble** | **soluble** |
| Conducts as a **solid** | **NO** | **NO** |
| Conducts **dissolved in water** | **YES** | **NO** |
| Melting and boiling points | **HIGH** | **LOW** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why ionic solids do not conduct but their solutions do.**
>
> **Conduction requires charged particles that are FREE TO MOVE.** In a solid, the ions are **held in fixed positions in the crystal lattice by strong forces** — the charges are there, but they cannot go anywhere. **Dissolving in water frees the ions**, and now they can move and carry current.
>
> **This also predicts the molten state:** melting frees the ions too, so **molten ionic compounds DO conduct**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why sugar dissolves but still does not conduct.** Sugar is a **covalent** compound — it dissolves as **whole neutral molecules**, and **provides no ions in solution**. No charged particles, no conduction. **Solubility and conductivity are different questions.**
>
> **Camphor and naphthalene do not conduct** for the same reason: they have no ions at all, in any state.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why ionic compounds melt at high temperatures:** the **inter-ionic attractions are strong**, and a great deal of energy is needed to pull the lattice apart. **Covalent molecules attract each other weakly**, so they melt and boil at much lower temperatures.

## 9.7 Molecular mass of covalent compounds

**Add up the atomic masses of all the atoms in the molecule.**

```
H₂O  = (1 × 2) + (16 × 1) = 18 u
CO₂  = (12 × 1) + (16 × 2) = 44 u
HNO₃ = 1 + 14 + (16 × 3) = 63 u
CH₄  = 12 + (1 × 4) = 16 u
```

## 9.8 Formula unit mass of ionic compounds

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Ionic compounds do NOT form molecules — their ions form 3-D crystals.**
>
> So we speak of a **FORMULA UNIT**: the collection representing the **simplest whole-number ratio of ions**. Its mass is the **FORMULA UNIT MASS**.

```
Na₂O      = (23 × 2) + (16 × 1) = 62 u
KCl       = 39 + 35.5 = 74.5 u
Ca(NO₃)₂  = 40 + 2 × (14 + 16 × 3) = 40 + 2 × 62 = 164 u
Mg(OH)₂   = 24 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 24 + 34 = 58 u
```

> [!TIP]
> **With brackets, work out the bracket FIRST, then multiply by the subscript.** In Ca(NO₃)₂ the NO₃ group is 62 u, and there are **two** of them.
