## 10.1 Production of sound

**Stretch a rubber band across an open box and pluck it.** As long as it is **vibrating**, sound is produced. **Stop the vibration and the sound stops.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SOUND IS PRODUCED BY VIBRATIONS.**
>
> **VIBRATION = the periodic to-and-fro motion (oscillation) of an object.**
>
> **The object that produces sound is called the SOURCE.**

**Sound can be produced by vibrating strings, membranes, air columns and many other vibrating objects.** Blow across a **bansuri (flute)** and the **air inside the hollow pipe** vibrates. In most musical instruments **more than one part vibrates**.

> [!NOTE]
> **How humans make sound.** Touch your throat while you speak and you feel the vibration. Sound is produced by the **VOCAL CORDS** — tightly stretched muscular flaps inside the **voice box or LARYNX**. The **tongue, lips, mouth and nasal cavity** then shape that sound into speech or music.
>
> **Grasshoppers and crickets** produce sound by **rubbing their wings or legs** together.

### 10.1.1 The tuning fork

**A U-shaped metal bar with a stem**, usually steel or aluminium. The sides of the U are the **PRONGS (tines)**, struck on a soft **rubber pad** to make them vibrate.

**Touch a vibrating prong to a water surface and WAVES form on the water** — visible proof that the prongs really are vibrating.

> [!WARNING]
> **Strike it on a soft rubber pad, never on a hard surface** — a hard strike can damage the fork and changes the sound it makes.

> [!TIP]
> **A tuning fork produces very nearly a SINGLE frequency**, which is why it is the standard instrument for sound experiments. Everyday sounds are mixtures of many frequencies.

> [!NOTE]
> **In Kongthong, a village near Shillong in Meghalaya — the "Whistling Village" — every person has a TUNE NAME** that can be sung or whistled. The tradition, **Jingrwai Iawbei**, begins at birth, when a mother composes a lullaby-like tune for her child.

## 10.2 Propagation of sound

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Friend knocks a desk; you listen **through air** | you hear it |
| You press your **ear against the desk** and listen | you hear it — **sound travels through SOLIDS** |
| Two spoons tapped **in air** | you hear it |
| Two spoons tapped while **submerged in water** | you hear it — **sound travels through LIQUIDS** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Sound can propagate through SOLIDS, LIQUIDS and GASES.**
>
> **The material through which sound propagates is called a MEDIUM.**

### 10.2.1 Sound needs a medium

**The bell jar experiment.** An electric bell rings inside a sealed glass jar. As **air is sucked out** with a vacuum pump, **the sound becomes fainter**. At near vacuum, **almost no sound is heard — even though the bell can still be SEEN ringing.** Let the air back in and the sound returns, gradually as loud as before.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SOUND CANNOT PROPAGATE IN A VACUUM. It needs a medium.**
>
> **A VACUUM is a space with no medium (no matter) in it.**
>
> **Why the "seen ringing" detail matters:** the bell is doing exactly the same thing throughout. **What changed is the medium, not the source** — so the medium must be what carries the sound.

**In outer space there is a near vacuum**, so **astronauts on a spacewalk cannot hear each other speak, nor hear metal clanking**. They communicate through **radio devices fitted into their spacesuits** — radio waves need no medium.

## 10.3 Sound waves

**A vibrating tuning fork can be heard whichever way you turn it** — so **sound propagates in multiple directions** from a source.

### The slinky analogy

**Push and pull one end of a stretched slinky.** You see:
- **regions where the turns are CLOSER together** and **regions where they are MORE SPREAD OUT**
- **these regions travel along the slinky**
- **but a MARK made on one turn does NOT travel** — it only **oscillates about its rest position, parallel to the direction of the disturbance**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Each turn oscillates about its own position, while the DISTURBANCE travels the length of the slinky.** That is the single most important idea in the chapter.

### Compressions and rarefactions

**Model the source as a piston oscillating in a long air-filled tube.**

| Piston motion | What happens |
|---|---|
| **not moving** | air has a uniform **AVERAGE DENSITY** throughout |
| **moves forward** | it pushes nearby air particles forward, compressing the air → a region of **HIGHER density: a COMPRESSION (C)** |
| **moves backward** | particles follow it back, leaving the air near the piston thinner → a region of **LOWER density: a RAREFACTION (R)** |
| **oscillates** | **alternate compressions and rarefactions** travel outward |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How the compression MOVES without the air moving:** the compressed particles **collide with the particles ahead**, passing the compression forward; those collide with their neighbours, and so on.
>
> **So the high-density region travels through the air even though the air particles themselves do not travel with it.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A SOUND WAVE is a disturbance consisting of a series of alternating COMPRESSIONS and RAREFACTIONS propagating through a medium, WITHOUT the actual flow of the particles of the medium.**
>
> **The particles of the medium do not travel with the wave. They vibrate about their mean positions.**

> [!NOTE]
> **When the medium is not confined to a tube**, the vibrating particles collide with neighbours **in all directions**, so a small source producing sound continuously sends out **SPHERICAL WAVES** spreading in every direction.

> [!NOTE]
> **Sudden loud sounds — firecrackers, thunder — arise slightly differently.** When air or gases are **heated rapidly they expand very rapidly**, creating a **sudden disturbance in air density** that travels outward and is heard as a loud pulse.
>
> A **SONIC BOOM** is a similar but more complex disturbance, produced when a **supersonic aircraft** flies **faster than the speed of sound**.

### Longitudinal waves

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In a sound wave, the particles of the medium vibrate back and forth PARALLEL to the direction of wave propagation. Such waves are LONGITUDINAL waves.**
>
> **In a TRANSVERSE wave the particles vibrate PERPENDICULAR to the direction of propagation.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **MECHANICAL WAVES are waves that require a material medium to propagate. A sound wave is a mechanical wave.**
>
> **Not all waves are mechanical.** **Light is a transverse wave that CAN travel through a vacuum** — which is why light from the Sun and other stars reaches the Earth while their sound never could.

> [!NOTE]
> **Earthquakes produce SEISMIC waves**, which may be longitudinal or transverse. **The longitudinal ones are the FIRST to be detected by seismographs** — because they travel faster.

## 10.4 Sound carries energy

**Stretch a rubber sheet over a wide-mouthed container and sprinkle grains on it. Make a loud sound nearby WITHOUT touching the container. The grains jump.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why, when nothing touched them:** the sound travels through the air, reaches the sheet and **makes it vibrate**, and that vibration throws the grains up.
>
> **This shows sound is a form of ENERGY.** When the source vibrates it **transfers energy to the surrounding medium**; the vibration of the medium's particles and their **collisions** pass that energy along.
>
> **In sound propagation it is the ENERGY that is transferred, not the particles of the medium.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Microphone and speaker are opposites.** In a **microphone**, sound waves make a thin membrane (the **diaphragm**) vibrate, and those vibrations are converted into an **electrical signal** — sound energy to electrical energy. In a **speaker**, an electrical signal makes a **cone or diaphragm** vibrate, producing sound — electrical energy back to sound energy.

> [!NOTE]
> **Particles in a medium are never truly at rest** — they always vibrate randomly because of thermal energy. **A passing sound wave temporarily INCREASES that vibration**; afterwards the particles return to their usual random motion.

## 10.5 Graphical representation

**Plot the DENSITY of the medium (y-axis) against DISTANCE (x-axis) at one instant, with the average density as a horizontal dashed line.**

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **CREST** — the highest point | maximum density — the middle of a **COMPRESSION** |
| **TROUGH** — the lowest point | minimum density — the middle of a **RAREFACTION** |
| the dashed line | the **average density** of the undisturbed medium |

> [!WARNING]
> **The wavy curve is NOT a picture of the sound moving up and down.** Sound is **longitudinal** — nothing moves sideways. **The graph plots DENSITY against DISTANCE**, and the "waves" are variations in **how crowded the particles are**.

## 10.6 Characteristics of a sound wave

### 10.6.1 Wavelength, frequency and time period

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WAVELENGTH (λ, lambda) = the distance between two consecutive crests, or two consecutive troughs. SI unit: metre (m).**
>
> **One complete OSCILLATION at a fixed point = the density going from maximum to minimum and back to maximum (or vice versa).**
>
> **FREQUENCY (ν, nu) = the number of density oscillations at a fixed point per unit time. SI unit: per second (s⁻¹), called the HERTZ (Hz).**
>
> **TIME PERIOD (T) = the time taken for ONE complete density oscillation at a fixed point. SI unit: second (s).**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> ν = 1 / T
> ```
> **Frequency and time period are INVERSELY related** — a shorter time period means a higher frequency. They are two ways of saying the same thing: *how many per second* versus *how long each one takes*.

**Worked:** 10 density oscillations in 2 seconds.
```
frequency   = 10 / 2 s = 5 Hz
time period = 2 s / 10 = 0.2 s        (check: 1/5 = 0.2 ✔)
```

> [!TIP]
> **A thin rubber band vibrates FASTER than a thick one**, so it produces a **higher frequency** and a **shorter time period**. That is why tightening or thinning a string on any instrument raises the note.

### 10.6.2 Amplitude and intensity

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **AMPLITUDE of a sound wave = the MAXIMUM CHANGE in the density of the air in a compression (or rarefaction), compared with the average density.**
>
> **A larger amplitude carries MORE ENERGY.**

**You can see this in the grains experiment:** strike the plate **harder** and more energy goes into the medium, the particles are displaced further from their mean positions, the sheet vibrates more — and **the grains jump higher**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **INTENSITY = the amount of sound energy passing through a unit area, perpendicular to the direction of propagation, in unit time.**
>
> **Why intensity falls with distance:** as the wave travels away, **it spreads over a larger area**. Energy is conserved, so **the same energy is now spread thinner** — the intensity drops.
>
> **Sounds produced with a larger initial amplitude carry more energy and travel further before their intensity dies away.**

### 10.6.3 Speed of sound

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SPEED OF SOUND = the distance a point on the wave, such as a crest, travels in unit time.**
>
> **Deriving the formula:** one wavelength λ is covered in one time period T, so
> ```
> v = λ / T
>
> and since ν = 1/T,
>
> v = λ × ν            speed = wavelength × frequency
> ```

| Medium | Approximate speed at 15 °C |
|---|---|
| **Steel (solid)** | **5000 m/s** |
| **Water (liquid)** | **1500 m/s** |
| **Air (gas)** | **340 m/s** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Sound travels FASTEST in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases** — about **4–5 times faster in water** and **15–20 times faster in solids** than in air.
>
> **Why:** sound is passed on by **collisions between particles**. In a solid the particles are packed close together and tightly bound, so a disturbance is handed on almost immediately. In a gas the particles are far apart and must travel further before colliding.

**The speed of sound in air also depends on TEMPERATURE and HUMIDITY — it INCREASES as either increases.** In dry air it is about **331 m/s at 0 °C** and nearly **344 m/s at 22 °C**.

> [!NOTE]
> **In most media the speed depends only on the MEDIUM, not on the source or the frequency.** If the frequency of the source changes, **the wavelength changes to match while the speed stays the same.**
>
> **This is why music sounds right:** if high notes travelled faster than low ones, the notes of a chord would arrive at different times and every performance would be scrambled by the time it reached the back of the hall.

**Worked — wavelengths at the two limits of human hearing (v = 344 m/s):**
```
ν = 20 Hz     →  λ = 344 / 20    = 17.2 m
ν = 20 kHz    →  λ = 344 / 20000 = 0.0172 m = 1.72 cm
```

**Worked — thunder heard 5 s after the lightning flash (v = 340 m/s):**
```
distance = v × t = 340 × 5 = 1700 m ≈ 1.7 km
```
**Light travels at 300,000 km/s, so it arrives essentially instantly** — the entire delay is the sound's journey.

**Worked — a sound wave in steel with λ = 50 m and v = 5000 m/s:**
```
ν = v / λ = 5000 / 50 = 100 Hz
T = 1 / ν = 1 / 100    = 0.01 s
```

### 10.6.4 Human perception: pitch and loudness

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The physical properties of sound — time period, wavelength, frequency, amplitude, speed — are well defined and MEASURABLE. How we EXPERIENCE sound is SUBJECTIVE.**
>
> **PITCH is how FREQUENCY is perceived.** High-pitched sounds (a whistle, a siren) have **higher frequency**; low-pitched sounds (thunder, an aircraft rumble) have **lower frequency**. The exact mathematical relation is complicated.
>
> **LOUDNESS is how AMPLITUDE is perceived.** Larger amplitude sounds louder; loudness also **decreases as you move away** from the source.

> [!WARNING]
> **Loudness and intensity are used interchangeably in everyday speech, but they are not the same.**
>
> **INTENSITY is a measurable physical quantity. LOUDNESS depends on the LISTENER'S hearing ability.** Two people can sit side by side in the same intensity of sound and find it differently loud.

### The audible range

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> INFRASONIC   below 20 Hz          humans cannot hear;  ELEPHANTS can
> AUDIBLE      20 Hz to 20,000 Hz   the human hearing range
> ULTRASONIC   above 20 kHz         humans cannot hear;  DOGS, CATS, BATS,
>                                   DOLPHINS can
> ```
> **The range varies from person to person and DECREASES WITH AGE.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Why voices sound different.** Male, female and children's voices differ not only in frequency but in **how the sound is shaped by the throat, mouth and nasal cavities**. During adolescence, boys' **vocal cords lengthen and thicken**, so they **vibrate less frequently** and the voice "deepens".

> [!NOTE]
> **Decibels and noise.** Loudness is commonly measured in **decibels (dB)** — rustling leaves a few dB, normal conversation about **60 dB**, firecrackers over **100 dB**. **Even a small increase in dB means a large increase in intensity.**
>
> **NOISE is unwanted or harmful sound.** Long exposure above recommended limits can affect **health, sleep and hearing**, and can cause **hearing loss**, tested using **audiograms**. **Hearing aids** contain a microphone, an amplifier and a speaker.

> [!NOTE]
> **How the ear works.** Sound entering the ear makes the **EARDRUM** vibrate; **tiny bones amplify** the vibrations; the **COCHLEA converts them into electrical signals** which travel to the brain.
>
> **Two ears let the brain locate the direction of a sound**, by comparing **which ear hears it first** — a gap often less than a thousandth of a second.
>
> **Snakes and fish sense vibrations through their bodies**; some insects have ear-like organs on their body parts.

> [!NOTE]
> **Tone, note, timbre and octave.**
> - A **TONE** is a sound of a **single frequency** — a tuning fork, or whistling.
> - A **MUSICAL NOTE** combines a lowest frequency called the **FUNDAMENTAL** with higher frequencies called **OVERTONES**, which together make a rich, pleasant sound. Indian string instruments such as the **sarangi, sitar and veena** use extra strings to enrich this combination.
> - **TIMBRE** is what makes a flute, an ektara and a tabla sound different **playing the same note at the same loudness**. It comes from their shape, material and construction, which set the **pattern and intensity of the overtones**.
> - An **OCTAVE** is the interval between two notes where one has **double the frequency** of the other (200 Hz and 400 Hz).

> [!NOTE]
> **Sir C. V. Raman** won **India's first Nobel Prize in Science** for discovering the **Raman Effect** in light. He also contributed to **acoustics**, studying Indian percussion instruments such as the **tabla and mridangam** to understand their rich sounds.
>
> **The syaahi** — the black patch at the centre of a tabla or mridangam head — **alters the vibration of the membrane**, giving these instruments a rich variety of sounds and a level of tonal control rarely found in other drums.

## 10.7 Reflection of sound

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Sound bounces off obstacles — REFLECTION OF SOUND — and follows the SAME LAWS OF REFLECTION as light:**
>
> **The incident and reflected directions make EQUAL ANGLES with the normal to the surface at the point of incidence, and all three lie in the same plane.**

### 10.7.1 Echo

**Shout near a mountain, a cliff or in a long corridor and you may hear your voice again — an ECHO**, caused by sound reflecting off a distant hard surface and returning.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why echoes are not heard everywhere:** in a small room the reflections **arrive too quickly** for the brain to separate them from the original sound.
>
> **Two sounds are heard as SEPARATE only if the gap between them is at least 0.1 s.**

**Calculating the minimum echo distance (v = 340 m/s):**
```
distance travelled in 0.1 s = 340 × 0.1 = 34 m

but that is the round trip — source → surface → back

minimum distance to the reflecting surface = 34 / 2 = 17 m
```

> [!WARNING]
> **The factor of 2 is the commonest mistake in this whole chapter.** In every echo or sonar problem, **the sound covers the distance TWICE.** Always ask: is the time given for the round trip or one way?

**Echoes are strongest from HARD, SMOOTH surfaces.** **Soft surfaces such as curtains ABSORB sound**, and **rough surfaces SCATTER it** in different directions, so no clear echo is heard.

**Worked — an echo 0.5 s after a clap (v = 340 m/s):**
```
distance from the wall = (340 × 0.5) / 2 = 170 / 2 = 85 m
```

### 10.7.2 Reverberation

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **REVERBERATION: sound persisting after the source has stopped, caused by MULTIPLE REFLECTIONS from the walls of a large hall.**
>
> **It occurs when reflections arrive with a time difference LESS than 0.05 s** — too close together to be heard as separate sounds.

| | **ECHO** | **REVERBERATION** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | a **single** reflection from a distant surface | **multiple** reflections |
| Time gap | **at least 0.1 s** | **less than 0.05 s** |
| Heard as | a **separate, repeated** sound | the sound **persisting / smearing** |

**Modern auditoriums and concert halls are designed to have DESIRABLE reverberation**, so everyone hears speech and music clearly without distortion. **Sound-absorbing panels, upholstered chairs, curtains and other soft porous surfaces reduce unwanted reverberation** — without them the sound would be garbled.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Whispering Gallery of the Gol Gumbaz in Bijapur, Karnataka** is designed so that even a **faint whisper is heard multiple times** across the large dome — one of many monuments built with deep acoustic insight.

## 10.8 Ultrasonic and infrasonic waves

| Range | Uses |
|---|---|
| **INFRASONIC** (below 20 Hz) | detecting **earthquakes and volcanic eruptions**; detecting **severe storms**, since these waves travel **long distances** through air and the Earth |
| **ULTRASONIC** (above 20 kHz) | **ULTRASONOGRAPHY** — imaging internal organs without surgery; **breaking kidney stones** into pieces small enough to pass out of the body; **ultrasonic welding** and **cleaning delicate machine parts**; **detecting defects inside metal blocks**; **locating objects** by reflection |

### 10.8.1 Echolocation

**Most bats are nocturnal, flying and hunting in the dark without colliding with anything.** They **emit short bursts of ultrasonic waves**, which **reflect from nearby objects**; by **sensing the echoes** the bat works out the position of obstacles and prey.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ECHOLOCATION = locating objects using reflected sound waves.** Also used by **dolphins, whales and some birds** for navigation and hunting.

**SONAR (SOund Navigation And Ranging)** applies the same principle underwater: **ultrasonic waves are sent into the water and the reflected waves analysed** to find the **distance, direction and speed** of underwater objects such as submarines or shipwrecks.

**Worked — a sonar signal returns after 0.90 s in seawater (v = 1530 m/s):**
```
time to reach the object = 0.90 / 2 = 0.45 s        (halve it — it is a round trip)

distance = 1530 × 0.45 = 688.5 m
```

> [!NOTE]
> **Audio surveillance.** Drones and aircraft produce characteristic sound from their motors. Even when hard to see, the **low-frequency humming** can be detected with sensitive sound sensors, helping monitor airspace for safety and security.
