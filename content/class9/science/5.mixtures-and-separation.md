## 5.1 Classifying mixtures

| | **HOMOGENEOUS** (a **SOLUTION**) | **HETEROGENEOUS** |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | **uniform throughout** — equally sweet in the first and last sip | **not uniform** |
| Particles | **not visible** | **easily visible**, may settle with time |
| Examples | sugar in water, **vinegar** (acetic acid in water), **soda** (carbon dioxide in water), brass | sand in water, **oil and water**, muddy water |

**A solution always remains homogeneous.**

**The three-beaker test** — salt + water (A), chalk powder + water (B), a few drops of milk + water (C):

| | A: salt | B: chalk | C: milk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particles visible? | **no** | **yes** | **no** |
| Laser beam path visible from the side? | **no** | **yes** | **YES** |
| Residue on filter paper? | **no** | **yes** | **no** |
| On standing? | stays clear | **settles** | stays dispersed |

> [!WARNING]
> **Never look directly into a laser beam — it can cause irreversible eye damage.** View it only from the side.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **C is the interesting one.** Milk-in-water **looks** homogeneous, passes through filter paper and does not settle — yet it **scatters the laser beam**. That single result shows it is neither a true solution nor a suspension. **It is a COLLOID** (Section 5.4.4).

## 5.2 Solutions

**SOLUTE** = the substance that gets **dissolved**. **SOLVENT** = the substance that **dissolves** it. In sugar-and-water, sugar is the solute and water the solvent.

### 5.2.1 Concentration

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **CONCENTRATION = the amount of solute dissolved in a given amount of solvent or solution.**

**Why proportion matters, not just ingredients:**
- **ORS** needs **specified** amounts of salt and sugar in a **fixed** volume of water. Change either and you still have a salt-and-sugar solution — **but it is no longer ORS**.
- **Pesticide spray:** **too little** may not protect the crop; **too much** can damage crops, soil and the environment.

> [!WARNING]
> **Not all sugary drinks sold or made at home are ORS.** Having the right ingredients is not the same as having the right concentration.

> [!NOTE]
> **Dilip Mahalanabis**, an Indian paediatrician, first developed and implemented **oral rehydration** treatment for dehydration from diarrhoea and cholera. The **ORS** he formulated revolutionised rehydration therapy and has **saved millions of lives** since the WHO popularised it worldwide.

### 5.2.2 Three ways to express concentration as a percentage

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> A.  mass by mass %   (% m/m)  =  mass of solute   ÷ mass of solution   × 100
> B.  mass by volume % (% m/v)  =  mass of solute   ÷ volume of solution × 100
> C.  volume by volume % (% v/v) = volume of solute ÷ volume of solution × 100
> ```
> **Note the denominator is always the SOLUTION, not the solvent.**

| Method | Meaning | Used when | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **% m/m** | grams of solute in **100 g of solution** | general; also for **heterogeneous** mixtures | milk powder, spice mixes, packaged-food labels |
| **% m/v** | grams of solute in **100 mL of solution** | **volume is easier to measure than mass** | **5% glucose solution**, medicines, labs |
| **% v/v** | mL of solute in **100 mL of solution** | **two miscible liquids** mixed | perfumes, cosmetics, **vinegar** |

**Worked (% m/m):** 10 g salt in 90 g water.
```
mass of solution = 10 + 90 = 100 g
% m/m = 10/100 × 100 = 10 % m/m
```

**Worked (% m/v):** 5 g glucose made up to 100 mL of solution.
```
% m/v = 5 g / 100 mL × 100 = 5 % m/v
```

**Worked (% v/v):** 1 mL pesticide made up to 100 mL of spray.
```
% v/v = 1 mL / 100 mL × 100 = 1 % v/v
```

> [!NOTE]
> **A hospital saline drip is usually 0.9 % m/v sodium chloride** — 0.9 g of salt in 100 mL of solution. That exact strength is **safe for blood** and replaces lost body fluids.
>
> **Industry often writes % w/w** (weight by weight), since weight and mass are used interchangeably there. **Numerically % m/m and % w/w are the same.**

> [!TIP]
> **Why the solute goes over the SOLUTION and not the solvent:** you can measure the finished solution directly, but you often cannot recover the solvent to weigh it. And when a solute dissolves, volumes do not simply add — 5 g of glucose plus 100 mL of water is **not** 100 mL of solution.

### 5.2.3 Solubility

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SOLUBILITY = the MAXIMUM amount of solute that dissolves in a fixed quantity of solvent (100 g or 100 mL) AT A GIVEN TEMPERATURE.**
>
> **A SATURATED SOLUTION is one that can dissolve no more solute at that temperature.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why temperature must always be stated:**
> - the solubility of a **SOLID in a liquid generally INCREASES** with temperature
> - the solubility of a **GAS in a liquid generally DECREASES** with temperature
>
> Without a temperature, a solubility number means nothing.

**A SOLUBILITY CURVE plots solubility (g per 100 g of water) against temperature.** A steeply rising curve means the solubility changes a lot with temperature; a nearly flat one means it barely changes.

| Salt | 10 °C | 20 °C | 30 °C | 40 °C | 60 °C | 80 °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Potassium nitrate** | 21 | 32 | 45 | 62 | 106 | **167** |
| **Sodium chloride** | 36 | 36 | 36.3 | 36.5 | 37 | **37** |
| **Potassium chloride** | 35 | 35 | 37.4 | 40 | 46 | **54** |
| **Ammonium chloride** | 24 | 37 | 41 | 41 | 55 | **66** |

> [!TIP]
> **Read the table as "how much does the number change?"** Potassium nitrate goes from 21 to 167 — a huge rise, so it crystallises beautifully on cooling. **Sodium chloride goes from 36 to 37 — almost no change**, which is exactly why common salt is obtained by **evaporating** seawater rather than by cooling it.

## 5.3 Separating homogeneous mixtures

### 5.3.1 Crystallization

**Take a saturated solution of a compound at 60 °C holding 287 g per 100 g of water. Cool it to 40 °C, where only 241 g can stay dissolved.**

```
287 g − 241 g = 46 g must come OUT of solution as pure solid — as CRYSTALS
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A CRYSTAL is a solid made of particles arranged in a REGULAR GEOMETRIC PATTERN.**
>
> **CRYSTALLIZATION = forming crystals from a saturated solution.**
>
> **The principle: differences in the solubility of a substance at DIFFERENT TEMPERATURES.**

**Uses:** separating two solids when **one is present in small quantity** and both dissolve in the same solvent; and **purifying** solids — newly prepared compounds usually come with unwanted impurities.

**Crystals in everyday life:** rock salt, **candy sugar (mishri)**, **snowflakes** (water vapour freezing in air), **frost on windows**. Naturally occurring crystal deposits are found in mines, caves such as the **Mawsmai Cave in Sohra (Cherrapunji)**, and in **quartz**.

**Making copper sulfate crystals:**

1. Take 1 g of copper sulfate in a beaker, add 25 mL water and **a drop of dilute sulfuric acid**; heat gently in a **water bath**, stirring.
2. Add more copper sulfate until the solution is **saturated**.
3. **Filter the HOT solution** to remove insoluble impurities.
4. Cover and let it **cool SLOWLY, undisturbed**.
5. Filter the crystals, rinse with cold water, dry on a watch glass.

> [!WARNING]
> **Copper sulfate is toxic** — do not touch it with bare hands, and work under adult supervision. **The teacher should add the sulfuric acid**; handle it very carefully.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why each step is there:**
> - **Sulfuric acid** helps make pure crystals by **preventing unwanted reactions** (needed for some salts only).
> - **Filter while HOT** — cool it first and your product would crystallise out and be thrown away with the impurities.
> - **Cool SLOWLY** — this gives the particles **enough time to come together in order**, producing **larger, shiny, well-shaped** crystals. Rapid cooling in ice water gives **small, poorly formed** ones.

> [!TIP]
> **How to test the slow-cooling claim properly:** make **one** hot saturated solution and **divide it into two equal parts** — one cooled slowly at room temperature, one plunged into ice-cold water. Same solution, same concentration, same starting temperature; **only the cooling rate differs**, so any difference in crystal size must be caused by it.

> [!NOTE]
> **Salt crystallization is an ancient Indian process** used by coastal communities. **Panga salt** was obtained by **boiling** concentrated sea brine, while **evaporating** seawater produced **karkatch salt** — the two methods giving crystals of different sizes.

**Getting salt from seawater:** seawater → **evaporate in shallow pans** until it becomes a **saturated solution** → continued evaporation deposits **salt crystals**.

> [!TIP]
> **Faster evaporation gives SMALLER crystals.** Crystal size depends on how much **time** particles have to arrange themselves. Slow growth = few, large, well-formed crystals; fast growth = many small ones. It is the same principle as slow versus rapid cooling.

### 5.3.2 Distillation

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **DISTILLATION: heat the mixture until the liquid with the LOWER BOILING POINT vaporises; cool the vapour in a CONDENSER so it turns back to liquid; collect it separately.**
>
> **It works when the boiling points differ by at least about 25 °C.**
>
> **Why the gap is needed:** a large difference lets one liquid vaporise **before significant vapour of the other forms**. With a small gap both boil off together and you separate nothing.

**Uses:** **recovering the solvent** (evaporation loses it; distillation keeps it), separating **two miscible liquids**, and obtaining a **liquid from a solution containing dissolved solids**.

**Acetone (b.p. about 56 °C) and water (100 °C)** separate cleanly — a 44 °C gap.

**The set-up:** distillation flask with the mixture, **thermometer** at the neck, **water condenser** with water inlet at the bottom and outlet at the top, and a conical flask to collect the **distillate**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Mitti ka Ittar, Kannauj.** The perfume capital of India captures the earthy fragrance that rises after the first rain, using the traditional **Deg-Bhapka distillation method** passed down through generations. The **Fragrance and Flavour Development Centre** there separates flavour and fragrance components from flowers, leaves and other plant parts, and supports farmers growing fragrance plants.

> [!NOTE]
> **FRACTIONAL DISTILLATION separates components whose boiling points differ by LESS than 25 °C.**
>
> In a **petroleum refinery**, crude oil extracted from the Earth's crust is separated into fractions — in rising order of boiling point: **petroleum gas, petrol, aviation fuel (kerosene), diesel, lubricating oil, bitumen.** The gaseous fraction is compressed into steel cylinders, where it liquefies, and sold as **LPG**.

### 5.3.3 Paper chromatography

**Draw a pencil line 2 cm from the bottom of a paper strip, put a spot of black ink on it, and stand the strip in a shallow layer of water with the water level BELOW the spot.** As the water rises through the paper, **the ink separates into different coloured spots**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **PAPER CHROMATOGRAPHY separates components by the differences in how they interact with the SOLVENT and with the PAPER.**
>
> **The liquid carries the substances up the paper, separating them by HOW FAST they move.** A component that clings to the paper lags behind; one that prefers the solvent races ahead.

> [!WARNING]
> **The solvent level must start BELOW the spot.** If it starts above, the ink simply **dissolves away into the liquid** instead of being carried up the paper, and nothing separates.
>
> **Draw the line in PENCIL, not ink** — pen ink would itself run and ruin the result.

**Also works for:** pigments in a **spinach leaf** extract, colours in **flower petals**, food colours, coloured mouth fresheners. **Water is not always the right solvent** — sometimes alcohol, a 2 % m/v salt solution, or a mixture of solvents is needed.

> [!NOTE]
> **'Chromatography' comes from Greek *chroma* (colour) and *graphein* (to write)** — "writing with colour", because it was first used on dyes and inks.

## 5.4 Separating heterogeneous mixtures

### 5.4.1 Two immiscible liquids — the separating funnel

**Pour 5 mL mustard oil and 20 mL water into a separating funnel and let it stand. Two layers form** — yellow mustard oil **on top**, water **below**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the layers form and in that order: DENSITY.** The **less dense** liquid floats on the **denser** one. Mustard oil is less dense than water, so it sits on top.
>
> **The method:** open the stopcock and run off the **lower** layer, close it, **discard the small middle portion** that contains both, then collect the upper layer separately.
>
> **Why discard the middle bit:** at the boundary the two liquids are mixed. Throwing away that small volume is the price of getting both fractions pure.

> [!TIP]
> **If two immiscible liquids had the SAME density, no layers would form** — neither could float on the other — and a separating funnel would be useless.

**Mixtures with a gas:** gas particles move freely in all directions, so **most gas mixtures are HOMOGENEOUS** (e.g. the hydrogen–oxygen mixture used as rocket fuel). But **smoke** (solid particles in air), **fog** (tiny water droplets in air) and **dust in air** are **heterogeneous**.

### 5.4.2 Sublimation

**Heat a mixture of crushed camphor and sand in a china dish under an inverted funnel (nozzle plugged with cotton). White solid camphor deposits on the inner wall of the funnel; sand stays in the dish.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SUBLIMATION: a solid changes DIRECTLY to vapour, below its melting point, without passing through the liquid state.**
>
> **DEPOSITION: the vapour cools back to solid, again without becoming a liquid.**
>
> **The separation works because camphor sublimes and sand does not.**

**Other sublimable substances:** **naphthalene**; and **dry ice** (solid carbon dioxide), used for ice-cream storage.

> [!WARNING]
> **Sublimation is not evaporation.**
> - **Evaporation** is **liquid → vapour**, from the surface, at any temperature.
> - **Sublimation** is **solid → vapour**, skipping the liquid state entirely.
>
> Different starting state, so different process — though both end in vapour.

### Alloys

**Metals do not dissolve in each other at room temperature. Melt them, mix, and let the hot mixture cool, and it solidifies into what looks like a single metal.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An ALLOY is a HOMOGENEOUS mixture of two or more metals, or of a metal and a non-metal.**
>
> **Physical methods cannot separate the components of an alloy** — they are mixed at the level of individual particles.
>
> **Why make them:** to produce materials that are **stronger, more rigid, or more corrosion-resistant** than the pure metals.

| Alloy | Composition |
|---|---|
| **Brass** | about **80% copper, 20% zinc** |
| **Bronze** | about **80% copper, 20% tin** |
| **Stainless steel** | iron with **carbon (0.03–0.8%), chromium (16–18%), nickel (10–14%), molybdenum (2–3%)** |

### 5.4.3 Suspensions

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A SUSPENSION is a heterogeneous mixture in which solid particles do NOT dissolve but remain suspended throughout the medium.**
>
> Its particles are **much larger than those in a solution** and are **visible to the naked eye**. Examples: sand in water, sawdust in water, tea leaves in water.

**Separating mud from water.** Leaving it undisturbed lets the heavier particles settle, but the water often stays cloudy; **filtering removes the larger particles but not the finest ones**. For those we use **centrifugation** and/or **coagulation**.

#### A. Centrifugation

**Spin a mixture in a tube at high speed. The tubes swing horizontal, and the outward (centrifugal) force pushes the heavier particles outwards, so they collect at the bottom of the tube; the lighter liquid stays on top.**

> [!TIP]
> **Feel it yourself:** hold a partner's crossed hands and spin — the **outward pull** you feel is the same effect. (The spinning game is **phugadi** in Marathi and **kikli** in Punjabi.)
>
> **Why it beats simply waiting:** settling under gravity is slow and cannot shift the finest particles at all. Spinning applies a **far larger effective force**, so what would take hours takes minutes — and particles too fine to settle on their own are separated.

**Used to separate blood into plasma, platelets, white cells and red cells**, and widely in chemical industries.

> [!NOTE]
> **The paperfuge.** Borrowing the idea from a common spinning toy — a disc on a loop of string with two handles — a hand-powered **paperfuge** spins blood samples fast enough to do a laboratory centrifuge's job **without electricity**. This low-cost tool helps detect **malaria and anaemia in remote areas**.

#### B. Coagulation

**Add powdered alum (fitkari) to muddy water. The fine suspended particles CLUMP TOGETHER; the larger clumps then settle under gravity (sedimentation) and can be removed by decantation or filtration.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **COAGULATION = making small particles clump together. The added substance (alum) is the COAGULANT.**
>
> **Why it works:** individual fine particles are too small to settle, and too small to be caught by filter paper. **Clumping turns many small particles into one big one** — which both settles quickly and is easily filtered. **You are not removing the particles, you are changing their size.**

**Everyday coagulation: making paneer.** Acid — **lemon juice or vinegar** — acts as the coagulant, clumping the **milk proteins** into cheese.

### 5.4.4 Colloids

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Solutions, suspensions and colloids differ mainly in PARTICLE SIZE:**
>
> | Mixture | Particle diameter |
> |---|---|
> | **Solution** | **less than 1 nm** |
> | **Colloid** | **1 – 1000 nm** |
> | **Suspension** | **more than 1000 nm** |
>
> **Colloid particles do NOT settle over time** — they stay uniformly dispersed, as in a solution — but they are **large enough to scatter light**, as in a suspension.

**Examples of colloids: blood, milk, tomato sauce, ice cream.**

**Blood is neither a solution nor a true suspension.** Its cells cannot be seen with the naked eye (so not a suspension), yet it can be separated by centrifugation and it coagulates (so not a solution). **It is a colloid.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Donate blood.** Donated blood is tested, the blood group identified, and then **separated by centrifugation into plasma, platelets, white and red blood cells**, stored in blood banks and supplied when needed. **The body replaces the donated blood naturally within a few weeks.**

## 5.5 The Tyndall effect

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE TYNDALL EFFECT = the SCATTERING of light by particles**, making the path of a light beam visible from the side.
>
> **Light is scattered when it passes through a COLLOID or a SUSPENSION, but NOT through a transparent solution.**
>
> **Why:** solution particles (`<` 1 nm) are **far too small** to interfere with light waves. Colloid and suspension particles are large enough to scatter them.

**Named after John Tyndall, who first explained the scattering of light by particles.**

**Where you see it:** a fine beam entering a dark room through a small hole (scattered by dust and smoke), **floodlights in a sports stadium**, sunbeams through gaps in the leaves of a dense tree, and **haze over cities with a lot of smoke and dust**.

> [!TIP]
> **This is the single test that tells a colloid from a solution.** Both look clear and homogeneous, both pass through filter paper, neither settles. **Shine a beam through: the colloid lights up a path, the solution does not.**

**Two parts of a colloid:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **DISPERSED PHASE** — the solute-like component; the dispersed particles
> - **DISPERSION MEDIUM** — the component in which they are suspended

> [!NOTE]
> **EMULSIONS** are colloids in which **both** the dispersed phase and the dispersion medium are **liquids**.
> - **Oil-in-water:** milk, vanishing creams
> - **Water-in-oil:** butter, body lotions, cold cream
>
> Some medicines are made as emulsions to disperse them in water, make them **palatable** and reduce the greasy feel.
>
> **EMULSIFYING AGENTS stabilise emulsions** — for example, the **proteins in milk and butter**. Make one yourself by shaking a few drops of cooking oil with water containing a little soap solution.

## Comparing the three

| Property | **Solution** | **Suspension** | **Colloid** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | **homogeneous** | **heterogeneous** | **heterogeneous** (looks homogeneous) |
| Particle size | `<` 1 nm | `>` 1000 nm | 1 – 1000 nm |
| Particles visible? | **no** | **yes, to the naked eye** | **no** |
| Separated by filtration? | **no** | **yes** | **no** |
| Settles on standing? | **no** | **yes** | **no** |
| Tyndall effect? | **no** | **yes** | **yes** |
| Examples | salt solution, brass | sand in water, mud | milk, blood, smoke, butter |

## Separation happens everywhere

- **In your body:** the **kidneys** remove waste from blood.
- **In sewage treatment:** **sedimentation, coagulation and filtration** — the clean water can be reused for flushing toilets or watering plants.
- **At home:** segregating **dry waste** (plastic, paper, glass, metal — recyclable) from **wet waste** (food scraps, peels — compostable).
- **In recycling research:** recovering valuable materials such as **lithium from old phone and laptop batteries**.

## At a glance

```
homogeneous (solution)  uniform throughout, particles < 1 nm, no Tyndall effect
heterogeneous           not uniform; suspensions (> 1000 nm) and colloids (1–1000 nm)

CONCENTRATION
  % m/m = mass solute   / mass solution   × 100     labels, spice mixes
  % m/v = mass solute   / volume solution × 100     5% glucose, 0.9% saline
  % v/v = volume solute / volume solution × 100     vinegar, perfumes
  denominator is always the SOLUTION

SOLUBILITY  max solute per 100 g solvent AT A STATED TEMPERATURE
            solids: usually rises with temperature   gases: usually falls

SEPARATION METHODS
  crystallization   pure solid from saturated solution; uses solubility vs
                    temperature; cool SLOWLY for big crystals
  distillation      miscible liquids, boiling points ≥ 25 °C apart; recovers solvent
  fractional dist.  boiling points < 25 °C apart — petroleum refinery
  chromatography    different rates of movement on paper; solvent BELOW the spot
  separating funnel immiscible liquids, by DENSITY; discard the middle portion
  sublimation       solid → vapour directly (camphor, naphthalene, dry ice)
  centrifugation    high-speed spin; heavier particles thrown outwards
  coagulation       alum clumps fine particles so they settle; paneer from milk

TYNDALL EFFECT   scattering of light — YES in colloids and suspensions,
                 NO in solutions. The test that distinguishes colloid from solution.
colloid parts    dispersed phase + dispersion medium; emulsion = liquid in liquid
```
