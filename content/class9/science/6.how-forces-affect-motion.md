## From describing motion to explaining it

**Chapter 4 described motion — position, velocity, acceleration. It never asked WHAT CAUSES motion to change.** This chapter does.

## 6.1 The concept of force

**A force can:**
- make an object **move from rest** — a kicked ball
- **change the speed** of a moving object
- **change the direction** of motion — a bat striking a cricket ball
- **change the shape** of an object — squeezing a lemon

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Force is a physical quantity that needs a DIRECTION as well as a magnitude and a unit** — like position, displacement, velocity and acceleration.
>
> **The SI unit of force is the NEWTON, symbol N.** The magnitude expresses its strength.
>
> **If either the magnitude OR the direction of a force changes, its effect changes.**

**Notice that every force you have met came with a direction attached:** friction acting **opposite** to the motion; magnetic poles **repelling or attracting**; charges **repelling or attracting**; the Earth pulling objects **towards itself**; buoyant force acting **upwards**.

> [!NOTE]
> **When a unit is named after a person, its full name starts with a SMALL letter and its symbol is CAPITAL** — **newton** and **N**, not Newton and n.

### 6.1.1 Measuring force

**A SPRING BALANCE measures the magnitude of a force.** You already used one to measure **weight** — and weight is just the **gravitational force with which the Earth pulls the object**. Pull on the free end and the balance reads **the force you are applying**.

> [!NOTE]
> **The smallest force you can feel** is of the order of **millinewtons (10^-3 N)** — a light touch. Scientists can measure down to **yoctonewtons (10^-24 N)** in specialised experiments.
>
> **What 1 N feels like:** hold a **100 g mass** in your palm — the upward force your palm applies is about **1 N**.

## 6.2 Balanced and unbalanced forces

**In real life, more than one force almost always acts on an object.** Push a box and **friction** acts too. A ball floats because **gravity pulls down** and **buoyancy pushes up**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **BALANCED FORCES are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction — the net force is ZERO and the object's motion does not change.**
>
> **UNBALANCED FORCES leave a NON-ZERO NET FORCE, and the object's motion changes.**

**Tug of war:** equal pulls → the rope does not move. One team pulls harder → **the rope moves in the direction of the LARGER force**.

### Adding forces along a line

| Situation | Net force |
|---|---|
| Two forces in the **SAME direction** | **SUM** of the magnitudes, in that direction |
| Two forces in **OPPOSITE directions** | **DIFFERENCE** of the magnitudes, in the direction of the **larger** force |

**Worked — a 10 N and a 6 N force on a block:**

```
(a) both to the right:            10 + 6 = 16 N to the right
(b) 10 N right, 6 N left:         10 − 6 =  4 N to the right
(c) 6 N right, 10 N left:         10 − 6 =  4 N to the left
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Multiple forces may act on an object, but its motion depends ONLY on the NET force.** Once you have added them up, the individual forces stop mattering.

> [!NOTE]
> Forces can also act **at an angle** to each other (calculated in higher grades), and **equal and opposite forces applied at the two ends of an extended object make it ROTATE** — as when you turn a handlebar or a tap.

## 6.3 Friction

**Push a box on the floor and it may not move at all.** You have to push harder.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The FORCE OF FRICTION acts between the surfaces in contact, in the direction OPPOSITE to the applied force / the motion.**
>
> **The box starts moving only when your push EXCEEDS the friction**, so that a net force acts in the direction of motion.

**Other forces on a pushed box:** the **gravitational force (weight)** acting **downwards** and the **NORMAL FORCE** exerted by the surface acting **upwards, perpendicular to it**. **These two balance each other.** Air also exerts friction on a moving box, usually small enough to neglect.

### Why moving things stop

**Stop pedalling and the bicycle slows and stops. Stop pushing a ball and it rolls to a halt.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **This does NOT mean motion needs a force to continue. It means friction needs to be COUNTERED.**
>
> On a moving object we must keep applying a force **only to cancel friction**. Remove friction and no force would be needed at all.

### Testing whether friction differs between surfaces

**Activity — the stack of coins.** Tape four ₹10 coins together, stretch a rubber band by **the same amount** each time, and launch the stack across different level surfaces: wooden table top, laminated top, polished marble or tiled floor.

| Surface | Distance travelled before stopping | Friction |
|---|---|---|
| Wooden table top | shortest | **largest** |
| Laminated top | longer | smaller |
| Polished marble / tile | **longest**, velocity falls most slowly | **smallest** |

**What happens, force by force:**
1. Before release, the coins are stationary — the forces on them are **balanced**.
2. On release, the **rubber band's force exceeds friction**, giving a **net forward force** → the stack **accelerates** from rest.
3. The instant it loses contact with the band, **only friction acts**, opposite to the motion → the stack **decelerates** and stops.

> [!TIP]
> **Why stretch the band the same amount every time?** So the **launching force is identical** in every trial. Then the only thing that differs between runs is the **surface**, and any difference in distance must be caused by friction. **Same reasoning as splitting one solution in two for the crystal experiment.**

**Activity — measuring the friction directly.** Attach a wooden block to a horizontal **spring balance** (zeroed) and pull with gradually increasing force. **Note the reading when the block just starts to move.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why that reading is the friction:** at that moment the block's velocity is neither increasing nor decreasing, so the **net force is zero** — which means **the spring's pull exactly equals the friction**. Reading the spring therefore reads the friction.
>
> **The results match the coin experiment:** the surface with the **smallest** reading is the one on which the coins travelled **furthest**.

### The thought experiment

**Suppose friction were exactly zero.** Launch the stack of coins and **its velocity would never decrease — it would keep moving forever.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **This is why we do thought experiments: when the required conditions cannot be created in the real world, we reason from the trend.** Every real surface we tested pointed the same way — less friction, less slowing — and the frictionless case is the limit of that trend.

> [!NOTE]
> **The historical mistake.** In ancient times it was well recognised that a force was needed to **start** or **stop** motion. But for ages it was **mistakenly thought a force was also needed to KEEP an object moving at constant velocity** — because friction is everywhere and always seemed to prove it.
>
> **In the 17th century Galileo Galilei argued, through thought experiments, that a body moving along a horizontal plane with all impediments removed would continue to move indefinitely.**

## 6.4 Newton's first law of motion

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **NEWTON'S FIRST LAW: an object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with CONSTANT VELOCITY, unless a NET FORCE acts upon it.**
>
> Equivalently: **if the net force is zero, the object cannot start moving or change its velocity — its acceleration is zero.**

> [!NOTE]
> **INERTIA** was Newton's word for **the tendency of objects to resist a change in their state of rest or uniform motion**. He built the first law on that idea, and presented all three laws in **1687**. The **unit of force is named after him**.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Constant velocity" means neither the MAGNITUDE nor the DIRECTION changes.** An object at rest has zero velocity — a special case. A non-zero constant velocity means motion **in a straight line, in the same direction, at the same speed**.
>
> So **going round a bend at a steady speed is NOT constant velocity**, and does require a net force.

**Worked:** a person pushes a moving box forward with a force **exactly equal to the friction**. Does the box stop?

**No — the two forces are equal and opposite, so they balance; the net force is zero; by the first law the box CONTINUES MOVING WITH CONSTANT VELOCITY.**

**Graphs when no net force acts** — there are exactly **two** possibilities:

| Case | Position–time graph | Velocity–time graph |
|---|---|---|
| **At rest** | horizontal line (position unchanged) | horizontal line **at zero** |
| **Constant velocity** | **sloping straight line** | horizontal line **at a non-zero height** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Once an object is moving, no further force is needed to keep it moving at constant velocity.** A net force is needed only to
> - **start** it from rest,
> - **change** its velocity (magnitude or direction), or
> - **stop** it.

## 6.5 Newton's second law of motion

**A force produces acceleration. But what exactly is the relationship?**

### Hypothesis 1 — bigger force, bigger acceleration

**From experience:** a gentle push starts a ball moving slowly; a strong push starts it moving fast. **So for the same object, larger force → larger acceleration.**

**Testing it (the cart experiment):** a cardboard-box cart on pencil axles and ball-bearing wheels, pulled by a thread over a pipe (pulley) with a **cup of coins** hanging from it. The falling cup pulls the cart with a **constant force**, and the force is changed by changing the **weight in the cup**.

**Both runs start from rest (u = 0) and cover the same distance s, so using `s = ½at²`:**

```
s = ½ a1 T1²    and    s = ½ a2 T2²

equating:       a1 / a2  =  T2² / T1²
```

**Doubling the weight in the cup shortens the time, and the ratio shows the acceleration increased.**

> [!TIP]
> **Why weights are the natural way to apply a known force:** the **weight of an object is the gravitational force on it**, and different objects have different weights. So a cup of coins is a ready-made adjustable force — and the same cup, released the same way, gives the **same force every trial**.

### Hypothesis 2 — bigger mass, smaller acceleration

**From experience:** with the same push, it is easier to set a **lighter** object moving.

**Testing it:** repeat the cart experiment, but keep the **cup and its contents fixed** and **double the mass of the cart**. **The acceleration decreases.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **NEWTON'S SECOND LAW: when a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates IN THE DIRECTION OF THE NET FORCE. The magnitude of the acceleration is PROPORTIONAL to the net force and INVERSELY PROPORTIONAL to the mass.**
> ```
> a = F / m        or        F = m a
> ```
> **The direction of the acceleration is the direction of the net force.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Defining the newton.** Put m = 1 kg and a = 1 m/s² into F = ma:
> ```
> F = 1 kg × 1 m/s² = 1 kg m/s² = 1 N
> ```
> **One newton is the force that gives a 1 kg object an acceleration of 1 m/s².**

### Weight

```
F = m g          where g = 9.8 m/s²  (about 10 m/s² for quick estimates)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **g does NOT depend on the mass of the object.** It is nearly constant near the Earth's surface. Heavier objects feel a **larger force** (F = mg), but that larger force acts on a **proportionally larger mass**, so `a = F/m = g` for everything.

> [!WARNING]
> **In the cart experiment, doubling the force should exactly double the acceleration — but you may measure a little less.** Besides measurement error, the **friction between the cart's wheels and the surface** is not really zero. **Real data rarely matches the ideal exactly, and knowing WHY is part of the experiment.**

> [!NOTE]
> **The fuller form of the second law** uses **MOMENTUM** — the product of an object's mass and velocity, pointing in the direction of the velocity. It states that **the rate of change of momentum is proportional to the net force, in the direction of the net force**. That version works even when the mass is not constant.

### The second law in daily life

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The pattern behind all these examples: a = F/m rearranged as F = ma. STRETCH OUT THE TIME over which a velocity change happens, and the acceleration falls — so the FORCE falls too.**

| Situation | What is done | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Catching a fast cricket ball** | the fielder **pulls their hands back** with the ball | **increases the time** the ball takes to stop → smaller acceleration → **smaller force** on hands → less injury |
| **Airbags** in a car | inflate into a soft compressible cushion | the head and chest push into the bag instead of a hard dashboard, so the stopping **time is longer** → smaller force → fewer serious injuries (especially with a seat belt) |
| **Landing mat / sand bed** for a high jump | soft, deep surface | the athlete stops **gradually** rather than instantly → far smaller force on the body |
| **Bubble wrap or hay** round glass | soft packing | the same reasoning — jolts are spread over a longer time |
| **Cracking a coconut** | bring it down **fast** onto a hard surface | it stops in a **very short time**, so the ground must exert a **very large force** — large enough to break the shell |

**Worked — holding a barbell.** Bar 10 kg plus 10 kg on each side = **30 kg**.
```
gravitational force = m g = 30 × 9.8 = 294 N downwards
```
**To hold it steady she must apply 294 N upwards** — balanced forces, zero net force, no acceleration.

**Worked — pushing a 25 kg block against 50 N of friction:**

```
(i) push = 50 N   → balanced, net force = 0 → the block STAYS STATIONARY

(ii) push = 55 N  → net force = 55 − 50 = 5 N
                    a = F/m = 5/25 = 0.2 m/s²
                    s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 0.2 × 2² = 0.4 m in 2 s
```

**Worked — a 1500 kg sports car from its velocity–time graph:**

| Interval | Graph | Acceleration | Force |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 s | sloping up, 0 → 10 m/s | `a = 10/5 =` **2 m/s²** | `F = 1500 × 2 =` **3000 N east** |
| 5–10 s | **flat** — constant velocity | **0** | **no net force at all** |
| 10–15 s | sloping down, 10 → 0 m/s | `a = (0−10)/5 =` **−2 m/s²** | `F =` **−3000 N**, i.e. **3000 N west** |

## 6.6 Newton's third law of motion

**A force needs at least TWO objects to interact.** Kick a ball and you **feel the ball push back on your foot**.

**Activity:** sit on a wheeled chair with your feet off the floor and **push a heavy table away** — **you and the chair move backwards**. **Pull** the table towards you — **you move forwards**.

**Activity — two spring balances hooked together**, one end fixed, and pulled. **Both scales read the SAME, every time, whatever the pull.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **NEWTON'S THIRD LAW: whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object SIMULTANEOUSLY exerts an EQUAL and OPPOSITE force on the first.**

> [!WARNING]
> **The two forces of a third-law pair act on TWO DIFFERENT OBJECTS, so they do NOT cancel each other.**
>
> **Balanced forces are different:** those are two equal and opposite forces acting **on the SAME object**, and they do cancel.
>
> **This one distinction resolves almost every puzzle about the third law.** If action and reaction cancelled, nothing could ever accelerate.

### Third-law examples

| Situation | You push | Something pushes back |
|---|---|---|
| **Walking or running** | your **feet push the ground BACKWARDS** | the ground pushes your feet **forwards** — the force is **friction**, so here **friction HELPS you move** |
| **Bicycle without pedalling** | feet push the ground backwards | ground pushes you and the bicycle forwards |
| **Rowing a canoe** | the paddle **pushes water backwards** | the water pushes the **paddle and canoe forwards**; push harder and the canoe goes faster |
| **Climbing a coconut tree** | the legs **push down** against the trunk | **friction pushes the person UP** — which is why a smooth trunk with less friction is harder to climb |
| **A balloon released** | the shrinking balloon **expels air backwards** | the air pushes the balloon **forwards** |
| **A rocket** | the engine **expels gas downwards** | the gas pushes the **rocket upwards** — and since this exceeds the rocket's weight, the **net force is upward** and it lifts off |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why grooves and treads exist.** When your foot tries to slide backwards, friction acts forwards and moves you. **Without friction your foot would slip backwards and you would fall.** **Grooves on soles** and **treads on tyres** increase that friction — which is exactly why wet polished floors, ice, and roads covered in water or snow are dangerous.

> [!NOTE]
> **Chandrayaan-3's soft landing** used the third law in reverse. Firing the engine **in the direction of motion** makes the exhaust push **backwards** on the craft, **slowing it down**. That is how the **Vikram lander** reached the velocity needed to land softly near the Moon's south pole.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Newton's third law applies to ALL forces — contact and non-contact alike:** two bar magnets push each other equally and oppositely; two similarly charged balloons repel each other equally; **the Earth and a falling fruit pull each other with equal and opposite gravitational forces**.

**Worked — why the fruit falls to the Earth and not the Earth to the fruit.** The forces are **equal in magnitude**. But `a = F/m`, and **the mass of the Earth is enormous compared with the fruit**, so the Earth's acceleration is **extraordinarily small** — far too small to notice. **The forces are equal; the accelerations are not.**

**Worked — a 0.1 kg bullet fired from a 5 kg gun with 2 N of force:**

```
by the third law, the recoil force on the gun is also 2 N

acceleration of the gun    = 2 / 5    = 0.4 m/s²
acceleration of the bullet = 2 / 0.1  = 20 m/s²
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Equal forces, unequal accelerations — because the masses differ.** This is the single most useful consequence of combining the second and third laws.

## 6.7 Forces on a system of objects

**Two boxes of masses m1 and m2 on a frictionless surface, joined by a string. A force F pulls Box 1.**

**Box 1 pulls Box 2 through the string, and by the third law Box 2 pulls back equally. That force is the TENSION, T.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The simpler way: treat the two boxes AND the string as a SINGLE SYSTEM.**
>
> - **INTERNAL forces** (the tension T, which acts on both boxes) can be **ignored** — they are third-law pairs inside the system.
> - **Only EXTERNAL forces** (here, F) matter.
> ```
> a = F / (mass of the system) = F / (m1 + m2)
> ```
> **The system accelerates exactly like a single object of mass m1 + m2.**

**Analysing each box separately gives the same answer** — but treating connected objects as one system is far quicker.

> [!NOTE]
> The other external forces are the **gravitational force (m1 g + m2 g)** downwards, **balanced by the normal force (N1 + N2)** from the ground.

> [!TIP]
> **The same idea lets you study a walking person.** Arms and legs move in a complicated way, yet the **overall motion** can be worked out by treating the whole body as a single object.
>
> **Science often becomes simpler when we stop looking at parts and start looking at the whole** — choosing what to include in the "system" is itself a modelling decision, exactly as in Chapter 1.

> [!NOTE]
> **Newton's laws describe motion across an enormous range of scales**, from everyday objects to planets and stars. They need modification only **very close to massive objects**, at **speeds near the speed of light**, and at **atomic scales**.

## At a glance

```
FORCE   needs magnitude AND direction; SI unit newton (N); measured by spring balance
        1 N ≈ the upward force your palm exerts holding a 100 g mass

net force  same direction → ADD        opposite directions → SUBTRACT,
                                        direction of the larger
balanced   net force zero → motion unchanged
unbalanced net force non-zero → motion changes

FRICTION  acts opposite to the motion, between surfaces in contact
          smaller friction → object travels further before stopping
          moving objects stop because of friction, NOT because motion needs a force
          normal force (up) balances weight (down)

FIRST LAW    at rest stays at rest; in motion continues at CONSTANT VELOCITY,
             unless a NET FORCE acts.  (inertia)
SECOND LAW   a = F/m, i.e. F = m a; acceleration is along the net force
             1 N gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²;  weight F = m g,  g = 9.8 m/s²
             lengthen the stopping TIME → smaller acceleration → SMALLER FORCE
             (catching a ball, airbags, landing mats, bubble wrap)
THIRD LAW    every force has an equal and opposite partner force
             the pair acts on TWO DIFFERENT OBJECTS, so it does NOT cancel
             walking, rowing, rockets, recoil
             equal forces ≠ equal accelerations, because masses differ

SYSTEM   treat connected objects as one: ignore internal forces,
         a = F / (total mass)
```
