## The question that starts the chapter

**Why do athletes in a 4 × 100 m relay start at different points?**

Because runners in **outer lanes travel a longer curve**. The offset between adjacent starting points is the **stagger** — and to work out how big it must be, we need to know **the length around a circle**.

## Perimeter

**The PERIMETER of a shape is the total length around its border** — the distance a tiny insect walks going once round without turning back.

| Shape | Perimeter |
|---|---|
| Square, side a | **4a** |
| Equilateral triangle, side a | **3a** |
| Rectangle, sides a and b | **2(a + b)** |

> [!TIP]
> **The square's formula is a SPECIAL CASE of the rectangle's** — put `a = b` in `2(a + b)` and you get `4a`.

**A key observation:** for a square, `perimeter : side = 4 : 1` — **whatever the size**. For an equilateral triangle it is always `3 : 1`.

**So it is natural to ask: for a circle, is `circumference : diameter` also fixed?**

## π — the C/D ratio

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The ratio of circumference to diameter is the SAME for every circle, big or small.** That constant is **π**.
>
> **`Circumference = πd = 2πr`**

**Measure it yourself:** wind thin thread **20 times** round a cotton reel of measured diameter D, unwind and measure the length L. Then `L ÷ (20D)` estimates π.

### The history

| Who | When | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mesopotamia** | c. 1900 BCE | `3 + 1/8 = 3.125` | saw that a circle's perimeter exceeds the inscribed hexagon's, so **π > 3** |
| **Archimedes** | c. 250 BCE | `3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7` | **trapped** π between inscribed and circumscribed polygons, up to **96 sides** |
| **Ptolemy** | c. 150 CE | `377/120 ≈ 3.14167` | refined Archimedes for astronomical tables |
| **Zu Chongzhi** | 480 CE | `22/7` and **`355/113 ≈ 3.1415929`** | pushed polygons to **24,576 sides**; `355/113` stayed the world's best for **800 years** |
| **Āryabhaṭa** | 499 CE | `62832/20000 = 3.1416` | called it **āsanna** — "approaching" — hinting no exact fraction exists |
| **Brahmagupta** | 628 CE | `√10 ≈ 3.1622` | less accurate but **algebraically elegant**; dominated the Arab world and medieval Europe |
| **Mādhava** | 14th c. | **exact** | `π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …` |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Mādhava's leap.** Everyone before him tried to **approximate** π by a fraction. Mādhava saw that π is **a limit to be reached**, not a fraction to be found — and gave the **first EXACT formula**, an **infinite series**.
>
> **Moving from cutting circles geometrically to summing numbers analytically, he birthed CALCULUS.** His series gave π to **11 decimal places** (3.14159265358).

**Later breakthroughs:** **Nīlakaṇṭha** (c. 1500), **Machin** (1706), **Ramanujan** (1914), and the **Chudnovsky brothers** (1988) who extended Ramanujan's ideas. **Today we know π to hundreds of trillions of digits.**

> [!NOTE]
> **The symbol π** was first used for this ratio by the Welsh mathematician **William Jones in 1706** — π is the first letter of the Greek *perimetros*, perimeter. **Leonhard Euler** made it popular.

### π is irrational

**Its digits go on forever with no repeating pattern**, so it **cannot be written as a ratio of two integers**. **Lambert proved this in 1761.**

> [!WARNING]
> **`22/7` is close to π but NOT equal to it.** Write **`π ≈ 22/7`** and **`π ≠ 22/7`** — exactly as `√2 ≈ 1.414` but `√2 ≠ 1.414`.
>
> **And since π is irrational, there is no "best fraction" for it** — however close a fraction gets, another gets closer.

> [!TIP]
> **A mnemonic:** *"How I wish I could recollect pi."* Count the letters — **3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2** → π ≈ 3.141592
>
> **14 March** ("3-14") is **Pi Day**; **22 July** ("22-7") is **Pi Approximation Day**.

## Arc length

**A semicircle is half the circle**, so its arc is `2πr ÷ 2 =` **πr**.
**A quarter circle** is `2πr ÷ 4 =` **πr/2**.

**Why the halves are equal:** reflect the circle in a diameter and the two semicircles **swap places** — so they must have the same length. (Rotating by 180° gives the same conclusion.)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Arc length for an angle θ° at the centre:**
>
> **`arc = 2πr × θ/360`**
>
> Because a semicircle is `2πr × 180/360` and a quarter is `2πr × 90/360`, the general rule follows.

**Perimeter of a SECTOR** = the arc **plus the two radii** = `2πr × θ/360 + 2r`.

### The athletics track

A 400 m track has **two straights of 84.39 m** and **two semicircles**, the innermost of radius **36.5 m**, with lanes **1.22 m** wide.

**For a runner 0.3 m out from the inner border:**

```
straights:   2 × 84.39               = 168.78 m
curves:      the two semicircles make one full circle
             2 × π × 36.8 = 2 × 3.1416 × 36.8 = 231.22 m
TOTAL                                 = 400.00 m  ✓
```

**Why staggers are needed:** on the straights both runners cover the same distance. **On the curves the outer runner's semicircles have a bigger radius**, so she covers more — and the stagger compensates exactly.

> [!TIP]
> **The stagger between adjacent lanes is `2π × 1.22 ≈ 7.67 m`** — the extra circumference from one lane's width, and **the same between every pair of adjacent lanes**, because each lane adds the same 1.22 m to the radius.
>
> **It does NOT depend on the size of the track.** A 200 m track needs the same stagger for a 4 × 100 m relay — because it depends only on the **lane width**, not the radius.

## Two perimeter puzzles

**Two equal circles, each passing through the other's centre.** With radius r, the triangle joining the two centres to an intersection point has all sides r, so it is **equilateral** — giving `∠CAB = 60°` and hence `∠CAD = 120°`.

So each dotted arc is **⅓** of its circle, and each red arc is **⅔**. Total red length `= 2 × ⅔ × 2πr =` **8πr/3**.

**One big semicircle vs three small ones.** With radii `a′` and `b′, c′, d′`, the paths are `πa′` and `π(b′ + c′ + d′)`.

**But `PQ = 2a′` and also `PQ = 2b′ + 2c′ + 2d′`, so `a′ = b′ + c′ + d′`.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The two paths are EXACTLY EQUAL in length** — however many small semicircles you use, and whatever their sizes.
>
> Most people guess the wiggly path is longer. It isn't: arc length is **proportional to radius**, so splitting one radius into pieces leaves the total unchanged.

## Area

**Area is measured against a unit: the 1 × 1 square, of area 1 sq unit.**

| Shape | Area |
|---|---|
| Rectangle, sides a and b | **ab** |
| Square, side a | **a²** |
| **Parallelogram** | **base × height** |
| **Triangle** | **½ × base × height** |

### Why the parallelogram formula works

**Cut off the triangle at one end and slide it to the other** — the parallelogram becomes a **rectangle with the same base and height**. Different shape, same area.

> [!WARNING]
> **You CANNOT find a parallelogram's area from its side lengths alone.**
>
> Push a rectangle over: the sides stay the same but the **height shrinks**, so the area falls. **The angle matters.**
>
> **A "thin" parallelogram** where the perpendicular foot falls outside the base needs a repeated version of the same cut-and-slide, but the formula is unchanged.

### Why the triangle formula works

**Two congruent copies of any triangle fit together to make a parallelogram** with the same base and height.

So `Area of triangle = ½ × Area of parallelogram =` **½ bh**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A MEDIAN of a triangle divides it into two triangles of EQUAL AREA.**
>
> **Why:** the two halves have **equal bases** (`BD = DC`) and the **same height** from A. Area depends on nothing else.
>
> **This is genuinely surprising** — the two triangles are usually **not congruent**, yet their areas match exactly.

## Heron's formula

**For a triangle with sides a, b, c and semi-perimeter `s = ½(a + b + c)`:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **`Area = √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)]`**

**Check 1 — equilateral triangle, side a.** `s = 3a/2`, so
`Area = √[(3a/2)(a/2)(a/2)(a/2)] =` **(√3/4)a²** ✓ — the standard result.

**Check 2 — isosceles, equal sides a and base 2b.** `s = a + b`, so
`Area = √[(a+b)(b)(b)(a−b)] = b√(a² − b²)` ✓ — matching ½ × base × height.

**Check 3 — sides 3, 4, 5.** `s = 6`, so `Area = √(6 × 3 × 2 × 1) = √36 =` **6** ✓
And since `3² + 4² = 5²`, the triangle is right-angled, giving `½ × 3 × 4 =` **6** ✓

> [!TIP]
> **Heron's formula is the tool for when you know the three SIDES but no height.** It's how you find the area of a triangular plot from a tape measure alone.

**Two more triangle-area formulas, both involving circles:**

```
Area = abc / (4R)          R = radius of the CIRCUMcircle
Area = r(a + b + c) / 2    r = radius of the INcircle
```

**The INCIRCLE is the one circle that fits tightly inside, touching all three sides.**

## Brahmagupta's formula

> [!WARNING]
> **You CANNOT find a quadrilateral's area from its four sides alone.** A rhombus with all sides 3 can have area 9, or 8.01, or 5.41 — squash it and the area changes while the sides don't.
>
> **You need one extra piece of information** — an angle, a diagonal, or a geometric property.

**One such property is being CYCLIC.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Brahmagupta (628 CE): for a CYCLIC quadrilateral with sides a, b, c, d and `s = ½(a + b + c + d)`:**
>
> **`Area = √[(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)(s − d)]`**

**Check — a rectangle** (all rectangles are cyclic). With sides a, b, a, b: `s = a + b`, so
`Area = √[(b)(a)(b)(a)] =` **ab** ✓

**Check — an isosceles trapezium** (all are cyclic). It simplifies to `(a + b)h`, exactly matching **half the sum of the parallel sides times the height**.

### Brahmagupta generalises Heron

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Set `d = 0`.** The quadrilateral collapses into a **triangle** (vertices A and D coincide), and every triangle is cyclic.
>
> Then `s = ½(a + b + c)` — the triangle's semi-perimeter — and
>
> `√[(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)(s−0)] = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)]`
>
> **which is exactly Heron's formula.** ∎

> [!TIP]
> **Special cases and generalisation.** A **special case** comes from a general result by adding a condition; the general result is a **generalisation**.
>
> - Square is a special case of rectangle → `A = a²` from `A = ab` with `b = a`
> - Isosceles right triangle is a special case of right triangle → `c = a√2` from `a² + b² = c²` with `a = b`
> - `(a + b)²` is a special case of `(a + b + c)²` with `c = 0`
> - **Heron is a special case of Brahmagupta with `d = 0`**

## Squaring a rectangle

**"Squaring a shape" means constructing a square of the same area.** Baudhāyana's *Śhulbasūtra* (800 BCE) shows how for a rectangle `a × b` with `a > b`.

```
Rectangle ABCD with AD = a, AB = b
  • Mark E on AD with AE = AB (= b)
  • Let F be the midpoint of ED
  • Draw square AFGH with side AF, H on AB produced
  • Draw arc AG centred at H, cutting BC at K
  • Draw a line through K parallel to AH, meeting GH at P
  • Square HPQS has the SAME AREA as rectangle ABCD
```

**Why it works** — it is a geometric version of an algebraic identity:

```
AF = (a + b)/2   so   HK = (a + b)/2   (a radius)
BH = AF − b = (a − b)/2
By Baudhāyana–Pythagoras in ∆HKP:
HP² = HK² − BH² = [(a+b)/2]² − [(a−b)/2]²
    = (a² + 2ab + b²)/4 − (a² − 2ab + b²)/4 = ab
```

**So the square on HP has area `ab` — the rectangle's area** ✓

> [!TIP]
> **The identity behind it is `((a+b)/2)² − ((a−b)/2)² = ab`** — a geometric translation of the difference of two squares.

## Area of a circle

**Why area must be proportional to the square of the perimeter.** For any fixed shape, the ratio `P² : A` is **the same at every scale**:

- **Square:** `P = 4a`, `A = a²` → `P² : A = 16a² : a² =` **16 : 1**
- **Equilateral triangle:** `P = 3a`, `A = (√3/4)a²` → `P² : A =` **36 : √3**

**So for circles too, `C² : A` must be a fixed constant.** The only question was: which?

| Who | Value |
|---|---|
| **Babylonians** (before 1500 BCE) | `C² : A ≈ 12 : 1`, i.e. `A ≈ C²/12` |
| **Egyptians** (c. 1500 BCE) and the **Baudhāyana Śhulbasūtra** (800 BCE) | `A ≈ (8d/9)² = (256/81)r²` |
| **Archimedes** (c. 250 BCE) | **exactly `A = πr²`** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Archimedes' insight:** *"The area of a circle equals the area of a right triangle with legs equal to the radius and the circumference."*
>
> `Area = ½ × circumference × radius = ½ × 2πr × r =` **πr²**
>
> **His proof** used the fact that **any regular polygon's area is ½ × perimeter × the radius of its incircle** — then asked what happens as the number of sides grows without limit.

### Nīlakaṇṭha's visual proof

**Slice the circle into thin sectors and lay them alternately point-up and point-down.**

**The result approaches a parallelogram with:**
- **base = half the circumference = πr** (half the sectors point each way)
- **height = the radius r**

**Area = base × height = πr × r =** **πr²** ✓

**The thinner the slices, the flatter each arc becomes and the closer the shape is to a true parallelogram.**

### Sector area

**A SECTOR is the region bounded by an arc and the two radii to its ends.**

**`Sector area = πr² × θ/360`** — the same fraction of the circle as the arc is of the circumference.

```

square 4a · equilateral triangle 3a · rectangle 2(a + b)
circle          C = 2πr = πd
arc (angle θ)   2πr × θ/360
sector perimeter  2πr × θ/360 + 2r

AREA
rectangle ab · square a² · parallelogram bh · triangle ½bh
Heron           √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)],  s = ½(a+b+c)
Brahmagupta     √[(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)(s−d)],  s = ½(a+b+c+d), cyclic only
triangle        abc/(4R)  and  r(a+b+c)/2
circle          A = πr²
sector          πr² × θ/360
```
