## Why the runners don't start in a line

**In a 4 × 100 m relay, runners in the outer lanes start AHEAD of those in the inner lanes**, though everyone finishes at the same line. The gap between starting points is the **stagger**. Is it unfair? To answer, we need the length of a **curved** path — the perimeter of a circle. That's where this chapter begins.

## 6.1 Perimeter

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **PERIMETER = the total length around the border of a shape** — how far a tiny insect walks going once round the edge, never turning back.

| Shape | Perimeter | perimeter ÷ side |
|---|---|---|
| square, side a | 4a | always **4** |
| equilateral triangle, side a | 3a | always **3** |
| rectangle a × b | 2(a + b) | (square is the special case a = b) |

**Double the side of a square → the perimeter doubles too.** The ratio perimeter : side stays fixed for every square, however big. **Is the same true for circles — is circumference : diameter fixed?**

## 6.2 The C/D ratio — π

**Yes. For every circle, circumference ÷ diameter is the same number. We call it π** ("pie").

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **C = πd = 2πr**
>
> **Why the ratio is the same for all circles:** any two circles are scaled copies of each other. Scaling by a factor k multiplies **every** length by k — the circumference *and* the diameter — so their ratio doesn't change.

**Home measurement:** wind thin thread 20 times round a cotton reel of diameter D, unwind and measure length L. Then L ÷ (20D) comes out a little over **3.1**.

### π through history

| Who | When | Value of π |
|---|---|---|
| Mesopotamia | c. 1900 BCE | 3 + 1/8 = **3.125** — a circle's perimeter is a bit **more** than its inscribed hexagon's (6r = 3d), so π > 3 |
| Archimedes | 250 BCE | trapped between polygons: **3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7** (96-sided polygons) |
| Ptolemy | 150 CE | 377/120 ≈ **3.14167** |
| Zu Chongzhi | 480 CE | **22/7** and the amazing **355/113 ≈ 3.1415929** (best for 800 years) |
| Āryabhaṭa | 499 CE | 62832/20000 = **3.1416**, called **āsanna** ("approximate") |
| Brahmagupta | 628 CE | **√10 ≈ 3.162** — less accurate but easy to calculate with |
| **Mādhava** | 14th c. | the first **exact** formula: **π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …** |

> [!NOTE]
> **Why the inscribed hexagon shows π > 3:** a regular hexagon in a circle of radius r is six equilateral triangles, so each side is r and its perimeter is 6r = 3d. The circle bulges outside every side, so C > 3d, i.e. π > 3. The **circumscribed** hexagon is bigger than the circle; its perimeter is 2√3 d ≈ 3.46d — so **3 < π < 3.46**.

**Mādhava's infinite series** turned π from "a number to approximate" into "a limit to reach" — the seed of **calculus**. He used it to get π to 11 decimal places (3.14159265358). Later: Nīlakaṇṭha, Machin, **Ramanujan (1914)**, the Chudnovsky brothers — today π is known to hundreds of trillions of digits. The symbol π (from Greek *perimetros*) was introduced by William Jones in 1706 and made popular by Euler.

### π is irrational

**π can't be written as p/q** (proved by Lambert, 1761). So **π ≠ 22/7** — it's only **π ≈ 22/7**, good enough for everyday use. There's no "best fraction": any fraction close to π can be beaten by a closer one.

> [!TIP]
> **"How I wish I could recollect pi"** — count the letters: 3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2 → **3.141592**. Pi Day is 14 March (3/14); Pi Approximation Day is 22 July (22/7).

## 6.3 Length of an arc

**A semicircle** is half the circle — reflecting the circle in a diameter swaps the two halves, so they're equal: length **πr**. **A quarter circle**: rotating by 90° moves each quarter onto the next, so each is **2πr ÷ 4 = πr/2**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An arc that subtends θ° at the centre has length 2πr × θ/360.**
>
> **Why:** turning the circle by equal angles moves equal arcs onto each other, so arc length is **proportional to the angle**. The whole circle is 360°, so θ° gets the fraction θ/360 of 2πr.

### The running track

**A 400 m track:** two straights of 84.39 m and two semicircular bends of inner radius 36.5 m; lanes are 1.22 m wide. A runner 0.3 m from the inner edge runs:

```
straights:  2 × 84.39                = 168.78 m
bends:      two semicircles = 1 circle of radius 36.8
            2 × 3.1416 × 36.8        = 231.22 m
total                                = 400.00 m
```

**One lane further out**, the radius is 1.22 m bigger, so one full circle of bend is longer by **2π × 1.22 ≈ 7.67 m**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The extra length depends only on the LANE WIDTH, not on the radius.** 2π(R + w) − 2πR = **2πw**. So every pair of neighbouring lanes needs the **same** stagger per bend.
>
> **School track question:** a 200 m track has a smaller radius, but that doesn't shrink the extra 2πw per full circle of bend. The same race distance has **more** bends on a small track, so the total stagger needed is actually **not smaller**.

### Perimeter puzzles

**Example 1 — two circles through each other's centres** (radius r, centres A and B, meeting at C and D). AB = AC = BC = r, so triangle ABC is **equilateral**: ∠CAB = 60°, and likewise ∠DAB = 60°, so ∠CAD = **120°**. The hidden (inside) arc of each circle is 120/360 = 1/3 of it; the visible arc is **2/3**. Outline = 2 × (2/3) × 2πr = **8πr/3**.

**Example 2 — one big semicircle versus three small ones** on the same line PQ. Lengths are πa′ and π(b′ + c′ + d′). But the diameters fill PQ both ways: 2a′ = 2b′ + 2c′ + 2d′, so a′ = b′ + c′ + d′. **The paths are exactly equal!**

> [!NOTE]
> **Why:** semicircle length is π × radius — **proportional to the diameter**. Splitting a diameter into pieces splits the length the same way.

## 6.4 Areas of rectangles, parallelograms and triangles

**Area** measures the space a flat region covers, in **unit squares** (1 × 1). A rectangle a × b holds **ab** unit squares.

### Parallelogram = base × height

**Cut a right triangle off one end of the parallelogram and slide it to the other end** — you get a rectangle with the **same base b and height h**. Nothing was added or lost, so:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a parallelogram = base × height = bh.**
>
> (For a very "thin" parallelogram where the cut won't fit, slide a sliver across repeatedly — each slide keeps the area — until the trick works.)

> [!WARNING]
> **Side lengths alone don't fix a parallelogram's area.** Push a rectangle 3 × 3 over into a rhombus: sides stay 3 but the height shrinks, so the area drops (9, then 8.01, then 5.41 …). You need the **height** (or an angle).

### Triangle = ½ × base × height

**Two copies of any triangle fit together into a parallelogram** (turn one copy round; the matching angles make alternate angles equal, so the sides are parallel). The parallelogram has area bh, so each triangle has:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height.**

### A surprise about medians

**A MEDIAN joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.** Median AD splits triangle ABC into ABD and ACD.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A median divides a triangle into two triangles of EQUAL AREA.**
>
> **Why:** BD = DC (equal bases) and both triangles have the **same height** (from A down to BC). ½ × base × height is the same for both — even though the two triangles usually have **different shapes**.

**Rectangles with perimeter 40:** infinitely many (a + b = 20). Area a(20 − a) is **largest for the square 10 × 10 (area 100)**; there's **no smallest** — a 19.9 × 0.1 rectangle has area under 2, and you can go lower forever.

## 6.5 Heron's formula — area from the three sides

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For a triangle with sides a, b, c, let s = (a + b + c)/2 (the SEMI-PERIMETER). Then**
>
> **Area = √[ s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c) ]**

**Testing it on triangles we already know:**

- **3-4-5 triangle:** s = 6 → √(6 × 3 × 2 × 1) = √36 = **6**. Check: it's right-angled (3² + 4² = 5²), so ½ × 3 × 4 = **6** ✓.
- **Equilateral, side a:** s = 3a/2 → √(3a/2 × a/2 × a/2 × a/2) = **(√3/4) a²**. Check: the height is √(a² − a²/4) = (√3/2)a, so ½ × a × (√3/2)a = (√3/4)a² ✓.
- **Isosceles, sides a, a, base 2b:** s = a + b → √[(a + b)(b)(b)(a − b)] = **b√(a² − b²)**. Check: height √(a² − b²), area ½ × 2b × √(a² − b²) ✓.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why trust it?** It agrees with ½bh every time we can check, and it can be **proved** using Baudhāyana–Pythagoras and the difference-of-squares identity again and again (Class 10). Two more area formulas: **abc / (4R)** (R = circumradius) and **½ r (a + b + c)** (r = inradius).

### Brahmagupta's formula — cyclic quadrilaterals

**A quadrilateral's sides don't fix its area** (the rhombus example). But if it is **cyclic** (corners on a circle), they do:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Brahmagupta (628 CE): for a cyclic quadrilateral with sides a, b, c, d and s = (a + b + c + d)/2,**
>
> **Area = √[ (s − a)(s − b)(s − c)(s − d) ]**

- **Rectangle a × b:** s = a + b → √(b × a × b × a) = **ab** ✓.
- **It GENERALISES Heron:** a triangle is a "quadrilateral" with **d = 0** (two corners merged), and every triangle is cyclic. Put d = 0: s − d = s, and Brahmagupta's formula **becomes Heron's**.

> [!TIP]
> **Special case and generalisation:** a square is a special case of a rectangle (b = a), so A = a² comes from A = ab. (a + b)² is the special case c = 0 of (a + b + c)². Heron is the special case d = 0 of Brahmagupta.

## 6.6 Squaring a rectangle (Baudhāyana, 800 BCE)

**"Squaring" a shape = building a square of equal area.** Baudhāyana's Śhulbasūtra squares an a × b rectangle with ruler and rope. The idea in algebra:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ab = ((a + b)/2)² − ((a − b)/2)²**
>
> **Why:** expand: (a² + 2ab + b²)/4 − (a² − 2ab + b²)/4 = 4ab/4 = ab.
>
> So the square on a right-triangle leg with hypotenuse (a + b)/2 and other leg (a − b)/2 has area **ab** (Baudhāyana–Pythagoras) — that is what his compass-and-cord construction draws.

## 6.7 Area of a circle

**Why A must be (constant) × C²:** for any shape, scaling all lengths by k multiplies perimeter by k and area by k². So **P² : A** is fixed for each shape — 16 for every square, 36 : √3 for every equilateral triangle. For circles, the Babylonians measured C² : A ≈ 12; the Egyptians (c. 1500 BCE) used A ≈ (8d/9)², i.e. π ≈ 256/81 ≈ 3.16 — and the **same rule appears in Baudhāyana's Śhulbasūtra**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a circle = πr².**
>
> **Archimedes (250 BCE):** a circle has the same area as a right triangle with legs **r** and **2πr**: ½ × 2πr × r = πr².
>
> **Nīlakaṇṭha's picture (c. 1500):** cut the circle into many thin slices and lay them alternately point-up and point-down. They form an almost-parallelogram with **height r** and **base = half the circumference = πr** (half the slices' arcs are on top, half on the bottom). Thinner slices → closer to a true parallelogram. **Area = πr × r = πr².**

### Sectors and segments

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **SECTOR** (region between two radii and an arc), angle θ°: **area = πr² × θ/360.**
> - **SEGMENT** (region between a chord and its arc): **area = sector − triangle.**
>
> **Why the sector formula:** exactly as for arc length — rotational symmetry makes area proportional to the angle. A semicircle is ½πr², a quadrant ¼πr².

## In a nutshell

- **π = C ÷ d** for every circle; **C = 2πr**; π is irrational (≈ 22/7 ≈ 3.1416). Mādhava: π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − …
- **Arc** = 2πr × θ/360. **Sector** = πr² × θ/360. **Segment** = sector − triangle.
- Track lanes: extra length per full bend = **2π × lane width**, whatever the radius.
- **Parallelogram = bh; triangle = ½bh** (two copies make a parallelogram). **A median halves the area.**
- **Heron:** √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)], s = half the perimeter. **Brahmagupta** (cyclic): √[(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)(s − d)] — Heron is the case d = 0.
- **Squaring a rectangle:** ab = ((a + b)/2)² − ((a − b)/2)².
- **Circle area = πr²** (Archimedes' triangle; Nīlakaṇṭha's slices).
- Scale lengths by k → perimeters × k, **areas × k²**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** π ≈ 22/7, not equal · use the **radius** in 2πr and πr² (halve a given diameter) · a sector's perimeter = arc + **2 radii** · in Heron, s is **half** the perimeter · equal sides don't mean equal areas (parallelograms, quadrilaterals) — need a height, an angle, or "cyclic" · doubling lengths **quadruples** area · convert units before calculating (km → cm for wheel turns) · semicircle of radius r is πr long, not 2πr.
