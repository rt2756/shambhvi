## What a coordinate system is

**A structured framework — like the grid lines on a map or graph paper — that lets us use NUMBERS to describe the exact physical location of a point.**

> [!NOTE]
> **The idea is very old, and has deep roots in India.**
>
> - **Sindhu-Sarasvatī Civilisation** — city streets laid out precisely **North–South and East–West about 10 metres apart**. A merchant could find a warehouse by **counting N–S and E–W units from the city centre** — a coordinate system in practice, on an urban scale.
> - **Baudhāyana** used East–West and North–South lines for his geometric constructions, laying the foundation of coordinate geometry.
> - **Ujjayinī** was described as early as the 4th century BCE as the **central longitude meridian** from which all other locations were measured. **Ptolemy** (c. 150 BCE) listed latitudes and longitudes of thousands of places including 'Ozine' (Ujjayinī).
> - **Āryabhaṭa** (c. 499 CE) replaced Greek 'chords' with **'sines'**, making it far easier to calculate the coordinates of a star or city, and mapped the sky using **celestial coordinates**.
> - **Brahmagupta** (c. 628 CE) formalised **zero and negative numbers** as algebraic entities. **Without them the four-quadrant Cartesian plane would be impossible** — the origin *is* zero and the negative axes *are* values less than zero.
> - His work reached Arabic as the *Sindhind*, and the Ujjayinī meridian became **'Arin'**, the zero-longitude reference of early Arabic maps. **Al-Bīrūnī** (c. 1000 CE) travelled to India, used Indian trigonometric methods to compute city coordinates, and perfected the **astrolabe** so sailors could find their position from the stars. **Ömar Khayyām** (c. 1100 CE) was the first to solve algebraic problems geometrically using coordinates.
> - The final step came in Europe: **Fermat (1636)** and **René Descartes (1637)** formalised that **any point in a plane is defined by just two numbers** — its distances from two perpendicular axes. **That is why the plane is called Cartesian.**

## The 2-D Cartesian coordinate system

**A number line is one-dimensional.** For two dimensions we need **two lines at right angles**:

```
                 y-axis
                    |
    Quadrant II  4  |   Quadrant I
       (−, +)    3  |     (+, +)
                 2  |
                 1  |
 ────────────────O──┼─────────────  x-axis
   −4 −3 −2 −1   0  | 1  2  3  4
                −1  |
    Quadrant III−2  |   Quadrant IV
       (−, −)   −3  |     (+, −)
```

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **x-axis** | the **horizontal** line |
| **y-axis** | the **vertical** line |
| **Origin O** | where they cross — coordinates **(0, 0)** |
| **Cartesian plane** | the plane the axes sit in (also *coordinate plane* or *xy-plane*) |
| **Quadrants** | the four parts the axes divide the plane into |

**Sign conventions:** **right of O and upwards are POSITIVE; left of O and downwards are NEGATIVE.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For a point `P (x, y)`:**
>
> - **x** is the perpendicular distance of P from the **y-axis**, measured along the x-axis
> - **y** is the perpendicular distance of P from the **x-axis**, measured along the y-axis
>
> **Alphabetical order is the memory aid: x before y, just as across comes before up.**

### The quadrants

| Quadrant | x | y | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | + | + | (3, 4) |
| **II** | **−** | + | (−5, 3) |
| **III** | **−** | **−** | (−2, −6) |
| **IV** | + | **−** | (3, −5) |

**Points on the axes:**

- **On the x-axis: `(x, 0)`** — the y-coordinate is **always 0**
- **On the y-axis: `(0, y)`** — the x-coordinate is **always 0**
- **The origin: `(0, 0)`**

> [!WARNING]
> **Order matters: `(x, y)` is NOT the same as `(y, x)`.**
>
> `(3, 5)` and `(5, 3)` are two different points. **They coincide only when x = y** — i.e. on the line through the origin at 45°.

> [!TIP]
> **When writing a point on a graph, drop the `=`:** write **P (x, y)** rather than P = (x, y).

## Distance between two points

**Along an axis it's just a subtraction:**

- Between `(x₁, y)` and `(x₂, y)` — same y — the distance is **|x₂ − x₁|**
- Between `(x, y₁)` and `(x, y₂)` — same x — the distance is **|y₂ − y₁|**

**When the segment is slanted, use the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem.**

```
        A (x₁, y₁)
          |\
          | \  ← the distance we want
  y₂ − y₁ |  \
          |   \
   F ─────┴────D (x₂, y₂)
        x₂ − x₁
```

**The horizontal leg is `x₂ − x₁`, the vertical leg is `y₂ − y₁`, and the distance is the hypotenuse:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **`Distance = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]`**
>
> **It makes no difference whether the differences come out positive or negative** — squaring removes the sign, and we're only measuring shifts along the two axes.

**Worked:** A (3, 4) and D (7, 1).
Horizontal shift `= 7 − 3 = 4`. Vertical shift `= 4 − 1 = 3`.
`AD = √(4² + 3²) = √25 =` **5 units**

**Worked:** D (7, 1) and M (9, 6) → `√(2² + 5²) =` **√29 units**
**Worked:** M (9, 6) and A (3, 4) → `√(6² + 2²) =` **√40 units**

### Reflection preserves distance

Reflect ∆ADM in the y-axis: A(3,4) → A′(−3,4), D(7,1) → D′(−7,1), M(9,6) → M′(−9,6).

`C′D′ = −3 − (−7) = 4` and `A′C′ = 4 − 1 = 3`, so `A′D′ =` **5 units** — unchanged.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Reflection changes the SIGNS of coordinates but not the LENGTHS of sides.**
>
> - Reflection in the **y-axis**: `(x, y) → (−x, y)`
> - Reflection in the **x-axis**: `(x, y) → (x, −y)`
>
> **All the distances stay the same either way**, because the squared differences are unaffected.

## Midpoints

**The midpoint of the segment joining `(x₁, y₁)` and `(x₂, y₂)` is**

**`( (x₁ + x₂)/2 , (y₁ + y₂)/2 )`** — **the average of the x's and the average of the y's.**

**Why:** the midpoint is halfway across and halfway up, and "halfway between two numbers" is their average.

**Check:** is (3, 4) the midpoint of (2, 3) and (4, 5)? `(2+4)/2 = 3` ✓ and `(3+5)/2 = 4` ✓ — **yes**.
Is (0, −2) the midpoint of (−8, 7) and (6, −3)? `(−8+6)/2 = −1 ≠ 0` — **no**.

**Working backwards.** If M (−7, 1) is the midpoint of A (3, −4) and B (x, y):

```
(3 + x)/2 = −7  →  x = −17
(−4 + y)/2 = 1  →  y = 6
```
**B = (−17, 6)**

### Points of trisection

**P and Q divide AB into three equal parts, P nearer A.**

For A (4, 7) and B (16, −2), the total shift is `(+12, −9)`, so each third is `(+4, −3)`:

**P = (8, 4)** and **Q = (12, 1)**

## Testing whether three points are in a straight line

> [!TIP]
> **Without plotting: check whether the distances add up.**
>
> Three points are collinear **exactly when the longest distance equals the sum of the other two**.

**Worked:** M (−3, −4), A (0, 0), G (6, 8).
`MA = √(3² + 4²) = 5` · `AG = √(6² + 8²) = 10` · `MG = √(9² + 12²) = 15`
Since `5 + 10 = 15`, **the points ARE collinear** ✓

**Worked:** R (−5, −1), B (−2, −5), C (4, −12).
`RB = √(3² + 4²) = 5` · `BC = √(6² + 7²) = √85 ≈ 9.22` · `RC = √(9² + 11²) = √202 ≈ 14.21`
`5 + 9.22 = 14.22 ≈ 14.21` — very close but **not exact**, so **not collinear** (they are very nearly so).

## Circles and coordinates

**A point lies on a circle centred at the origin exactly when its distance from the origin equals the radius.**

**Worked:** A (1, −8), B (−4, 7), C (−7, −4) — all have distance from O of
`√(1 + 64) = √65` · `√(16 + 49) = √65` · `√(49 + 16) = √65`
**All three lie on a circle of radius √65** ✓

**Then** (with the radius `√65 ≈ 8.06`):

- **D (−5, 6)** → `√(25 + 36) = √61 ≈ 7.81` — **less** than the radius → **inside** the circle
- **E (0, 9)** → distance **9** — **more** than the radius → **outside** the circle

## Worked shape example

**A (2, 1), B (−1, 2), C (−2, −1), D (1, −2). Is ABCD a square?**

All four sides: `AB = √(3² + 1²) = √10`, `BC = √(1² + 3²) = √10`, `CD = √(3² + 1²) = √10`, `DA = √(1² + 3²) = √10` — **all equal** ✓

Diagonals: `AC = √(4² + 2²) = √20`, `BD = √(2² + 4²) = √20` — **equal** ✓

**Equal sides + equal diagonals = a square** (Class 8, Chapter 4). **Area `= (√10)² =` 10 square units.**

## Summary of the rules

```
origin                     (0, 0)
point on the x-axis        (x, 0)
point on the y-axis        (0, y)
quadrant signs             I (+,+)  II (−,+)  III (−,−)  IV (+,−)
horizontal distance        |x₂ − x₁|
vertical distance          |y₂ − y₁|
general distance           √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]
midpoint                   ( (x₁+x₂)/2 , (y₁+y₂)/2 )
```

> [!TIP]
> **What if we had no negative numbers?** We could only describe **one quadrant** — everything up and to the right of the origin.
>
> That's exactly why Brahmagupta's formalisation of zero and negatives was the missing piece that made the full coordinate plane possible.
