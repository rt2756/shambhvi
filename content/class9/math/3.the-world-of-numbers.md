## The big picture: numbers grew because people needed them

**Every new kind of number was invented to answer a question the old numbers couldn't.**

| Question people asked | Numbers invented | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| "How many cows?" | **natural numbers** 1, 2, 3, … | N |
| "How many are left if I give all away?" | **zero** | |
| "What if I owe more than I have?" | **negative numbers** → **integers** …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, … | Z |
| "How much is each share?" | **fractions** → **rational numbers** p/q | Q |
| "How long is a square's diagonal?" | **irrational numbers** like √2, π | |
| all of the above together | **real numbers** — the whole number line | R |

## 3.1 Counting: the natural numbers

**Before words for numbers, herders used ONE-TO-ONE CORRESPONDENCE:** one pebble in a pot for each cow that went out, one pebble removed for each cow that came back. Pebbles left over → cows missing. **Matching objects one to one is the idea of counting** — the birth of the **natural numbers N = {1, 2, 3, 4, …}**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Numbers carved in bone.**
>
> - **Lebombo bone** (southern Africa, about 35,000 years old) — 29 deliberate notches, probably a **lunar calendar**.
> - **Ishango bone** (Congo, around 20,000 BCE) — one column groups notches as **11, 13, 17, 19: exactly the primes between 10 and 20**; another seems to show **doubling**.
> - **India:** Harappa and Lothal used standard weights for trade. The **Vedas** named every power of 10 up to **10¹² (parārdha)**; the **Lalitavistara** (4th century BCE) goes up to **10⁵³ (tallakṣhaṇa)**. Thinking in powers of 10 led to the **place-value system** and, at last, to **zero**.

### Exercise Set 3.1, worked

1. **15 ingots per 2 bags; 12 bags.** 12 bags = 6 lots of 2 bags → 6 × 15 = **90 ingots**.
2. **11, 13, 17, 19** are all **prime** (only factors 1 and themselves). The next three primes: **23, 29, 31**.
3. **Are natural numbers closed under subtraction?** **No.** 7 − 3 = 4 is natural, but **3 − 5 = −2** and **4 − 4 = 0** are not. One counter-example is enough to show "not closed".
4. **Finger joints:** each of the 4 fingers has 3 joints → **12** countable with the thumb on one hand — a natural reason for **base-12** counting (dozens, 12 months, 12 hours).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **CLOSED under an operation** means: do the operation on any two members, and the answer is **always** still a member.
>
> **Why it matters:** each time a set was *not* closed under something people needed, a bigger set was invented. Natural numbers aren't closed under subtraction → integers. Integers aren't closed under division → rationals.

## 3.2 Zero — when nothing became a number

**For thousands of years the number line started at 1.** Babylonians and Mayans had a **placeholder** symbol for an empty column, but nobody calculated *with* "nothing".

- Indian philosophy prized **śhūnyatā** ("emptiness") — the still, empty mind of meditation (Upanishads, Buddhist texts, Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras). A culture comfortable with "nothing" as an idea was ready to make it a number.
- The **Bakhśhālī manuscript** (early centuries CE) shows a bold **dot (bindu)** for zero.
- **Brahmagupta** (*Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, 628 CE) **defined zero as a − a**, and gave its rules.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Brahmagupta's rules for zero:**
>
> - **a + 0 = a** — adding nothing changes nothing.
> - **a − 0 = a** — taking away nothing changes nothing.
> - **a × 0 = 0** — "a, taken zero times" is nothing at all.

## 3.3 Integers — fortunes and debts

**What is 3 − 5?** Brahmagupta answered with **money**: positive numbers are **fortunes (dhana)**, negative numbers are **debts (ṛiṇa)**. Moving **left of zero** on the number line gives the negatives. **Positives, negatives and zero together are the INTEGERS, Z** (from German *Zahlen*, "numbers").

**Brahmagupta's rules, still used exactly as he wrote them:**

| Rule | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| fortune + fortune = fortune | 5 + 4 = 9 | |
| debt + debt = debt | (−5) + (−4) = −9 | owe ₹5, borrow ₹4 more, owe ₹9 |
| debt × fortune = debt | (−3) × 4 = −12 | four debts of ₹3 = ₹12 debt |
| debt × debt = fortune | (−3) × (−4) = 12 | see below |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why negative × negative = positive:** a negative number is a debt; multiplying by a negative means **taking away**. If someone **takes away four of your debts of ₹3 each**, you are **₹12 better off**. So (−3) × (−4) = +12.
>
> **Pattern check:** (−3) × 2 = −6, (−3) × 1 = −3, (−3) × 0 = 0 — each step the answer goes **up by 3**. Keep going: (−3) × (−1) = **3**, (−3) × (−2) = **6**. The pattern forces it.

### Exercise Set 3.2, worked

1. **Ladakh:** 4 °C, drops 15 °C → 4 − 15 = **−11 °C**.
2. **Trader:** −850 + 1200 − 450 = **−100** → he is **₹100 in debt**.
3. (i) (−12) × 5 = **−60** (ii) (−8) × (−7) = **56** (iii) 0 − (−14) = **14** (iv) (−20) ÷ 4 = **−5**.
4. **10 − (−5) = 15:** you have ₹10 and someone **cancels a ₹5 debt** you owed. Removing a debt makes you richer by 5 — exactly like adding 5.

## 3.4 Rational numbers

**Sharing and measuring need parts of a whole — fractions.** Give each positive fraction a negative partner (2/3 has −2/3), put them together with the integers, and you get the **RATIONAL NUMBERS, Q** (for *quotient*).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A RATIONAL NUMBER is any number that can be written as p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.**
>
> **Why q ≠ 0:** p/q means "the number which, multiplied by q, gives p". If q = 0, then (anything) × 0 = 0, so there is **no** number that gives p ≠ 0 — and **every** number would "give" 0. Division by zero has no single answer, so it's not allowed.

**Things to notice:**

- **Integers are rational too:** 5 = 5/1, −10 = −10/1. So N ⊂ Z ⊂ Q.
- **One rational number has many names:** −3/4 = −6/8 = −9/12 = −30/40 … (**equivalent fractions**). You may cancel any common factor from top and bottom: 30/36 = 5/6 (dividing both by 6).
- **The standard name** uses p and q with **no common factor except 1** (co-prime): among 2/3, 4/6, 6/9, … we use **2/3**.
- The minus sign can sit anywhere: −(2/3) = (−2)/3 = 2/(−3).

### The laws of arithmetic for rationals (Brahmagupta's rules)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> 1. **Equality:** a/b = c/d exactly when **ad = bc**.
> 2. **Add/subtract:** first rewrite both with the **same denominator**, then a/b ± c/b = (a ± c)/b.
> 3. **Multiply:** (a/b) × (c/d) = **ac/bd**.
> 4. **Divide:** (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c) = **ad/bc** (c ≠ 0).
> 5. Addition and multiplication are **commutative**, and **p(q + r) = pq + pr** (distributive).

**Why the equality test works:** multiply both fractions by bd. a/b × bd = ad and c/d × bd = bc. Equal fractions stay equal when multiplied by the same thing, so a/b = c/d ⇔ ad = bc. (It's cross-multiplication.)

**Why we flip and multiply to divide:** dividing by c/d asks "how many c/d's fit?" One whole contains d/c of them (e.g. 1 ÷ 1/4 = 4 = 4/1), so a/b contains (a/b) × (d/c).

> [!NOTE]
> **Rationals are CLOSED under +, −, × and under ÷ by non-zero numbers.** The formulas above always produce an integer over a non-zero integer.

> [!TIP]
> **Cancel before multiplying.** In −4/7 × 14/5, the 14 and the 7 share 7: −4 × 2 / 5 = −8/5. Smaller numbers, fewer mistakes.

### On the number line, and absolute value

**To mark p/q:** cut each unit into **q equal parts**, then move **p parts** from 0 — right if positive, left if negative. 3/4: cut 0–1 into 4, go 3 parts right. 9/4 = 2¼: go to 2, cut 2–3 into 4, go 1 part right.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ABSOLUTE VALUE |x| = the distance of x from 0.** |5/3| = 5/3, |−5/3| = 5/3, |0| = 0. **It is never negative**, because a distance can't be.
>
> **The distance between a and b is |a − b|.** Between −4 and 3: |−4 − 3| = |−7| = **7**.

### Density — there's always another rational in between

**Between 1 and 2 lies 3/2. Between 1 and 3/2 lies 5/4. Between 1 and 5/4 lies 9/8 …** It never stops.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Between any two rationals a and b there is another rational: their AVERAGE (a + b)/2.**
>
> **Why it's rational:** a + b is rational and halving a rational gives a rational (closure).
> **Why it's between them:** if a < b, then a + a < a + b < b + b, i.e. 2a < a + b < 2b; halving, **a < (a + b)/2 < b**.
>
> Repeat forever → **infinitely many rationals between any two**. We say the rationals are **DENSE**.

## 3.5 Irrational numbers

**Draw a square with side 1.** By Baudhāyana–Pythagoras its diagonal d has d² = 1² + 1² = **2**, so **d = √2**. Baudhāyana met this length around 800 BCE while designing fire altars; the Greeks met it later. **Can √2 be written as p/q? No.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **IRRATIONAL numbers are points on the number line that CANNOT be written as p/q** (with integers p, q, q ≠ 0).

### Proof that √2 is irrational (proof by contradiction)

**Idea of the method:** assume the opposite of what you want, and show it leads to something impossible. Then the assumption must be false. (First done by **Hippasus**, c. 400 BCE.)

1. **Suppose** √2 = p/q in **lowest terms** (p, q have no common factor).
2. Square: 2 = p²/q², so **2q² = p²**.
3. So **p² is even**. Then **p is even** — because if p were odd, p × p would be odd × odd = odd. Write **p = 2k**.
4. Substitute: 2q² = (2k)² = 4k², so **q² = 2k²**.
5. So **q² is even**, hence **q is even**.
6. **Both p and q are even** — they share the factor 2. But we said p/q was in lowest terms. **Contradiction!**
7. The only step that could be wrong is the assumption. **So √2 is irrational.** ∎

> [!NOTE]
> **The same proof works for √3, √5, √7, √10** — anything that isn't a perfect square. For √5 the key step becomes "if 5 divides p², then 5 divides p" — true because **5 is prime** (if a prime divides a product, it divides one of the factors). It fails for √4 = 2 precisely because 4 *is* a perfect square.

### Constructing √2 on the number line

1. On the number line mark **OA = 1**.
2. At A draw a perpendicular, and mark **AB = 1** on it.
3. **OB = √(1² + 1²) = √2** (right angle at A).
4. With compass at O and radius OB, draw an arc to cut the number line at **P**. **OP = √2**, so P marks √2.

> [!TIP]
> **Keep going to get √3, √4, √5, …** At B draw a perpendicular BC = 1; then OC = √(2 + 1) = **√3**. Each new 1-unit step at right angles adds 1 under the root. This chain of triangles is the **SQUARE ROOT SPIRAL**: its hypotenuses are **√2, √3, √4 = 2, √5, √6, …**, the nth one being √(n + 1).

### π and Mādhava's infinite series

**π = circumference ÷ diameter.** **Āryabhaṭa** (499 CE) gave π ≈ 62832/20000 = **3.1416**, calling it **āsanna** ("approximate") — he suspected no exact fraction existed. **Lambert proved π irrational in 1761.**

**Mādhava of Sangamagrāma** (14th century, Kerala school) found an *exact* formula — not a fraction, but an **infinite sum**:

```
π = 4 × (1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + 1/9 − …)
```

**Adding infinitely many terms** means: the value the partial sums get **closer and closer to** as you add more terms.

## 3.6 Real numbers and their decimals

**Rationals + irrationals = the REAL NUMBERS (R)** — the complete, gap-free number line. The decimal expansion tells you which kind a number is.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every rational number's decimal either TERMINATES (7/20 = 0.35) or REPEATS (5/11 = 0.454545… = 0.45̄).**
> **An irrational number's decimal NEVER ends and NEVER repeats** (√2 = 1.41421356…, π = 3.14159265…).

**Why a rational must repeat (dividing by 7):** at each step of long division the remainder is one of **0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6**. If 0 appears, the division stops (terminates). Otherwise only **six** remainders are possible, so within at most 6 steps **a remainder must come back** — and from then on the division repeats exactly what it did before. Same for any denominator q: at most q − 1 steps before a repeat.

### Predicting "terminating" without dividing

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Write p/q in lowest terms. The decimal TERMINATES exactly when q's prime factors are only 2s and/or 5s.**
>
> **Why:** 10 = 2 × 5, so every power of 10 is made of only 2s and 5s. If q = 2ᵃ × 5ᵇ, multiply top and bottom by enough 2s or 5s to make the bottom a power of 10 — and a fraction over 1000… is a terminating decimal. If q has any other prime (3, 7, 11 …), no power of 10 can ever be a multiple of q, so it can't terminate.

**Worked:** 3/20, 20 = 2² × 5 → multiply by 5: 15/100 = **0.15**. 18/125: 125 = 5³ → multiply by 2³ = 8: 144/1000 = **0.144** (3 places). 4/15: 15 = 3 × 5 — a 3 is present → **repeating** (0.2666…).

> [!TIP]
> **Number of decimal places = the bigger power of 2 or 5 in q.** Denominator 2³ × 5 → make it 10³ by multiplying by 5² → **3 decimal places**.

### Converting decimals back to p/q

**Terminating:** 0.35 = 35/100 = **7/20**.

**Pure repeating — 0.6̄ = 0.666…**

```
   x = 0.666…
 10x = 6.666…        (one digit repeats → multiply by 10)
 10x − x = 6         (the endless tails cancel exactly)
   9x = 6  →  x = 6/9 = 2/3
```

**0.45̄ = 0.4545…:** two digits repeat → 100x = 45.45…; 99x = 45 → x = 45/99 = **5/11**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why multiply by 10ⁿ, n = length of the repeating block:** it shifts the number by exactly one full cycle, so the two numbers have **identical tails** after the decimal point. Subtracting kills the infinite tail and leaves an ordinary equation.

**General repeating — 0.16̄ = 0.1666…** (1 non-repeating digit, then 6 repeats):

```
  10x = 1.666…      (move the non-repeating part left of the point)
 100x = 16.666…     (move one full cycle more)
  90x = 15  →  x = 15/90 = 1/6
```

**The book's 2.357̄ (only the 7 repeats) = 2.35777…:** 100x = 235.777…, 1000x = 2357.777…, so 900x = 2122 → x = 2122/900 = **1061/450**.

**2.4537̄ with "37" repeating:** 100x = 245.3737…, 10000x = 24537.3737…, so 9900x = 24292 → **x = 6073/2475**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Recipe:** let x = the decimal; multiply by 10ᵐ (m = non-repeating digits after the point); multiply by a further 10ⁿ (n = repeating digits); subtract the two; solve.

### 0.999… = 1

x = 0.999…; 10x = 9.999…; 9x = 9; **x = 1**. It isn't "a tiny bit less" — there is **no** number between 0.999… and 1 (any gap would show up in some decimal place). Every terminating decimal has a twin with 9s: 2.47 = 2.46999…

### The cyclic number 142857

**1/7 = 0.142857142857…** Multiply the block by 1 to 6:

```
142857 × 2 = 285714      142857 × 5 = 714285
142857 × 3 = 428571      142857 × 6 = 857142
142857 × 4 = 571428
```

**The same six digits, just rotated.** Why: 2/7, 3/7, … 6/7 are produced by the **same long division of 1 by 7**, just starting at a different remainder in the cycle 1 → 3 → 2 → 6 → 4 → 5 → 1. Other primes with this property include **17, 19, 23, 29** (1/17 has a 16-digit cyclic block).

## 3.7 The family of numbers

```
 Real numbers R
 ├── Rational numbers Q   (p/q — terminating or repeating decimals)
 │     └── Integers Z     (…, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …)
 │           └── Natural numbers N  (1, 2, 3, …)
 └── Irrational numbers   (√2, √3, π — never-ending, never-repeating)
```

**Is that the end?** No real number squared gives −1 (positive × positive and negative × negative are both positive). Mathematicians invented **imaginary numbers** (i, with i² = −1) — used in electrical engineering and quantum physics. That's for later years.

## In a nutshell

- **N ⊂ Z ⊂ Q ⊂ R**, and the **irrationals** fill the rest of R.
- **Zero** = a − a (Brahmagupta); a + 0 = a, a − 0 = a, a × 0 = 0. Debt × debt = fortune.
- **Rational = p/q**, integers, q ≠ 0. Equal iff ad = bc. Closed under +, −, ×, ÷ (not by 0).
- **|x|** = distance from 0; distance between a and b = |a − b|.
- **Dense:** (a + b)/2 is always a rational strictly between a and b.
- **√2 is irrational** — proof by contradiction (p and q would both be even). Same for √(non-square).
- **Construct √n** by stacking right triangles with a 1-unit leg — the square root spiral.
- **π**: Āryabhaṭa's 3.1416 (āsanna); Mādhava's π = 4(1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − …).
- **Decimals:** rational ⇔ terminating or repeating. Terminating ⇔ (lowest-terms) denominator = 2ᵃ5ᵇ.
- **Repeating → p/q:** multiply by 10ⁿ (n = block length) and subtract. **0.999… = 1.**
