## Where counting began

**Mathematics did not begin in a classroom — it began on bark and bone.**

**One-to-one correspondence.** A herder with no words or symbols for numbers could still keep count: **drop one pebble into a pot for each cow leaving; take one out for each cow returning.** An empty pot at dusk means the herd is safe.

**That simple matching of one object to another is the birth of the NATURAL NUMBERS: ℕ = {1, 2, 3, 4, …}.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Numbers written in bone.**
> - **The Lebombo Bone** (South Africa/Swaziland, ~**35,000 years old**) has **29 deliberate, uniformly sized notches** — probably a **lunar phase counter** or menstrual calendar.
> - **The Ishango Bone** (near the headwaters of the Nile, ~**20,000 BCE**) has three columns of notches. One column groups them as **11, 13, 17, 19 — the primes between 10 and 20** — and another shows **doubling**. The abstract idea of "number" is tens of thousands of years old.

> [!NOTE]
> **Large numbers in India.** The **Vedas** name every power of 10 up to **10¹²** (*parārdha*). The **Lalitavistara** (4th century BCE) has Buddha naming powers up to **10⁵³** (*tallakṣhaṇa*). Expressing quantities as **powers of 10** in the **Ṛigveda** set the stage for the place-value system India later gave the world — and paved the way for **zero**.

## Zero — Śhūnya

**For millennia the number line started at 1.** Babylonians and Mayans used **placeholders** for an empty column — but never treated "nothing" as a **number you could add, subtract and multiply**.

> [!NOTE]
> **From philosophy to mathematics.** In the **Upanishads** and Buddhist literature, **Śhūnyatā** (emptiness) was the goal of yoga and meditation — emptying the mind of all **vṛttis** (fluctuations) to reach perfect stillness. **Patanjali's Yoga Sutras** (~3rd century BCE) describe it too.
>
> **Because Indian thinkers revered this state of emptiness, they already had the conceptual framework to welcome "nothingness" as a thing in its own right.** That idea travelled into architecture, literature, linguistics — and finally into mathematics with **Āryabhaṭa** and **Brahmagupta**.
>
> The **Bakhśhālī Manuscript** (early centuries CE) shows the physical step: a bold **dot (bindu)** for zero.

**But a symbol is just a mark until it has RULES.** In the **Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE)**, **Brahmagupta** defined zero as **the result of subtracting a number from itself: `a − a = 0`**, and laid down:

| Brahmagupta's rules for zero |
|---|
| `a + 0 = a` |
| `a − 0 = a` |
| `a × 0 = 0` |

## Integers

**Brahmagupta then asked: if `5 − 5 = 0`, what is `3 − 5`?**

He grounded the answer in commerce:

- **Fortunes (Dhana)** — positive numbers, wealth
- **Debts (Ṛiṇa)** — negative numbers, what you owe

**Natural numbers + their negatives + zero = the INTEGERS, written ℤ** (from the German *Zahlen*, "numbers").

**His rules, still used exactly as written 1,300 years ago:**

| Rule | Example | In his language |
|---|---|---|
| fortune + fortune = fortune | `5 + 4 = 9` | |
| debt + debt = debt | `(−5) + (−4) = −9` | owe ₹5, borrow ₹4 more → owe ₹9 |
| anything − 0 is unchanged | `7 − 0 = 7`, `−6 − 0 = −6` | |
| debt × fortune = debt | `(−3) × 4 = −12` | take on 4 debts of ₹3 → owe ₹12 |
| debt × debt = **fortune** | `(−3) × (−4) = 12` | |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why does negative × negative = positive?**
>
> A negative number is a **debt**. Multiplying by a negative means **REMOVING** those debts.
>
> **If someone takes away four of your ₹3 debts, you are ₹12 richer** — so `(−3) × (−4) = +12`.

> [!TIP]
> **Subtracting a negative is adding a positive.** `10 − (−5) = 15` — **cancelling a ₹5 debt leaves you ₹5 better off**, exactly as if you'd been given ₹5.

## Rational numbers

**When you combine all integers and all fractions (positive and negative), you get the RATIONAL NUMBERS, written ℚ** (for *quotient*).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A rational number is any number expressible as `p/q`, where p and q are INTEGERS and `q ≠ 0`.**

**Three things to notice:**

1. **Rationals include everything before them.** `5 = 5/1` and `−10 = −10/1`, so **natural numbers, whole numbers and integers are all rational**.

2. **The representation isn't unique.** `−1/3 = −2/6 = −3/9 = −10/30 = …` — these are **equivalent fractions**. That's why you can freely cancel a common factor: `12/30 = 2/5` after dividing both by 6.

3. **By convention we use the lowest terms.** Among the infinitely many fractions equal to ½, we pick **½** — with p and q **co-prime** (no common factor but 1).

> [!WARNING]
> **Why must `q ≠ 0`?** Because division by zero is undefined — there is no number that, multiplied by 0, gives a non-zero result.

**In a negative fraction the minus sign can sit anywhere:** `−1/5 = (−1)/5 = 1/(−5)`.

### Arithmetic laws

| Operation | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Equality** | `a/b = c/d` exactly when **`ad = bc`** |
| **Addition/subtraction** | make the denominators the **same**, then `a/b ± c/b = (a ± c)/b` |
| **Multiplication** | `(a/b) × (c/d) = ac/bd` |
| **Division** | `(a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c) = ad/bc`, needing `c ≠ 0` too |

**Both addition and multiplication are commutative, and distributivity holds:** `p(q + r) = pq + pr`.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **CLOSURE.** Rational numbers are **closed** under addition, subtraction and multiplication — combining two rationals always gives a rational. They are **also closed under division, provided you never divide by zero.**
>
> **Natural numbers are NOT closed under subtraction:** `3 − 5 = −2` is not a natural number. That gap is exactly why integers had to be invented.

## Rationals on the number line

**To place `p/q`: divide each unit interval into q equal parts, then move p parts** — right if positive, left if negative.

**¾:** divide 0 to 1 into four parts, move three right.
**9/4 = 2¼:** it lies between 2 and 3 — divide that interval into four and move one part right of 2.

**ABSOLUTE VALUE.** `|x|` is the **distance of x from 0**.

`|5/3| = 5/3` · `|−5/3| = 5/3` · `|0| = 0`

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **`|x| ≥ 0` always** — a distance is never negative.
>
> **And the distance between two numbers a and b is `|a − b|`.** From −4 to 3 is `|−4 − 3| =` **7**.

## Density

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Between ANY two rational numbers there is another rational number — so there are INFINITELY MANY between any two.**
>
> **How to find one: take their AVERAGE.** Between 1 and 3/2: `(1 + 3/2)/2 =` **5/4**.
>
> **Why the average always works:** `(a + b)/2` is a sum and a division of rationals, so it's rational (closure); and it lies **strictly between** a and b whenever `a ≠ b`.

**It feels as though rationals must completely fill the number line, leaving no gaps. But do they?**

## Irrational numbers

**No.** Take a square of side 1. By the **Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem** its diagonal d satisfies `1² + 1² = d²`, so **`d = √2`**.

**Baudhāyana met such lengths around 800 BCE** while building geometric fire altars in the *Śhulbasūtra*; the Greeks hit the same crisis centuries later.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Numbers on the number line that CANNOT be expressed as a ratio of integers are IRRATIONAL NUMBERS.**

### Proof that √2 is irrational

**By CONTRADICTION** — assume the opposite and show it leads to a logical disaster. (First given by **Hippasus** of the Pythagorean school, c. 400 BCE.)

```
Step 1  Assume √2 = p/q in SIMPLEST form (p, q co-prime, q ≠ 0)
Step 2  Square:            2 = p²/q²
Step 3  Multiply by q²:    2q² = p²
Step 4  p² is 2 × (an integer), so p² is EVEN → p is EVEN.  Let p = 2k
Step 5  Substitute:        2q² = (2k)² = 4k²
Step 6  Divide by 2:       q² = 2k²
Step 7  So q² is EVEN → q is EVEN
Step 8  CONTRADICTION: p and q are BOTH even, so they share a
        factor of 2 — but we said the fraction was in simplest form!
```

**The logic is flawless, so the ASSUMPTION must be wrong. √2 cannot be written as a fraction — it is irrational.** ∎

> [!TIP]
> **Step 4 uses a fact worth remembering: if a number's SQUARE is even, the number itself is even.** (An odd number squared is always odd.)
>
> **The same proof works for √3, √5, √7, √10** — and for the square root of any number that isn't a perfect square.

### Constructing √2 on the number line

```
Step 1  On the number line mark OA = 1 unit; draw a perpendicular at A
Step 2  On it mark B with AB = 1 unit; join OB.  Then OB = √2 (Pythagoras)
Step 3  With centre O and radius OB, draw an arc cutting the
        number line at P.  Then OP = √2, so P represents √2
```

**Repeating from there gives √3, √5, and in general √n** for any positive integer n.

### π

**π is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter.**

**Āryabhaṭa (499 CE)** gave the very accurate `3927/1250 = 3.1416` — but explicitly called it an **āsanna (approximation)**, indicating an exact fraction likely could not be found. **Lambert proved π irrational in 1761.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Mādhava of Sangamagrama** (14th century), who founded the **Kerala School of Mathematics**, realised that **to express an irrational number you cannot use a single fraction — you must use an INFINITE SUM**:
>
> **`π = 4 × (1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …)`**
>
> "Adding infinitely many terms" means finding the value you get **closer and closer to** as you add more and more terms.

## Real numbers

**Rationals ∪ irrationals = the REAL NUMBERS, ℝ** — the unbroken, continuous number line.

**The easiest way to tell rational from irrational is the DECIMAL EXPANSION.**

### Rational decimals — always terminate or repeat

**Dividing p by q, exactly one of two things happens:**

1. **It TERMINATES** — the remainder eventually becomes 0. `3/8 = 0.375`
2. **It REPEATS** — the digits loop forever. `5/11 = 0.454545… = 0.4̄5̄`

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why repetition is inevitable.** Dividing by 7, the only possible remainders are **1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6** (0 would mean it terminated).
>
> **With only finitely many possible remainders, one must eventually repeat — and once a remainder repeats, the whole division loops.**

### Predicting the type without dividing

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For `p/q` in lowest terms, the decimal TERMINATES exactly when the prime factors of q are ONLY 2s, ONLY 5s, or both.**
>
> **Why:** in that case you can multiply numerator and denominator to make the denominator a **power of 10**.
>
> `3/20`: `20 = 2² × 5`, so `3/20 = (3 × 5)/(2² × 5 × 5) = 15/100 =` **0.15** ✓
>
> Anything else in the denominator — a 3, a 7, an 11 — makes it **repeat**.

**Quick tests:** `7/20` (`20 = 2²×5`) **terminates** · `4/15` (`15 = 3×5`) **repeats** · `13/250` (`250 = 2×5³`) **terminates**.

### Converting decimals back to fractions

**Terminating:** `0.35 = 35/100 =` **7/20**

**Pure repeating** — the repetition starts immediately after the point:

```
Let x = 0.6̄
1 digit repeats → multiply by 10¹:   10x = 6.6̄
Subtract:                             9x = 6
                                       x = 6/9 = 2/3
```

```
Let x = 0.4̄5̄
2 digits repeat → multiply by 10²:  100x = 45.4̄5̄
Subtract:                             99x = 45
                                       x = 45/99 = 5/11
```

**General repeating** — some non-repeating digits first:

```
Let x = 0.16̄        ('1' non-repeating, '6' repeating)
Multiply by 10¹ (1 non-repeating digit):   10x = 1.6̄
Multiply by 10¹ again (1 repeating digit): 100x = 16.6̄
Subtract:                                   90x = 15
                                              x = 15/90 = 1/6
```

```
Let x = 2.357̄       ('35' non-repeating, '7' repeating)
×10² :   100x = 235.7̄
×10¹ :  1000x = 2357.7̄
Subtract: 900x = 2122  →  x = 2122/900 = 1061/450
```

```
Let x = 2.45̄3̄7̄…  i.e. 2.45 with '37' repeating
×10² :    100x = 245.3̄7̄
×10² :  10000x = 24537.3̄7̄
Subtract: 9900x = 24292  →  x = 24292/9900 = 6073/2475
```

> [!TIP]
> **The recipe in one line:**
> - **Pure repeating** — multiply by `10ⁿ` (n = number of repeating digits), subtract, solve.
> - **General repeating** — multiply by `10ᵐ` (m = non-repeating digits) to clear the non-repeating part, then by `10ⁿ` again, subtract, solve.

### Cyclic numbers

`1/7 = 0.142857142857… = 0.1̄42857̄`

**Multiply the repeating block 142857 by 1 to 6:**

```
142857 × 1 = 142857
142857 × 2 = 285714
142857 × 3 = 428571
142857 × 4 = 571428
142857 × 5 = 714285
142857 × 6 = 857142
```

**The same six digits, simply rotated!** A **cyclic number** — the hallmark of `1/7`.

### Irrational decimals — never end, never repeat

```
√2 = 1.4142135623730950488 …
π  = 3.1415926535897932384 …
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The test:** a decimal that **terminates** or **repeats a block forever** is **rational**. One that **never ends AND never repeats** is **irrational**.
>
> `1.01001000100001…` **is irrational** — it has a pattern, but **no single block that repeats**. A pattern is not the same as a repeating block.

### 0.999… = 1

```
Let x = 0.9̄
10x = 9.9̄
Subtract:  9x = 9  →  x = 1
```

**So `0.999… = 1` exactly.** Most people expect it to be slightly less.

> [!TIP]
> **Decimal representations aren't unique.** Every terminating decimal has an alternative ending in repeating 9s:
> `1.000… = 0.999…` · `2.47000… = 2.46999…`
>
> Just as `1 = 10/10 = 100/100` as fractions, a number can wear more than one decimal costume.

## The whole family

```
ℕ  natural numbers      1, 2, 3, …
   + zero               whole numbers
   + negatives          ℤ  integers
   + fractions          ℚ  rational numbers  (p/q, q ≠ 0)
   + irrationals        ℝ  real numbers      (the unbroken line)
```

Each set **contains** the ones before it, and each was invented because the previous one **wasn't closed** under some operation — subtraction gave us integers, division gave us rationals, and taking square roots gave us irrationals.
