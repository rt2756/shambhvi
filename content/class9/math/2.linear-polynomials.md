## The vocabulary of an algebraic expression

Raju buys **x** red boxes (4 pens each) and **y** blue boxes (5 pencils each), plus **3 free pens**:

**`4x + 5y + 3`**

| Word | In this example |
|---|---|
| **Terms** | `4x`, `5y`, `3` |
| **Variables** (formerly "letter-numbers") | `x` and `y` |
| **Coefficients** | **4** (of x) and **5** (of y) |
| **Constant** | **3** |

## Polynomials in one variable

**Expressions involving ONE variable and its powers are called one-variable (univariate) polynomials — or simply polynomials.**

Examples: `4x` · `x² + 1` · `2y − 5` · `5y³ + y² + 2y − 1` · `3z + 7`

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The DEGREE of a polynomial is the HIGHEST power of the variable in it.**

| Polynomial | Degree | Name |
|---|---|---|
| `5y³ + y² + 2y − 1` | **3** | **cubic** |
| `x² + 5x + 1` | **2** | **quadratic** |
| `3z + 7` | **1** | **linear** |
| `8` | **0** | **constant** |

> [!TIP]
> **Why is the constant 8 degree 0?** Because it can be written as **`8x⁰`** — and `x⁰ = 1`. So the variable is present, raised to the power zero.

**Reading off coefficients.** In `x⁴ − 3x³ + 6x² − 2x + 7`: the coefficient of `x³` is **−3** (take the sign with it) and of `x²` is **6**.

In `4z³ + 5z² − 11` the coefficient of `z` is **0** — there is no z term, and that counts as zero, not "nothing".

In `9x³ + 5x² − 8x − 10` the constant term is **−10**.

## Linear polynomials

**A polynomial of degree 1** — of the form **`ax + b`**.

**Example:** the perimeter of a square of side x is **`4x`** — linear in x.

**Example:** a chess club charges ₹200 to join plus ₹50 a match.

| Matches m | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | … | m |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Amount (₹) | 250 | 300 | 350 | 400 | 450 | … | **200 + 50m** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The characteristic feature of a linear polynomial: the DIFFERENCE between successive values is CONSTANT.**
>
> Here it's **₹50** every match. For the square's perimeter, each 0.5 cm added to the side adds **2 cm** to the perimeter.
>
> **Such patterns are called LINEAR PATTERNS.**

**A LINEAR EQUATION** is what you get when you **equate a linear polynomial to a constant**.

**Worked:** two numbers sum to 64, one being 10 more than the other.
Let the smaller be x; the larger is `x + 10`.
`x + (x + 10) = 64` → `2x + 10 = 64` → `2x = 54` → `x = 27`
**The numbers are 27 and 37.**

**Worked:** a player paid ₹750 at the chess club.
`200 + 50m = 750` → `50m = 550` → **m = 11 matches**

## Polynomials as input–output machines

**Feed in a value of x, get out a value of the polynomial.**

For `2x + 3`: `x = 4 → 2(4) + 3 = 11`; `x = −6 → 2(−6) + 3 = −9`

**This is a FUNCTION** — the expression `2x + 3` is a function of x.

> [!TIP]
> `2x + 3` is a **linear function**; `10x − x²` is a **quadratic function**. The degree of the polynomial names the function.

## Linear patterns from growing shapes

A pattern of square tiles: **1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, …**

**The number of squares at stage n is one less than twice n**: `2 × 2 − 1 = 3`, `2 × 5 − 1 = 9`.

**Number of squares at stage n = `2n − 1`** — a linear polynomial.

**The difference between consecutive terms is the constant 2.**

**Stage 15** → `2(15) − 1 =` **29 tiles** · **Stage 26** → **51 tiles**
**21 tiles** → `2n − 1 = 21` → **stage 11** · **47 tiles** → **stage 24**

**Worked (pocket money):** Bela has ₹100 and spends ₹5 a day. The amount left on day n is **`100 − 5n`**.
Left with ₹40 → `100 − 5n = 40` → **n = 12 days**. Entirely spent → `100 − 5n = 0` → **20 days**.

**Worked (auto fare):** ₹25 for the first 2 km, then ₹15 per km.
For n km (n ≥ 2): `25 + 15(n − 2) =` **`15n − 5`**
For 10 km: `25 + 15 × 8 =` **₹145**. For ₹130: `15n − 5 = 130` → **n = 9 km**.

## Linear growth and linear decay

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **LINEAR GROWTH** — the quantity **increases by a constant amount** over equal intervals.
> - **LINEAR DECAY** — the quantity **decreases by a constant amount** over equal intervals.

**Growth example:** journey cost `C(d) = 100 + 60d`.

| d (km) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C (₹) | 100 | 160 | 220 | 280 | 340 | 400 |

**Every extra km adds a fixed ₹60.** For 15 km: `100 + 900 =` **₹1000**. For ₹700: `100 + 60d = 700` → **d = 10 km**.

**Decay example:** tank height `h(t) = 3 − 0.5t`.

| t (months) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| h (m) | 3 | 2.5 | 2 | 1.5 | 1 |

**Every month the height drops a fixed 0.5 m.** After 5 months: `3 − 2.5 =` **0.5 m**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Not every "increasing" quantity is linear growth.** The amount must increase by the **same amount** each interval, not by the same *percentage* — that would be exponential (Class 8, compound interest).

## Finding a linear relationship from two data points

**A linear relationship between two variables is `y = ax + b`.**

**Worked:** a telecom bill is ₹350 for 10 GB and ₹550 for 20 GB. Find `y = ax + b`.

```
350 = 10a + b        …(1)
550 = 20a + b        …(2)

From (1): b = 350 − 10a
Substituting in (2): 550 = 20a + 350 − 10a
                     550 = 10a + 350
                      10a = 200  →  a = 20
                        b = 350 − 200 = 150
```

**`y = 20x + 150`**

> [!TIP]
> **The two numbers have real meanings:** **a = 20** is the **cost per GB**, and **b = 150** is the **fixed monthly fee** (what you'd pay for 0 GB).
>
> In `y = ax + b`, **b is always the starting value and a is always the rate of change.**

**Worked (Celsius and Fahrenheit):** `C = a·F + b`, with `C = 0` when `F = 32`, and `C = 100` when `F = 212`.

```
0 = 32a + b       →  b = −32a
100 = 212a + b = 212a − 32a = 180a
                  →  a = 100/180 = 5/9
                  →  b = −32 × 5/9 = −160/9
```

**`C = (5/9)F − 160/9 = (5/9)(F − 32)`** — the familiar conversion formula, derived from two data points.

## Graphing a linear equation

**To plot `y = ax + b`, find any TWO points and join them.**

**For `y = 2x + 1`:** `x = 0 → y = 1`, giving A (0, 1). `x = 3 → y = 7`, giving B (3, 7). Join and extend.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **If a point lies on a line, its coordinates must SATISFY the equation.**
>
> Check (7, 15) on `y = 2x + 1`: `2(7) + 1 = 15` ✓ — it's on the line.

**Filling a table for `y = 2x + 1`:**

| x | 1 | 2 | 5 | 7 | 9 | 12 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 3 | 5 | 11 | 15 | 19 | 25 | 41 |

**Reading an equation off a set of points.** For (−1, −3), (0, 0), (1, 3), (3, 9), (4, 12) — **each y is three times its x**, so the line is **`y = 3x`**.

For (−3, 6), (−2, 4), (0, 0), (1, −2), (2, −4), (3, −6) — **each y is −2 times its x**, so **`y = −2x`**.

## What a and b do — slope and intercept

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In `y = ax + b`:**
>
> - **a is the SLOPE** — how steep the line is
> - **b is the y-INTERCEPT** — the line cuts the y-axis at **(0, b)**

### The slope, a

**All lines `y = ax` pass through the origin (0, 0)** — because `x = 0` gives `y = 0`.

| | Effect |
|---|---|
| `a > 1` (e.g. `y = 2x`, `y = 3x`) | **steeper** than `y = x` |
| `a = 1` (`y = x`) | equally inclined to both axes — 45° |
| `0 < a < 1` (e.g. `y = ½x`) | **less steep** than `y = x` |
| `a` negative (`y = −x`, `y = −3x`) | the line goes **downwards** left to right |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Positive slope = LINEAR GROWTH. Negative slope = LINEAR DECAY.**
>
> **And the slope is exactly the constant difference between consecutive terms of the sequence.** The tile pattern 1, 3, 5, 7, … has constant difference 2, and its line `y = 2x − 1` has slope **2**.

### The intercept, b

`y = 2x + 5` cuts the y-axis at **(0, 5)** · `y = x + 3` at **(0, 3)** · `y = 3x − 2` at **(0, −2)**.

**A negative intercept means the line cuts the y-axis BELOW the origin.**

### Changing one while holding the other

| Change | Effect on the graph |
|---|---|
| Change **a**, keep **b** fixed | the **slope changes**; the lines all still cross the y-axis at the same point |
| Change **b**, keep **a** fixed | the lines **shift up or down but stay PARALLEL** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Lines with equal slopes but different y-intercepts are PARALLEL.**
>
> `y = 2x − 1`, `y = 2x + 1` and `y = 2x + 5` are three parallel lines — same steepness, different starting heights.

**Comparing `y = 3x + 1` and `y = −3x + 1`:** both cut the y-axis at **(0, 1)**, but one **rises** steeply and the other **falls** equally steeply — mirror images in the vertical line through that point.

## Summary

```
polynomial degree      highest power of the variable
degree 0 → constant · 1 → linear · 2 → quadratic · 3 → cubic

linear polynomial      ax + b
linear pattern         constant difference between consecutive terms
linear growth          increases by a constant amount → positive slope
linear decay           decreases by a constant amount → negative slope

y = ax + b             a = slope,  b = y-intercept (line cuts y-axis at (0, b))
y = ax                 always passes through the origin
same a, different b    parallel lines
```
