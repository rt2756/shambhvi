## What probability is

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Probability is a MEASUREMENT — like length, area or volume — but of the LIKELIHOOD of events** rather than of a physical quantity.
>
> It expresses **how confident we are that a particular event will occur**.

**Random events** are ones where **we know the possible outcomes but not which will occur**:

- Will it rain today? → {rain, no rain}
- Will our school win the hockey match? → {win, draw, lose}
- Will my name be drawn in the lucky draw?

**SUBJECTIVE probability** is a personal judgement from evidence — *"the sun is shining, so rain is unlikely."* **This chapter is about measuring probability OBJECTIVELY instead.**

## Randomness

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **RANDOMNESS is when you know all the possible outcomes but cannot predict which will occur.**
>
> A **RANDOM EXPERIMENT** is something you can **repeat**, where the result may differ each time and cannot be known in advance.

**Tossing a coin** — you know it's heads or tails, but not which. **Rolling a die** — you know it's 1 to 6, but not which.

**Why is rain random?** Because it depends on **so many complex, sensitive factors** — temperature, humidity, wind, pressure — that exact prediction is impossible. **But we can still estimate its likelihood from patterns in past data.**

> [!TIP]
> **Randomness is sometimes exactly what you want.** A coin toss decides who bats first in cricket **because it is unpredictable** — neither captain can influence it, so neither can complain. **Fairness here comes FROM the randomness.**

> [!NOTE]
> **FAIR and UNBIASED.** Calling a coin "fair" means it is **symmetrical**, with no reason to land on one side more than the other — we call that **unbiased**. A **"random toss"** means it falls freely, without interference.

## The probability scale

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Probability is measured on a scale from 0 to 1.**
>
> | Value | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **0** | **impossible** |
> | between 0 and 0.5 | **less likely** than not |
> | **0.5** | **even chance** — equally likely either way |
> | between 0.5 and 1 | **more likely** than not |
> | **1** | **certain** |
>
> **Most events fall strictly between 0 and 1.**

`0.75` means a **75% chance** — more likely than not. `0.5` means **equally likely** to happen or not.

**A deck of six cards, some purple.** The probability of drawing purple runs from **impossible** (no purple cards) through **less likely**, **even chance** and **more likely**, all the way to **certain** (all six purple) — sliding smoothly along the scale, just like a number line.

| Event | Where on the scale |
|---|---|
| Getting a number greater than 6 on a die | **impossible** — dice only show 1–6 |
| Rolling a 3 on a standard die | **less likely** — but not impossible |
| A coin landing heads | **even chance** |
| Drawing a 2–10 from a 52-card deck | **more likely** — 36 of the 52 cards qualify |
| Choosing a red sweet from a bag of all red sweets | **certain** |

## Two ways to measure it objectively

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **1. EXPERIMENTAL probability** — from **evidence and data**: perform the experiment many times, or analyse past observations, and compute the **relative frequency**.
>
> **2. THEORETICAL probability** — from **reasoning**: assume all outcomes are equally likely, and count favourable outcomes against total outcomes. **No data needed.**

### Experimental probability

```
Experimental probability = (number of times the event occurred) ÷ (total number of trials)
```

**Worked:** roll a die 50 times and get a 4 exactly 8 times.
`8/50 =` **0.16 or 16%** — also called the **relative frequency** of rolling a 4.

**This is what you use when you can't assume fairness** — a bent coin, a paper cup, a real-world survey.

### Theoretical probability

```
P(event) = (number of favourable outcomes) ÷ (number of possible outcomes)
```

**Worked:** `P(rolling a 4)` on a fair die `= 1/6 ≈` **0.167 or 16.7%**

**Worked:** a letter is picked at random from **PROBABILITY**. `P(B) = 2/11 ≈` **0.182 or 18.2%** — there are **two** Bs among **eleven** letters.

> [!WARNING]
> **Theoretical probability ASSUMES all outcomes are equally likely.** That's fine for a fair die, but not for "will it rain" or "will the cup land on its side" — there you must use **experimental** probability.

### Statistical data and sampling

**Worked:** of 50 students, 20 like mango, 15 apples, 10 bananas, 5 grapes.

`P(a random student likes mango) = 20/50 =` **0.4 or 40%**

**Scaling up to the whole school of 1500:** `40% of 1500 =` **about 600 mangoes**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The 50 students are a SAMPLE; the 1500 are the POPULATION.**
>
> **Why sample at all?** Because collecting data from everyone is usually **impractical**.
>
> **To be more confident:** use a **larger** sample, and make it **more representative** — students from different classes and grades, not just one.

## Sample spaces and events

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The SAMPLE SPACE, S, is the list of all possible outcomes.** Each outcome is an **element** of it, and the number of elements is the **sample size, n(S)**.
>
> **Two rules: S must include EVERY possible outcome, and no outcome may be listed twice.**

| Experiment | Sample space S | n(S) |
|---|---|---|
| Will it rain? | {Rain, No Rain} | 2 |
| A team's match result | {Win, Lose, Draw} | 3 |
| Tossing a coin | {H, T} | 2 |
| Rolling a die | {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} | 6 |
| Tossing two coins | {HH, HT, TH, TT} | **4** |

> [!TIP]
> **Two coins give FOUR outcomes, not three.** HT and TH are **different** — the first coin heads and second tails is not the same event as the reverse. Listing them as one is the commonest error in the chapter.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An EVENT is any single outcome or combination of outcomes — a SUBSET of the sample space.**

**For a die, S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}:**
- "rolling a 4" is the event **{4}**
- "rolling an even number" is the event **{2, 4, 6}**, so `P = 3/6 =` **0.5**
- "rolling more than 4" is **{5, 6}**, so `P = 2/6 =` **1/3**

> [!TIP]
> **Make the sample space detailed enough for the question.** {Rain, No Rain} is fine for "will it rain?" — but to ask about *how much*, you'd need **{No Rain, Drizzle, Light Rain, Heavy Rain}**.

## Experimental vs theoretical — and why they differ

| | **Experimental** | **Theoretical** |
|---|---|---|
| Based on | **actual data from trials** | **assumption of equally likely outcomes** |
| Needs data? | **yes** | **no** |
| Works when outcomes aren't equally likely? | **yes** | **no** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS: as the number of trials increases, the experimental probability tends to get CLOSER to the theoretical probability.**
>
> With **few** trials the two can differ a lot. Roll a die 12 times and get three 3s — that's an experimental probability of `3/12 = 0.25`, against a theoretical `1/6 ≈ 0.167`. **Roll it 6000 times and the gap will have almost vanished.**

## The Gambler's Fallacy

> [!WARNING]
> **THE GAMBLER'S FALLACY: believing that because something random has happened several times in a row, the opposite is now "due".**
>
> **It is false. The coin has no memory.**

**A fair coin comes up heads six times running.** Many people feel tails is now overdue.

**But the probability of tails on the next flip is still exactly ½.** The coin does not keep track of what happened before — **every flip starts afresh**.

**Same with dice:** having rolled three 6s in Snakes and Ladders, the chance of another 6 is **still 1/6 ≈ 0.166**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Each roll or toss is an INDEPENDENT event** — its probability doesn't depend on what came before.
>
> **Probability does not tell you what will happen NEXT; it predicts what happens IN THE LONG RUN.**
>
> The Law of Large Numbers says the *proportion* settles down over thousands of trials — **not** that a "correction" is coming on the next throw.

> [!NOTE]
> **Snakes and Ladders comes from ancient India.** It evolved from **Jñān-Chaupaḍ**, a traditional dice game used as an **educational tool** — each **ladder symbolised a virtue** and each **snake a vice**, so the gameplay taught the consequences of good and bad behaviour. Versions have been found across India; a 19th-century Jain cloth board is in the National Museum.

## Summary

```
probability scale     0 = impossible  ·  0.5 = even chance  ·  1 = certain

experimental P  =  (times the event occurred) ÷ (total trials)
theoretical  P  =  (favourable outcomes) ÷ (possible outcomes)

sample space S   every possible outcome, each listed once
sample size n(S) the number of elements in S
event            a subset of the sample space

Law of Large Numbers   more trials → experimental P approaches theoretical P
Gambler's Fallacy      believing past results change future probabilities — they don't
```
