## What probability measures

**Length measures how long, area how much space — probability measures HOW LIKELY.** It puts a number on questions with no fixed answer: *Will it rain today? Will our school win tomorrow's hockey match? Will my name be drawn in the lucky draw?*

**For each, we know the possible results** (rain or no rain; win, draw or lose; one name out of all the students) **but not which one will happen.** That's what makes them **random**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Subjective vs objective.** "The sun is shining, so rain is unlikely" is a **subjective** guess — two people looking at the same sky may disagree. This chapter is about **objective** probability: numbers anyone can calculate from **data** or from **reasoning about equally likely outcomes**.

## 7.1 Randomness

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A RANDOM EXPERIMENT (or trial) is something you can repeat, where you know all the possible results but cannot predict which one will happen on any single try.**
>
> - Toss a coin: heads or tails — but which, this time?
> - Roll a die: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 — but which, this time?

**Why a coin toss is fair for deciding who bats first:** nobody can predict or control the result, and each side has the same chance. Randomness is useful exactly *because* it's unpredictable.

**Why rain is random:** it depends on temperature, humidity, wind and pressure, and is so **sensitive** to them that tiny changes alter the result. We can't predict it exactly — but past data tells us how **likely** it is.

## 7.2 The probability scale: 0 to 1

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every probability is a number from 0 to 1:  0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1.**
>
> | P | meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **0** | **impossible** |
> | between 0 and 0.5 | less likely |
> | **0.5** | **even chance** (equally likely to happen or not) |
> | between 0.5 and 1 | more likely |
> | **1** | **certain** |
>
> **Why it can't go below 0 or above 1:** probability compares "how many times it happens" with "how many tries" (or favourable with all outcomes). The part can't be negative and can't be more than the whole.

**Percent is the same scale ×100:** 0.75 = 75% (more likely than not), 0.5 = 50%.

**Book examples placed on the scale:**

| Event | Where |
|---|---|
| a number greater than 6 on a die | **impossible** (0) |
| rolling a 3 | **less likely** (1/6) |
| a coin landing heads | **even chance** (1/2) |
| drawing a 2–10 from a 52-card pack | **more likely** (36/52) |
| a red sweet from a bag of only red sweets | **certain** (1) |

**Exercise Set 7.1, worked:** (i) Monday comes after Sunday → **certain**. (ii) Snow in Mumbai in July (hot, humid monsoon at sea level) → **impossible**, or as near as makes no difference. (iii) An elephant walks through your classroom today → **impossible / extremely unlikely**. (iv) You greet at least one friend tomorrow → **more likely** (almost certain on a school day).

## 7.3 Two objective ways to measure probability

> [!IMPORTANT]
> 1. **EXPERIMENTAL probability** — from **evidence**: repeat an experiment, or look at past data, and count.
>
>    **P = (number of times the event occurred) ÷ (total number of trials)**
>
>    This fraction is the event's **RELATIVE FREQUENCY**.
>
> 2. **THEORETICAL probability** — from **reasoning**, when all outcomes are **equally likely**:
>
>    **P(E) = (number of favourable outcomes) ÷ (number of possible outcomes)**

### Experimental probability

**Words first:** an **outcome** is one possible result of an experiment; the **SAMPLE SPACE** is the set of all possible outcomes, written in curly brackets.

- Toss a coin: **{H, T}**.
- Roll a die: **{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}**.

**Example:** a die rolled 50 times lands on 4 exactly 8 times. Experimental P(4) = 8/50 = **0.16 = 16%**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Snakes and Ladders is Indian.** It grew out of **Jñān-Chaupaḍ**, a dice game used to teach values — ladders were virtues, snakes were vices.

### Theoretical probability

**Example:** P(rolling a 4) with a fair die = 1 favourable ÷ 6 possible = **1/6 ≈ 0.167**.

**Example:** a letter picked at random from **PROBABILITY** (11 letters, two B's): P(B) = **2/11 ≈ 0.182**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Count every copy.** PROBABILITY has 11 letters (not 9 different ones), and 2 of them are B — the sample space is the 11 letter-cards.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **"Equally likely" is the key assumption of the theoretical formula.** It's only true when nothing favours one outcome — a **fair** (symmetric, unbiased) coin, a fair die, a well-shuffled pack, a **random** toss.
>
> **Why the formula needs it:** if the 6 faces really are equally likely, each gets 1/6 of the chances; "4" is one of them, so 1/6. If the die were loaded, counting faces would give the wrong answer.

### Probability from statistical data — and sampling

**Example (favourite fruit):** 50 students: 20 mango, 15 apple, 10 banana, 5 grapes. P(a random student's favourite is mango) = 20/50 = **0.4**.

**Using it to plan:** the school has **1500** students. Expect about 0.4 × 1500 = **600** mango-lovers.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SAMPLING:** we asked a **sample** (50 students) to estimate a **population** (1500). Estimates are better when the sample is **larger** and **representative** (students from several classes, not one).
>
> **Expected number ≈ probability from the sample × population.**

### Exercise Set 7.2, worked

1. **Sweets: 10 red, 8 green, 7 yellow, 5 blue (sample of 30).** (i) P(green) = 8/30 = **4/15**. (ii) Yellow in 600: 7/30 × 600 = **140**.
2. **Clubs, sample of 40: 14 science, 11 arts, 9 sports, 6 debate; 800 in school.** (i) P(arts) = **11/40**. (ii) Sports in the school: 9/40 × 800 = **180**.
3. **Toss 20 times** — your own counts; experimental P(H) = (heads) ÷ 20. **The next toss still has P(T) = 1/2.**
4. **Paper cup tossed 100 times** — probabilities are your counts ÷ 100. (A cup isn't symmetric, so the three landings are **not** equally likely — only an experiment can tell you.)
5. **Even number on a fair die:** {2, 4, 6} → 3/6 = **1/2**.
6. **12 rolls, three 3's:** (i) experimental 3/12 = **1/4**. (ii) theoretical **1/6**. (iii) 12 rolls is too few — chance wobbles are large. With 60, 600, 6000 rolls the experimental value gets **closer and closer to 1/6**.

### The law of large numbers, and the gambler's fallacy

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS:** the more trials you do, the closer the experimental probability tends to get to the theoretical probability.
>
> **Why:** a few lucky or unlucky results matter a lot in 10 tries, but are swamped in 10,000.

> [!WARNING]
> **GAMBLER'S FALLACY:** "Six heads in a row, so tails is due!" — **wrong.** A coin has **no memory**; each toss is **independent**, so P(tails) is still **1/2**. After three sixes in a row in Snakes and Ladders, P(6) on the next roll is still **1/6**.
>
> **Probability describes the long run, not the next try.** The law of large numbers works by *swamping* streaks with many more results — not by "correcting" them.

## 7.4 Sample spaces and events

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SAMPLE SPACE S** — the list of all possible outcomes.
> - It must include **every** possible outcome.
> - **No outcome is listed twice.**
> - The number of outcomes is the **sample size n(S)**.
>
> **EVENT** — any outcome or group of outcomes you're interested in. **An event is a SUBSET of the sample space.**

| Experiment | Sample space | n(S) |
|---|---|---|
| tomorrow's rain | {Rain, No rain} | 2 |
| a match | {Win, Lose, Draw} | 3 |
| one coin | {H, T} | 2 |
| one die | {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} | 6 |
| two coins | {HH, HT, TH, TT} | 4 |

> [!WARNING]
> **HT and TH are DIFFERENT outcomes** (first coin heads vs second coin heads). Listing two coins as {two heads, one each, two tails} gives three outcomes that are **not** equally likely — "one each" happens twice as often.

**The sample space should match the question.** For "how much rain?", use {No rain, Drizzle, Light rain, Heavy rain}.

**Events, examples:** two coins, "at least one head" = **{HH, HT, TH}** → P = 3/4. Die, "greater than 4" = **{5, 6}** → P = 2/6 = 1/3. Fruit basket {Apple, Banana, Orange}, "yellow fruit" = **{Banana}**.

### Exercise Set 7.3, worked

1. One die: **6** outcomes.
2. (i) Die and coin: {1H, 1T, 2H, 2T, …, 6H, 6T} — **12** outcomes. (ii) An integer strictly between −5 and 5: **{−4, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4}** (9 outcomes; 11 if −5 and 5 are included). (iii) 5 green and 7 red balls: label them **{G1, …, G5, R1, …, R7}** — 12 equally likely outcomes. (Writing just {G, R} is allowed, but then G and R are **not** equally likely: P(G) = 5/12.)
3. (i) Snack × drink: **{Samosa-Chai, Samosa-Lassi, Pakora-Chai, Pakora-Lassi, Bhaji-Chai, Bhaji-Lassi}** — 3 × 2 = 6. (ii) "Samosa" = **{Samosa-Chai, Samosa-Lassi}**.

> [!TIP]
> **Counting rule:** if step 1 has m outcomes and step 2 has n outcomes (whatever happened in step 1), there are **m × n** outcomes altogether. **Why:** each of the m first results branches into n second results.

## 7.5 Tree diagrams

**A tree diagram lists every outcome of a multi-step experiment.** From a starting point draw a branch for each result of step 1; from the end of each, branches for step 2; and so on. **Each path from start to tip is one outcome.**

```
Toss a coin twice:
                ┌── H   →  HH
        ┌── H ──┤
        │       └── T   →  HT
 start ─┤
        │       ┌── H   →  TH
        └── T ──┤
                └── T   →  TT
```

**S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}**, all equally likely. P(HH) = **1/4**. P(one head and one tail) = {HT, TH} → **2/4 = 1/2**.

### With and without replacement

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To find the probability along a path, MULTIPLY the probabilities on its branches.**
>
> **Why:** if the first step succeeds in a fraction p of the trials, and in a fraction q **of those** the second step succeeds, then both happen in a fraction p × q of all trials. (E.g. half the time the first coin is H; of those, half the time the second is H → ½ × ½ = ¼.)
>
> - **With replacement:** the second draw has the same probabilities as the first.
> - **Without replacement:** the second draw's numbers change — one item is gone.

### Exercise Set 7.4, worked

1. **Basket A: apple, orange, orange; basket B: banana, mango.** Six equally likely pairs: Apple-Banana, Apple-Mango, Orange₁-Banana, Orange₁-Mango, Orange₂-Banana, Orange₂-Mango. P(apple and banana) = **1/6** (or 1/3 × 1/2).
2. **3 red, 4 black, 2 green pens, picked with replacement by you and then your friend.** P(same colour) = (3/9)² + (4/9)² + (2/9)² = (9 + 16 + 4)/81 = **29/81**.

## End-of-chapter questions, worked

1. Impossible event: **0**. All possible outcomes: the **sample space**. Certain event: **1**. Heads on a fair coin: **1/2**.
2. 15 of 50 like football: the **frequency** is 15; the **relative frequency** is 15/50 = **0.3**.
3. **Equally likely:** (ii) fair coin and (iii) fair die. **Not:** (i) a car starting (usually it does), (iv) 3 red vs 7 blue marbles, (v) boy or girl (close to even, but real data shows slightly more boys — it's a fact about the world, not about symmetry).
4. (i) Two coins, at least one head: **3/4**. (ii) Cards 1–10, even: **5/10 = 1/2**. (iii) Die, greater than 4: **2/6 = 1/3**. (iv) 3 red, 2 blue, 1 green, not red: **3/6 = 1/2**. (v) Three coins, exactly two heads: {HHT, HTH, THH} out of 8 → **3/8**.
5. One of three candies: **1/3**.
6. Outfits: Red-Jeans, Red-Khakis, Red-Shorts, Blue-Jeans, Blue-Khakis, Blue-Shorts — **2 × 3 = 6**.
7. **Tyres (1000 cases: 20, 210, 325, 445):** (i) under 4000 km: **20/1000 = 0.02**. (ii) 4000–14000 km: (210 + 325)/1000 = **0.535**. (iii) over 14000 km: **445/1000 = 0.445**. (They add to 1 ✓.)
8. **PEACE (P, E, A, C, E):** (i) P, E or C: 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 → **4/5**. (ii) Not E: **3/5**.
9. **Spinner 1–8:** P(8) = **1/8**; odd = **1/2**; greater than 2 = **6/8 = 3/4**; less than 9 = **1** (certain); multiple of 3 {3, 6} = **2/8 = 1/4**.
10. **4 red, 5 blue, drawn without replacement:** red then blue = 4/9 × 5/8 = **5/18**; two blue = 5/9 × 4/8 = **5/18**.
11. **Two dice:** "the total is 13" has probability **0**; "the total is between 2 and 12" has probability **1**.
12. (i) Two dice, sum a prime greater than 5 → 7 (6 ways) or 11 (2 ways): **8/36 = 2/9**. (ii) 4 red, 3 green, 2 blue, two drawn without replacement, different colours: P(same) = (4/9 × 3/8) + (3/9 × 2/8) + (2/9 × 1/8) = (12 + 6 + 2)/72 = 20/72, so P(different) = **52/72 = 13/18**. (iii) First coin H and exactly two heads: {HHT, HTH} → **2/8 = 1/4**. (iv) Four-digit number from 1, 2, 3, 4 (no repeats) is even if it ends in 2 or 4 → **2/4 = 1/2**. (v) Guessing 3 four-option questions, exactly 2 right: 3 positions for the wrong one × (1/4)(1/4)(3/4) = **9/64**.
13. **Balls 1–4, two draws:** with replacement **16** outcomes (4 × 4); without replacement **12** (4 × 3, no repeats like 1-1).
14. **Coin + card 1–6:** {H1, …, H6, T1, …, T6} — **12** outcomes.
15. **Number of heads in three tosses: {0, 1, 2, 3}** (iv). (i) misses 0, (ii) misses 3, (iii) includes the impossible 4.
16. **Dropped on a 3 m × 2 m rectangle; circle of diameter 1 m inside:** P = circle area ÷ rectangle area = (π × 0.5²) ÷ 6 = **π/24 ≈ 0.13**. (Here "equally likely" means every bit of area is equally likely.)

## In a nutshell

- **Probability** measures likelihood on a scale **0 (impossible) to 1 (certain)**; 0.5 is an even chance.
- **Experimental P** = times it happened ÷ trials (relative frequency). **Theoretical P** = favourable ÷ possible, **only for equally likely outcomes**.
- From a **sample**, estimate a population: expected number ≈ P × population.
- **Law of large numbers:** many trials → experimental P approaches theoretical P.
- **Gambler's fallacy:** independent trials have no memory.
- **Sample space** = all outcomes, none repeated; **event** = a subset of it.
- **Tree diagrams** list multi-step outcomes; multiply along a branch; without replacement, the second step's numbers change.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** probability is never below 0 or above 1 · the favourable ÷ possible formula needs **equally likely** outcomes · HT and TH are two different outcomes · count repeated letters/items separately · "not E" = 1 − P(E) · without replacement, reduce both the count of that colour **and** the total · a streak doesn't make the other result "due" · a small number of trials can be far from the theoretical value.
