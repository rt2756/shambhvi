**Circles are everywhere in nature** — raindrops falling on water, the cross-section of a plant stem, a sunflower's inflorescence, the full Moon, the Sun during a total eclipse. **The cave paintings of Gudahandi in Odisha** already show triangles, squares, circles and ovals.

## Definitions

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A CIRCLE is the set of all points in a plane that are EQUIDISTANT from a given point.**
>
> That given point is the **CENTRE**; the fixed distance is the **RADIUS**.

**LOCUS** — the set of points satisfying a given condition. So a circle is **the locus of points equidistant from a given point**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Chord** | a line segment joining **two points on the circle** |
| **Diameter** | a chord **passing through the centre** |
| **Arc** | a connected portion of the circle, defined by two end points |
| **Minor arc** | the **smaller** of the two arcs between two points |
| **Major arc** | the **larger** one |
| **Angle subtended by chord BC at the centre A** | ∠BAC |

## Symmetry

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A circle has COMPLETE ROTATIONAL SYMMETRY** — rotate it by **any** angle about its centre and it looks exactly the same.
>
> **And every DIAMETER is a line of reflection symmetry** — there are infinitely many.

**Why the wheel argument works:** watch a rotating wheel and you can never tell whether the point now touching the ground is the same one as before. **A rotating circle looks identical at every instant.**

**Fold a paper circle so the boundaries overlap.** The crease is a line of reflection symmetry — and **it always passes through the centre**, so it is a diameter.

> [!TIP]
> **That gives a way to find the centre of a paper circle:** fold it twice so the boundaries overlap each time. **The two creases are diameters, and they cross at the centre.**

**The longest chord is the DIAMETER**; in a circle of radius 5, that's **10 units**. **There is no smallest chord** — a chord can be made as short as you like without ever reaching zero.

## How many circles through given points?

**Through TWO points A and B: infinitely many.**

**Why:** the centre must satisfy `OA = OB`, so it lies on **the perpendicular bisector of AB** — and every point on that line works.

- **Smallest such circle:** centre at the **midpoint** of AB, radius **½ AB**, with AB as a **diameter**.
- **There is no largest** — move further along the perpendicular bisector and the radius grows without limit, while the circle looks **less and less curved**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 1: There is a UNIQUE circle passing through three NON-COLLINEAR points.**
>
> **Why:** the centre must lie on the perpendicular bisector of AB (since `OA = OB`) **and** on the perpendicular bisector of AC (since `OA = OC`). Because A, B, C are not collinear, **those two bisectors intersect at exactly one point** — and that is the centre.

**Through three COLLINEAR points: no circle at all.** The perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC would be **parallel**, so they never meet and there is no possible centre.

> [!TIP]
> **Consequence: a straight line can cut a circle in at most TWO points.** Three points of a circle can never be collinear.

### The circumcircle

**The circle through the three vertices of a triangle is its CIRCUMCIRCLE; its centre is the CIRCUMCENTRE.**

**The circle circumscribes the triangle; the triangle is inscribed in the circle.**

| Triangle | Where the circumcentre lies |
|---|---|
| **Acute** | **inside** the triangle |
| **Obtuse** | **outside** the triangle |
| **Right-angled** | **at the midpoint of the hypotenuse** |

**Every triangle has exactly ONE circumcircle**, because the perpendicular bisectors meet at a single point.

## Chords and the angles they subtend

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 2: Equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre.**
>
> **Proof:** for chords AB and DE with `AB = DE`, we have `CA = CB = CD = CE = r`. By **SSS**, `∆CAB ≅ ∆CDE`, so `∠ACB = ∠DCE`. ∎

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 3 (the converse): Chords subtending equal angles at the centre are equal.**
>
> **Proof:** `CA = CD` and `CB = CE` (all radii), and the included angles are equal — so by **SAS**, `∆ACB ≅ ∆DCE`, giving `AB = ED`. ∎

> [!TIP]
> **The intuition:** stand at the centre with your arms at a fixed angle, touching the circle at A and B. **Rotate without changing the angle** — your hands still touch the circle, and the distance between them is unchanged. So the new chord has the same length.

**Note that a chord plus the two radii to its ends always forms an ISOSCELES triangle**, since both radii are equal. Most of these proofs rest on that one fact.

## The perpendicular from the centre

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 4: The line joining the centre to the MIDPOINT of a chord is PERPENDICULAR to the chord.**
>
> **Proof:** `∆CAB` is isosceles (`CA = CB`), so `∠A = ∠B`. With `AM = BM` and CM common, **SAS** gives `∆CMA ≅ ∆CMB`, so `∠CMA = ∠CMB`. But they sit on a straight line and add to **180°** — so **each is 90°**. ∎

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 5 (the converse): The perpendicular from the centre to a chord BISECTS the chord.**

**Together these say: for a chord, "line from centre through the midpoint" and "perpendicular from the centre" are the SAME line.**

> [!TIP]
> **Paper-folding version.** Fold a paper circle inwards from the boundary — the crease is a **chord**. Fold again so the chord's end points meet — the second crease **bisects the chord at right angles and passes through the centre**.

**Consequence:** in an isosceles triangle inscribed in a circle with `AB = AC`, **the altitude from A passes through the centre** — because it is the perpendicular bisector of BC.

## Distance of a chord from the centre

**"Distance from the centre to a chord" always means the PERPENDICULAR distance.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 6: Chords of equal length are EQUIDISTANT from the centre.**
>
> **Proof 1 (SSS):** `∆CAB ≅ ∆CFG` by SSS (two radii each, plus `AB = FG`), so their corresponding altitudes are equal.
>
> **Proof 2 (RHS):** the perpendicular from the centre bisects each chord, so `AE = FH`. With `∠CEA = ∠CHF = 90°` and `CA = CF` (radii), **RHS** gives `∆CEA ≅ ∆CHF`, so `CE = CH`. ∎

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 7 (the converse): Chords equidistant from the centre have equal length.**

### The chord-length formula

**If a chord is at perpendicular distance d from the centre of a circle of radius r:**

**`chord length = 2√(r² − d²)`**

**Why:** the perpendicular **bisects** the chord, making a right triangle with hypotenuse **r** (a radius), one leg **d**, and the other leg **half the chord**. By Baudhāyana–Pythagoras, half the chord is `√(r² − d²)`.

**Worked:** `r = 7`, `d = 6` → chord `= 2√(49 − 36) = 2√13 ≈` **7.21 cm**
**Worked:** `r = 5`, `d = 3` → chord `= 2√16 =` **8 cm** · `r = 5, d = 4` → chord `= 2√9 =` **6 cm**

**Two parallel chords of 8 cm and 6 cm on opposite sides of the centre of a 5 cm circle** are at distances 3 cm and 4 cm, so the **distance between their midpoints is `3 + 4 =` 7 cm**.

### Which chord is nearer the centre?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 8: The LONGER chord is CLOSER to the centre.**
>
> **Proof:** `CF² + AF² = CG² + GD²` (both equal r² by Pythagoras). If `AB > DE`, then the half-chords satisfy `AF > GD`, so `AF² > GD²`, forcing **`CF² < CG²`** and hence `CF < CG`. ∎

> [!TIP]
> **The two extremes.**
> - **Nearest possible chord:** the one **through the centre** — the **diameter**, at distance **0**. So **the diameter is the LONGEST chord**.
> - **Furthest possible:** push a chord out until it shrinks to a **single point**, of length 0 and at distance **r**.

## Angles subtended by an arc

**The angle an arc subtends at the centre** is the angle swept as you move from one radius to the other **along that arc**.

- **Minor arc** → the angle at the centre is **less than 180°**
- **Major arc** → the angle at the centre is **more than 180°** (a reflex angle)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 9: The angle subtended by an arc at the CENTRE is DOUBLE the angle it subtends at any point on the circle OUTSIDE the arc.**
>
> **`∠at centre = 2 × ∠at circumference`**

**Proof sketch.** Join D (the point on the circle) to the centre C and extend to meet the circle at E.

`∆DCB` is isosceles (`CB = CD`), so `∠CBD = ∠CDB`. By the **exterior angle theorem**, `∠BCE = ∠CBD + ∠CDB = 2∠BDC`.
Similarly `∆ADC` is isosceles, giving `∠ACE = 2∠CDA`.
Adding: `∠BCA = 2(∠BDC + ∠CDA) = 2∠BDA`. ∎

*(A second case, where the extension meets the circle outside the arc, needs the same argument with a subtraction instead of an addition.)*

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The beautiful consequence: ANGLES IN THE SAME SEGMENT ARE EQUAL.**
>
> Take an arc AB. **Wherever you place a point D on the circle outside that arc, `∠ADB` is exactly the same.**
>
> **Why:** every such angle is **half the same fixed central angle**.
>
> **This property distinguishes the circle from every other shape.** For points *inside* or *outside* the circle the angles all differ — only on the circle are they equal.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Corollary: The angle subtended by a DIAMETER at any point on the circle is 90°.**
>
> **Why:** a diameter's arc subtends a **straight angle, 180°**, at the centre — and half of 180° is **90°**.
>
> (A **corollary** is a fact that follows immediately from an already-proved result.)

**Worked:** in a circle of radius 12 cm, a central angle of 60° gives a chord that... **`∆AOB` has two sides of 12 and the included angle 60°, so all three angles are 60° — it's EQUILATERAL.** The chord is **12 cm**.

## Concyclic points and cyclic quadrilaterals

**Points lying on the same circle are CONCYCLIC.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 10: If a segment AB subtends EQUAL angles at two points C and D on the SAME SIDE of AB, then A, B, C, D are concyclic.**

**Proof by elimination.** A, B, C are non-collinear, so a unique circle passes through them (Theorem 1). Suppose D is **not** on it.

- **If D is outside**, AD cuts the circle at E. Then `∠AEB` is an **exterior angle** of `∆BED`, so `∠AEB > ∠ADB`. But `∠AEB = ∠ACB` (same segment) and `∠ACB = ∠ADB` (given) — giving `∠ACB > ∠ACB`, **impossible**.
- **If D is inside**, the same argument runs with the roles swapped, and is equally impossible.

**Both options are eliminated, so D must lie ON the circle.** ∎

**A quadrilateral whose vertices are concyclic is a CYCLIC QUADRILATERAL.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 11: The OPPOSITE angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°.**
>
> **Proof:** `∠BAD` is half the angle arc BCD subtends at the centre (the **reflex** ∠BOD); `∠BCD` is half the angle arc BAD subtends (the ordinary ∠BOD). Together those two central angles make a **complete rotation, 360°**.
>
> So `∠BAD + ∠BCD = ½ × 360° =` **180°** ∎

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 12 (the converse): If two opposite angles of a quadrilateral add to 180°, its vertices are concyclic.**

**Test:** can a cyclic quadrilateral have `∠A = 80°, ∠B = 110°, ∠C = 100°, ∠D = 70°`?
`∠A + ∠C = 180°` ✓ and `∠B + ∠D = 180°` ✓ — **yes, it can be drawn.**
