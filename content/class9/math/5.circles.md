## Why circles are special

**Circles are everywhere in nature** — ripples from a raindrop, a cut plant stem, a sunflower head, the full moon, the Sun. Early humans painted them on cave walls (the **Gudahandi** caves in Odisha show circles, triangles and ovals).

**What do all circles share?** Each has a **centre**, and **every point on it is the same distance from that centre**. Mathematicians turn that observation into the definition.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A CIRCLE is the set of all points in a plane at a fixed distance from a fixed point.**
>
> - The fixed point is the **centre**; the fixed distance is the **radius**.
> - "The set of all points satisfying a condition" is called a **LOCUS**. So a circle is the **locus of points at a given distance from a given point**.

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **chord** | a segment joining two points on the circle |
| **diameter** | a chord through the centre (= 2 × radius) |
| **angle subtended by chord BC at the centre A** | ∠BAC, the angle the chord "makes" at the centre |
| **arc** | a connected piece of the circle between two points |
| **minor / major arc** | the shorter / longer way round between the two points |

## 5.1 Symmetries of a circle

- **Rotational symmetry through ANY angle.** A spinning wheel looks the same at every instant — you can't tell which point is touching the ground. Turn a circle about its centre by any angle at all and it fits on itself.
- **Reflection symmetry in EVERY diameter.** Fold a paper circle so the edges meet: the crease always passes through the centre — it's a diameter. Every diameter is a mirror line.

> [!NOTE]
> **Compare:** a square has 4 rotations (90°, 180°, 270°, 360°) and 4 mirror lines; a regular pentagon 5 and 5; a regular hexagon 6 and 6. A regular n-gon has n of each. **The circle is like an "infinitely many sided" regular polygon** — infinitely many of both.

**Longest chord?** A **diameter** (10 units if r = 5) — proved in 5.4. **Smallest chord?** There isn't one: chords can be as short as you like.

## 5.2 How many circles pass through given points?

### The perpendicular bisector is a locus

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The locus of points equidistant from A and B is the PERPENDICULAR BISECTOR of AB.**
>
> **Why (both directions are needed):**
> - **Every point P on the bisector is equidistant:** if M is the midpoint, triangles PMA and PMB have PM common, MA = MB, and right angles at M → congruent (SAS) → **PA = PB**.
> - **Every equidistant point is on the bisector:** if PA = PB, triangles PMA and PMB have all three sides equal (SSS) → ∠PMA = ∠PMB, and they add to 180°, so each is 90° → P is on the perpendicular through M.

### Through two points: infinitely many circles

**A circle through A and B needs a centre O with OA = OB** — so O can be **any point on the perpendicular bisector of AB**.

- The **smallest** such circle has its centre at the midpoint of AB: radius = **AB/2**, and AB is a diameter.
- There is **no largest**: moving the centre further along the bisector makes the radius grow without limit, and the circle **looks less and less curved** near A and B.

### Through three points: exactly one circle (if they aren't in a line)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 1: Through three non-collinear points there is exactly ONE circle.**
>
> **Why:** the centre O must satisfy OA = OB (so O is on the perpendicular bisector of AB) **and** OA = OC (so O is on the perpendicular bisector of AC). A, B, C aren't in a line, so these two bisectors are not parallel — they meet in **exactly one point**. That point is O; draw the circle with radius OA. One centre → one circle.

**Three collinear points: NO circle.** The perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC are both perpendicular to the same line, so they're **parallel** and never meet — no point is equidistant from all three. (Equivalently: **a line can cut a circle in at most 2 points.**)

### The circumcircle of a triangle

**Three non-collinear points form a triangle.** The circle through its vertices is the **CIRCUMCIRCLE**; its centre — where the perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet — is the **CIRCUMCENTRE**.

| Triangle | Circumcentre is |
|---|---|
| acute-angled | **inside** the triangle |
| right-angled | at the **midpoint of the hypotenuse** |
| obtuse-angled | **outside** the triangle |

> [!TIP]
> **Why all three perpendicular bisectors meet at one point:** the point where two of them meet is equidistant from all three vertices — so it's also on the third bisector. **To find the circumcentre you only need to draw TWO bisectors.**

**Exercise Set 5.1, worked:** (1) ∠A = 70°, ∠B = 60° → ∠C = 50°, all acute → centre **inside**. (2) ∠A = 100° → obtuse → centre **outside**. (3) For any triangle, **OA = OB = OC** — that's what a circumcentre means. (4) Least radius through A and B = **AB/2**.

## 5.3 Chords and the angles they make at the centre

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 2: Equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre.**
>
> **Why:** chords AB = DE in a circle with centre C. Triangles CAB and CDE have CA = CD (radii), CB = CE (radii) and AB = DE (given). **SSS → congruent**, so ∠ACB = ∠DCE.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 3 (converse): Chords that subtend equal angles at the centre are equal.**
>
> **Why:** if ∠ACB = ∠DCE, triangles ACB and DCE have two radii each and the included angle equal. **SAS → congruent**, so AB = DE.
>
> **Picture it:** stand at the centre with your arms at a fixed angle; spin round. Your hands always trace a chord of the same length.

**Exercise Set 5.2:** (1) The triangle formed by a chord AB and the centre C has **CA = CB** (radii) → **isosceles**. (2) Two such triangles with equal bases have all three sides equal (two radii + the base) → **congruent by SSS**.

## 5.4 The centre, the midpoint and the perpendicular

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 4: The line from the centre to the MIDPOINT of a chord is PERPENDICULAR to the chord.**
>
> **Why:** chord AB, midpoint M, centre C. Triangles CMA and CMB: CA = CB (radii), AM = BM (midpoint), CM common → **SSS congruent**. So ∠CMA = ∠CMB. They sit on a straight line, so they add to 180° — **each is 90°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 5 (converse): The PERPENDICULAR from the centre to a chord BISECTS the chord.**
>
> **Why:** triangles CMA and CMB are right-angled at M, with hypotenuses CA = CB (radii) and CM common. **RHS → congruent**, so AM = BM.

**Consequence — the perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the centre.** (The centre is equidistant from the chord's ends, so it lies on that bisector.) **Book activity:** to find the centre of a paper circle, fold two different chords' perpendicular bisectors — **where the creases cross is the centre**. (That's likely Amina's tip to Jamuna.)

**Worked (Exercise Set 5.3, Q3):** parallel chords 6 cm and 8 cm, radius 5, on **opposite** sides of the centre.

- Half-chords are 3 and 4. Distance of each chord from the centre: √(5² − 3²) = **4** and √(5² − 4²) = **3**.
- Opposite sides → distances add: **4 + 3 = 7 cm** between the midpoints.

**Q2:** in isosceles ABC (AB = AC) inscribed in a circle, the altitude from A meets BC at its midpoint (isosceles), so it is the **perpendicular bisector of chord BC** — and that passes through the centre.

## 5.5 How far is a chord from the centre?

**"Distance from the centre to a chord" always means the PERPENDICULAR distance** — to the chord's midpoint.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The key right triangle:** radius r (hypotenuse), distance d (from centre to midpoint), half-chord c/2.
>
> **r² = d² + (c/2)², so chord length c = 2√(r² − d²).**
>
> **Why:** the perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord (Theorem 5) and makes a right angle — so Baudhāyana–Pythagoras applies.

```
            C (centre)
           /|
        r / | d
         /  |
        A───M───────B
         c/2
```

**Theorem 6: Equal chords are equidistant from the centre.** **Why:** if c is the same, then d = √(r² − (c/2)²) is the same. (The book also proves it with congruent triangles: RHS on CEA and CHF.)

**Theorem 7 (converse): Chords equidistant from the centre are equal.** **Why:** same d → same c = 2√(r² − d²).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 8: The LONGER chord is NEARER the centre.**
>
> **Why:** r² = d² + (c/2)² is fixed. If the half-chord is bigger, d² must be smaller to keep the same total.
>
> - **The longest chord** passes through the centre (d = 0): c = 2r, the **diameter**. **No chord is longer than a diameter.**
> - Push a chord outward until d = r and it shrinks to a single point (c = 0).

**Exercise Set 5.5:** (1) r = 7, d = 6 → c = 2√(49 − 36) = **2√13 cm ≈ 7.2 cm**. (3) If AB is twice as far from the centre as CD, is CD = 2AB? **No.** Try r = 5: d = 2 gives CD = 2√21 ≈ 9.2, d = 4 gives AB = 2√9 = 6 — not double. Length depends on √(r² − d²), not on d in proportion.

## 5.6 Angles subtended by an arc

**Arc AB, seen from the centre O,** subtends ∠AOB (sweeping from OA to OB **along the arc**). A **minor arc** subtends less than 180°; a **major arc** more than 180° (a reflex angle).

**Arc AB, seen from a point D on the rest of the circle,** subtends ∠ADB.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 9: The angle an arc subtends at the CENTRE is TWICE the angle it subtends at any point on the remaining part of the circle.**
>
> **∠AOB = 2 × ∠ADB**

**Why (the main case):** join D to the centre O and extend to E on the arc.

1. **OA = OD** (radii) → triangle AOD is isosceles → ∠OAD = ∠ODA.
2. ∠AOE is an **exterior angle** of triangle AOD → ∠AOE = ∠OAD + ∠ODA = **2∠ODA**.
3. In the same way, OB = OD → **∠BOE = 2∠ODB**.
4. Add: ∠AOB = ∠AOE + ∠BOE = 2(∠ODA + ∠ODB) = **2∠ADB**. ∎

(If DE falls outside the arc, the same steps work with **subtraction** instead of addition.)

> [!NOTE]
> **Exterior angle theorem reminder:** an exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles — because all three interior angles add to 180°, and so does the exterior angle with its neighbour.

### Two beautiful consequences

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Angles in the same segment are equal.** Every point D on the circle outside arc AB sees AB at **the same angle** — half the central angle. **No other shape has this property.** Points inside the circle see AB at a bigger angle; points outside, at a smaller one.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The angle in a semicircle is 90°.** If AB is a diameter, the central angle is a straight angle, 180°, so **∠ADB = ½ × 180° = 90°** for every D on the circle.
>
> **Another proof (book Fig. 5.30):** join D to the centre O. OA = OD = OB, so triangles AOD and BOD are isosceles, with base angles a, a and b, b. Angles of triangle ABD: a + b + (a + b) = 180°, so **a + b = 90°** — and a + b is exactly ∠ADB.

**Exercise Set 5.6, Q1:** central angle 60°, radius 12 → triangle AOB has OA = OB and apex 60°, so the base angles are 60° too → **equilateral → AB = 12 cm**.

## 5.7 Concyclic points and cyclic quadrilaterals

**Points on one circle are CONCYCLIC.** Any 3 non-collinear points are concyclic. When are **4**?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 10: If segment AB subtends EQUAL angles at C and D on the SAME side of AB, then A, B, C, D are concyclic.**
>
> **Why (by contradiction):** draw the circle through A, B, C. If D were **outside** it, AD would cut the circle at E; ∠AEB = ∠ACB (same segment), but ∠AEB is an exterior angle of triangle BED so ∠AEB > ∠ADB = ∠ACB — so ∠ACB > ∠ACB, impossible. If D were **inside**, extend AD to E and the same argument gives ∠ADB > ∠AEB = ∠ACB, again impossible. So **D is on the circle**.

**A quadrilateral whose four corners lie on one circle is a CYCLIC QUADRILATERAL.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Theorem 11: Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°.**
>
> **Why:** ∠A is half the central angle on arc BCD; ∠C is half the central angle on arc BAD. **Those two central angles together go all the way round the centre — 360°.** So ∠A + ∠C = ½ × 360° = **180°**. Same for ∠B + ∠D.
>
> **Theorem 12 (converse): If a pair of opposite angles add to 180°, the quadrilateral is cyclic.**

**Book exercise:** angles 80°, 110°, 100°, 70°: A + C = 180°, B + D = 180° → **yes, it can be cyclic**.

> [!TIP]
> **Exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral = interior opposite angle.** Extend CD to E: ∠CDE = 180° − ∠ADC = ∠ABC (since ∠ABC + ∠ADC = 180°).

## In a nutshell

- **Circle** = locus of points at distance r from the centre. Infinite rotational symmetry; every diameter is a mirror line.
- **Perpendicular bisector of AB** = locus of points equidistant from A and B.
- **2 points → infinitely many circles** (centres on the bisector). **3 non-collinear points → exactly one** (the circumcircle). Collinear → none.
- Equal chords ⇔ equal central angles (SSS / SAS).
- Centre → midpoint is ⊥ chord; ⊥ from centre bisects the chord. **c = 2√(r² − d²).**
- Equal chords ⇔ equidistant. **Longer chord ⇔ nearer the centre**; the diameter is longest.
- **Angle at centre = 2 × angle at the circle.** Angles in the same segment are equal. **Angle in a semicircle = 90°.**
- **Cyclic quadrilateral ⇔ opposite angles add to 180°.**

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** "distance to a chord" is the **perpendicular** distance · use the **half**-chord in r² = d² + (c/2)² · chords on **opposite** sides: add the distances; **same** side: subtract · the angle at the centre is **double**, not half · angles in the same segment are equal only on the **same side** of the chord · cyclic: **opposite** angles add to 180°, not adjacent ones · a circle through 3 points needs them **non-collinear** · a longer chord is **closer** to the centre, not farther.
