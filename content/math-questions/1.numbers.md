At dawn the temperature was **−4°C**. By afternoon it had risen by **11°C**, and by night it fell by **6°C**. What was the night temperature?

<!-- ===== Prime or composite? ===== -->
<!--
  Identifying primes — a number is PRIME when its only factors are 1 and
  itself, COMPOSITE when it has more. To test, try dividing by the primes
  2, 3, 5, 7, … using the divisibility shortcuts. You only need to test
  primes up to the number's square root (explained in the answers).
  Easy → harder; 51 and 91 LOOK prime but aren't, and 97 really is.
-->

Is **7** prime or composite?

> [!ANSWER]
> Try the small primes: 7 isn't even, `7+…` no — 2, 3, 5 don't divide it, and nothing between 1 and 7 does either.
>
> Its only factors are **1 and 7** → exactly two factors → **prime**.

---

Is **9** prime or composite?

> [!ANSWER]
> 9 is odd, but odd does **not** mean prime. Check 3: `9 = 3 × 3`.
>
> So its factors are **1, 3, 9** — more than two → **composite**.

---

Is **1** prime or composite?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neither.** A prime needs **exactly two** factors (1 and itself); 1 has only **one** factor — itself.
>
> As the notes say, **0 and 1 are neither prime nor composite** — they can't be factorised.

---

Is **51** prime or composite?

> [!ANSWER]
> It looks prime, but use the **÷3 shortcut**: digit sum `5 + 1 = 6`, which is divisible by 3.
>
> So `51 = 3 × 17` → more than two factors → **composite**.

---

Is **91** prime or composite?

> [!ANSWER]
> A classic trap. It's odd (not ÷2), digit sum `9 + 1 = 10` (not ÷3), doesn't end in 0/5 (not ÷5). Keep going — try **7**:
>
> `7 × 13 = 91` ✓ → **composite**.

---

Is **97** prime or composite?

> [!ANSWER]
> Test the primes **2, 3, 5, 7**: not even; digit sum `9 + 7 = 16` (not ÷3); doesn't end in 0/5; `97 ÷ 7 ≈ 13.9` (no). Nothing divides it → **prime**.
>
> **Why stop at 7?** `√97 ≈ 9.8`. If 97 had a factor **bigger** than its square root, the matching partner would be **smaller** than the square root — and we'd have already found it. So testing primes up to 9 (2, 3, 5, 7) is enough to be sure.

<!-- ===== HCF — highest common factor ===== -->
<!--
  HCF worked with the notes' method: prime-factorise both numbers, take the
  SMALLER power of every SHARED prime, then multiply. Two-digit pairs first,
  then three-digit, then three numbers at once, then a real-life problem —
  increasingly difficult. Includes a co-prime pair (HCF = 1).
-->

Find the **HCF of 12 and 18**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `12 = 2² × 3` · `18 = 2 × 3²`. Shared primes: **2** and **3**.
>
> Take the **smaller** power of each: `2¹` and `3¹` → HCF = `2 × 3 = ` **6**.
>
> **Why the smaller power:** the HCF has to divide **both** numbers, so of each shared prime it can only use as many copies as the number that has **fewer** — one more and it wouldn't divide that number.

A gardener has **84 roses** and **126 marigolds**. She plants them in equal rows — each row all one kind of flower — using every flower and making the rows **as long as possible**. How many flowers are in each row?

> [!ANSWER]
> "Same size, as large as possible, dividing both" → this is the **HCF**.
>
> `84 = 2² × 3 × 7` · `126 = 2 × 3² × 7`. Shared: **2, 3, 7**. Smaller powers: `2¹`, `3¹`, `7¹` → HCF = `2 × 3 × 7 = ` **42**.
>
> **42 flowers per row.** Check: roses `84 ÷ 42 = 2` rows, marigolds `126 ÷ 42 = 3` rows — every flower used. ✓

<!-- ===== LCM — lowest common multiple ===== -->
<!--
  LCM worked with the notes' method: prime-factorise, take the LARGER power
  of EVERY prime that appears in either number, then multiply. Two-digit
  pairs, a co-prime pair, three numbers, then a real-life "when do they line
  up again?" problem — increasingly difficult.
-->

Find the **LCM of 6 and 8**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `6 = 2 × 3` · `8 = 2³`. Primes in either: **2** and **3**.
>
> Take the **larger** power of each: `2³` and `3¹` → LCM = `8 × 3 = ` **24**.
>
> **Why the larger power:** the LCM has to be a **multiple of both**, so it needs enough copies of each prime to cover whichever number has **more** of them.
>
> **Check with the notes' rule:** `HCF × LCM = product`. Here `HCF(6,8) = 2`, and `2 × 24 = 48 = 6 × 8` ✓.

Two lighthouses flash **together right now**. One flashes every **8 seconds**, the other every **12 seconds**. After how many seconds do they next flash **together**?

> [!ANSWER]
> "When do both events line up again?" → this is the **LCM**.
>
> `8 = 2³` · `12 = 2² × 3`. Larger powers: `2³` and `3¹` → LCM = `8 × 3 = ` **24**.
>
> They flash together again after **24 seconds**. Check: `24 ÷ 8 = 3` flashes of the first, `24 ÷ 12 = 2` of the second — both land exactly on 24. ✓
