Why does a field of sugarcane show almost no variation while a class of students shows a great deal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of HOW each was produced.**
>
> **Sugarcane is propagated VEGETATIVELY — asexually.** The only differences that can arise are the **small inaccuracies in DNA copying**, which are minor. Every plant is essentially a copy of one parent.
>
> **Human beings reproduce SEXUALLY**, which not only introduces new copying errors but also **RECOMBINES the variations that two different parents already carried.**
>
> **So sexual reproduction generates far greater diversity** — it shuffles an existing store of variation rather than waiting for new mistakes.

---

Trait A is found in 10% of an asexually reproducing population and trait B in 60%. Which arose earlier, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Trait B — the one in 60%.**
>
> **Why: a new variation begins in ONE individual, and it can only spread as that individual's descendants multiply.** That takes many generations.
>
> **A trait present in 60% of the population has therefore had far LONGER to spread than one present in only 10%.**
>
> **A caution worth adding: this reasoning holds for an ASEXUALLY reproducing species, where traits pass straight down a line of descent.** In a sexually reproducing population, selection could spread an advantageous trait much faster, so frequency would be a poorer guide to age.

---

How does the creation of variation in a species promote its survival?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the environment can change in ways no organism controls** — temperatures rise or fall, water levels change, food disappears.
>
> ```
> a population with NO variation, perfectly suited to current conditions
>    → if conditions change drastically, ALL of them are equally unsuited
>      and the population can be WIPED OUT
>
> a population with variation
>    → some individuals happen to suit the NEW conditions and survive
> ```
> **Example: bacteria in temperate water during a heat wave. Most die, but the few HEAT-RESISTANT variants survive and multiply.**
>
> **The selection of variants by environmental factors is the BASIS OF EVOLUTION** — the environment does not create the variation, it only picks among what already exists.

---

What made Mendel's work different from earlier studies of inheritance?

> [!ANSWER]
> **He COUNTED.**
>
> **Many others had studied inheritance in peas and other organisms before him. Mendel was the FIRST to keep count of the number of individuals showing each trait in every generation**, blending his knowledge of science with **mathematics**.
>
> **Why counting was the breakthrough: the laws of inheritance are laws about RATIOS — 3 : 1, 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.**
>
> **Someone who merely observes that "some are tall and some are short" can never find them.** The pattern only appears in the numbers.

---

Describe Mendel's monohybrid cross and state the two conclusions it forces.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PARENTS   tall (TT)  ×  short (tt)
>
> F1        ALL TALL (Tt) — no medium-height plants at all
>
> F1 self-pollinated
>
> F2        TT : Tt : tt = 1 : 2 : 1
>           tall : short = 3 : 1     (ONE QUARTER were short)
> ```
> **Conclusion 1 — from the F1 being ALL tall with no intermediates: TRAITS DO NOT BLEND.** Only one of the two parental traits appears, not a mixture of the two.
>
> **Conclusion 2 — from one quarter of F2 being SHORT: the shortness trait was NEVER LOST.** It was present in the F1 plants all along and merely **not expressed** — otherwise it could not have reappeared out of nothing.

---

How do Mendel's experiments show that traits may be dominant or recessive?

> [!ANSWER]
> **From the F1 generation, where BOTH traits were inherited but only ONE appeared.**
> ```
> TT  tall     a SINGLE copy of T is enough to make the plant tall
> Tt  tall     → T is DOMINANT
> tt  SHORT    BOTH copies must be t for the plant to be short
>              → t is RECESSIVE
> ```
> **The F1 plants were all Tt — carrying both factors — yet all were tall.** So T masked t.
>
> **And a recessive trait appears only when NO dominant copy is present**, which is exactly why shortness could hide for a whole generation and return in one quarter of F2.

---

TT and Tt plants look identical. How would you tell them apart experimentally?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Self-pollinate each tall plant and look at ITS offspring.**
> ```
> a TT plant → ALL its offspring are tall
> a Tt plant → its offspring are tall and short in a 3 : 1 ratio
> ```
> **This is how the 1 : 2 : 1 ratio of TT : Tt : tt in F2 can be CONFIRMED**, even though only a 3 : 1 ratio is visible.
>
> **The general principle worth taking away: you cannot see a genotype directly — you DEDUCE it from what the individual PRODUCES.** The hidden difference between two identical-looking plants shows up in the next generation.

---

Describe Mendel's dihybrid cross and give the F2 ratio.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PARENTS   round green seeds (RRyy)  ×  wrinkled yellow seeds (rrYY)
>
> F1        ALL round and yellow (RrYy)
>           → ROUND and YELLOW are the DOMINANT traits
>
> F1 × F1 by self-pollination
>
> F2        round yellow : round green : wrinkled yellow : wrinkled green
>                 9      :      3      :        3        :       1
>
> Mendel's actual counts out of 556 seeds:
>    315 round yellow · 108 round green · 101 wrinkled yellow · 32 wrinkled green
> ```
> **Those raw counts are worth noticing — they are close to 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 but not exact**, which is what real biological data looks like.

---

How do Mendel's experiments show that traits are inherited independently?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because NEW COMBINATIONS appeared in F2 that NEITHER PARENT had.**
>
> **The parents were round-GREEN and wrinkled-YELLOW.**
>
> **But F2 contained round-YELLOW and wrinkled-GREEN plants** — pairings that did not exist in the parent generation at all.
>
> **Conclusion: seed SHAPE and seed COLOUR are NOT tied together.** Either version of one can travel with either version of the other, so the two traits are **independently inherited**.
>
> **If they had been linked, only the two original combinations could ever have appeared.**

---

What is a gene, and how does a gene end up controlling a visible trait like height?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A GENE is a section of DNA that provides the information for making ONE protein.**
>
> **The full chain, for tallness:**
> ```
> plants have HORMONES that trigger growth
>    → height depends on the AMOUNT of a particular hormone
>    → that amount depends on the EFFICIENCY of the process making it
>    → that process depends on an ENZYME
>
> efficient enzyme        → plenty of hormone → TALL plant
> gene altered so the
> enzyme is LESS efficient → less hormone     → SHORT plant
> ```
> **So: GENE → PROTEIN (enzyme) → amount of HORMONE → HEIGHT.** Nothing in the chain is left unexplained.

---

Using that chain, explain why "T" behaves as dominant.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because ONE working copy of the gene still makes ENOUGH enzyme.**
>
> **A Tt plant has one normal gene and one altered one. The normal copy produces a working enzyme, which produces plenty of hormone — so the plant grows tall.** The altered copy is simply carried along, contributing little.
>
> **Only when BOTH copies are altered (tt) does the enzyme run short, the hormone run short, and the plant end up short.**
>
> **This is why dominance is not a matter of one trait "beating" another** — it is simply that having some working protein is usually as good as having twice as much.

---

Why must genes be carried on separate chromosomes rather than on one long thread of DNA?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because if a whole gene set travelled as ONE unit, the dihybrid experiment could not work.**
>
> **The argument: 'R' and 'y' entered the cross together, on one parent's set. If a whole set passed as a single thread, R and y would be PERMANENTLY LINKED and could never separate.**
>
> **Round-yellow and wrinkled-green offspring would then be impossible — but they appeared.**
>
> **So each gene set must exist NOT as one long thread but as SEPARATE INDEPENDENT PIECES: the CHROMOSOMES.**
> ```
> every cell has TWO copies of each chromosome — one from each parent
> every germ-cell takes ONE chromosome from each pair, either maternal
>    or paternal in origin
> when two germ-cells combine, the normal number is RESTORED
> ```
> **This gives both things at once: STABILITY of the species' DNA, and free SHUFFLING of traits.**

---

How is the equal genetic contribution of both parents ensured in the progeny?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By each germ-cell carrying exactly ONE set of chromosomes instead of two.**
> ```
> ordinary body cells — TWO copies of every chromosome
> germ-cells          — ONE copy of each, taken from each pair
> fertilisation       — the two single sets combine, restoring the full number
> ```
> **So each parent supplies exactly half the chromosomes**, and neither can contribute more than the other.
>
> **Why this had to be so: Mendel's results only make sense if BOTH parents can influence a trait**, which requires both to contribute a copy of the **same** gene.

---

How is the sex of a child determined in human beings?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 22 pairs of chromosomes are matched — one maternal, one paternal copy
> the 23rd pair — the SEX CHROMOSOMES — is not always a perfect pair
>
>    WOMEN  XX   a perfect pair
>    MEN    XY   mismatched: a normal-sized X and a short Y
>
>         MOTHER XX  →  all eggs carry X
>         FATHER XY  →  half the sperms carry X, half carry Y
>
>              father's X  →  XX  →  GIRL
>              father's Y  →  XY  →  BOY
> ```
> **So half the children are boys and half girls.**

---

Why can a mother never be responsible for the sex of her child?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because EVERY child inherits an X chromosome from the mother, regardless of whether it is a boy or a girl.**
>
> **She has nothing else to give — both her sex chromosomes are X.** So her contribution is identical in every case and **cannot make any difference at all.**
>
> **The father contributes either an X or a Y, and that alone decides.**
>
> **This matters far beyond the exam.** Women have been, and still are, blamed for the sex of their children — **and the biology says plainly that they cannot possibly be responsible.**

---

Name two ways, other than sex chromosomes, in which sex can be determined in other species.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **By ENVIRONMENTAL CUES.** In some **reptiles**, the **TEMPERATURE at which the fertilised eggs are kept** decides whether the animals developing inside will be male or female.
> - **By CHANGING SEX.** In **snails**, individuals can change sex during their lives — showing that sex there is **not genetically determined at all**.
>
> **Why this variety is worth knowing: it shows the XX/XY system is not a universal law of life but ONE solution among several.** Humans happen to use a genetic method; other species do not.

---

A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. Can you tell which trait is dominant?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — this information is NOT enough.**
>
> **Why: we do not know the FATHER's genotype.** He could be:
> - **AA**, in which case the daughter would have to inherit an A from him — and her being O would contradict that
> - **AO**, in which case she could have inherited the O from him and the O from her mother
>
> **Since the daughter is O, the father must be AO — but that tells us only what he carries, not which trait dominates.**
>
> **What would settle it: knowing how the trait behaves across more offspring, or seeing the grandparents' groups.** A single child cannot establish dominance, because **one observation is consistent with more than one explanation.**

---

Children with light-coloured eyes tend to have parents with light-coloured eyes. Does this show light eye colour is recessive?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — by itself it shows nothing about dominance.**
>
> **The observation is consistent with light being EITHER dominant or recessive:**
> - **if light is RECESSIVE**, then light-eyed children must have received a light gene from each parent — and parents carrying two light genes would themselves be light-eyed ✔
> - **if light is DOMINANT**, a light-eyed child needs only one light gene, which could well have come from a light-eyed parent ✔
>
> **Both stories fit the data equally well**, so the data cannot choose between them.
>
> **What would settle it: whether light-eyed children ever appear from TWO dark-eyed parents.** If they do, light must be recessive — a hidden trait reappearing is the signature of recessiveness.

---

Outline a project to find the dominant coat colour in dogs.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1. Choose two contrasting coat colours, say black and white.
> 2. Cross PUREBRED black dogs with PUREBRED white dogs.
>    (Purebred = their ancestors have shown only that colour for
>     several generations, so each parent is likely homozygous.)
> 3. Record the coat colour of ALL the F1 puppies, keeping COUNTS.
>    → whichever colour appears in ALL the F1 puppies is DOMINANT
> 4. Cross the F1 dogs with each other and count the F2 puppies.
>    → a 3 : 1 ratio confirms the conclusion
> ```
> **Why step 4 is not optional: the F1 result alone could be a coincidence in a small litter.** The reappearance of the recessive colour in **one quarter** of F2 is what turns an observation into evidence.
>
> **And why counts matter throughout: the answer is a RATIO, and ratios cannot be seen without numbers.**

A tall pea plant with violet flowers is crossed with a short plant with white flowers. All the progeny bear violet flowers, but almost half are short. What is the genetic make-up of the tall parent?

> [!ANSWER]
> **TtWW.**
>
> **Reasoning, one trait at a time.**
>
> **Flower colour: ALL the progeny are violet.** The short parent was white (`ww`), so every progeny received a `w` from it — yet all are violet. **So the tall parent must have given a W to every one of them, meaning it is WW.**
>
> **Height: ABOUT HALF the progeny are short (`tt`).** They must have received a `t` from each parent. The short parent is `tt`, so **the tall parent must also carry a t** — but it is itself tall, so it must be **Tt**.
>
> ```
> Tt WW  ×  tt ww  →  half Tt (tall), half tt (short); ALL Ww (violet)  ✔
> ```
> **The general method: split a two-trait problem into two ONE-trait problems and solve each separately** — which works precisely because the traits are inherited independently.

Two plants, both heterozygous tall (Tt), are crossed. What fraction of the offspring will be short, and what fraction of the tall ones will breed true?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>          T        t
>    T    TT       Tt
>    t    Tt       tt
>
> TT : Tt : tt  =  1 : 2 : 1
> ```
> **ONE QUARTER (1/4) of the offspring will be SHORT** — only `tt` is short.
>
> **Of the three tall offspring, only ONE is TT — so ONE THIRD of the tall plants will breed true.**
>
> **The second part is the one usually got wrong.** It asks about a fraction of the **tall plants**, not of all the offspring — so the denominator is 3, not 4. **Read carefully which group the question is asking about.**

---

Why do asexually reproducing organisms also follow rules of inheritance, even though there is only one parent?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because inheritance is fundamentally about COPYING DNA, and that happens in asexual reproduction too.**
>
> **The offspring receives a copy of the parent's DNA — so it receives the parent's genes**, and those genes control its traits in exactly the same way.
>
> **What is MISSING is the RECOMBINATION.** With only one parent there is no pairing of different gene sets, so:
> - **no dominant and recessive copies meet in a new individual**
> - **no new combinations of traits can be formed**
>
> **So the rules still apply, but the interesting consequences of having two copies never arise.** Variation is limited to whatever copying errors occur.

---

Summarise what a single Mendelian cross can and cannot tell you.

> [!ANSWER]
> **What it CAN tell you:**
> - **which trait is DOMINANT** — the one that appears in all the F1 progeny of purebred parents
> - **whether traits BLEND** — they do not, if no intermediate forms appear
> - **whether two traits are INHERITED INDEPENDENTLY** — they are, if new combinations show up in F2
>
> **What it CANNOT tell you:**
> - **the genotype of an individual showing the DOMINANT trait** — TT and Tt look identical, and only their offspring can distinguish them
> - **anything reliable from a SINGLE offspring** — the laws are about ratios, and a ratio needs numbers
> - **which trait is dominant, if the parents were not PUREBRED** to begin with
>
> **The single thread running through all of this: heredity is read in COUNTS ACROSS GENERATIONS, never in one individual.**
