Why does carbon form covalent bonds rather than ionic ones?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Carbon is (2,4) — it has FOUR valence electrons and would need to gain or lose four to reach a noble-gas arrangement. Neither is possible:**
>
> - **Gaining 4 → C⁴⁻.** A nucleus with only **six protons could not hold ten electrons**.
> - **Losing 4 → C⁴⁺.** It would take an **enormous amount of energy** to strip four electrons away, leaving six protons gripping just two.
>
> **So carbon SHARES its electrons instead.** The shared pairs count towards the outermost shell of **both** atoms, so both reach a filled shell without any transfer at all.

---

Why do covalent compounds have low melting points and poor electrical conductivity?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both follow from one fact: covalent bonding produces NO IONS.**
>
> - **No ions means no charged particles free to move — so no current flows.**
> - **The bond INSIDE a molecule is strong, but the force BETWEEN separate molecules is weak.** Melting only has to overcome the forces *between* molecules, so very little energy is needed.
> ```
> NaCl (ionic)    melts at 1074 K
> methane (covalent) melts at   90 K
> ```
> **The confusion to avoid: a low melting point does NOT mean a weak bond.** Methane's C–H bonds are strong; it is the pull of one methane molecule on the next that is feeble.

---

Name the two properties of carbon responsible for the huge number of carbon compounds, and explain each.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. CATENATION — carbon's ability to bond to other CARBON atoms**, making long chains, branched chains and rings, linked by single, double or triple bonds.
>
> **2. TETRAVALENCY — carbon has a valency of FOUR**, so it can bond to four other atoms: more carbons, or hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, chlorine and others.
>
> **Together they give millions of compounds — more than all other elements combined.** Catenation supplies the skeleton; tetravalency supplies the variety of what can hang off it.

---

Silicon can also form chains. Why does it not give millions of compounds the way carbon does?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the silicon–silicon bond is far weaker than the carbon–carbon bond.**
>
> **Silicon manages chains of only seven or eight atoms with hydrogen, and those compounds are VERY REACTIVE** — they fall apart rather than persisting.
>
> **The underlying reason is SIZE.** Carbon is a small atom, so its nucleus is close to the shared pairs and grips them tightly. **Bigger atoms hold shared electrons more loosely, and their bonds are correspondingly weaker.**
>
> **Strong, stable C–C bonds are what let carbon chains grow long enough to be interesting.**

---

Distinguish saturated from unsaturated compounds, and name the three families of hydrocarbons.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **SATURATED — only SINGLE bonds between carbon atoms.**
> - **UNSATURATED — containing at least one DOUBLE or TRIPLE bond.**
> ```
> ALKANES  — saturated,  CnH2n+2   CH4, C2H6, C3H8
> ALKENES  — double bond, CnH2n    C2H4, C3H6, C4H8
> ALKYNES  — triple bond, CnH2n−2  C2H2, C3H4, C4H6
> ```
> **HYDROCARBONS are compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen.**
>
> **Why "saturated": the carbon has taken on as many hydrogen atoms as it possibly can.** An unsaturated compound still has room to accept more — which is exactly why it undergoes addition reactions.

---

Draw the structure of ethane and count its covalent bonds.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>      H   H
>      |   |
>   H— C — C —H
>      |   |
>      H   H
> ```
> **SEVEN covalent bonds: six C–H bonds plus one C–C bond.**
>
> **How to build any such structure reliably:**
> 1. **link the CARBON atoms first**
> 2. **use HYDROGEN to fill every remaining valency**
>
> **The check: every carbon must end up with exactly FOUR bonds and every hydrogen with exactly one.**

---

What are structural isomers? Draw those of C4H10 and explain why C3H8 has none.

> [!ANSWER]
> **STRUCTURAL ISOMERS have the SAME molecular formula but DIFFERENT structures.**
> ```
> C—C—C—C          straight chain (butane)
>
> C—C—C            branched chain (isobutane)
>   |
>   C
> ```
> **C3H8 has NO isomers** — with only three carbons there is just one way to join them. Putting the third carbon "on a branch" gives the same chain drawn differently.
>
> **Isomerism becomes possible only from FOUR carbons onwards**, because four is the smallest number that can be arranged in genuinely different shapes.

---

How many structural isomers does pentane (C5H12) have? Describe them.

> [!ANSWER]
> **THREE.**
> ```
> 1.  C—C—C—C—C            a straight chain of five
>
> 2.  C—C—C—C              a four-carbon chain with one branch
>       |
>       C
>
> 3.      C
>         |
>     C—C—C                a three-carbon chain with two branches
>         |                 on the same carbon
>         C
> ```
> **The systematic way to find them: start with the LONGEST chain, then shorten it by one and place the spare carbons as branches**, checking each time that you have not simply redrawn one you already had.

---

What is a functional group? Name five, with their formulae and suffixes.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A FUNCTIONAL GROUP is an atom or group of atoms that gives a carbon compound its characteristic properties.**
>
> | Class | Group | Prefix / suffix |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Haloalkane** | `—Cl`, `—Br` | prefix **chloro-, bromo-** |
> | **Alcohol** | `—OH` | suffix **-ol** |
> | **Aldehyde** | `—CHO` | suffix **-al** |
> | **Ketone** | `—CO—` | suffix **-one** |
> | **Carboxylic acid** | `—COOH` | suffix **-oic acid** |
>
> **The key fact: the functional group decides the chemistry REGARDLESS of chain length.** CH3OH and C4H9OH behave alike because both carry `—OH`.

---

Define a homologous series and state what successive members differ by.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A HOMOLOGOUS SERIES is a family of compounds in which the SAME functional group substitutes for hydrogen in carbon chains of increasing length.**
> ```
> CH4  → C2H6  → C3H8  → C4H10
> 16 u    30 u    44 u    58 u
> ```
> **Successive members differ by a `—CH2—` unit — a difference of 14 u in molecular mass.**
>
> **Why the series is so useful: learn ONE member's chemistry and you know them all**, since the functional group is the same throughout.

---

In a homologous series, why do physical properties change gradually while chemical properties stay the same?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **PHYSICAL properties change** because they depend on **molecular mass**. Bigger molecules attract one another more strongly, so more energy is needed to separate them — **melting and boiling points climb steadily**, and solubility shifts too.
> - **CHEMICAL properties stay the same** because they are decided **solely by the FUNCTIONAL GROUP**, which is identical throughout the series.
>
> **The chain is a passenger; the group does the chemistry.**
>
> **The practical payoff: you can PREDICT the boiling point of a member you have never met**, just by seeing where it sits in the series.

---

Write the general formula for alkanes, alkenes and alkynes, and give the first three of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ALKANES  CnH2n+2    CH4 (methane)   C2H6 (ethane)   C3H8 (propane)
> ALKENES  CnH2n      C2H4 (ethene)   C3H6 (propene)  C4H8 (butene)
> ALKYNES  CnH2n−2    C2H2 (ethyne)   C3H4 (propyne)  C4H6 (butyne)
> ```
> **Alkenes and alkynes start at TWO carbons, not one.** You cannot have a double or triple bond between carbon atoms with only one carbon atom — there is nothing to bond to.

---

Name these: CH3CH2Br, CH3COOH, CH3COCH3, CH3CH2CHO, CH≡CH.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> CH3CH2Br    bromoethane        2 carbons + Br as a PREFIX
> CH3COOH     ethanoic acid      2 carbons + -oic acid
> CH3COCH3    propanone          3 carbons + -one (ketone)
> CH3CH2CHO   propanal           3 carbons + -al (aldehyde)
> CH≡CH       ethyne             2 carbons + triple bond
> ```
> **The method every time: count the carbons for the stem, then attach the family ending.**
>
> **And the rule that trips people up: if the suffix begins with a VOWEL, drop the final 'e' of the stem.** propane − e + one = **propanone**. **Prefixes like chloro- change nothing** — chloropropane keeps its 'e'.

---

Give the structures of butanone and hexanal.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> BUTANONE  — four carbons, KETONE group
>    CH3—CO—CH2—CH3
>
> HEXANAL   — six carbons, ALDEHYDE group
>    CH3—CH2—CH2—CH2—CH2—CHO
> ```
> **The essential difference between the two groups: an ALDEHYDE group sits at the END of the chain; a KETONE group sits INSIDE it.**
>
> **Why: `—CHO` has a hydrogen attached, so it needs only one bond to the chain and must be terminal. `—CO—` bonds to carbon on both sides**, so it has to be in the middle.

---

Why does a saturated hydrocarbon burn with a clean blue flame while an unsaturated one gives a sooty yellow flame?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The soot is UNBURNT CARBON.**
>
> **An unsaturated compound has a higher proportion of carbon** relative to hydrogen, so there is not enough oxygen to burn all of it — and the leftover carbon escapes as black smoke, which glows yellow while hot.
>
> **A saturated hydrocarbon has enough hydrogen for the available oxygen to burn everything, so the flame is clean and blue.**
>
> **But note: limiting the AIR SUPPLY makes even a saturated hydrocarbon burn sooty** — so the cause is really the balance of carbon to oxygen, not saturation as such.

---

While cooking, the bottom of a vessel gets blackened. What does this tell you and what should be done?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It means the FUEL IS NOT BURNING COMPLETELY** — the black deposit is unburnt carbon.
>
> **The cause: the AIR HOLES of the stove are BLOCKED**, so an oxygen-poor mixture is burning.
>
> **The remedy: clean the air inlets** so a sufficiently oxygen-rich mixture burns with a clean blue flame.
>
> **Why it matters beyond the mess: FUEL IS BEING WASTED.** The carbon leaving as soot never released its energy, so you are paying for heat you do not get.

---

Why do some substances burn with a flame while others merely glow?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A FLAME is produced only when GASEOUS substances burn.**
>
> **Wood and charcoal give a flame at first**, while the volatile substances inside them vaporise and burn as gases. **Once those are exhausted, the charcoal simply GLOWS red** — hot solid carbon reacting with oxygen, with no gas to feed a flame.
>
> **A LUMINOUS flame is glowing atoms**, and the colour is characteristic of the element — which is why a copper wire held in a flame gives its own distinctive colour.
>
> **The yellow of a candle flame is glowing SOOT particles**, produced by incomplete combustion of the wax.

---

Why is the conversion of ethanol to ethanoic acid called an oxidation reaction? What are oxidising agents?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> CH3CH2OH  --[alkaline KMnO4 or acidified K2Cr2O7]-->  CH3COOH
> ```
> **It is an OXIDATION because OXYGEN IS ADDED** to the ethanol to make the acid.
>
> **An OXIDISING AGENT is a substance that adds oxygen to another substance.** Alkaline potassium permanganate and acidified potassium dichromate are the two used here.
>
> **A nice self-indicating feature: the purple colour of KMnO4 DISAPPEARS while alcohol remains to be oxidised, and PERSISTS once the alcohol is used up.** The colour tells you when the reaction is finished.

---

What is an addition reaction? Explain the hydrogenation of vegetable oils and its health implication.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>                       Ni or Pd catalyst
> CH2=CH2 + H2  ---------------------------→  CH3—CH3
> ```
> **UNSATURATED hydrocarbons ADD hydrogen in the presence of a catalyst to become SATURATED.** A **CATALYST** changes the rate of a reaction without itself being changed.
>
> **Hydrogenation of vegetable oils uses a NICKEL catalyst** to turn liquid oil into a solid fat.
>
> **The health implication: vegetable OILS have unsaturated chains, animal FATS have saturated ones, and saturated fats are considered harmful.**
>
> **So hydrogenation moves an oil in the UNHEALTHY direction** — it makes the product more like animal fat. Oils with unsaturated fatty acids should be preferred for cooking.

---

What is a substitution reaction? Why do saturated hydrocarbons undergo substitution while unsaturated ones undergo addition?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>                Sunlight
> CH4 + Cl2  -------------→  CH3Cl + HCl
> ```
> **SUBSTITUTION: one atom or group takes the place of another.**
>
> **Why saturated compounds substitute: they have NO DOUBLE BOND to open up.** Every valency is already used, so nothing can simply be added — something must be **thrown out** to make room.
>
> **Why unsaturated compounds add: the double or triple bond CAN open**, giving two free valencies that accept new atoms with nothing displaced.
>
> **The type of reaction is decided by the STRUCTURE, not by preference.**

---

Why is a mixture of ethyne and oxygen used for welding, rather than ethyne and air?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because burning ethyne in AIR gives an incomplete, sooty flame that is not hot enough.**
>
> **Air is only about one-fifth oxygen.** With ethyne — an unsaturated compound already rich in carbon — that is not enough oxygen for complete combustion, so much of the carbon leaves as soot and its energy is never released.
>
> **PURE OXYGEN gives complete combustion and therefore a much HOTTER flame** — hot enough to melt metal, which is exactly what welding requires.

---

Give the reaction of ethanol with sodium and with hot concentrated sulphuric acid.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> WITH SODIUM
>    2Na + 2CH3CH2OH → 2CH3CH2O⁻Na⁺ + H2
>                      (sodium ethoxide)
>
> WITH HOT CONC. H2SO4 at 443 K — DEHYDRATION
>    CH3CH2OH → CH2=CH2 + H2O
> ```
> **The sodium reaction releases HYDROGEN — the same gas metals give with acids.**
>
> **In the second reaction, concentrated sulphuric acid acts as a DEHYDRATING AGENT** — it removes water from the ethanol, and what is left is an unsaturated compound, ethene.

---

Why is methanol far more dangerous than ethanol?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Even a very small quantity of methanol can KILL.**
>
> **What happens: methanol is oxidised in the LIVER to METHANAL.** Methanal reacts rapidly with the components of cells and **COAGULATES THE PROTOPLASM** — in much the same way that cooking coagulates an egg.
>
> **It also attacks the OPTIC NERVE, causing BLINDNESS.**
>
> **Ethanol is harmful in a different way** — it depresses the central nervous system, causing loss of coordination, confusion, drowsiness and stupor, and long-term use causes many health problems. **But it does not destroy cells outright the way methanal does.**

---

What is denatured alcohol and why is it made?

> [!ANSWER]
> **DENATURED ALCOHOL is industrial ethanol deliberately made UNFIT TO DRINK.**
>
> **How: poisonous substances such as METHANOL are added, along with a blue DYE so it can be recognised at a glance.**
>
> **Why: ethanol is an important industrial SOLVENT and is produced in large quantities.** Denaturing prevents it being diverted and sold for drinking — the poison makes misuse impossible and the dye makes it obvious.

---

Why is pure ethanoic acid called glacial acetic acid? What is vinegar?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because its melting point is 290 K — about 17 °C — so in a cold climate it FREEZES in winter** into ice-like crystals. Hence "glacial".
>
> **VINEGAR is a 5–8% solution of acetic acid in water**, used widely as a preservative in pickles.
>
> **The freezing point is a useful reminder that 290 K is not cold at all by everyday standards** — this substance solidifies at a temperature many rooms fall to.

---

Both dilute HCl and dilute acetic acid turn blue litmus red. How would you show that one is much stronger than the other?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Use a UNIVERSAL INDICATOR (or pH paper) instead of litmus.**
>
> **Litmus only reports "acidic or not", so it cannot tell them apart.** A universal indicator reports the **pH**, and at the same concentration dilute acetic acid shows a **noticeably higher pH** than dilute HCl.
>
> **Why: HCl is a mineral acid and is COMPLETELY ionised, releasing many H⁺ ions. Carboxylic acids are WEAK acids and ionise only partly**, so far fewer H⁺ ions are released.
>
> **The difference is in how much ionises, not in how much acid is present.**

---

What is esterification? Write the reaction and state a use of esters.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>                            acid catalyst
> CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH  ---------------------→  CH3COOC2H5 + H2O
> (ethanoic acid) (ethanol)                   (ester)
> ```
> **ESTERIFICATION is the reaction of an ACID with an ALCOHOL to give an ESTER.**
>
> **Esters are generally SWEET-SMELLING — used in making PERFUMES and as FLAVOURING AGENTS.**
>
> **A small practical point: the smell is the test.** After warming the mixture in a water bath, pouring it into water and smelling it is how you confirm an ester has formed.

---

What is saponification, and how is it connected to soap?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> CH3COOC2H5 + NaOH → C2H5OH + CH3COONa
> (ester)             (alcohol) (sodium salt of the acid)
> ```
> **SAPONIFICATION is the treatment of an ESTER with an ALKALI, giving back the alcohol plus the sodium salt of the carboxylic acid.**
>
> **The connection: SOAPS ARE EXACTLY THAT — sodium or potassium salts of LONG-CHAIN carboxylic acids.**
>
> **So the name is literal: saponification means "soap-making", and it describes the reaction by which soap is actually manufactured** from natural fats and oils, which are esters.

---

How would you distinguish experimentally between an alcohol and a carboxylic acid?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Add SODIUM HYDROGENCARBONATE (or sodium carbonate) to each.**
> ```
> CH3COOH + NaHCO3 → CH3COONa + H2O + CO2      BRISK EFFERVESCENCE
> alcohol + NaHCO3 → no reaction               NOTHING HAPPENS
> ```
> **Confirm the gas by passing it through lime water — it turns MILKY, proving it is carbon dioxide.**
>
> **Why this test is reliable: only the acid has an acidic `—COOH` group** capable of reacting with a carbonate. **The alcohol's `—OH` group is not acidic enough to do anything at all**, so the difference is unmistakable rather than a matter of degree.

---

Explain how soap cleans an oily stain, using the idea of a micelle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A soap molecule has TWO ENDS WITH OPPOSITE TASTES:**
> ```
> ————————————————— COO⁻ Na⁺
> HYDROCARBON tail    IONIC head
> HYDROPHOBIC —       HYDROPHILIC —
> dissolves in oil    dissolves in water
> ```
> **Most dirt is OILY, and oil does not dissolve in water — which is why water alone fails.**
>
> **The soap molecules surround the oil droplet: TAILS pointing INWARDS into the oil, IONIC HEADS pointing OUTWARDS into the water.** That cluster is a **MICELLE**, with the dirt trapped in the centre.
>
> **Why the micelles do not clump together again: their outsides are all NEGATIVELY charged, so they REPEL one another** and stay suspended as a colloid, to be rinsed away with the dirt inside.
>
> **And a soap solution looks CLOUDY because the micelles are big enough to SCATTER LIGHT.**

---

Why must clothes be beaten, scrubbed or agitated when washing, even after soap is added?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because soap can surround the oily dirt, but the dirt is still STUCK TO THE FABRIC.**
>
> **Agitation supplies the MECHANICAL ENERGY to knock the dirt loose** from the fibres so that the micelles can carry it away.
>
> **A neat way to see the division of labour: soap does the CHEMISTRY (trapping the oil), agitation does the PHYSICS (detaching it).** Neither alone is enough — which is why a washing machine both adds detergent and tumbles the load.

---

What is hard water, what is scum, and why do detergents work where soap fails?

> [!ANSWER]
> **HARD WATER contains CALCIUM and MAGNESIUM salts.**
>
> **SCUM is the insoluble white curdy precipitate formed when soap reacts with those calcium and magnesium ions.** The result: **very little foam, a great deal more soap needed, and a residue left on the clothes.**
>
> **DETERGENTS are sodium salts of sulphonic acids, or ammonium salts with chloride or bromide ions** — also with a long hydrocarbon chain.
>
> **Why they succeed: their CHARGED ENDS do NOT form insoluble precipitates with calcium and magnesium ions**, so they lather and clean normally in hard water. They are used in shampoos and washing products.

---

Can you use a detergent to test whether water is hard? Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — you must use SOAP.**
>
> **A detergent lathers well in BOTH soft and hard water**, so it behaves identically in each and tells you nothing.
>
> **Soap gives good foam in soft water, but poor foam and a white curdy SCUM in hard water** — a clear, visible difference.
>
> **The test works precisely because soap FAILS in hard water.** A reagent that copes with everything is useless as a detector.
