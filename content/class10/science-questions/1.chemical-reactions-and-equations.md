Name four observations that tell you a chemical reaction has taken place.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **change of state**
> - **change of colour**
> - **evolution of a gas**
> - **change of temperature**
>
> **Why these work as signs: a chemical reaction makes a NEW substance, and a new substance has new properties.** Bonds between atoms are broken and remade in a different arrangement, so the colour, the state, or the energy content must change — and that is something you can see or feel.

---

Why must a magnesium ribbon be cleaned with sandpaper before it is burnt?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because magnesium reacts slowly with the air and gets covered by a dull white layer of MAGNESIUM OXIDE.**
>
> **That oxide layer is already a burnt product** — it blocks the fresh metal underneath from reaching the oxygen, so the ribbon will not catch fire easily.
>
> **Sandpapering removes the layer and exposes clean metal**, which then burns with the dazzling white flame.

---

State the law of conservation of mass and explain why it forces us to balance equations.

> [!ANSWER]
> **LAW OF CONSERVATION OF MASS: mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.**
>
> **A reaction only REARRANGES atoms** — it breaks bonds and makes new ones. No atom appears from nowhere and none disappears.
>
> **So the number of atoms of each element must be identical on both sides.** An unbalanced equation says that atoms were created or destroyed — which is impossible, so the equation is simply wrong until it is balanced.

---

While balancing, why may you change a coefficient but never a subscript?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the formula IS the substance, while the coefficient is only how much of it there is.**
>
> ```
> 4H2O   =  four molecules of WATER          ✔
> H2O4   =  a completely different substance ✘
> ```
> **Changing a subscript changes what the reaction is about**; changing a coefficient changes only the quantity.
>
> **Balancing is meant to get the amounts right, not to swap in different chemicals** — so only the coefficients are yours to adjust.

---

Balance: Fe + H2O → Fe3O4 + H2, showing each step.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> start      Fe:  1 → 3      H:  2 → 2      O:  1 → 4
>
> begin with the largest compound Fe3O4 and its oxygen
>
> balance O   Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + H2
> balance H   now 8 H on the left, so 4H2 on the right
>             Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2
> balance Fe  3 Fe on the right, so 3Fe on the left
>             3Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2
>
> check       Fe: 3 = 3     H: 8 = 8     O: 4 = 4     ✔
> ```
> **Why start with Fe3O4: it carries the most atoms**, so fixing it first settles the hardest constraint while you still have freedom everywhere else. **Free elements like Fe are left till last because they can be adjusted without disturbing anything.**

Balance these: (i) hydrogen + chlorine → hydrogen chloride; (ii) sodium + water → sodium hydroxide + hydrogen; (iii) barium chloride + aluminium sulphate → barium sulphate + aluminium chloride.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (i)   H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl
>
> (ii)  2Na + 2H2O → 2NaOH + H2
>
> (iii) 3BaCl2 + Al2(SO4)3 → 3BaSO4 + 2AlCl3
> ```
> **In (iii), treat SO4 as a single unit.** There are 3 sulphate groups on the left inside Al2(SO4)3, so 3 BaSO4 are needed on the right — **balancing whole polyatomic groups instead of individual S and O atoms saves a great deal of work.**
>
> **Check (iii): Ba 3 = 3, Cl 6 = 6, Al 2 = 2, SO4 3 = 3** ✔

---

Write balanced equations with state symbols: (i) solutions of barium chloride and sodium sulphate give insoluble barium sulphate and a solution of sodium chloride; (ii) sodium hydroxide solution reacts with hydrochloric acid solution to give sodium chloride solution and water.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (i)  BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
>
> (ii) NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)
> ```
> **The state symbols carry real information here.** In (i) the `(s)` on BaSO4 is the whole point — it says a **precipitate** forms and leaves the solution, while everything else stays dissolved as `(aq)`.
>
> ```
> (s) solid   (l) liquid   (g) gas   (aq) dissolved in water
> ```

---

Define combination and decomposition reactions, and explain how they are related.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **COMBINATION — two or more reactants form a SINGLE product.** `A + B → AB`
> - **DECOMPOSITION — a SINGLE reactant breaks into two or more products.** `AB → A + B`
>
> **They are exact opposites** — one runs the other backwards.
> ```
> combination:    CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2
> decomposition:  CaCO3 → CaO + CO2      (on heating)
> ```

---

Why are combination reactions usually exothermic and decomposition reactions usually endothermic?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because MAKING bonds releases energy and BREAKING bonds absorbs it.**
>
> - **Combination is mostly bond-MAKING**, so energy comes out — the beaker gets warm. That is an **EXOTHERMIC** reaction.
> - **Decomposition is mostly bond-BREAKING**, so energy must be supplied — as **heat, light or electricity**. That is an **ENDOTHERMIC** reaction.
>
> **This is why every decomposition in the chapter needs a source:** thermal decomposition needs a burner, electrolysis needs a battery, and silver chloride needs sunlight. **Nothing falls apart for free.**

---

A substance X is used for whitewashing. Name it, write its reaction with water, and explain why the wall becomes shiny after two or three days.

> [!ANSWER]
> **X is calcium oxide (quick lime), CaO.**
> ```
> CaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(aq) + heat
> (quick lime)       (slaked lime)
> ```
> **The slaked lime is painted on the wall. Then it reacts SLOWLY with carbon dioxide from the air:**
> ```
> Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)
> ```
> **The shine comes from the thin layer of CALCIUM CARBONATE that builds up over two or three days** — the same compound as marble, which is why the finish looks polished.
>
> **The delay is explained too: the CO2 in air is scarce**, so the second reaction cannot be quick.

---

Write the equation for heating ferrous sulphate crystals and say what you observe.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>                    Heat
> 2FeSO4(s)      ---------→  Fe2O3(s) + SO2(g) + SO3(g)
> (green crystals)           (ferric oxide)
> ```
> **Observations:**
> - **the GREEN colour of the crystals disappears**, leaving a brownish solid
> - **a sharp smell of burning sulphur** — that is SO2 and SO3
>
> **Two things happen in order.** The crystals FeSO4·7H2O first **lose their water of crystallisation** (which is why the green fades), and only then does the ferrous sulphate itself decompose.

---

In the electrolysis of water, why is the volume of gas in one test tube twice that in the other? Name both gases.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2H2O(l) → 2H2(g) + O2(g)
> ```
> **The larger volume is HYDROGEN, collected at the cathode; the smaller is OXYGEN, at the anode.**
>
> **Why exactly twice: the balanced equation says 2 molecules of H2 for every 1 of O2.** Equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules, so the 2 : 1 ratio in the equation appears directly as a 2 : 1 ratio of volumes.
>
> **This is one of the few places where you can literally SEE a balanced equation.**
>
> **Testing them:** hydrogen burns with a **pop**; oxygen makes a glowing splint **flare up**.

---

Why does silver chloride turn grey in sunlight? Where is this used?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>            Sunlight
> 2AgCl(s) ----------→ 2Ag(s) + Cl2(g)
> (white)              (grey silver metal)
> ```
> **The white AgCl decomposes into silver metal and chlorine gas.** The grey colour is the **silver** that is left behind.
>
> **The energy comes from LIGHT, not heat** — this is a decomposition driven by sunlight, so it is endothermic.
>
> **Used in black-and-white photography:** wherever light falls on the film, silver is deposited — so the picture is literally written in silver. **Silver bromide behaves the same way.**

---

Define a displacement reaction and explain what you see when an iron nail is left in copper sulphate solution.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s)
> ```
> **A DISPLACEMENT REACTION is one where a more reactive element pushes a less reactive one out of its compound.**
>
> **What you see:**
> - **the blue colour of the solution FADES** — the blue belongs to copper sulphate, and the copper is leaving
> - **the nail turns BROWNISH** — that is the displaced copper depositing on it
>
> **The two observations are the same event seen from two sides.** Copper leaves the solution and lands on the nail; iron takes its place in solution as pale-green iron sulphate.

---

Why can iron displace copper from copper sulphate, but copper cannot displace iron from iron sulphate?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because IRON IS MORE REACTIVE THAN COPPER.**
>
> **A more reactive metal holds on to the sulphate more strongly**, so it can take it from a less reactive one. **The reverse is not possible** — copper simply is not reactive enough to prise iron away.
>
> **Displacement runs in one direction only, decided by the reactivity of the two metals.** Zinc and lead are also above copper, which is why they too can displace it.

---

Define a double displacement reaction and a precipitate, using sodium sulphate and barium chloride.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Na2SO4(aq) + BaCl2(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
>                          a WHITE precipitate
> ```
> **DOUBLE DISPLACEMENT: the two reactants EXCHANGE ions.** Ba²⁺ pairs up with SO4²⁻, and Na⁺ with Cl⁻.
>
> **A PRECIPITATE is an insoluble solid formed within a solution.**
>
> **Why the exchange happens at all: BaSO4 is insoluble, so the moment it forms it drops out of the solution and cannot come back.** That removal drags the reaction forwards. The sodium chloride is soluble and just stays dissolved.

---

Lead nitrate solution is mixed with potassium iodide solution. Name the precipitate, write the equation, and state the type of reaction.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)
>                          YELLOW precipitate of LEAD IODIDE
> ```
> **It is a DOUBLE DISPLACEMENT reaction** — Pb²⁺ and K⁺ swap partners — **and also a PRECIPITATION reaction**, since an insoluble solid is formed.
>
> **The two names are not alternatives.** "Double displacement" describes the mechanism; "precipitation" describes the visible result.

---

Define oxidation and reduction in two ways each, and explain why they always occur together.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **OXIDATION — gain of OXYGEN, or loss of HYDROGEN.**
> - **REDUCTION — loss of OXYGEN, or gain of HYDROGEN.**
>
> **They must occur together because the oxygen has to come from somewhere and go somewhere.**
>
> **If one substance gains oxygen, whatever supplied that oxygen has lost it — and is therefore reduced.** There is no such thing as an oxidation with no matching reduction.
>
> **Reactions where both happen are called REDOX reactions.**

---

In CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O, identify what is oxidised and what is reduced, and explain how you decided.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> CuO  loses oxygen  →  REDUCED    (it becomes Cu)
> H2   gains oxygen  →  OXIDISED   (it becomes H2O)
> ```
> **How to decide: track the oxygen.** Whichever substance ends up with **more** oxygen than it started with was **oxidised**; whichever ends up with **less** was **reduced**.
>
> **Visible proof: the black CuO coating turns brown**, because the black copper oxide is being turned back into brown copper metal.

---

In ZnO + C → Zn + CO and MnO2 + 4HCl → MnCl2 + 2H2O + Cl2, name the substance oxidised and the one reduced in each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ZnO + C → Zn + CO
>    C   gains oxygen (becomes CO)   →  OXIDISED
>    ZnO loses oxygen (becomes Zn)   →  REDUCED
>
> MnO2 + 4HCl → MnCl2 + 2H2O + Cl2
>    HCl  loses hydrogen (becomes Cl2)  →  OXIDISED
>    MnO2 loses oxygen  (becomes MnCl2) →  REDUCED
> ```
> **The second one shows why the "loss of hydrogen" definition is needed.** HCl gains no oxygen at all — but it loses its hydrogen, and that counts as oxidation just the same.

---

Is magnesium oxidised or reduced when it burns in air? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO
> ```
> **Magnesium is OXIDISED — it GAINS oxygen** to become magnesium oxide.
>
> **And oxygen is reduced at the same time**, since it has been taken up into a compound. **The two are inseparable, as always in a redox reaction.**

---

What is corrosion? Give the colour of the coating formed on iron, silver and copper.

> [!ANSWER]
> **CORROSION is a metal being slowly eaten away by things around it — moisture, air, acids.**
> ```
> iron    →  reddish-brown   (RUST)
> silver  →  black
> copper  →  green
> ```
> **Why it matters practically: corrosion destroys car bodies, bridges, railings and ships**, and enormous sums are spent every year replacing damaged iron.
>
> **It is an oxidation** — the metal is combining with oxygen from its surroundings.

---

What is rancidity, and how is it prevented in packaged food?

> [!ANSWER]
> **RANCIDITY: the fats and oils in food are slowly OXIDISED, and their taste and smell turn unpleasant.**
>
> **Four ways to slow it, and why each one works:**
> - **ANTIOXIDANTS** — they get oxidised in the food's place, using up the oxygen before it can attack the fat
> - **AIR-TIGHT containers** — less oxygen can get in
> - **flushing packets with NITROGEN** — nitrogen is unreactive, so it pushes the oxygen out without harming the food
> - **REFRIGERATION** — cold slows every reaction, this one included
>
> **All four attack the same thing: contact between the fat and oxygen.** Once you see that, you never have to memorise the list.

---

Why are chips packets filled with nitrogen rather than simply sealed with air inside?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because sealed air still contains oxygen, and oxygen is what turns the oil in the chips rancid.**
>
> **Nitrogen is chosen because it is UNREACTIVE.** It fills the space, keeps the oxygen out, and does nothing to the food itself.
>
> **A second benefit: the puffed-up packet cushions the chips** and stops them being crushed — but the chemistry is the main reason.

Classify each of these: (i) CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2; (ii) 2KClO3 → 2KCl + 3O2; (iii) Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2; (iv) BaCl2 + Na2SO4 → BaSO4 + 2NaCl.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (i)   COMBINATION        — two reactants make one product
> (ii)  DECOMPOSITION      — one reactant breaks into two products
> (iii) DISPLACEMENT       — zinc, being more reactive, pushes hydrogen out
> (iv)  DOUBLE DISPLACEMENT — the ions swap partners (also a PRECIPITATION)
> ```
> **The classification is done by shape, not by memory.** Count the reactants and products first, then look at whether an element has been pushed out or whether partners have been exchanged.

---

Write one decomposition reaction each for energy supplied as heat, as light and as electricity.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> HEAT (thermal decomposition)
>    CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)
>
> LIGHT
>    2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl2(g)
>
> ELECTRICITY (electrolysis)
>    2H2O(l) → 2H2(g) + O2(g)
> ```
> **All three are ENDOTHERMIC** — every one needs energy put in, because bonds are being broken.
>
> **The form of the energy differs, but the requirement does not.** Nothing decomposes on its own.

---

Why is respiration described as an exothermic reaction?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> C6H12O6(aq) + 6O2(aq) → 6CO2(aq) + 6H2O(l) + energy
> (glucose)
> ```
> **Because energy is RELEASED along with the products.**
>
> **Carbohydrates in rice, bread and potatoes are broken down during digestion into glucose. That glucose then combines with oxygen inside your cells**, and the energy given out is what powers everything you do.
>
> **This is also why your body stays warm** — some of that released energy appears as heat.

---

A student heats lead nitrate in a test tube and sees brown fumes. Write the equation, name the gas, and classify the reaction.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>                    Heat
> 2Pb(NO3)2(s)   ---------→  2PbO(s) + 4NO2(g) + O2(g)
> ```
> **The brown fumes are NITROGEN DIOXIDE, NO2.**
>
> **It is a THERMAL DECOMPOSITION** — a single reactant broken into several products by heat — **and therefore ENDOTHERMIC.**
>
> **Check the balancing: Pb 2 = 2, N 4 = 4, O 12 = 4 + 8 = 12** ✔ — the oxygens are easy to miscount here, since six come from each Pb(NO3)2.

Give one everyday example each of a combination reaction that is useful and a decomposition reaction that is useful.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Useful COMBINATION — whitewashing.** Slaked lime combines with the CO2 of the air to leave a shiny layer of calcium carbonate on the wall.
>
> **Useful DECOMPOSITION — making quick lime from limestone.**
> ```
> CaCO3 → CaO + CO2
> ```
> **Quick lime is used to manufacture cement**, so this single reaction stands behind almost every building around you.
>
> **Both are worth knowing because they connect a page of chemistry to something you can point at.**
