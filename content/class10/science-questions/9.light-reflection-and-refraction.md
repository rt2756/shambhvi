Define the pole, centre of curvature, principal focus and focal length of a spherical mirror, and state the relation between f and R.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> POLE  P              — the centre of the mirror's reflecting surface
> CENTRE OF CURVATURE C — the centre of the sphere the mirror is part of
> RADIUS OF CURVATURE R — the distance PC
> PRINCIPAL FOCUS F    — where rays parallel to the principal axis meet
>                        (concave) or appear to come from (convex)
> FOCAL LENGTH f       — the distance PF
>
> for a mirror of SMALL APERTURE:     f = R/2
> ```
> **Why f = R/2: the principal focus lies exactly MIDWAY between the pole and the centre of curvature**, provided the aperture is small. **This is why the condition "small aperture" is always stated** — for a wide mirror the rays do not all meet at one point and the relation breaks down.

---

How can you find the focal length of a concave mirror in about a minute?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Hold the mirror facing the Sun and catch the reflected light on a sheet of paper. Move the paper until you get the smallest, brightest spot. The distance from the mirror to the paper is the FOCAL LENGTH.**
>
> **Why it works: the Sun is so far away that its rays arrive effectively PARALLEL to each other** — and a concave mirror brings parallel rays to a point at the **focus**.
>
> **What that bright spot actually is: a real, inverted, tiny IMAGE OF THE SUN.** It is not just a patch of light.
>
> **Any distant object will do**, which is why the same trick works with a far-off building through a window.

---

State the three ray rules used to locate the image formed by a spherical mirror, and explain why each works.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. A ray PARALLEL to the principal axis** — after reflection it **passes through F** (concave) or **appears to diverge from F** (convex).
>
> **2. A ray through (or directed towards) F** — it emerges **PARALLEL to the principal axis.**
>
> **3. A ray through (or directed towards) C** — it is reflected **straight back along the same path.**
>
> **Why rules 1 and 2 are one rule read backwards: LIGHT PATHS ARE REVERSIBLE.** If a parallel ray returns through the focus, then a ray through the focus must return parallel.
>
> **Why rule 3 works: a ray aimed at the centre of curvature strikes the mirror ALONG A RADIUS, which is perpendicular to the surface.** It arrives at 0°, so it reflects at 0° and comes straight back.
>
> **Two rays are enough — the image sits where the reflected rays INTERSECT** (or appear to).

---

Complete the table of image formation by a concave mirror.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Object position | Image position | Size | Nature |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **At infinity** | at F | highly diminished, point-sized | real, inverted |
> | **Beyond C** | between F and C | diminished | real, inverted |
> | **At C** | at C | same size | real, inverted |
> | **Between C and F** | beyond C | enlarged | real, inverted |
> | **At F** | at infinity | highly enlarged | real, inverted |
> | **Between P and F** | **behind the mirror** | enlarged | **virtual, erect** |
>
> **The pattern worth holding on to: as the object moves TOWARDS the mirror, the image moves AWAY from it and grows** — until the object crosses the focus, at which point the image flips to the other side and becomes virtual.

---

Why does a concave mirror give a virtual image only when the object is between P and F?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a REAL image requires the reflected rays to actually MEET.**
>
> **With the object beyond F, the reflected rays CONVERGE and cross — a real image forms where they cross.**
>
> **Bring the object closer than the focus and the reflected rays DIVERGE.** They never meet, no matter how far you extend them forwards.
>
> **But extended BACKWARDS they appear to come from a single point behind the mirror** — and that apparent meeting point is the **VIRTUAL image**.
>
> **This is why a virtual image can never be caught on a screen: no light actually arrives there.**

---

Why are convex mirrors used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles, despite making everything look smaller?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a convex mirror always gives an ERECT, VIRTUAL, DIMINISHED image — and a diminished image means a WIDER FIELD OF VIEW.**
>
> **The driver sees far more of the road behind than a flat mirror of the same size could show**, and the image is always the right way up.
>
> **The trade-off is genuine and worth stating: objects appear SMALLER, and therefore FARTHER AWAY than they really are** — which is exactly why such mirrors carry a printed warning.
>
> **A flat mirror would show true size but a much narrower view; a concave one would invert distant objects.** The convex mirror is the only one that gives both an erect image and a wide view.

---

State the New Cartesian Sign Convention and give the sign of u, f and v in each case.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (i)   the OBJECT is always placed to the LEFT of the mirror
> (ii)  all distances parallel to the axis are measured FROM THE POLE
> (iii) distances to the RIGHT are POSITIVE; to the LEFT, NEGATIVE
> (iv)  distances ABOVE the principal axis are POSITIVE
> (v)   distances BELOW the principal axis are NEGATIVE
> ```
> **What follows automatically:**
> ```
> object distance u — ALWAYS NEGATIVE (the object is always on the left)
> concave mirror  f — NEGATIVE (its focus is in front, on the left)
> convex mirror   f — POSITIVE (its focus is behind, on the right)
> real image      v — NEGATIVE (formed in front of the mirror)
> virtual image   v — POSITIVE (formed behind it)
> ```
> **These are not extra facts to memorise — every one of them is read straight off rules (i) and (iii).**

---

Write the mirror formula and the magnification formula, and explain how to read the sign of m.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> MIRROR FORMULA     1/v + 1/u = 1/f
>
> MAGNIFICATION      m = h'/h = −v/u
> ```
> **Reading the answer:**
> ```
> m NEGATIVE   →  the image is REAL and INVERTED
> m POSITIVE   →  the image is VIRTUAL and ERECT
> |m| greater than 1  →  ENLARGED
> |m| less than 1     →  DIMINISHED
> ```
> **So one number tells you three things at once.** `m = −0.23` means: real, inverted, and about a quarter of the object's size.

---

An object is placed 20 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the image position, magnification and nature.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = −20 cm      f = −15 cm      (concave → f negative)
>
> 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/15 − (−1/20) = −1/15 + 1/20
>     = (−4 + 3)/60 = −1/60
>
> v = −60 cm
>
> m = −v/u = −(−60)/(−20) = −3
> ```
> **The image is 60 cm IN FRONT of the mirror (v is negative), REAL and INVERTED (m is negative), and THREE TIMES enlarged.**
>
> **Sanity check against the table: the object at 20 cm lies between C (30 cm) and F (15 cm), so the image should be beyond C, enlarged, real and inverted** — and 60 cm is indeed beyond 30 cm ✔

---

An object is placed 10 cm from a concave mirror of radius of curvature 30 cm. Find the image.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> f = R/2 = 15 cm, so f = −15 cm       u = −10 cm
>
> 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/15 + 1/10 = (−2 + 3)/30 = 1/30
>
> v = +30 cm
>
> m = −v/u = −(30)/(−10) = +3
> ```
> **The image is 30 cm BEHIND the mirror (v positive), VIRTUAL and ERECT (m positive), and THREE TIMES enlarged.**
>
> **Why it came out virtual: the object at 10 cm is CLOSER than the focus at 15 cm** — the one region where a concave mirror gives a virtual image. **This is exactly the shaving-mirror or make-up-mirror arrangement.**

---

A convex mirror of focal length 10 cm has an object 20 cm in front of it. Find the image.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = −20 cm      f = +10 cm       (convex → f positive)
>
> 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/10 + 1/20 = (2 + 1)/20 = 3/20
>
> v = 20/3 = +6.67 cm
>
> m = −v/u = −(6.67)/(−20) = +0.33
> ```
> **The image is 6.67 cm BEHIND the mirror, VIRTUAL, ERECT and DIMINISHED to about one third.**
>
> **A convex mirror gives this kind of image for EVERY object position** — the image always lies between P and F, however far away the object is. That constancy is what makes it useful for surveillance and for rear-view mirrors.

---

Where must an object be placed before a concave mirror so that the image is the same size as the object?

> [!ANSWER]
> **AT THE CENTRE OF CURVATURE, C — that is, at a distance of R (= 2f) from the mirror.**
>
> **The image also forms at C, is the SAME SIZE, and is REAL and INVERTED**, so m = −1.
>
> **Checking it with the formula, for f = −20 cm and u = −40 cm:**
> ```
> 1/v = −1/20 + 1/40 = (−2 + 1)/40 = −1/40
> v = −40 cm            m = −(−40)/(−40) = −1     ✔
> ```
> **Why this position is special: it is the only one where the object and image distances are EQUAL**, and equal distances on the two sides means equal size.

---

Give three everyday observations caused by refraction and explain one of them.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the bottom of a pond appears RAISED
> a pencil half in water appears DISPLACED at the surface
> letters under a thick glass slab appear RAISED
> a lemon in water looks BIGGER from the side
> a coin that had disappeared from view becomes visible when water is poured in
> ```
> **Taking the pencil: the light reaching your eye from the part UNDER water arrives from a DIFFERENT DIRECTION than the light from the part above**, because it bent on leaving the water.
>
> **Your brain assumes light travels in straight lines, so it places the submerged part along the direction the light was travelling when it arrived** — which is not where the pencil actually is. **The two halves therefore appear to be out of line.**

---

State the laws of refraction and say which way a ray bends in each case.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> LAW 1 — the incident ray, the refracted ray and the NORMAL at the point of
>         incidence all lie in the SAME PLANE
>
> LAW 2 — SNELL'S LAW:  sin i / sin r = a constant for a given pair of media
>                                     = the refractive index
> ```
> **Which way it bends:**
> ```
> RARER  → DENSER   the ray SLOWS DOWN and bends TOWARDS the normal
> DENSER → RARER    the ray SPEEDS UP  and bends AWAY FROM the normal
> ```
> **And a ray falling ALONG the normal does NOT bend at all** — there is no oblique crossing, so there is nothing to bend.

---

Why does a ray emerging from a rectangular glass slab come out parallel to the ray that entered?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because refraction happens TWICE, and the two bends are EQUAL AND OPPOSITE.**
> ```
> at the AIR → GLASS surface   the ray bends TOWARDS the normal
> at the GLASS → AIR surface   the ray bends AWAY FROM the normal, by the
>                              SAME amount
> ```
> **The two surfaces of a rectangular slab are PARALLEL**, so the second bend exactly undoes the first.
>
> **The direction is restored, but the POSITION is not — the ray emerges shifted sideways.** That sideways shift is called **LATERAL DISPLACEMENT**.

---

What does it mean to say the refractive index of water is 1.33? Find the speed of light in water.

> [!ANSWER]
> **It means light travels 1.33 times FASTER in air than in water** — the refractive index is simply a ratio of speeds.
> ```
> n = c/v
>
> v = c/n = (3 × 10⁸)/1.33 = 2.26 × 10⁸ m/s
> ```
> **So light slows from 3 × 10⁸ m/s in air to about 2.26 × 10⁸ m/s in water.**
>
> **And this slowing is the CAUSE of the bending, not a separate fact.** A ray crossing obliquely has one side entering the slower medium before the other, which swings its direction round.

---

Find the speed of light in diamond, given n = 2.42. What does the high value tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> v = c/n = (3 × 10⁸)/2.42 = 1.24 × 10⁸ m/s
> ```
> **Light travels in diamond at less than HALF its speed in air** — the largest slowing in the table.
>
> **What follows: diamond bends light very strongly**, since a large refractive index means a large change of direction at each surface.
>
> **That is why a cut diamond sparkles.** Light entering it is bent sharply, bounces about inside, and emerges in flashes — an optical consequence of one number.

---

Is an optically denser medium always denser in mass? Justify with an example.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No. OPTICAL density and MASS density are different things.**
>
> **OPTICALLY DENSER simply means a LARGER REFRACTIVE INDEX — and therefore SLOWER light.**
> ```
> KEROSENE  n = 1.44     optically DENSER
> WATER     n = 1.33     optically RARER
> ```
> **Yet kerosene FLOATS on water**, so its **mass** density is lower.
>
> **So the two properties can point in opposite directions**, and the words must be kept apart.
>
> **Throughout this chapter, "denser medium" always means OPTICALLY denser** — it is a statement about the speed of light, not about weight.

Distinguish convex and concave lenses, and state the special property of the optical centre.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> CONVEX lens  — thicker in the MIDDLE, thinner at the edges — CONVERGING
> CONCAVE lens — thicker at the EDGES, thinner in the middle — DIVERGING
> ```
> **A lens has TWO centres of curvature, C1 and C2, because it has two spherical surfaces. The line through them is the PRINCIPAL AXIS, and the central point of the lens is the OPTICAL CENTRE, O.**
>
> **The special property: a ray passing through the OPTICAL CENTRE emerges WITHOUT ANY DEVIATION.**
>
> **Why that matters: it gives you a ray whose path you know without any construction at all** — which is why it is one of the three ray rules for lenses.

---

An object is placed 30 cm from a convex lens of focal length 20 cm. Find the image position, magnification and nature.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = −30 cm      f = +20 cm       (convex → f positive)
>
> 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/20 − 1/30 = (3 − 2)/60 = 1/60
>
> v = +60 cm
>
> m = v/u = 60/(−30) = −2
> ```
> **The image is 60 cm on the OTHER side of the lens, REAL, INVERTED and TWICE enlarged.**
>
> **Check against the table: the object at 30 cm lies between F (20 cm) and 2F (40 cm), so the image should be BEYOND 2F, enlarged, real and inverted** — and 60 cm is beyond 40 cm ✔

---

An object is 30 cm from a concave lens of focal length 15 cm. Find the image.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = −30 cm      f = −15 cm       (concave → f NEGATIVE)
>
> 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = −1/15 − 1/30 = (−2 − 1)/30 = −1/10
>
> v = −10 cm
>
> m = v/u = (−10)/(−30) = +1/3
> ```
> **The image is 10 cm from the lens on the SAME side as the object, VIRTUAL, ERECT and DIMINISHED to one third.**
>
> **A concave lens gives this kind of image for EVERY object position** — always virtual, erect, diminished, and always between the focus and the optical centre. **There is no arrangement that makes a concave lens produce a real image.**

---

Write the lens formula and lens magnification, and contrast them carefully with the mirror equations.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> MIRROR    1/v + 1/u = 1/f          m = −v/u
> LENS      1/v − 1/u = 1/f          m = +v/u
> ```
> **Two differences, both easy to get wrong:**
> - **the lens formula has a MINUS sign between the terms, the mirror formula a PLUS**
> - **the lens magnification has NO minus sign, the mirror magnification does**
>
> **This is the single commonest source of wrong answers in the chapter.**
>
> **A safeguard: after every calculation, check the answer against the TABLE.** If your convex lens with the object beyond 2F comes out enlarged, you have used the wrong formula.

---

Define the power of a lens, give its unit, and explain why power is the reciprocal of focal length.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> P = 1/f         with f in METRES
>
> SI unit: the DIOPTRE (D).      1 D = 1 m⁻¹
> 1 dioptre is the power of a lens of focal length 1 metre.
>
> CONVEX  → POSITIVE power
> CONCAVE → NEGATIVE power
> ```
> **Why the reciprocal: POWER measures how STRONGLY a lens bends light.**
>
> **A lens with a SHORT focal length bends rays through a LARGE angle**, bringing them to a focus close to the optical centre. **A lens with a long focal length bends them only gently.**
>
> **So stronger bending goes with a shorter focus — power must RISE as f FALLS**, which is exactly what a reciprocal does.

---

Find the power of a convex lens of focal length 25 cm and of a concave lens of focal length 50 cm. If they are placed in contact, what is the combination?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> CONVEX    f = +25 cm = +0.25 m    →  P1 = 1/0.25 = +4 D
> CONCAVE   f = −50 cm = −0.50 m    →  P2 = 1/(−0.50) = −2 D
>
> IN CONTACT   P = P1 + P2 = +4 − 2 = +2 D
>
> so  f = 1/P = 1/2 = +0.5 m = +50 cm
> ```
> **The combination behaves as a CONVEX lens of focal length 50 cm**, since the total power is positive.
>
> **Note the mistake this prevents: you cannot add the focal lengths.** 25 cm and −50 cm do not combine to 50 cm in any direct way — **only the POWERS add.**

---

Why do opticians prescribe lenses by power rather than by focal length?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because POWERS SIMPLY ADD.**
> ```
> P = P1 + P2 + P3 + …      for lenses placed in contact
> ```
> **During an eye test, the optician slots several lenses of known power into the testing frame and finds the required total by ORDINARY ADDITION.**
>
> **With focal lengths it would be far messier** — you would have to combine reciprocals every single time.
>
> **And the sign carries information too: a prescription of +2.0 D immediately tells you the lens is CONVEX with f = +0.50 m; −2.5 D tells you it is CONCAVE with f = −0.40 m.**

---

A lens forms an erect, magnified image of an object placed close to it. Which lens is it, and where is the object?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It is a CONVEX lens, with the object BETWEEN the focus F1 and the optical centre O.**
>
> **Why it cannot be concave: a concave lens ALWAYS gives a diminished image**, whatever the object position — it can never magnify.
>
> **Why the object must be inside the focus: for every other position a convex lens gives a REAL, INVERTED image.** Only inside the focus does it turn virtual and erect.
>
> **This is exactly the MAGNIFYING GLASS arrangement** — which is why you must hold a magnifying glass close to the page for it to work.

---

A concave mirror and a convex lens can both magnify. Compare the two arrangements.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | Concave mirror | Convex lens |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Magnified, virtual, erect image when** | object between **P and F** | object between **F1 and O** |
> | **Image forms** | **behind** the mirror | on the **same side** as the object |
> | **Works by** | **reflection** | **refraction** |
> | **Magnified real image when** | object between **C and F** | object between **F1 and 2F1** |
>
> **The deep similarity: both give a virtual, erect, enlarged image ONLY when the object is closer than the focus.**
>
> **Why that is not a coincidence: in both cases, bringing the object inside the focus makes the outgoing rays DIVERGE instead of converging** — and diverging rays can only produce a virtual image.

---

Why can a virtual image never be caught on a screen?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because NO LIGHT ACTUALLY ARRIVES at the position of a virtual image.**
>
> **The rays leaving the mirror or lens DIVERGE — they never really meet.** They only *appear* to come from a common point when you extend them **backwards**.
>
> **A screen works by intercepting real light and scattering it to your eye. Where there is no light, there is nothing to intercept.**
>
> **A real image is the opposite: the rays genuinely converge and cross at a point, so a screen placed there catches actual light** — which is exactly how a cinema screen works.
