You have three test tubes — distilled water, an acid and a base — and only RED litmus paper. How do you identify all three?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Step 1: dip the red paper in each.** Only one turns it **blue** — that is the **BASE**.
>
> **Step 2: take that blue strip and touch it with the other two solutions.**
> - the one that turns it **back to red** is the **ACID**
> - the one that leaves it **blue** is **distilled water**
>
> **Why this works: an indicator changes colour in BOTH directions.** A strip already turned blue is now, in effect, blue litmus paper — **so one piece of red litmus gives you both tests.**

Complete the colour changes: litmus, methyl orange, phenolphthalein and turmeric, in acid and in base.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Indicator | ACID | BASE |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Litmus** (purple when neutral) | **red** | **blue** |
> | **Methyl orange** | **red** | **yellow** |
> | **Phenolphthalein** | **colourless** | **pink** |
> | **Turmeric** | stays yellow | **red** |
>
> **Litmus is a natural dye extracted from LICHEN.** Red cabbage, turmeric and the petals of Hydrangea, Petunia and Geranium act as indicators too.

---

What is an olfactory indicator? Give two, and say why they are useful.

> [!ANSWER]
> **An OLFACTORY INDICATOR changes its SMELL, not its colour, in acid or base.**
>
> **Examples: onion, vanilla essence and clove oil — all of them LOSE their characteristic smell in a base.**
>
> **Why it is worth having them: a visually impaired student cannot use a colour change**, but can use a smell change perfectly well. **The same chemistry, reported through a different sense.**

---

Which gas is released when an acid reacts with a metal? Write an example and describe the test.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas
>
> Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2
> ```
> **The gas is HYDROGEN.** The metal **displaces** hydrogen from the acid.
>
> **The test: pass the gas through soap solution and bring a burning candle to a gas-filled bubble.** It burns with a **squeaky POP**.
>
> **Why the bubbles form at all: the gas is collected inside soap films**, which makes an invisible gas easy to see and to test.

---

Why should curd and other sour foods not be kept in brass or copper vessels?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because sour food contains ACID — curd has lactic acid — and acid reacts with the metal of the vessel.**
>
> **The reaction produces salts of copper and zinc, which are TOXIC**, and it also spoils the food's taste.
>
> **The general rule behind it: an acid attacks a metal.** Steel or glass containers are safe because they resist that attack.

---

Metal compound A reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid producing effervescence, and the gas evolved puts out a burning candle. One of the products is calcium chloride. Identify A and write the balanced equation.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A is CALCIUM CARBONATE, CaCO3.**
> ```
> CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
> ```
> **How to reason it out:**
> - **effervescence** = a gas coming off
> - **the gas puts out a flame** — so it is **carbon dioxide**, which does not support burning
> - **the salt is calcium chloride**, so the metal in A is calcium
> - **a compound giving CO2 with an acid must be a carbonate or hydrogencarbonate** — so A is calcium carbonate

---

Why does lime water turn milky with carbon dioxide, and why does the milkiness disappear if excess gas is passed?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)
> (lime water)           WHITE precipitate = the milkiness
> ```
> **The milkiness is a fine suspension of INSOLUBLE calcium carbonate.**
>
> **With EXCESS carbon dioxide it goes clear again:**
> ```
> CaCO3(s) + H2O(l) + CO2(g) → Ca(HCO3)2(aq)
>                              SOLUBLE
> ```
> **Why: the insoluble carbonate is converted into soluble calcium HYDROGENcarbonate**, which dissolves away.
>
> **Practical consequence: the test must be done with a limited amount of gas** — keep bubbling and you will undo your own result.

---

Write the neutralisation reaction and describe what phenolphthalein shows during it.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Base + Acid → Salt + Water
>
> NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)
> ```
> **What you see: NaOH with phenolphthalein is PINK. Add HCl drop by drop and the pink DISAPPEARS. Add NaOH again and the pink RETURNS.**
>
> **Why: the indicator is reporting which of the two is in excess.** Pink means the base is winning; colourless means it has been cancelled by the acid. **The colour is a live readout of the neutralisation.**

---

Why are metal oxides called basic and non-metal oxides called acidic?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of how they BEHAVE, not because of a rule.**
> ```
> METAL oxide + acid → salt + water
>    CuO + 2HCl → CuCl2 + H2O          the solution turns blue-green
> ```
> **A base does exactly this with an acid — so a metal oxide is BASIC.**
> ```
> NON-METAL oxide + base → salt + water
>    CO2 + Ca(OH)2 → CaCO3 + H2O
> ```
> **An acid does exactly this with a base — so a non-metal oxide is ACIDIC.**
>
> **The classification is earned by the reaction, which is why you never have to memorise it.**

---

Glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen but are not acids. Describe an experiment that proves it, and explain the result.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The bulb experiment.** Fix two nails in a cork, stand it in a beaker, and connect them through a bulb and switch to a 6 V battery.
> ```
> dilute HCl, dilute H2SO4  →  the bulb GLOWS
> glucose, alcohol          →  the bulb does NOT glow
> ```
> **Why: a solution conducts electricity only if it contains free IONS.**
>
> **Acids conduct because they release H⁺(aq) ions in water.** Glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen atoms, **but do not release them as ions** — so no current flows.
>
> **The conclusion: an acid is not "a substance containing hydrogen" — it is a substance that produces H⁺(aq) ions in water.**

---

Why does dry HCl gas not turn dry litmus paper red?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because acidity requires H⁺ ions, and HCl cannot release them without water.**
> ```
> HCl + H2O → H3O⁺ + Cl⁻
> ```
> **In the dry gas the H and the Cl are still bonded together** — no ions exist, so there is nothing for the litmus to detect.
>
> **Moisten the litmus and the same gas turns it red at once**, because the water on the paper is enough to let the ionisation happen.
>
> **Water is not a bystander in acid chemistry; it is a participant.**

---

Why must H⁺ always be written as H⁺(aq) or H3O⁺?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a bare H⁺ ion is a naked proton and cannot exist alone in solution.**
> ```
> H⁺ + H2O → H3O⁺        the HYDRONIUM ion
> ```
> **It immediately attaches itself to a water molecule.**
>
> **So writing a lonely H⁺ describes something that isn't there.** The notation H⁺(aq) or H3O⁺ records the truth: the proton is always carried by water.

---

While diluting a concentrated acid, why must the acid be added to water and never the reverse?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because dissolving a concentrated acid in water is HIGHLY EXOTHERMIC — a great deal of heat is released.**
>
> **Adding acid slowly to a large volume of water** spreads that heat through all the water, which warms up gently.
>
> **Adding a little water to concentrated acid** releases the same heat into a tiny volume. The result:
> - **the mixture can boil and SPLASH OUT, causing burns**
> - **the glass container may CRACK from the sudden local heating**
>
> **This is the one rule in the chapter that can genuinely injure you** — which is why concentrated acid bottles carry a warning sign.

---

How is the concentration of H3O⁺ ions affected when an acid is diluted? And of OH⁻ when more NaOH is dissolved?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **On DILUTING an acid, the concentration of H3O⁺ DECREASES.** The same number of ions is spread through more water, so there are fewer per unit volume.
> - **On dissolving MORE NaOH, the concentration of OH⁻ INCREASES**, since more base means more hydroxide ions released.
>
> **Note carefully what dilution does NOT do: it does not destroy the acid.** The ions are only spread out — which is why the pH moves towards 7 but never past it.

---

What does pH measure? Draw the scale and state which way it runs.

> [!ANSWER]
> **pH measures the HYDROGEN ION CONCENTRATION of a solution.** The **p** comes from the German *potenz*, meaning **power**.
> ```
> 0 ------------- 7 ------------- 14
> very acidic   NEUTRAL     very alkaline
>
> pH below 7   acidic   — more H⁺
> pH exactly 7 neutral
> pH above 7   basic    — more OH⁻
> ```
> **THE SCALE RUNS BACKWARDS: the MORE hydrogen ions there are, the LOWER the pH.**
>
> **This reversal is the commonest mistake in the chapter** — a pH of 1 is far more acidic than a pH of 6.

---

Solution A has pH 6 and solution B has pH 8. Which has more hydrogen ions, and what is the nature of each?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A has more hydrogen ions.**
> ```
> A: pH 6 — below 7 — ACIDIC
> B: pH 8 — above 7 — BASIC
> ```
> **Why A wins on hydrogen ions: lower pH means higher H⁺ concentration.** The scale runs the other way round from the thing it measures.
>
> **Note that neither is extreme** — both are close to 7, so A is only mildly acidic and B only mildly basic.

---

Do basic solutions contain H⁺ ions? If so, why are they basic?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — a basic solution still contains SOME H⁺ ions.**
>
> **It is basic because the OH⁻ ions vastly OUTNUMBER them**, not because the H⁺ ions have vanished.
>
> **The distinction matters:** if H⁺ disappeared entirely, pH would have no meaning above 7, and the scale would stop at neutral. **It is the balance between H⁺ and OH⁻ that decides the nature, not the presence of one alone.**

---

Distinguish between a strong acid and a concentrated acid.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **STRENGTH is about how much of the acid IONISES.** A strong acid releases many H⁺ ions; a weak acid releases few.
> - **CONCENTRATION is about how much acid is DISSOLVED** in a given volume of water.
>
> **These are independent.** Take one molar HCl and one molar acetic acid — **same concentration**, but HCl releases far more H⁺, so HCl is **strong** and acetic acid **weak**.
>
> **So you can have a dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid.** Confusing the two words is a classic exam trap.

---

A solution turns red litmus blue. Its pH is likely to be 1, 4, 5 or 10?

> [!ANSWER]
> **10.**
>
> **Turning RED litmus BLUE means the solution is BASIC**, and a basic solution has **pH above 7**. Of the four options, only 10 qualifies.
>
> **The other three (1, 4, 5) are all below 7 and therefore acidic** — they would leave red litmus red and turn blue litmus red.

---

What is acid rain, and why does it matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **ACID RAIN is rainwater with a pH BELOW 5.6.**
>
> **Why it matters: when it flows into rivers it lowers the pH of the river water**, and aquatic life then struggles to survive.
>
> **The underlying reason: living organisms function only within a NARROW pH range** — the human body works between about 7.0 and 7.8. **A shift of even one unit is a large change in hydrogen-ion concentration**, so small-sounding pH drops are biologically serious.

---

Why does the stomach make acid, what goes wrong in indigestion, and how does an antacid help?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The stomach produces HYDROCHLORIC ACID, which helps digest food without harming the stomach itself.**
>
> **In indigestion, too much acid is produced**, causing pain and irritation.
>
> **An ANTACID is a mild BASE that NEUTRALISES the excess acid.** Magnesium hydroxide — milk of magnesia — is commonly used.
> ```
> excess H⁺ + OH⁻ from the antacid → H2O
> ```
> **Why a MILD base: a strong one would over-correct** and damage the stomach lining. The aim is to cancel the excess, not to make the stomach alkaline.

---

Explain tooth decay in terms of pH, and why brushing after meals prevents it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Tooth decay begins when the pH of the mouth falls BELOW 5.5.**
>
> **Tooth enamel is calcium hydroxyapatite — a crystalline calcium phosphate and the hardest substance in the body.** It does not dissolve in water, **but it is corroded below pH 5.5**.
>
> **Where the acid comes from: bacteria in the mouth break down sugar and food particles left after eating, producing acid.**
>
> **So the prevention follows directly:**
> - **clean the mouth after eating** — remove the food the bacteria feed on
> - **use toothpaste, which is generally BASIC** — it neutralises the acid already made
>
> **Both attack the same cause: acid sitting on the enamel.**

---

A bee sting and a nettle sting both hurt. What relieves each, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A BEE STING leaves an ACID under the skin.** Rubbing on **baking soda** — a mild base — **neutralises it** and relieves the pain.
>
> **A NETTLE sting injects METHANOIC ACID** from its stinging hairs. The traditional remedy is to rub the area with a **DOCK LEAF**, which grows beside nettles in the wild.
>
> **What the dock leaf must be: BASIC** — that is the only way it could relieve an acid sting.
>
> **The chemistry is the same in both cases: neutralisation.** Once you know the sting is acidic, you know the remedy must be a mild base.

---

Under what soil condition would a farmer add quick lime, slaked lime or chalk to a field?

> [!ANSWER]
> **When the soil is too ACIDIC.**
>
> **All three — quick lime CaO, slaked lime Ca(OH)2 and chalk CaCO3 — are BASIC**, so they neutralise the excess acid and raise the soil's pH.
>
> **Why it is worth doing: plants grow well only within a specific pH range.** Soil that is too acidic will not support a healthy crop no matter how well it is watered or fertilised.

---

Ten mL of a NaOH solution is exactly neutralised by 8 mL of an HCl solution. How much of the same HCl will neutralise 20 mL of the same NaOH?

> [!ANSWER]
> **16 mL.**
>
> **Why: neutralisation happens in a fixed ratio.**
> ```
> 10 mL NaOH  needs   8 mL HCl
> 20 mL NaOH  is TWICE as much base
>             so it needs TWICE the acid  =  16 mL
> ```
> **The reason the ratio is fixed: every OH⁻ ion needs exactly one H⁺ ion.**
> ```
> H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l)
> ```
> **Doubling the hydroxide ions doubles the hydrogen ions required.**

---

Predict whether NaCl, NH4Cl and Na2CO3 give a neutral, acidic or basic solution, and explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> NaCl     from HCl (strong)     + NaOH (strong)     →  NEUTRAL, pH 7
> NH4Cl    from HCl (strong)     + NH4OH (weak)      →  ACIDIC, pH below 7
> Na2CO3   from carbonic acid (weak) + NaOH (strong) →  BASIC, pH above 7
> ```
> **The rule: THE STRONGER PARENT WINS.**
>
> **Why: in a salt of a strong base and a weak acid, the base's character survives** — the weak acid does not hold its ground, so the solution ends up basic. **When both parents are equally strong they cancel, and the salt is neutral.**

---

Describe the chlor-alkali process and name all three products with a use for each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl2(g) + H2(g)
> ```
> **Electricity is passed through BRINE — concentrated sodium chloride solution.**
> ```
> CHLORINE   Cl2   at the ANODE     → bleaching powder, PVC, disinfectants
> HYDROGEN   H2    at the CATHODE   → fuels, margarine, ammonia
> SODIUM HYDROXIDE near the cathode → soaps, detergents, paper
> ```
> **The name explains itself: "chlor" for chlorine and "alkali" for sodium hydroxide.**
>
> **Why the process is worth running: ALL THREE products are valuable** — nothing is wasted, which is what makes it economic at industrial scale.

---

How is bleaching powder made, and what are its three main uses?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2Ca(OH)2 + 2Cl2 → Ca(ClO)2 + CaCl2 + 2H2O
> ```
> **Chlorine gas is passed over DRY SLAKED LIME.** The chlorine itself comes from the chlor-alkali process, so bleaching powder is ultimately made from common salt.
>
> **Uses:**
> - **bleaching** cotton and linen in textiles, wood pulp in paper mills, and washed clothes in a laundry
> - **as an OXIDISING AGENT** in chemical industries
> - **disinfecting drinking water** — making it free of germs
>
> **The lime must be DRY** — that is why the equation shows water as a product rather than a reactant.

---

Why does a cake rise when baking powder is used? Why is tartaric acid included?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Baking POWDER = baking soda (NaHCO3) + a mild edible acid (tartaric acid).**
> ```
> NaHCO3 + H⁺ → CO2 + H2O + sodium salt of the acid
> ```
> **The CARBON DIOXIDE released gets trapped in the dough as bubbles, and those bubbles make the cake soft and spongy.**
>
> **Why the acid is needed: baking soda ALONE leaves sodium carbonate behind on heating, which tastes bitter.**
> ```
>             Heat
> 2NaHCO3 ----------→ Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2
> ```
> **The tartaric acid reacts with that sodium carbonate and removes the bitterness** — so the acid is there for taste, not for the rising.

---

Give the chemical name and two uses of washing soda, and say what makes it different from sodium carbonate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Washing soda is HYDRATED SODIUM CARBONATE, Na2CO3·10H2O**, made by recrystallising sodium carbonate.
> ```
> Na2CO3 + 10H2O → Na2CO3·10H2O
> ```
> **Uses:** glass, soap and paper industries; making sodium compounds such as borax; a domestic cleaning agent; and **removing PERMANENT HARDNESS of water.**
>
> **The difference from plain Na2CO3 is the ten water molecules of crystallisation** built into the crystal — the salt is chemically the same, but hydrated.

---

Blue copper sulphate crystals turn white on heating and blue again on adding water. Explain, and define water of crystallisation.

> [!ANSWER]
> **WATER OF CRYSTALLISATION is the FIXED number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt.**
> ```
> CuSO4·5H2O   hydrated copper sulphate — BLUE
>      heated  →  CuSO4 (white) + water driven off as droplets
>      + water →  CuSO4·5H2O (blue again)
> ```
> **The crystals are NOT wet.** The water is built into the crystal structure in a fixed ratio — which is exactly why the formula can state a precise number, 5.
>
> **The blue colour belongs to the HYDRATED form**, so it disappears when the water is removed and returns when it is put back. **The colour is a direct visual signal of whether the water is present.**

---

What is Plaster of Paris? Explain the formula CaSO4·½H2O and give its main use.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>                373 K
> CaSO4·2H2O ----------→ CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O
> (gypsum)               (Plaster of Paris)
>
> and adding water reverses it:
> CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O    sets into a hard solid mass
> ```
> **How half a water molecule is possible: it isn't.** The formula means **TWO formula units of CaSO4 SHARE ONE molecule of water**, which averages to a half per unit.
>
> **Main use: doctors' plaster casts, to hold fractured bones in position.** Also for toys, decorative material and smoothing surfaces.
>
> **Why it works as a cast: adding water turns the powder back into gypsum, which sets hard** around the limb.

---

Why must Plaster of Paris be stored in a moisture-proof container?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because it reacts with water — and moisture from the air is enough.**
> ```
> CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O    (hard gypsum)
> ```
> **Left in a damp place it will absorb moisture and set into a solid lump inside its own bag**, long before anyone tries to use it.
>
> **The very property that makes it useful — setting hard with water — is what makes it fragile in storage.** It must meet water when you choose, not before.
