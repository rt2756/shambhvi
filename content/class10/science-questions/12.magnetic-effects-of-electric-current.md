What is a magnetic field, and how would you draw a field line using a compass?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A MAGNETIC FIELD is the region around a magnet in which the magnet's force can be detected.** It has both **direction and magnitude**.
>
> **Drawing a field line with a compass:**
> ```
> 1. Place the compass near the magnet's north pole and MARK BOTH ENDS
>    of the needle on the paper.
> 2. MOVE the needle so that its SOUTH pole now occupies the place its
>    NORTH pole just left.
> 3. Mark the ends again, and repeat, step by step, until you reach the
>    magnet's south pole.
> 4. Join all the marks with a SMOOTH CURVE — that curve is a field line.
> ```
> **Why the needle must be moved that particular way: each step continues the line from exactly where the last one ended**, so no gaps or overlaps creep in.

---

List the properties of magnetic field lines, with a reason for each.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. A field line is the path along which a hypothetical free NORTH POLE would move.** Its direction at any point is the direction a compass needle's north pole takes there.
>
> **2. OUTSIDE the magnet they run from NORTH to SOUTH; INSIDE, from SOUTH to NORTH — so they are CLOSED CURVES.** A field line never simply stops in mid-air.
>
> **3. Where the lines are CROWDED, the field is STRONGER.** You can check this directly: the compass deflection increases as the needle is moved towards the poles, which is exactly where the lines bunch up.
>
> **4. NO TWO FIELD LINES EVER CROSS.**
>
> **These are not four separate facts to memorise — the first one generates the rest.** Once a line is defined as the path a north pole would take, everything else follows from the fact that a north pole has exactly one direction to move in at each point.

---

Why can two magnetic field lines never intersect?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because if they did, then at the point of intersection a compass needle would have to point in TWO DIRECTIONS AT ONCE — which is impossible.**
>
> **The direction of the field at any point is defined as the direction a north pole would move there.** A north pole placed at a crossing would have to move along both lines simultaneously.
>
> **So the field has ONE definite direction at every point**, and a diagram showing crossing lines is simply wrong.
>
> **This same argument works for electric field lines and for streamlines in a fluid** — anywhere a direction is defined point by point.

---

What happens to a compass needle placed near a current-carrying wire, and what does the observation prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The needle DEFLECTS — which proves that A CURRENT-CARRYING WIRE HAS A MAGNETIC FIELD AROUND IT.**
> ```
> current flowing NORTH to SOUTH  → the needle's north pole moves EAST
> REVERSE the current             → the needle moves WEST instead
> ```
> **What the reversal tells us: REVERSING THE CURRENT REVERSES THE FIELD.**
>
> **So the field's direction is tied to the current's direction** — the current is not merely creating "some magnetism", it is creating a field with a definite orientation determined by the way it flows.
>
> **This observation, made by Oersted, is the foundation of the entire chapter** — it is the first link ever found between electricity and magnetism.

---

State the right-hand thumb rule and describe the field around a straight current-carrying wire.

> [!ANSWER]
> **RIGHT-HAND THUMB RULE: hold the wire in your RIGHT hand with the THUMB pointing along the DIRECTION OF THE CURRENT. Your FINGERS then wrap around the wire in the direction of the MAGNETIC FIELD LINES.**
>
> **The field lines form a series of CONCENTRIC CIRCLES** in a plane perpendicular to the wire.
>
> **The field is STRONGER near the wire and WEAKER further away** — the circles get larger and the lines more spread out, which by property 3 means a weaker field.
>
> **It is also called MAXWELL'S CORKSCREW RULE:** imagine driving a corkscrew in the direction of the current — **the direction it TURNS is the direction of the field.**

---

A current in a horizontal power line flows from east to west. What is the direction of the magnetic field directly above and directly below it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Apply the right-hand thumb rule with the thumb pointing WEST (the direction of the current).**
>
> **The field circles the wire in a plane perpendicular to it:**
> ```
> viewed from the EAST end  → the field turns CLOCKWISE
> viewed from the WEST end  → the field turns ANTI-CLOCKWISE
> ```
> **Both above and below the wire, the field lies in that circling plane.**
>
> **Why the answer depends on where you stand to look: a circle has no absolute "clockwise".** You must state the viewing direction for the answer to mean anything — which is exactly why the textbook phrases it that way.

---

Why does the magnetic field at the centre of a circular loop appear as straight lines?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of how the concentric circles grow.**
>
> **At every point of the loop, the field lines are concentric circles around that bit of wire.** As you move away from the wire, **those circles become larger and larger.**
>
> **By the time you reach the CENTRE of the loop, the arcs of these very large circles appear as STRAIGHT LINES** — a big enough circle looks flat over a small stretch.
>
> **And applying the right-hand rule to each section shows that EVERY part of the loop contributes at the centre IN THE SAME DIRECTION** — so the contributions add up instead of cancelling.

---

Why does a coil of n turns produce a field n times as strong as a single turn?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because THE CURRENT IN EACH TURN FLOWS IN THE SAME DIRECTION, so the field due to each turn simply ADDS to the rest.**
>
> **The field of a single turn is proportional to the current through it.** With n turns stacked together, the same current passes n times through nearly the same place, and the fields superpose.
>
> **The practical importance is large: this is how a strong magnetic field is built from a modest current.**
>
> **Adding turns is cheap; increasing the current is not** — a bigger current would need a bigger supply and would heat the wire as I². Winding more turns achieves the same field for nothing.

---

What is a solenoid? Describe its magnetic field.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A SOLENOID is a coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire, wound closely in the shape of a CYLINDER.**
>
> **Its field pattern is SIMILAR TO THAT OF A BAR MAGNET — one end behaves as a north pole, the other as a south pole.**
>
> **Inside the solenoid the field lines are PARALLEL STRAIGHT LINES**, which means the field there is **THE SAME AT ALL POINTS — a UNIFORM field.**
>
> **How to read a field diagram: evenly spaced PARALLEL lines always mean a UNIFORM field** — same direction (they are parallel) and same strength (they are equally spaced).

---

What is an electromagnet, and why is soft iron used for its core rather than steel?

> [!ANSWER]
> **An ELECTROMAGNET consists of a core of SOFT IRON wrapped around with a coil of insulated copper wire.** The strong uniform field inside the solenoid magnetises the core.
>
> **Why SOFT IRON: it magnetises strongly when the current flows, but LOSES its magnetism as soon as the current stops.**
>
> **That is precisely what an electromagnet is for — magnetism you can SWITCH ON AND OFF.**
>
> **Steel would be useless here: it would retain its magnetism after the current was cut**, so the crane could pick up scrap but never put it down. **The choice of material is dictated by the requirement to switch off.**

---

The magnetic field inside a long current-carrying solenoid is (a) zero, (b) decreasing towards its end, (c) increasing towards its end, (d) the same at all points. Justify your choice.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(d) — THE SAME AT ALL POINTS.**
>
> **The evidence is in the field lines: inside the solenoid they are PARALLEL STRAIGHT LINES, evenly spaced.**
> - **PARALLEL means the DIRECTION is the same everywhere**
> - **EVENLY SPACED means the STRENGTH is the same everywhere**, since crowding indicates strength
>
> **Together those two facts define a UNIFORM field.**
>
> **Why the other options are wrong: (a) is contradicted by the field lines being there at all, and (b) and (c) would require the spacing to change along the length — which it does not, inside a long solenoid.**

Describe the experiment showing that a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1. Suspend a small aluminium rod AB horizontally from a stand using
>    two connecting wires.
> 2. Place a strong HORSESHOE MAGNET so the rod lies between its poles,
>    with the field directed upwards.
> 3. Connect the rod in series with a battery, a key and a rheostat.
> 4. Pass current from B to A → the rod is DISPLACED to the LEFT.
> 5. REVERSE the current → it is displaced to the RIGHT.
> 6. REVERSE the field, by swapping the poles → the direction reverses again.
> ```
> **Conclusion: the direction of the force depends on BOTH the direction of the CURRENT and the direction of the FIELD.**
>
> **And the force is LARGEST when the current is at RIGHT ANGLES to the field**, acting perpendicular to both.
>
> **The idea behind the experiment, due to Ampère: since a current exerts a force on a magnet, THE MAGNET MUST EXERT AN EQUAL AND OPPOSITE FORCE ON THE CURRENT.**

---

State Fleming's left-hand rule and explain when to use it rather than the right-hand thumb rule.

> [!ANSWER]
> **FLEMING'S LEFT-HAND RULE: stretch the THUMB, FOREFINGER and MIDDLE FINGER of your LEFT hand mutually perpendicular.**
> ```
> FOREFINGER            → direction of the magnetic FIELD
> MIDDLE (second) finger → direction of the CURRENT
> THUMB                 → direction of the FORCE (the conductor's motion)
> ```
> **A memory hook: Fore = Field, Centre = Current, Thumb = Thrust.**
>
> **When to use which — they answer different questions:**
> ```
> RIGHT-HAND THUMB RULE — what FIELD does a current PRODUCE?
> FLEMING'S LEFT-HAND RULE — what FORCE does a field EXERT on a current?
> ```
> **Getting the hand wrong reverses your answer**, so it is worth pausing to ask which question you are being asked.

---

How will the displacement of the rod in that experiment change if (i) the current is increased, (ii) a stronger magnet is used, (iii) the rod is longer?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The displacement INCREASES in ALL THREE cases.**
>
> - **(i) A larger CURRENT means more moving charge in the field, so a greater force.**
> - **(ii) A STRONGER MAGNET means a stronger field, so each bit of current is pushed harder.**
> - **(iii) A LONGER ROD means more of the conductor lies within the field, so more of it is being pushed.**
>
> **The pattern: the force grows with the current, with the field strength, and with the length of conductor in the field.**
>
> **All three are ways of putting "more current-carrying wire into more field"** — which is why they all work in the same direction.

---

An electron enters a magnetic field at right angles to it. What must you remember before applying Fleming's left-hand rule?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That THE DIRECTION OF CURRENT IS OPPOSITE TO THE DIRECTION OF ELECTRON MOTION.**
>
> **Fleming's rule is stated in terms of CURRENT, not in terms of electron flow.** An electron moving to the right constitutes a current to the **left**.
>
> **So the procedure is:**
> ```
> 1. Note the direction the ELECTRON is moving.
> 2. REVERSE it to get the direction of the CURRENT.
> 3. Point the middle finger along THAT direction, the forefinger along
>    the field, and read off the force from the thumb.
> ```
> **Forgetting step 2 gives an answer that is exactly backwards** — and it is the single commonest error in questions about charged particles.

---

A positively charged particle moving west is deflected north by a magnetic field. In which direction is the field?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The field is directed DOWNWARD.**
>
> **The reasoning, using Fleming's left-hand rule:**
> ```
> the particle is POSITIVE, so the CURRENT is in its direction of motion: WEST
>    → middle finger points WEST
> the FORCE is NORTH
>    → thumb points NORTH
> the FOREFINGER must then point DOWNWARD — that is the field
> ```
> **Because the particle is positive, no reversal is needed** — current and motion coincide. **Had it been an electron, the current would have been east and the answer would flip.**
>
> **The habit worth forming: set the two directions you KNOW on the correct fingers first, then read off the third.**

---

Which properties of a proton can change as it moves freely in a magnetic field: mass, speed, velocity, momentum?

> [!ANSWER]
> **VELOCITY and MOMENTUM can change. MASS and SPEED cannot.**
>
> **Why velocity changes: the magnetic force is always PERPENDICULAR to the motion**, so it continually changes the **direction** of travel — and velocity includes direction.
>
> **Why momentum changes: momentum is mass × VELOCITY**, so a change of direction changes the momentum even if the mass does not alter.
>
> **Why SPEED does not change: a force perpendicular to the motion does NO WORK on the particle.** It steers without pushing forwards or backwards, so the kinetic energy — and hence the speed — stays the same.
>
> **Why MASS does not change: nothing in a magnetic interaction adds or removes matter.**

Name four devices that use current-carrying conductors in magnetic fields.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ELECTRIC MOTOR · ELECTRIC GENERATOR · LOUDSPEAKER · MICROPHONE
> and MEASURING INSTRUMENTS
> ```
> **They all rest on the same physics of this chapter: a current in a magnetic field feels a force.**
>
> **The motor and the loudspeaker turn that force into MOTION** — the loudspeaker vibrating a cone to make sound.
>
> **The generator and the microphone run the idea the other way**, using motion in a field to produce a current.
>
> **So a loudspeaker and a microphone are essentially the same device operated in opposite directions.**

Name the three wires in a domestic supply, their colours, and the voltage between live and neutral.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> LIVE wire     — RED insulation      (also called positive)
> NEUTRAL wire  — BLACK insulation    (also called negative)
> EARTH wire    — GREEN insulation
> ```
> **The potential difference between LIVE and NEUTRAL is 220 V in India, at a frequency of 50 Hz.**
>
> **The EARTH wire is connected to a metal plate buried deep in the ground near the house.**
>
> **Why the colours are standardised: an electrician must be able to identify a wire at a glance, with the power off and no way to test.** A colour code is a safety measure in its own right.

---

Why are two separate circuits with different ratings used in a house?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 15 A circuit — for HIGH-POWER appliances: geysers, air coolers, ovens
>  5 A circuit — for bulbs, fans and other low-power devices
> ```
> **Why separate them: different appliances draw very different currents.**
>
> **A geyser drawing 9 A would blow a 5 A fuse every time it was switched on.**
>
> **And running everything on a 15 A circuit would be worse: a fault in a table lamp could then draw 14 A before anything melted** — far more than that circuit's thin wiring can safely carry.
>
> **The rating must MATCH the load: high enough for normal working, low enough to catch a fault.**

---

Why are domestic appliances connected in parallel, each with its own switch?

> [!ANSWER]
> **So that EACH APPLIANCE RECEIVES THE SAME FULL POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE of 220 V.**
>
> **In parallel, each device draws exactly the current it needs, independently of the others.**
>
> **And each has a SEPARATE SWITCH so it can be turned on or off without affecting anything else.**
>
> **In series, none of this would work:** the 220 V would be shared out, no appliance would get its rated voltage, the same current would be forced through all of them, and **one failure would stop everything.**

---

Explain how the earth wire protects a user, and why its low resistance is essential.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The metallic body of an appliance — an electric press, toaster, table fan, refrigerator — is connected to the EARTH WIRE, which runs to a metal plate buried deep in the ground.**
>
> **If current leaks to the metallic body, the earth wire provides a LOW-RESISTANCE conducting path for it to flow away to the earth.**
>
> **The body's potential therefore stays at EARTH POTENTIAL, and the user does not get a severe shock.**
>
> **Why LOW RESISTANCE is the essential point: current takes the easiest path available.**
>
> **The earth wire is deliberately made a far easier route than a human body**, so almost all of the leaked current goes down it and almost none through the person touching the appliance. **A high-resistance earth wire would offer no such preference and would not protect anyone.**

---

What is short-circuiting, what else can cause overloading, and how does a fuse deal with both?

> [!ANSWER]
> **SHORT-CIRCUITING: the LIVE and NEUTRAL wires come into DIRECT CONTACT** — because the insulation is damaged or there is a fault inside an appliance. **The current in the circuit then increases abruptly.**
>
> **Overloading can also arise from:**
> - **an accidental HIKE IN THE SUPPLY VOLTAGE**
> - **connecting TOO MANY APPLIANCES to a single socket**
>
> **How the fuse handles both: the unusually large current causes JOULE HEATING in the fuse wire, which MELTS and BREAKS the circuit** — stopping the current before it can damage the wiring or the appliances.
>
> **The fuse does not care WHY the current is too big.** It responds to the current itself, which is why one device protects against several quite different faults.

---

A 2 kW electric oven is plugged into a 220 V circuit with a 5 A rating. What happens, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> I = P/V = 2000/220 = 9.09 A
> ```
> **The oven needs 9.09 A, but the circuit is rated for only 5 A — so THE FUSE WILL BLOW and the circuit will be cut off.**
>
> **This is not a malfunction; it is the system working exactly as designed.** Without the fuse, 9 A flowing through wiring meant for 5 A would heat it dangerously and could start a fire.
>
> **The correct action: plug the oven into the 15 A circuit**, which is provided precisely for high-power appliances.

---

Name two safety measures used in domestic circuits and state what each protects against.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. THE ELECTRIC FUSE — protects the CIRCUIT AND APPLIANCES** against short-circuiting and overloading, by melting and breaking the circuit when the current becomes unduly high.
>
> **2. THE EARTH WIRE — protects THE USER** against electric shock, by giving leaked current a low-resistance path to the ground instead of through the person.
>
> **The distinction is worth being clear about: the fuse mainly saves EQUIPMENT, the earth wire mainly saves PEOPLE.**
>
> **They are not alternatives — a house needs both**, because they guard against different dangers.

---

What precautions should be taken to avoid overloading a domestic circuit?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Do not connect TOO MANY APPLIANCES to a single socket** — the total current is what matters, not the number of plugs.
> - **Use the CORRECT CIRCUIT for the appliance** — high-power devices belong on the 15 A circuit, not the 5 A one.
> - **Do not run several high-power appliances at once** on the same circuit.
> - **Use fuses of the PROPER RATING** — never replace a blown fuse with a heavier one to "stop it blowing".
>
> **That last one deserves emphasis: substituting a bigger fuse does not solve an overload, it REMOVES THE PROTECTION.** The current that was melting the fuse will then go on to heat the wiring instead.

---

Both a compass needle near a wire and a rod between magnet poles move when current flows. Are these the same phenomenon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are two sides of the same interaction, seen from opposite ends.**
>
> ```
> COMPASS NEAR A WIRE — the CURRENT creates a field, which pushes the MAGNET
>    → answered by the RIGHT-HAND THUMB RULE
>
> ROD BETWEEN MAGNET POLES — the MAGNET's field pushes the CURRENT-carrying rod
>    → answered by FLEMING'S LEFT-HAND RULE
> ```
> **Ampère's insight connects them: since a current exerts a force on a magnet, the magnet must exert an EQUAL AND OPPOSITE force on the current.**
>
> **This is Newton's third law applied to magnetism** — and it means the two experiments are not independent discoveries but one fact observed twice.
