Name the parts of the human eye and state the function of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Part | Function |
> |---|---|
> | **CORNEA** | the thin transparent bulge at the front — **MOST of the refraction happens here** |
> | **IRIS** | a dark muscular diaphragm that **controls the size of the pupil** |
> | **PUPIL** | the opening that **regulates how much light enters** |
> | **EYE LENS** | provides the **finer adjustment** of focal length |
> | **RETINA** | a delicate membrane with an enormous number of **light-sensitive cells** |
> | **OPTIC NERVE** | carries the electrical signals to the **brain** |
>
> **The eyeball is roughly spherical, about 2.3 cm across, and the image formed on the retina is REAL and INVERTED.**

---

Why does most of the refraction in the eye happen at the cornea rather than at the lens?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the cornea is the boundary between AIR and the eye's material — the BIGGEST jump in refractive index anywhere in the eye.**
>
> **The bigger the difference in refractive index at a surface, the more the light bends there.**
>
> **The eye lens, by contrast, sits surrounded by FLUID on both sides.** The refractive indices on either side of it are much closer together, so it bends light far less.
>
> **The lens's job is therefore only the FINE ADJUSTMENT** needed to focus objects at different distances — the cornea has already done the heavy work.

---

The image on the retina is inverted. Why does the world not look upside down?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the correction happens in the BRAIN, not in the eye.**
>
> **The light-sensitive cells of the retina generate ELECTRICAL SIGNALS when illuminated. These travel along the optic nerves to the brain, and the brain INTERPRETS them** so that we perceive objects as they actually are.
>
> **The optics genuinely does deliver an inverted image — nothing in the eye turns it back.**
>
> **The lesson is worth keeping: SEEING IS NOT SIMPLY OPTICS.** It begins with refraction and ends with interpretation.

---

What is meant by the power of accommodation of the eye? Explain the mechanism.

> [!ANSWER]
> **ACCOMMODATION is the ability of the eye lens to ADJUST ITS FOCAL LENGTH so that objects at different distances are focused on the retina.**
>
> **The mechanism: the eye lens is a fibrous, jelly-like material whose CURVATURE can be changed by the CILIARY MUSCLES.**
> ```
> muscles RELAXED   → the lens becomes THIN  → focal length INCREASES
>                   → DISTANT objects are seen clearly
>
> muscles CONTRACT  → the lens becomes THICK → focal length DECREASES
>                   → NEARBY objects are seen clearly
> ```
> **Why changing curvature changes focal length: a more curved surface bends light more sharply, bringing it to a focus sooner.** A fatter lens is a stronger lens.

---

Define the near point and far point of a normal eye.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **NEAR POINT (least distance of distinct vision) — the MINIMUM distance at which an object can be seen distinctly WITHOUT STRAIN.** For a young adult with normal vision it is about **25 cm**.
> - **FAR POINT — the FARTHEST distance at which objects can be seen clearly.** For a normal eye it is **INFINITY**.
>
> **So a normal eye sees clearly everything from 25 cm out to infinity.**
>
> **Both numbers are properties of the eye's range of accommodation, not of the objects.** They mark the two ends of what the lens can be adjusted to.

---

Why can a normal eye not see clearly an object placed closer than 25 cm?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because THE FOCAL LENGTH OF THE EYE LENS CANNOT BE DECREASED BELOW A CERTAIN MINIMUM.**
>
> **To focus a very near object, the lens would have to become even thicker and more strongly curved than the ciliary muscles can make it.**
>
> **Beyond that limit the image cannot be brought onto the retina, so it forms behind it and the print blurs.**
>
> **And the discomfort you feel is real: the ciliary muscles are straining at their limit.** That is exactly why 25 cm is defined as the distance of distinct vision *without strain*.

---

What happens to the image distance inside the eye as an object is moved farther away?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It does NOT change — the image always forms ON THE RETINA.**
>
> **The image distance inside the eye is FIXED, because the retina is at a fixed distance from the lens.** The eyeball cannot lengthen or shorten.
>
> **What changes instead is the FOCAL LENGTH of the eye lens.** As the object moves away, the ciliary muscles relax, the lens thins, and its focal length increases — keeping the image exactly on the retina.
>
> **This is the opposite of a camera, which focuses by MOVING ITS LENS.** The eye cannot move its lens, so it changes the lens's shape instead.

---

What is myopia? State its causes and how it is corrected.

> [!ANSWER]
> **MYOPIA (near-sightedness): the person CAN see nearby objects clearly but CANNOT see distant objects distinctly. The FAR POINT is nearer than infinity** — perhaps only a few metres.
>
> **What goes wrong: the image of a distant object forms IN FRONT OF the retina, not on it.**
>
> **Causes:**
> - **excessive curvature of the eye lens**, or
> - **elongation of the eyeball**
>
> **Correction: a CONCAVE lens of suitable power.**
>
> **Why concave: the eye is already converging light TOO strongly.** A concave lens **DIVERGES** the incoming rays first, so the over-strong eye then converges them exactly onto the retina. **You are handicapping the eye to cancel its excess power** — which is why the corrective power is negative.

---

What is hypermetropia? State its causes and how it is corrected.

> [!ANSWER]
> **HYPERMETROPIA (far-sightedness): the person CAN see distant objects clearly but CANNOT see nearby objects distinctly. The NEAR POINT is farther away than the normal 25 cm**, so reading material must be held well beyond the usual distance.
>
> **What goes wrong: light from a nearby object is focused BEHIND the retina.**
>
> **Causes:**
> - **the focal length of the eye lens is too long**, or
> - **the eyeball has become too small**
>
> **Correction: a CONVEX lens of appropriate power.**
>
> **Why convex: the eye is not converging strongly enough, so the focus falls beyond the retina.** A converging lens supplies the **ADDITIONAL FOCUSSING POWER** needed to pull the image forward — hence a positive power.

---

What is presbyopia, and why do some people need bifocal lenses?

> [!ANSWER]
> **PRESBYOPIA: the POWER OF ACCOMMODATION decreases with AGE, so the near point gradually recedes and nearby objects become hard to see comfortably.**
>
> **Two causes, both of ageing:**
> - **gradual WEAKENING of the ciliary muscles**
> - **diminishing FLEXIBILITY of the eye lens**
>
> **BI-FOCAL LENSES are needed by a person suffering from BOTH myopia and hypermetropia:**
> ```
> UPPER portion — a CONCAVE lens, for DISTANT vision
> LOWER portion — a CONVEX lens,  for NEAR vision
> ```
> **Why that way round and not the reverse: you look UP at distant things and DOWN at a book.** The lens is arranged to match how the eyes actually move.

---

A student sitting in the last row cannot read the blackboard clearly. What defect is this, and how is it corrected?

> [!ANSWER]
> **MYOPIA — near-sightedness.**
>
> **The evidence: the student can presumably read a book held close, but cannot see the distant blackboard.** That is exactly the myopic pattern — near vision fine, distant vision blurred.
>
> **What is happening inside: the image of the distant blackboard forms IN FRONT OF the retina.**
>
> **The correction: SPECTACLES WITH A CONCAVE LENS of suitable power**, which diverge the light slightly before it enters the eye, pushing the image back onto the retina.

---

A myopic person cannot see beyond 1.2 m. What lens does he need, and of what power?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A CONCAVE lens. The lens must form an image of a very distant object at the eye's own FAR POINT, 1.2 m.**
> ```
> u = infinity        v = −1.2 m       (a virtual image, on the same side)
>
> 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = 1/(−1.2) − 0 = −0.83
>
> f = −1.2 m          P = 1/f = −0.83 D
> ```
> **A concave lens of power about −0.83 D.**
>
> **The general rule for myopia: THE FOCAL LENGTH EQUALS MINUS THE FAR POINT.** The lens simply brings infinity in to where the eye can already cope.

---

The far point of a myopic person is 80 cm. Find the nature and power of the correcting lens.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> u = infinity        v = −80 cm = −0.80 m
>
> 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = 1/(−0.80) − 0 = −1.25
>
> f = −0.80 m         P = −1.25 D
> ```
> **A CONCAVE lens of power −1.25 D.**
>
> **Compare with the previous question: a far point of 1.2 m needed −0.83 D, and a far point of 80 cm needs −1.25 D.**
>
> **The closer the far point, the more severe the myopia and the stronger the lens required** — which is exactly what the reciprocal relationship predicts.

---

The near point of a hypermetropic eye is 1 m. Find the power of the lens needed to correct it. (Normal near point = 25 cm.)

> [!ANSWER]
> **The lens must take an object at the NORMAL near point (25 cm) and form its image at the eye's OWN near point (1 m), where the eye can see it.**
> ```
> u = −25 cm = −0.25 m       v = −100 cm = −1.00 m
>
> 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = (−1) − (−4) = 3
>
> f = +0.33 m                P = +3 D
> ```
> **A CONVEX lens of power +3 D.**
>
> **Note that v is NEGATIVE: the corrective lens forms a VIRTUAL image on the same side as the object**, and it is that virtual image the eye actually looks at.

---

A person needs a lens of power −5.5 D for distant vision and +1.5 D for near vision. Find both focal lengths and name the defects.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> DISTANT vision   P = −5.5 D  →  f = 1/P = 1/(−5.5) = −0.18 m = −18 cm
>                  a CONCAVE lens → the defect is MYOPIA
>
> NEAR vision      P = +1.5 D  →  f = 1/P = 1/1.5 = +0.67 m = +67 cm
>                  a CONVEX lens → the defect is HYPERMETROPIA
> ```
> **This person suffers from BOTH defects and would be given BI-FOCAL lenses** — concave in the upper part for distance, convex in the lower part for reading.
>
> **The sign of the power alone identifies the defect**, which is one reason opticians prescribe in dioptres.

Why does a prism deviate light while a rectangular glass slab does not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of the ANGLE BETWEEN THE TWO REFRACTING SURFACES.**
> ```
> SLAB  — its two surfaces are PARALLEL
>         the ray bends TOWARDS the normal going in, and AWAY from it by
>         the SAME amount coming out → the two bends CANCEL
>         the emergent ray is PARALLEL to the incident ray, merely shifted
>
> PRISM — its refracting surfaces are INCLINED to each other
>         so the second bend ADDS to the first instead of undoing it
>         the emergent ray is turned through the ANGLE OF DEVIATION, ∠D
> ```
> **So the peculiar SHAPE of the prism is the entire reason it deviates light** — the refraction at each surface obeys exactly the same laws in both cases.

---

What is dispersion? Give the order of colours and explain why they separate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **DISPERSION is the splitting of white light into its component colours.** The band of colours produced is the **SPECTRUM**.
> ```
> VIBGYOR — Violet · Indigo · Blue · Green · Yellow · Orange · Red
> ```
> **Why they separate: DIFFERENT COLOURS BEND THROUGH DIFFERENT ANGLES in the prism.**
> ```
> RED bends the LEAST
> VIOLET bends the MOST
> ```
> **So the rays of each colour emerge along DIFFERENT PATHS and become distinct** — which is why you see a band rather than a single beam.

---

How did Newton prove that a prism does not create the colours but merely separates them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By RECOMBINING them.**
> ```
> 1. He obtained the spectrum of sunlight with a prism.
> 2. He tried to split those colours FURTHER with a second identical prism
>    — and could get NO more colours.
> 3. He then placed the second prism INVERTED with respect to the first,
>    letting all the spectrum's colours pass through it.
> 4. A beam of WHITE LIGHT emerged from the other side.
> ```
> **Why the recombination is the decisive step: if the prism CREATED the colours, a second prism should have created more, or at least should not have undone them.**
>
> **Getting white light back proves the colours were IN the sunlight all along** — the prism only sorted them. **Any light giving a spectrum like sunlight's is called white light.**

---

Explain how a rainbow is formed and why it always appears opposite the Sun.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A RAINBOW is a natural spectrum, caused by dispersion of sunlight by tiny WATER DROPLETS in the atmosphere after a rain shower.**
>
> **Each droplet acts as a small prism and does THREE things in turn:**
> ```
> 1. REFRACTS and DISPERSES the sunlight entering it
> 2. REFLECTS it internally off the back of the drop
> 3. REFRACTS it again as it leaves
> ```
> **Why it is always OPPOSITE the Sun: step 2 sends the light BACK OUT the way it came.**
>
> **So the light returns towards the side the Sun is on — and you must have the SUN BEHIND YOU to receive it.**
>
> **This also explains why you can see a rainbow in a waterfall or fountain on a sunny day**, provided you stand with your back to the Sun.

---

Why do objects seen through the hot air above a fire appear to waver?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of ATMOSPHERIC REFRACTION on a small scale.**
> ```
> the air just above the fire is HOTTER, therefore LIGHTER (less dense),
>    and has a slightly SMALLER refractive index than the cooler air above it
>
> the physical conditions of this refracting medium are NOT STATIONARY —
>    the hot air is churning upwards
>
> → the light path keeps changing → the object's APPARENT POSITION fluctuates
> ```
> **The key point: it is the CHANGING nature of the medium that causes the wavering, not the refraction alone.** A steady refracting medium would displace the image but hold it still.

---

Why do stars twinkle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of ATMOSPHERIC REFRACTION of starlight.**
>
> **Starlight entering the atmosphere is refracted CONTINUOUSLY, through a medium of GRADUALLY CHANGING refractive index.**
>
> **Since the atmosphere bends starlight TOWARDS the normal, a star appears slightly HIGHER than its actual position when seen near the horizon.**
>
> **And that apparent position keeps changing, because the atmosphere's physical conditions are not stationary.**
>
> **Crucially, stars are so distant that they are effectively POINT-SIZED sources.** As the light path varies, the amount of starlight entering the eye **flickers — sometimes brighter, sometimes fainter.** That flicker is the twinkling.

---

Why do planets not twinkle, even though they are seen through the same atmosphere?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because planets are much CLOSER, so they appear as EXTENDED sources rather than points.**
>
> **Think of a planet as a large collection of point-sized sources.** Every one of them is twinkling, exactly as a star does.
>
> **But at any instant, some are brightening while others are dimming — so the variations AVERAGE OUT TO ZERO.**
>
> **The total light arriving from the planet therefore stays steady, and the twinkling is nullified.**
>
> **This is a genuinely satisfying explanation: the effect has not stopped happening — it is CANCELLING ITSELF OUT.**

---

Why is the Sun visible about two minutes before actual sunrise?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of ATMOSPHERIC REFRACTION.**
>
> **"Actual" sunrise means the Sun's real crossing of the horizon.** But the atmosphere BENDS the Sun's light over the horizon, so we can see it **while it is still geometrically below**.
>
> **The same happens in reverse at the other end of the day — the Sun remains visible about 2 minutes AFTER actual sunset.**
>
> **So atmospheric refraction lengthens the day by about 4 minutes in total.**
>
> **The APPARENT FLATTENING of the Sun's disc at sunrise and sunset has the same cause** — light from the lower edge is bent more than light from the upper edge, squashing the disc vertically.

---

What is the Tyndall effect, and where can you observe it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The TYNDALL EFFECT: when a beam of light strikes fine particles, the PATH OF THE BEAM ITSELF BECOMES VISIBLE.**
>
> **Why: the light is diffusely REFLECTED off the particles and reaches our eyes from the side** — so we see the beam, not just its destination.
>
> **Where to observe it:**
> - **a fine beam of sunlight entering a smoke-filled room through a small hole**
> - **sunlight passing through the canopy of a dense forest**, where tiny water droplets in the mist scatter it
>
> **The earth's atmosphere is a heterogeneous mixture of minute particles** — smoke, water droplets, dust and air molecules — which is why the effect is so common.

---

Why is the clear sky blue?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the molecules of air and other fine particles in the atmosphere are SMALLER than the wavelength of visible light, and such particles scatter SHORT wavelengths far more effectively than long ones.**
>
> **Red light has a wavelength about 1.8 TIMES greater than blue light.**
>
> **So as sunlight passes through the atmosphere, the fine particles scatter BLUE much more strongly than red — and it is that scattered blue light, arriving from all over the sky, that enters our eyes.**
>
> **Note what is being scattered: not the light coming straight from the Sun, but light redirected sideways.** That is why the whole sky glows blue and not just the region around the Sun.

---

Why does the sky appear dark to an astronaut instead of blue?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because there is NO ATMOSPHERE up there to scatter the light.**
>
> **The sky's blue colour is entirely due to scattering by air molecules. Remove the air and there is nothing to scatter** — so no light reaches the eye except directly from the Sun and the stars, and the rest of the sky is black.
>
> **The same effect at a smaller scale: the sky appears dark to passengers flying at very high altitudes**, because scattering is not prominent where the air is so thin.
>
> **This is the decisive test of the whole explanation.** The theory predicts something surprising — that the sky would be dark without air — **and observation confirms it exactly.**

---

Why is the colour of scattered light different in different situations?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because THE COLOUR OF SCATTERED LIGHT DEPENDS ON THE SIZE OF THE SCATTERING PARTICLES.**
> ```
> VERY FINE particles  → scatter mainly BLUE (short wavelengths)
> LARGER particles     → scatter longer wavelengths
> LARGE ENOUGH         → the scattered light may appear WHITE
> ```
> **This single rule explains several everyday sights at once:** the blue of a clear sky (very fine air molecules), the whiteness of clouds and mist (large water droplets), and the grey-white of smoke and fog.
>
> **So "why is it that colour?" is really the question "how big are the particles?"**

---

Why are danger signals and traffic stop lights red?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because RED IS SCATTERED THE LEAST by fog or smoke.**
>
> **Since it is scattered least, red light travels FURTHEST without being deflected out of the beam** — so the signal can be seen from a great distance, and still looks red rather than washed out.
>
> **A blue signal would be scattered away in every direction long before it reached a distant driver.**
>
> **The same reasoning explains the REDDENING OF THE SUN at sunrise and sunset.** Near the horizon, sunlight travels through a far greater thickness of atmosphere; almost all the blue is scattered away along that long path, and mostly red survives to reach the eye.

---

Both the twinkling of stars and the blueness of the sky involve the atmosphere. How do the two explanations differ?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are entirely different phenomena, and the distinction is worth being clear about.**
>
> | | Twinkling of stars | Blue sky |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Cause** | **REFRACTION** — light bending as it passes through | **SCATTERING** — light being redirected off particles |
> | **What matters** | the **changing** refractive index of the air | the **size** of the particles relative to the wavelength |
> | **Colour involved?** | **No** — all colours are bent alike enough | **Yes** — blue is scattered far more than red |
> | **What changes** | the **direction and brightness** of a point source | which colours reach us **from the sky as a whole** |
>
> **The one-line contrast: refraction BENDS a beam that continues on its way; scattering SENDS light off in new directions, so we see it coming from where no source is.**
