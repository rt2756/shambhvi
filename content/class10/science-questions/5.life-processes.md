What are life processes, and why does a body need them even while asleep?

> [!ANSWER]
> **LIFE PROCESSES are the maintenance jobs a body must keep doing to stay alive:**
> ```
> NUTRITION       — taking in food for energy and materials
> RESPIRATION     — releasing energy from that food
> TRANSPORTATION  — moving materials around the body
> EXCRETION       — removing harmful metabolic wastes
> ```
> **Why they run even during sleep: a living body is a state of ORDER, and order breaks down by itself.**
>
> **Molecules are constantly being damaged and must be repaired or replaced**, and that takes energy whether you are running a race or lying still.

---

Distinguish autotrophic from heterotrophic nutrition, and explain why heterotrophs depend on autotrophs.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **AUTOTROPHIC — the organism MAKES its own food from simple INORGANIC materials (CO2 and water), using an outside energy source such as the Sun.** Green plants and some bacteria.
> - **HETEROTROPHIC — the organism takes in COMPLEX substances already made by other organisms**, and must break them down with **ENZYMES** before use. Animals and fungi.
>
> **Why the dependence is unavoidable: a heterotroph cannot build organic matter from CO2 and water.** It can only rearrange what something else built.
>
> **So every food chain must START with an autotroph** — directly or indirectly, everything alive is eating sunlight.

---

Write the equation for photosynthesis and list the three events involved.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>                      Sunlight
> 6CO2 + 12H2O  ------------------→  C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O
>                     Chlorophyll
> ```
> **The three events:**
> 1. **ABSORPTION of light energy by chlorophyll**
> 2. **CONVERSION of light energy to chemical energy, and SPLITTING of water into hydrogen and oxygen**
> 3. **REDUCTION of carbon dioxide to carbohydrates**
>
> **They need NOT happen one immediately after the other.** Desert plants take in CO2 **at night** and make an intermediate, which is acted on by the chlorophyll's absorbed energy **during the day** — so the steps can be separated in time.

---

Describe the experiment that proves chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Use a plant with VARIEGATED leaves — green and white patches, such as croton or money plant.**
> ```
> 1. Keep the plant in the DARK for three days.
> 2. Put it in sunlight for about six hours.
> 3. Pluck a leaf and TRACE the green areas on paper.
> 4. Boil the leaf in water, then in alcohol (this removes the green).
> 5. Dip it in dilute iodine solution.
> ```
> **Result: only the areas that were GREEN turn blue-black** — matching your tracing exactly.
>
> **Why the dark period is essential: it uses up any starch already in the leaf.** Without it, old starch would show up and ruin the result.
>
> **The conclusion: starch is made only where chlorophyll was present.**

How would you show that carbon dioxide is necessary for photosynthesis?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two destarched potted plants of the same size, each sealed under its own bell jar with vaseline. Beside ONE of them, put a watch-glass of POTASSIUM HYDROXIDE, which absorbs carbon dioxide.**
>
> **After two hours in sunlight, test a leaf from each for starch.**
>
> **Result: only the plant WITHOUT the KOH shows starch.**
>
> **Why the design is sound: the two set-ups are IDENTICAL in every respect except one — the presence of carbon dioxide.** Same plants, same light, same soil, same time. **Changing exactly one factor is what lets you name it as the cause.**

---

What are stomata, how do they open and close, and why does a plant close them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **STOMATA are tiny pores on the leaf surface through which massive gaseous exchange takes place.**
> ```
> water flows INTO the guard cells → they SWELL  → the pore OPENS
> water flows OUT of them          → they SHRINK → the pore CLOSES
> ```
> **Why a plant closes them: a great deal of WATER is lost through stomata.**
>
> **The plant faces a genuine trade-off** — open pores let carbon dioxide in but let water escape. **So when CO2 is not needed for photosynthesis, the pores are shut and water is saved.**
>
> **Note that gas exchange also happens across stems and roots**, not only leaves.

---

Where does a plant get each raw material it needs?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> CARBON DIOXIDE — from the AIR, through the STOMATA
> WATER          — from the SOIL, taken up by the ROOTS
> SUNLIGHT       — absorbed by CHLOROPHYLL in the chloroplasts
> NITROGEN, PHOSPHORUS, IRON, MAGNESIUM — from the SOIL, through the roots
> ```
> **NITROGEN is essential for making PROTEINS.** It is taken up as **inorganic nitrates or nitrites**, or as organic compounds prepared by **bacteria** from atmospheric nitrogen.
>
> **Why plants cannot use nitrogen straight from the air, though it is 78% of the atmosphere: the N≡N triple bond is far too strong to break.** Only certain bacteria can do it — which is why the soil, not the air, is the plant's nitrogen source.

---

Name and describe three types of heterotrophic nutrition with examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **SAPROPHYTIC — the food is broken down OUTSIDE the body and then absorbed.** Bread moulds, yeast, mushrooms.
> - **HOLOZOIC — whole material is taken in and broken down INSIDE the body.** Amoeba, humans.
> - **PARASITIC — nutrition is taken from a plant or animal WITHOUT killing it.** Cuscuta (amar-bel), ticks, lice, leeches, tapeworms.
>
> **What decides which strategy an organism uses: its BODY DESIGN and how its food is available.** A stationary food source like grass and a mobile one like a deer call for quite different apparatus — which is why a cow and a lion are built so differently.

---

How does Amoeba obtain and digest its food?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Amoeba pushes out TEMPORARY FINGER-LIKE EXTENSIONS of the cell surface, which fuse over the food particle to form a FOOD VACUOLE.**
>
> **Inside the food vacuole, complex substances are broken down into simpler ones**, which then diffuse into the cytoplasm. The undigested remains are pushed out at the surface.
>
> **Why Amoeba needs no digestive system: it is a SINGLE CELL.** Food can be taken in anywhere on its surface and every part of the cell is close to the vacuole. **Specialised organs only become necessary as an organism grows more complex.**

Trace the path of food through the human alimentary canal, naming what is added at each stage.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Region | Added | Effect |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Mouth** | saliva with **salivary amylase** | starch → simple sugar; teeth crush, saliva wets |
> | **Oesophagus** | — | **peristalsis** pushes food down |
> | **Stomach** | **HCl, pepsin, mucus** | pepsin digests **protein** |
> | **Small intestine** | **bile, pancreatic juice, intestinal juice** | digestion **completed**, then absorbed |
> | **Large intestine** | — | absorbs more **water** |
>
> **Why the food is crushed and wetted first: every kind of food travels the same tube**, so it must be reduced to small particles of uniform texture, and the soft lining needs a smooth passage.

---

What is peristalsis, and why must food be moved in a regulated way?

> [!ANSWER]
> **PERISTALSIS is the rhythmic contraction of the muscles lining the alimentary canal, which pushes food forward. It happens all along the gut.**
>
> **Why regulation matters: each region does a DIFFERENT job and needs TIME to do it.**
>
> **If food rushed through, the stomach could not act on the protein and the small intestine could not finish digestion or absorb the products.** **Moving it too slowly would be no better.** Peristalsis gives a controlled, steady pace.

---

Name the three secretions of the gastric glands and give the role of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **HYDROCHLORIC ACID** — creates the **ACIDIC MEDIUM that pepsin needs to work**, and **kills bacteria** in the food.
> - **PEPSIN** — the **protein-digesting** enzyme.
> - **MUCUS** — **PROTECTS the stomach's own lining** from the acid.
>
> **The acid has two jobs, not one** — the enzyme requirement is the one usually remembered, but sterilising the food matters too.
>
> **"Acidity" is what happens when the balance fails:** too much acid, or too little mucus, and the acid begins to attack the stomach wall itself.

---

What are the two functions of bile, and why is emulsification so important?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. Bile makes the food ALKALINE.** The food arriving from the stomach is acidic, but **pancreatic enzymes need an alkaline medium** to work.
>
> **2. Bile salts EMULSIFY fats** — they break large fat globules into many small ones.
>
> **Why emulsification matters: enzymes can only act on a SURFACE.**
>
> **A large globule has very little surface for its volume, so digestion would be painfully slow.** Breaking it into many small globules **multiplies the surface area enormously**, so lipase can work far faster.
>
> **This is exactly the emulsifying action of soap on oily dirt** from Chapter 4 — the same physics, doing a different job.

---

Name the enzymes of the pancreas and the intestinal juice, and state what each nutrient finally becomes.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PANCREATIC JUICE
>    trypsin — digests PROTEINS
>    lipase  — breaks down EMULSIFIED FATS
>
> INTESTINAL JUICE completes everything:
>    proteins      → AMINO ACIDS
>    carbohydrates → GLUCOSE
>    fats          → FATTY ACIDS and GLYCEROL
> ```
> **Why digestion must go this far: only SMALL molecules can be absorbed through the intestinal wall.**
>
> **A protein molecule is far too large to cross into the blood** — it has to be taken apart into amino acids first, which the body then reassembles into the proteins it needs.

---

How is the small intestine designed to absorb digested food?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Its inner lining carries numerous finger-like projections called VILLI.**
>
> **They INCREASE THE SURFACE AREA available for absorption**, and are **richly supplied with blood vessels** that carry the absorbed food to every cell in the body.
>
> **It is also the LONGEST part of the canal**, coiled to fit a compact space — again, more length means more absorbing surface.
>
> **The recurring design principle worth spotting: wherever a body must exchange something quickly, it grows folds, projections or branches.** Villi in the intestine, alveoli in the lungs, capillary clusters in the kidney — the same trick, three times over.

---

Why is a herbivore's small intestine longer than a carnivore's?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because grass contains CELLULOSE, which is slow and difficult to digest.**
>
> **A longer intestine means a longer journey, and therefore more TIME for the cellulose to be broken down** before the food leaves.
>
> **Meat is much easier to digest**, so a carnivore such as a tiger has a **shorter** small intestine — it simply does not need the extra length.
>
> **The general point: body design follows diet.** You could guess an animal's food from the length of its gut.

---

Distinguish breathing from respiration.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **BREATHING is taking air INTO the body and letting it OUT** — a mechanical process involving the lungs, ribs and diaphragm.
> - **RESPIRATION is the BREAKDOWN OF FOOD INSIDE CELLS to release energy** — a chemical process, ending in ATP.
>
> **They are not the same thing, and confusing them is a common error.**
>
> **Breathing merely SUPPLIES the oxygen that aerobic respiration needs, and carries away the CO2 it makes.** Respiration is the point; breathing is the delivery service.

---

Describe the different ways glucose is broken down to release energy in various organisms.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every pathway begins the same way, in the CYTOPLASM:**
> ```
> GLUCOSE (6 carbons) → PYRUVATE (3 carbons)
> ```
> **Then they diverge:**
> ```
> (a) ANAEROBIC, in YEAST — no oxygen, in the cytoplasm
>     pyruvate → ETHANOL + CO2 + a little energy      (FERMENTATION)
>
> (b) AEROBIC — with oxygen, in the MITOCHONDRIA
>     pyruvate → CO2 + WATER + a LOT of energy
>
> (c) LACK OF OXYGEN in our MUSCLES
>     pyruvate → LACTIC ACID + a little energy
> ```
> **AEROBIC respiration releases FAR MORE energy** — which is why oxygen is worth the trouble of breathing for.

---

Why do you get muscle cramps after sudden strenuous exercise?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of the BUILD-UP OF LACTIC ACID in the muscles.**
>
> **What happens: during sudden hard activity, the muscles need energy faster than oxygen can be DELIVERED to them.**
>
> **With too little oxygen, the pyruvate cannot be broken down aerobically**, so the muscle cells take the anaerobic route and convert it into **lactic acid** — a three-carbon molecule that accumulates and causes the cramp.
>
> **Why the pain fades with rest: once you stop, oxygen catches up and the lactic acid is cleared.**

---

What is ATP and why does the cell use it instead of using the released energy directly?

> [!ANSWER]
> **ATP is the ENERGY CURRENCY of the cell.**
>
> **The energy released in respiration is used immediately to make ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. Breaking ATP's terminal phosphate link with water releases about 30.5 kJ/mol**, which drives the cell's endothermic reactions.
>
> **Why a currency rather than direct use: the SAME molecule can power ANY job** — muscle contraction, protein synthesis, conduction of nerve impulses, and much else.
>
> **Think of a battery.** One battery can be turned into mechanical, light or electrical energy depending on what you plug it into. **ATP works the same way — one standard package of energy, universally accepted.**

---

Do plants release oxygen at night? Explain what happens during the day and the night.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — at night a plant RELEASES CARBON DIOXIDE.**
> ```
> NIGHT — there is no photosynthesis, so CO2 RELEASE is the major exchange
> DAY   — the CO2 made by respiration is USED UP by photosynthesis, so there
>         is no CO2 release; OXYGEN RELEASE is the major event
> ```
> **The crucial point, and the one usually got wrong: PLANTS RESPIRE ALL THE TIME, day and night.**
>
> **During the day photosynthesis simply outruns respiration and HIDES it** — the plant is consuming its own CO2 faster than it makes it.

---

Why do fish breathe much faster than land animals?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the amount of oxygen DISSOLVED in water is very small compared with the oxygen available in air.**
>
> **A fish must therefore pass a great deal of water over its gills** to obtain what a land animal gets from a few breaths — so its mouth and gill covers open and close far more often than you breathe.
>
> **Terrestrial animals have the easier deal**, which is one of the real advantages of living on land.
>
> **How a fish does it: it takes water in through the mouth and forces it past the GILLS, where the dissolved oxygen is taken up by the blood.**

---

How are human lungs designed to maximise gas exchange?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The air passage divides into smaller and smaller tubes, ending in balloon-like ALVEOLI.**
>
> **Spread out flat, the alveolar surface would cover about 80 m² — far more than the surface area of your whole body.**
>
> **Their walls carry an EXTENSIVE NETWORK OF BLOOD VESSELS**, so blood is always right beside the air.
>
> **Why this surface is kept INSIDE the body: it must be very FINE and DELICATE for gases to cross it** — and anything that fine has to be protected. **Hence the passages that carry air in to it, and the ribs and diaphragm that move the air in and out.**

---

Why do the lungs always keep a residual volume of air?

> [!ANSWER]
> **So that there is SUFFICIENT TIME for oxygen to be absorbed and carbon dioxide to be released.**
>
> **If the lungs emptied completely with every breath out, gas exchange would be INTERRUPTED** each time — it would happen only in bursts.
>
> **The residual air keeps the alveoli inflated and the exchange running CONTINUOUSLY**, between breaths as well as during them.

---

How are oxygen and carbon dioxide transported in human beings, and why is the method different for each?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **OXYGEN is carried by HAEMOGLOBIN**, the respiratory pigment in the red blood corpuscles, which has a **very high affinity for oxygen**.
> - **CARBON DIOXIDE is mostly transported DISSOLVED in the blood.**
>
> **Why the difference: CO2 is much MORE SOLUBLE in water than oxygen is**, so it needs no special carrier — the plasma can simply dissolve it.
>
> **Why oxygen needs a carrier at all: in a large body, DIFFUSION ALONE IS FAR TOO SLOW.** It is estimated that an oxygen molecule would take **about three years** to travel from your lungs to your toes by diffusion.

---

Name the components of the human transport system and give the function of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> HEART         — the pump that pushes blood around the body
> BLOOD VESSELS — arteries, veins and capillaries: the network reaching
>                 every tissue
> BLOOD         — the transport medium:
>    PLASMA     carries food, CO2 and nitrogenous wastes in dissolved form
>    RED BLOOD CORPUSCLES carry oxygen
>    PLATELETS  plug leaks by clotting the blood at an injury
> LYMPH         — carries absorbed fat and drains excess tissue fluid
> ```
> **Three requirements underlie the design: a PUMP to push the blood, a NETWORK to reach every tissue, and a REPAIR system for when the network leaks.**

---

Why must oxygenated and de-oxygenated blood be kept separate in birds and mammals, and how do fish and amphibians differ?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because separation allows a HIGHLY EFFICIENT supply of oxygen to the body.**
>
> **Birds and mammals need that efficiency because they CONSTANTLY USE ENERGY TO MAINTAIN THEIR BODY TEMPERATURE** — a demand that never stops.
>
> ```
> FISH        — TWO chambers. Blood goes heart → gills → body, passing
>               through the heart ONCE per circuit: SINGLE circulation.
> AMPHIBIANS
> and many
> REPTILES    — THREE chambers, tolerating some MIXING. They do not
>               maintain body temperature, so they can afford it.
> BIRDS and
> MAMMALS     — FOUR chambers, no mixing; blood passes through the heart
>               TWICE per circuit: DOUBLE CIRCULATION.
> ```
> **The pattern to notice: heart design tracks ENERGY NEED, not evolutionary rank.**

---

Compare arteries, veins and capillaries, explaining each difference.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | ARTERIES | VEINS | CAPILLARIES |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Direction** | **away from** the heart | **towards** the heart | within a tissue |
> | **Wall** | **thick, elastic** | thin | **ONE CELL thick** |
> | **Valves** | no | **yes** | no |
>
> **Every difference follows from PRESSURE.**
>
> **Arteries have thick elastic walls because blood leaves the heart under HIGH pressure** and must be contained.
>
> **Veins need no thick wall because the blood is no longer under pressure — but for that very reason it could flow BACKWARDS, so veins have VALVES** to keep it moving one way.
>
> **Capillaries are ONE cell thick so that materials can be EXCHANGED across the wall.** That exchange is the entire point of the circulation; everything else is plumbing.

---

Why do the ventricles have thicker walls than the atria?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the VENTRICLES must pump blood right out to the organs — the left ventricle to the whole body, the right ventricle to the lungs.**
>
> **The ATRIA only have to pass blood down into the chamber immediately below them**, which is a far shorter and easier push.
>
> **More muscle for the harder job.** And it is no accident that the **left** ventricle, which serves the entire body, is the thickest chamber of all.

---

What is lymph, how is it formed, and what does it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **LYMPH (tissue fluid) forms when some PLASMA, PROTEIN and BLOOD CELLS ESCAPE through the pores in capillary walls into the spaces between cells.**
>
> **It is similar to plasma but COLOURLESS and with LESS PROTEIN.**
>
> **Its route: lymph drains into lymphatic capillaries, which join to form larger lymph vessels, which finally open into the veins.**
>
> **Its two jobs:**
> - **carries DIGESTED AND ABSORBED FAT from the intestine**
> - **drains EXCESS FLUID from the extracellular space back into the blood**
>
> **Without that drainage the fluid would simply accumulate in the tissues** — which is exactly what causes swelling when the lymph system fails.

Name the two transport tissues in plants and state what each carries.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> XYLEM  — carries WATER and MINERALS from the soil, roots UPWARDS
> PHLOEM — carries the soluble products of PHOTOSYNTHESIS from the leaves
>          to everywhere else, both UPWARDS and DOWNWARDS
> ```
> **Why a plant can afford a SLOW transport system: it does not move, and a large proportion of its tissue consists of dead cells — so its energy needs are LOW.**
>
> **But the DISTANCES can be enormous in a tall tree**, which is why a transport system is needed at all rather than relying on diffusion.

---

Explain how water rises from the roots to the top of a tall tree.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two forces, dominant at different times.**
>
> **1. ROOT PRESSURE.** Root cells in contact with the soil **actively take up ions**, creating a concentration difference between root and soil. **Water then moves into the root to even out that difference**, pushing a column of water steadily upwards.
>
> **But root pressure alone is NOT enough to reach the height of a real tree.**
>
> **2. TRANSPIRATION PULL.** Water evaporates from the leaf cells through the stomata, and is replaced from the xylem. **That evaporation creates a SUCTION which pulls water up from the roots.**
>
> ```
> at NIGHT — stomata shut, so ROOT PRESSURE dominates
> by DAY   — stomata open, so TRANSPIRATION PULL is the major force
> ```

---

What is transpiration, and what does it do for the plant besides moving water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **TRANSPIRATION is the loss of water as VAPOUR from the aerial parts of a plant.**
>
> **Its two roles:**
> - **it drives the ABSORPTION and UPWARD MOVEMENT of water and dissolved minerals** from roots to leaves
> - **it helps regulate TEMPERATURE** — evaporation cools the leaf, just as sweating cools you
>
> **A simple demonstration: cover two pots — one with a plant, one with a stick of the same height — with plastic bags in sunlight.** Only the bag over the **plant** collects water droplets, and the water can only have come from the plant.

---

What is translocation, and why does it require energy when xylem transport does not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **TRANSLOCATION is the transport of the soluble products of photosynthesis in the PHLOEM**, through **sieve tubes with the help of companion cells**, in **both** directions.
>
> **How the energy is used:**
> ```
> sucrose is moved INTO the phloem using energy from ATP
>    → this raises the OSMOTIC PRESSURE of the tissue
>    → water moves in
>    → the pressure pushes material towards tissues of LOWER pressure
> ```
> **Why the energy is worth spending: it lets the plant send food WHERE IT IS NEEDED, in either direction.**
>
> **Xylem transport is driven by simple physical forces and can only go UP.** Phloem transport can be **directed** — which is why, in spring, sugar stored in the root can be sent **up** to the buds that need energy to grow.

---

Describe the structure and functioning of a nephron.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A NEPHRON is the basic filtration unit of the kidney; each kidney contains large numbers of them packed close together.**
> ```
> 1. A cluster of very thin-walled blood CAPILLARIES sits inside the
>    cup-shaped BOWMAN'S CAPSULE, which collects the filtrate.
>
> 2. As the filtrate flows along the coiled tube, useful substances are
>    SELECTIVELY RE-ABSORBED — glucose, amino acids, salts and most of
>    the water.
>
> 3. The remaining fluid — urine — enters the URETER, is stored in the
>    URINARY BLADDER, and leaves through the URETHRA.
> ```
> **How much water is re-absorbed depends on how much EXCESS WATER the body has and how much dissolved waste must be excreted** — which is why urine is concentrated when you are thirsty.

---

About 180 L of filtrate is produced daily but only 1–2 L is passed out. Why filter so much and then take most of it back?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because it is far simpler to filter by SIZE than by IDENTITY.**
>
> **The capillary cluster lets through anything small enough** — useful substances and wastes alike. It makes no attempt to be selective.
>
> **The tubule then RECLAIMS what is worth keeping** — glucose, amino acids, salts and nearly all the water.
>
> **One crude step plus one selective step beats trying to be selective from the start.** Designing a filter that could recognise urea and reject glucose would be enormously harder than filtering everything and sorting afterwards.

---

How does an artificial kidney work, and how does it differ from a real one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **HEMODIALYSIS: a number of tubes with a semi-permeable lining are suspended in a tank of DIALYSING FLUID.**
>
> **That fluid has the SAME OSMOTIC PRESSURE as blood, but contains NO nitrogenous wastes.**
>
> **The patient's blood is passed through the tubes; the wastes DIFFUSE OUT into the fluid** because of the concentration difference, and the purified blood is pumped back.
>
> **The key difference from a real kidney: there is NO RE-ABSORPTION.** The machine can only remove; it cannot selectively reclaim glucose, salts and water the way a nephron does. **Matching the osmotic pressure is what prevents useful substances being stripped out along with the waste.**

---

How do plants get rid of their waste products?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> OXYGEN is itself a waste product of photosynthesis
> excess WATER goes by TRANSPIRATION
> many wastes are stored in CELLULAR VACUOLES
> some are stored in LEAVES, which then FALL OFF
> others are stored as RESINS and GUMS, especially in old xylem
> some are excreted into the SURROUNDING SOIL
> ```
> **Why plants can manage without an excretory system: much of a plant's tissue consists of DEAD CELLS, and a plant can afford to LOSE parts of itself.**
>
> **An animal cannot drop a limb to get rid of waste; a plant can drop a leaf.** That single difference in body design explains the whole contrast.
