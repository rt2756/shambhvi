Define electric current and give its unit. What is an electric circuit?

> [!ANSWER]
> **ELECTRIC CURRENT is the RATE OF FLOW of electric charge.**
> ```
> I = Q/t
>
> SI unit: the AMPERE (A).     1 A = 1 coulomb per second
> ```
> **An ELECTRIC CIRCUIT is a CONTINUOUS AND CLOSED path for the current.**
>
> **Why the path must be closed: charge cannot pile up at a dead end.** Break the circuit anywhere — flick a switch off — and the current stops everywhere at once, not just at the break.

---

Why is the conventional direction of current opposite to the direction in which electrons move?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because ELECTRONS WERE NOT KNOWN when electricity was first studied.**
>
> **Current was assumed to be a flow of POSITIVE charge, and the direction of that flow was defined as the direction of the current.**
>
> **When electrons were later discovered — and found to be NEGATIVE — the convention was already in universal use in every book, formula and diagram.** Changing it would have been more trouble than keeping it.
>
> **So it is a HISTORICAL ACCIDENT, not a physical fact.** Nothing in nature prefers one direction; only our bookkeeping does.

---

A charge of 20 C flows through a conductor in 4 s. Find the current, and the number of electrons involved.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> I = Q/t = 20/4 = 5 A
>
> number of electrons = Q / (charge on one electron)
>                     = 20 / (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)
>                     = 1.25 × 10²⁰ electrons
> ```
> **The number is worth pausing over: 125 billion billion electrons pass in four seconds** — and that is only a 5 A current, less than an electric iron draws.
>
> **This is why we never count electrons in practice.** The coulomb exists precisely so that we can work with manageable numbers.

---

What is potential difference? Define the volt.

> [!ANSWER]
> **POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE is the WORK DONE to move a UNIT CHARGE from one point to another.**
> ```
> V = W/Q
>
> SI unit: the VOLT (V).      1 V = 1 J/C
> ```
> **1 VOLT is the potential difference between two points when 1 JOULE of work is done to move a charge of 1 COULOMB from one to the other.**
>
> **Why a "difference" and not just a potential: only the difference makes charge move**, exactly as only a difference in water level makes water flow.

---

Why do charges not flow in a wire by themselves? Use the water analogy.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because they need a difference of "electric pressure" — a POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE — along the conductor.**
>
> **The analogy: water in a perfectly HORIZONTAL tube does not flow.** Connect one end to a tank at a higher level, creating a **pressure difference**, and water flows out of the other end.
>
> **For charges in a metal wire, gravity plays no part — but the principle is identical.** Electrons move only if there is a potential difference across the conductor.
>
> **Where the difference comes from: a CELL.** Chemical action inside it generates a potential difference across its terminals, **even when no current is being drawn.**

---

How much work is done in moving a charge of 3 C across two points with a potential difference of 6 V?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> V = W/Q      so     W = VQ
>
> W = 6 × 3 = 18 J
> ```
> **Reading the numbers back: it takes 6 J to move each coulomb, so 3 coulombs need 18 J.**
>
> **Where that energy goes: it is spent pushing the charge against the opposition of the circuit, and reappears as heat, light or motion** — depending on what the circuit contains.

---

Why is an ammeter connected in series but a voltmeter in parallel?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **An AMMETER measures CURRENT, so the whole current must pass THROUGH it** — it therefore goes **in the path**, in series.
> - **A VOLTMETER compares the potential at TWO POINTS, so it must be connected ACROSS them** — in parallel.
>
> **What goes wrong if you swap them:**
> - **an ammeter connected across a battery SHORT-CIRCUITS it**, since an ammeter has almost no resistance
> - **a voltmeter placed in series would BLOCK the current**, since a voltmeter has very high resistance
>
> **So the connection rule is not arbitrary — it follows from what each instrument is designed to have inside it.**

---

State Ohm's law, including its condition, and describe the V–I graph.

> [!ANSWER]
> **OHM'S LAW: the potential difference across a conductor is DIRECTLY PROPORTIONAL to the current through it, PROVIDED ITS TEMPERATURE REMAINS THE SAME.**
> ```
> V ∝ I        so      V = IR       and      R = V/I
>
> unit of resistance: the OHM (Ω).      1 Ω = 1 V/A
> ```
> **The V–I graph is a STRAIGHT LINE THROUGH THE ORIGIN, and its SLOPE is the resistance.**
>
> **The temperature condition is part of the law, not a footnote.** Resistance changes with temperature, so a conductor that heats up as the current rises will **not** give a straight line — which is exactly what happens with a bulb filament.

---

A resistor of 6 Ω is connected across a 12 V battery. Find the current. If a second identical resistor is added in series, what happens?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ALONE       I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A
>
> IN SERIES   Rs = 6 + 6 = 12 Ω
>             I = 12/12 = 1 A
> ```
> **Adding a resistor in series HALVED the current.**
>
> **Why: the equivalent resistance doubled, and the voltage stayed the same, so by `I = V/R` the current must halve.**
>
> **Physically, joining the two end to end is like using ONE WIRE OF TWICE THE LENGTH** — and resistance is proportional to length.

---

On what three factors does the resistance of a conductor depend? Give the formula and explain each dependence.

> [!ANSWER]
> **On its LENGTH, its AREA OF CROSS-SECTION, and the NATURE OF ITS MATERIAL.**
> ```
> R ∝ l           directly proportional to LENGTH
> R ∝ 1/A         inversely proportional to AREA
>
> R = ρ l/A       ρ = RESISTIVITY of the material, in Ω m
> ```
> **Why R rises with length: a longer wire means more obstruction to push through** — like a longer stretch of rough road. Twice the length, twice the opposition.
>
> **Why R falls with area: a thicker wire gives more room to flow through** — like widening the road, so each electron meets less crowding.
>
> **RESISTIVITY is a property of the MATERIAL alone** — it does not depend on the size or shape of the piece, whereas resistance does.

---

A wire has resistance R. If its length is doubled and its area of cross-section is halved, what is the new resistance?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> R = ρ l/A
>
> new R = ρ (2l)/(A/2) = ρ × 2l × 2/A = 4 × (ρl/A) = 4R
> ```
> **The resistance becomes FOUR TIMES the original.**
>
> **Why both changes push the same way: doubling the length doubles R, and halving the area doubles it again.** The two effects multiply rather than cancel.
>
> **A physical check: this is exactly what happens when you STRETCH a wire** — it gets longer and thinner at the same time, so its resistance rises sharply.

---

Find the resistance of a copper wire 1 m long and 10⁻⁶ m² in cross-section. (ρ of copper = 1.62 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m.)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> R = ρ l/A = (1.62 × 10⁻⁸ × 1) / (10⁻⁶)
>
> R = 1.62 × 10⁻² Ω = 0.0162 Ω
> ```
> **Barely more than a hundredth of an ohm for a whole metre of wire.**
>
> **That is exactly why copper is used for connecting wires and transmission lines** — over long distances its resistance stays small, so very little energy is wasted heating the wire instead of reaching the appliance.

---

Why are alloys used in heating devices, copper in transmission lines, and tungsten in bulb filaments?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Each choice follows from what the material has to do.**
> ```
> COPPER and ALUMINIUM — transmission lines
>    VERY LOW resistivity, so little energy is wasted as heat on the way
>
> ALLOYS (nichrome and others) — irons, toasters, heaters
>    resistivity is generally HIGHER than that of their constituent metals
>       → more heat for the same current
>    AND they do not OXIDISE (burn) readily at high temperature
>       → they survive being red-hot day after day
>
> TUNGSTEN — bulb filaments, almost exclusively
>    a very high MELTING POINT (3380 °C), so the filament can glow
>    white-hot without melting
> ```
> **The contrast is instructive: for wiring you want the LOWEST possible resistance, for a heater the HIGHEST you can safely manage.** Same physics, opposite requirements.

---

Three resistors of 1 Ω, 2 Ω and 3 Ω are joined in series across a 6 V battery. Find the current and the potential difference across each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Rs = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 Ω
>
> I = V/Rs = 6/6 = 1 A          the SAME through all three
>
> V1 = IR1 = 1 × 1 = 1 V
> V2 = IR2 = 1 × 2 = 2 V
> V3 = IR3 = 1 × 3 = 3 V
> ```
> **Check: V1 + V2 + V3 = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 V** ✔ — the individual voltages must add up to the supply.
>
> **The pattern worth noticing: the LARGEST resistor takes the LARGEST share of the voltage.** With the same current through all of them, `V = IR` makes the voltage proportional to the resistance.

---

Derive the formula for resistors in parallel.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> In parallel, the SAME potential difference V is across each resistor,
> and the currents ADD:
>
>     I = I1 + I2 + I3
>
> Ohm's law on the combination:   I = V/Rp
> Ohm's law on each resistor:     I1 = V/R1,  I2 = V/R2,  I3 = V/R3
>
> substituting:
>
>     V/Rp = V/R1 + V/R2 + V/R3
>
> dividing through by V:
>
>     1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3
> ```
> **The whole derivation rests on two physical facts** — that the voltage is shared equally and the current is split. **Everything else is algebra.**

---

Find the equivalent resistance of 1 Ω in parallel with 10⁶ Ω, and comment.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1/Rp = 1/1 + 1/10⁶ = 1 + 0.000001 = 1.000001
>
> Rp = 0.999999 Ω  ≈  1 Ω, just slightly LESS
> ```
> **The enormous resistor makes almost no difference at all.**
>
> **Why: with the same voltage across both, the 10⁶ Ω branch carries a millionth of the current the 1 Ω branch carries.** It is very nearly an open circuit.
>
> **But note it does still lower the total, if only in the sixth decimal place** — and it must, because **the parallel equivalent is ALWAYS less than the smallest individual resistance.** An extra path can never make things harder.

---

A lamp of 100 Ω, a toaster of 50 Ω and a water filter of 500 Ω are connected in parallel to a 220 V supply. Find the equivalent resistance and the total current.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1/Rp = 1/100 + 1/50 + 1/500
>      = 0.01 + 0.02 + 0.002 = 0.032
>
> Rp = 1/0.032 = 31.25 Ω
>
> I = V/Rp = 220/31.25 = 7.04 A
> ```
> **Notice that 31.25 Ω is smaller than the SMALLEST of the three (50 Ω)** — as it must be for a parallel combination.
>
> **A useful cross-check by adding the branch currents:**
> ```
> 220/100 = 2.20 A · 220/50 = 4.40 A · 220/500 = 0.44 A
> total = 7.04 A     ✔
> ```

---

Why is household wiring done in parallel rather than in series?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A series circuit would fail for THREE separate reasons.**
>
> **1. The same current flows through everything.** But a bulb and a heater need **currents of widely different values** to work properly, so they cannot share one.
>
> **2. If one component fails, the circuit BREAKS and NOTHING works.** This is the misery of fairy lights — one dead bulb kills the whole string, and every bulb has to be tested to find it.
>
> **3. The supply voltage would be SHARED**, so no appliance would receive the 220 V it is designed for.
>
> **A parallel circuit fixes all three at once: it DIVIDES the current between the gadgets, gives each the FULL voltage, and lets each be switched on and off independently.**

---

A wire of resistance R is cut into five equal parts, which are then joined in parallel. Find the ratio R/R′.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> each part has resistance R/5      (resistance ∝ length)
>
> five equal resistors in parallel:
>    R' = (R/5)/5 = R/25
>
> R/R' = 25
> ```
> **The resistance falls to a TWENTY-FIFTH of the original.**
>
> **Why 25 and not 5: the factor of 5 is applied TWICE.** Cutting the wire divides each piece's resistance by 5, and putting five equal resistors in parallel divides again by 5.
>
> **A general result worth remembering: n equal resistors in parallel give R/n.**

---

State Joule's law of heating and derive it.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> work done in moving charge Q through potential difference V  =  VQ
> so the source supplies energy VQ in time t
>
> power input      P = VQ/t = VI
> energy in time t = VIt
>
> in a purely resistive circuit, ALL of it appears as heat:
>
>       H = VIt
>
> and substituting V = IR:
>
>       H = I²Rt          JOULE'S LAW OF HEATING
> ```
> **Heat produced is directly proportional to the SQUARE of the current, to the RESISTANCE, and to the TIME.**

---

Why does doubling the current in a wire make it dangerously hotter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because heat depends on the SQUARE of the current.**
> ```
> H = I²Rt
>
> double I  →  H becomes 2² = FOUR times as great
> triple I  →  H becomes 3² = NINE times as great
> ```
> **So a modest overload produces a dramatic amount of extra heat.**
>
> **This is exactly why an overloaded wire heats up so alarmingly fast, and why circuits must be protected.** A 50% overload does not raise the heating by 50% — it more than doubles it.

---

100 J of heat is produced each second in a 4 Ω resistor. Find the current and the potential difference across it.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 100 J per second means P = 100 W
>
> P = I²R      so     I² = P/R = 100/4 = 25
>
> I = 5 A
>
> V = IR = 5 × 4 = 20 V
> ```
> **Cross-check with a different formula:**
> ```
> P = VI = 20 × 5 = 100 W    ✔
> ```
> **Using a second form of the power equation to check is a good habit** — it catches an arithmetic slip immediately, since all three forms must agree.

---

Explain how a fuse protects a circuit, and why it must be connected in series.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A FUSE is a piece of wire, of a metal or alloy with an appropriate MELTING POINT (aluminium, copper, iron, lead), encased in a porcelain cartridge with metal ends.**
> ```
> too much current → the fuse wire heats up (H = I²Rt)
>                  → it MELTS
>                  → the circuit BREAKS and the current stops
> ```
> **Why it must be in SERIES:**
> - **in series it carries the WHOLE current of the circuit, so it can sense an overload**
> - **when it melts, it breaks the ONLY path, cutting everything off**
>
> **In parallel it would carry only part of the current, and on melting would cut off nothing at all** — the current would simply continue through the other branch.
>
> **Domestic fuses are rated 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A and so on.**

---

An electric heater of 2 kW is used on a 220 V supply. Which fuse should be used?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> I = P/V = 2000/220 = 9.09 A
> ```
> **Use a 10 A fuse** — the next standard rating above the working current.
>
> **Why it must be ABOVE the working current: a fuse rated below 9.09 A would blow every time the heater was switched on normally.**
>
> **Why it must not be FAR above: a 30 A fuse would happily let a dangerous overload continue without ever melting** — it would be there for decoration.
>
> **The rule: a fuse must TOLERATE normal working and FAIL on an overload.** That is why the standard ratings exist in a graded series.

---

Why is tungsten used for bulb filaments, and why is a bulb filled with nitrogen and argon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **TUNGSTEN is used because of its very high MELTING POINT — 3380 °C.**
>
> **The filament must get white-hot to emit light, so it has to survive a temperature that would destroy almost any other metal.**
>
> **It is also THERMALLY ISOLATED using insulating supports, so that it RETAINS as much of the heat generated as possible** and reaches that temperature.
>
> **The bulb is filled with chemically INACTIVE nitrogen and argon to PROLONG THE FILAMENT'S LIFE.** In air, tungsten at that temperature would burn away almost at once; an unreactive gas keeps oxygen away from it.
>
> **An honest note: MOST of the power still appears as HEAT, and only a small part as light** — which is why filament bulbs have largely been replaced.

---

Write the three forms of the electric power formula and say when each is convenient.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> P = VI          when you know the voltage and the current
> P = I²R         when you know the current and the resistance
> P = V²/R        when you know the voltage and the resistance
>
> SI unit: the WATT (W).     1 W = 1 V × 1 A
> ```
> **All three are the SAME statement, rearranged using Ohm's law** — none is more correct than the others.
>
> **1 watt is the power consumed by a device carrying 1 A at a potential difference of 1 V.**
>
> **Practical tip: pick the form that uses the two quantities you already have**, and use a second form afterwards as a check.

---

A 100 W bulb works on a 220 V supply. Find the current it draws and its resistance.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> I = P/V = 100/220 = 0.45 A
>
> R = V²/P = (220 × 220)/100 = 48400/100 = 484 Ω
> ```
> **Check with the other route:**
> ```
> R = V/I = 220/0.45 = 484 Ω    ✔
> ```
> **A useful comparison: a 60 W bulb on the same supply has R = 48400/60 = 807 Ω — HIGHER resistance.**
>
> **So on a fixed supply voltage, the DIMMER bulb has the GREATER resistance** — which follows directly from `P = V²/R`. This surprises people who expect "more resistance" to mean "more powerful".

---

Two lamps rated 100 W and 60 W, both at 220 V, are connected in parallel to the mains. Find the total current drawn.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 100 W lamp:  I1 = P/V = 100/220 = 0.45 A
>  60 W lamp:  I2 = P/V =  60/220 = 0.27 A
>
> in PARALLEL the currents ADD:
>
> total I = 0.45 + 0.27 = 0.72 A
> ```
> **Why the currents simply add: in parallel each lamp gets the FULL 220 V and draws whatever current it needs**, independently of the other.
>
> **This is the whole point of household parallel wiring** — switching one lamp off does not change what the other draws.

---

An electric refrigerator rated 400 W runs 8 hours a day. Find the cost of running it for 30 days at ₹3.00 per unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> energy = power × time
>        = 400 W × 8 hours/day × 30 days
>        = 96000 W h
>        = 96 kW h        (96 "units")
>
> cost = 96 × 3.00 = ₹288
> ```
> **The COMMERCIAL UNIT of electrical energy is the KILOWATT HOUR (kW h), commonly called a "unit".**
> ```
> 1 kW h = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J
> ```
> **Note that the bill charges for ENERGY, not power.** A 400 W appliance costs nothing if it is never switched on — the hours matter as much as the watts.

---

Four 60 W bulbs are used for 5 hours a day. Find the monthly (30-day) cost at ₹4.00 per unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total power = 4 × 60 = 240 W
>
> energy = 240 × 5 × 30 = 36000 W h = 36 kW h
>
> cost = 36 × 4.00 = ₹144
> ```
> **A comparison worth making: replacing them with four 9 W LED bulbs of similar brightness gives**
> ```
> 36 W × 5 × 30 = 5400 W h = 5.4 kW h → ₹21.60
> ```
> **The saving is not marginal — it is about 85%.** This is the arithmetic behind every appeal to switch to efficient lighting.

---

Some people believe electrons are "used up" in a circuit. Why is this wrong, and what are you actually paying for?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Electrons are NOT consumed.** The same electrons stay in the wires; they merely move round the circuit and come back.
>
> **What you pay the electricity company for is the ENERGY used to move those electrons through your bulbs, fans and motors.**
>
> **Where the energy actually goes: into heat, light and motion** in the appliances — not into destroying anything.
>
> **A way to see it: a circuit is like water in a closed pipe loop driven by a pump.** The water is never used up; the **energy** supplied by the pump is what turns the wheel. **The bill is for the pumping, not for the water.**
