Define line of sight, angle of elevation and angle of depression.

> [!ANSWER]
> **LINE OF SIGHT — the line drawn from the eye of an observer to the point being viewed.**
>
> **ANGLE OF ELEVATION — the angle the line of sight makes with the HORIZONTAL when the point viewed is ABOVE the horizontal level.** You **raise** your head.
>
> **ANGLE OF DEPRESSION — the angle the line of sight makes with the HORIZONTAL when the point viewed is BELOW the horizontal level.** You **lower** your head.
>
> **Both are measured from the HORIZONTAL, never from the vertical — and both at the OBSERVER's position.**

---

Explain the alternate-angles trick used in almost every angle-of-depression problem.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The horizontal through the observer's eye and the horizontal at ground level are PARALLEL, and the line of sight is a TRANSVERSAL.**
>
> **So the ANGLE OF DEPRESSION from the top equals the ANGLE OF ELEVATION from the bottom** — they are **alternate angles**.
>
> **Why it matters: the depression angle is marked OUTSIDE the triangle you want to work in.** Transferring it to the bottom of the figure puts it **inside** a right triangle whose sides you actually know — and only then can a ratio be used.

---

How do you decide which trigonometric ratio to use in a problem?

> [!ANSWER]
> **List what you HAVE and what you WANT, then pick the ratio containing exactly those two sides.**
> ```
> adjacent given, opposite wanted      →  tan
> opposite given, adjacent wanted      →  tan (or cot)
> hypotenuse given, opposite wanted    →  sin
> hypotenuse given, adjacent wanted    →  cos
> a side given, hypotenuse wanted      →  sin or cos
> ```
> **Decide this BEFORE calculating anything.** Most of the real work in this chapter is choosing the right ratio; the arithmetic that follows is short.

A tower stands vertically. From a point 15 m from its foot, the angle of elevation of the top is 60°. Find its height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> we HAVE the adjacent side (15 m) and WANT the opposite side  →  use tan
>
> tan 60° = height/15
> √3 = height/15
> height = 15√3 m ≈ 25.98 m
> ```

---

A circus artist climbs a 20 m rope stretched from the top of a vertical pole to the ground at 30° to the ground. Find the pole's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the ROPE is the hypotenuse; the POLE is the opposite side  →  use sin
>
> sin 30° = height/20
> 1/2 = height/20
> height = 10 m
> ```
> **The pole is exactly half the rope's length**, because `sin 30° = 1/2` — a useful fact to recognise instantly.

---

A tree breaks in a storm and its top touches the ground 8 m from the foot, making 30° with the ground. Find the original height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the BROKEN part is the hypotenuse, the STANDING part is the opposite side,
> and 8 m is the adjacent side
>
> standing part:  tan 30° = h/8    →    h = 8/√3
>
> broken part:    cos 30° = 8/L    →    L = 8/(√3/2) = 16/√3
>
> original height = standing + broken
>                 = 8/√3 + 16/√3 = 24/√3 = 8√3 m ≈ 13.86 m
> ```
> **The trap is answering `8/√3`.** The tree's original height is the **standing part PLUS the broken part** — the broken piece is still part of the tree, just bent over.

---

A slide 1.5 m high is inclined at 30°; another 3 m high at 60°. Find each slide's length.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the SLIDE is the hypotenuse and the HEIGHT the opposite side  →  use sin
>
> slide 1:  sin 30° = 1.5/L    →    1/2 = 1.5/L    →    L = 3 m
>
> slide 2:  sin 60° = 3/L      →    √3/2 = 3/L     →    L = 6/√3 = 2√3 ≈ 3.46 m
> ```
> **Note the second slide is TWICE as high but not twice as long** — because it is much steeper, which is exactly what the contractor intended for the older children.

---

From a point 30 m from a tower's foot, the angle of elevation of the top is 30°. Find the tower's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> tan 30° = h/30
> 1/√3 = h/30
> h = 30/√3 = 10√3 m ≈ 17.32 m
> ```
> **Compare with the earlier 60° example: the same 30 m base at 60° would give `30√3 ≈ 51.96 m`** — three times as tall. **A doubled angle does not double the height.**

---

A kite flies 60 m above the ground with its string at 60° to the ground and no slack. Find the string's length.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the STRING is the hypotenuse and 60 m the opposite side  →  use sin
>
> sin 60° = 60/L
> √3/2 = 60/L
> L = 120/√3 = 40√3 m ≈ 69.28 m
> ```
> **"No slack" is what makes the string a straight line** — and hence the hypotenuse of a genuine right triangle.

---

A 1.5 m tall boy walks towards a 30 m building; the angle of elevation from his eyes rises from 30° to 60°. How far did he walk?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the triangle sits ABOVE eye level, so its height is 30 − 1.5 = 28.5 m
>
> at 30°:  tan 30° = 28.5/d₁    →    d₁ = 28.5√3
> at 60°:  tan 60° = 28.5/d₂    →    d₂ = 28.5/√3 = 9.5√3
>
> distance walked = 28.5√3 − 9.5√3 = 19√3 m ≈ 32.9 m
> ```
> **Subtracting the boy's height at the START is essential.** His eyes, not his feet, are the observer's position — and forgetting this is the single commonest error in the chapter.

---

From a point on the ground, the angles of elevation of the bottom and top of a transmission tower on a 20 m building are 45° and 60°. Find the tower's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> FIRST use the known building height to find the distance:
>
>       tan 45° = 20/d      →      1 = 20/d      →      d = 20 m
>
> THEN use that distance for the total height:
>
>       tan 60° = (20 + t)/20
>       √3 = (20 + t)/20
>       20 + t = 20√3
>       t = 20(√3 − 1) ≈ 14.64 m
> ```
> **The strategy for every two-triangle problem: find the SHARED side first.** Here both triangles stand on the same base d, so once d is known the second triangle is straightforward.

---

A 1.6 m statue stands on a pedestal. From a point on the ground the angle of elevation of the statue's top is 60° and of the pedestal's top is 45°. Find the pedestal's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the pedestal be h and the distance d
>
> tan 45° = h/d          →      h = d                    …(1)
> tan 60° = (h + 1.6)/d  →      h + 1.6 = d√3            …(2)
>
> substituting h = d into (2):
>       d + 1.6 = d√3
>       1.6 = d(√3 − 1)
>       d = 1.6/(√3 − 1) = 1.6(√3 + 1)/2 = 0.8(√3 + 1)
>
> h = d = 0.8(√3 + 1) = 0.8√3 + 0.8 ≈ 2.18 m
> ```
> **Rationalising `1.6/(√3 − 1)` by multiplying above and below by `(√3 + 1)` is what makes the arithmetic manageable**, since `(√3 − 1)(√3 + 1) = 2`.

---

The angle of elevation of a building's top from a tower's foot is 30°, and of the tower's top from the building's foot is 60°. If the tower is 50 m, find the building's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the distance between them be d, and the building's height b
>
> from the BUILDING's foot, looking at the TOWER:
>       tan 60° = 50/d      →      d = 50/√3
>
> from the TOWER's foot, looking at the BUILDING:
>       tan 30° = b/d
>       b = d · tan 30° = (50/√3)(1/√3) = 50/3 ≈ 16.67 m
> ```
> **Both triangles share the SAME horizontal distance d**, which is what links the two pieces of information. **The building is exactly one-third the tower's height**, since `tan 30° · tan 30° = 1/3`.

---

Two poles of equal height stand on opposite sides of an 80 m road. From a point between them the angles of elevation of the tops are 60° and 30°. Find the poles' height and the point's distances.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the point be x from the nearer pole, so 80 − x from the farther
> let the common height be h
>
> tan 60° = h/x         →      h = x√3                  …(1)
> tan 30° = h/(80 − x)  →      h = (80 − x)/√3          …(2)
>
> equate:   x√3 = (80 − x)/√3
>           3x = 80 − x
>           4x = 80    →    x = 20
>
> h = 20√3 ≈ 34.64 m
> ```
> **The poles are 20√3 m tall, and the point is 20 m from one and 60 m from the other.**
>
> **Note the distances are in the ratio 1 : 3**, matching `tan 60° : tan 30°` inverted — the steeper angle means the nearer pole.

A TV tower stands on a canal bank. From directly opposite, the angle of elevation of the top is 60°; from a point 20 m further back it is 30°. Find the tower's height and the canal's width.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the canal width be w and the tower height h
>
> tan 60° = h/w         →      h = w√3                  …(1)
> tan 30° = h/(w + 20)  →      h = (w + 20)/√3          …(2)
>
> equate:   w√3 = (w + 20)/√3
>           3w = w + 20
>           2w = 20    →    w = 10 m
>
> h = 10√3 ≈ 17.32 m
> ```
> **Canal 10 m wide; tower 10√3 m tall.**
>
> **Same structure as the tower-and-shadow problem** — two triangles sharing a height, differing by a known distance along the base.

---

From the top of a 7 m building, the angle of elevation of a cable tower's top is 60° and the angle of depression of its foot is 45°. Find the tower's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the DEPRESSION of the foot gives the horizontal distance:
>       by alternate angles, tan 45° = 7/d    →    d = 7 m
>
> the ELEVATION of the top gives the part ABOVE the building:
>       tan 60° = x/7    →    x = 7√3
>
> total tower height = 7 + 7√3 = 7(1 + √3) ≈ 19.12 m
> ```
> **The observer is at the top of the building, so the tower is split into TWO pieces by the observer's eye level** — the part level with and below the building (7 m) and the part above it (7√3 m). **Both must be added.**

---

From a 75 m lighthouse, the angles of depression of two ships in line are 30° and 45°. Find the distance between the ships.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> by alternate angles the elevations at the ships are 30° and 45°
>
> nearer ship (45°):  tan 45° = 75/d₁   →   d₁ = 75 m
> farther ship (30°): tan 30° = 75/d₂   →   d₂ = 75√3 m
>
> distance between = 75√3 − 75 = 75(√3 − 1) ≈ 54.9 m
> ```
> **The LARGER angle of depression belongs to the NEARER ship** — a steeper downward look means a closer object. **Check this in every such problem; assigning the angles the wrong way round is easy and gives a negative answer.**

---

A 1.2 m tall girl watches a balloon at a height of 88.2 m. The angle of elevation falls from 60° to 30°. How far did the balloon travel?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> height above the girl's EYES:  88.2 − 1.2 = 87 m
>
> at 60°:  tan 60° = 87/d₁   →   d₁ = 87/√3 = 29√3
> at 30°:  tan 30° = 87/d₂   →   d₂ = 87√3
>
> distance travelled = 87√3 − 29√3 = 58√3 m ≈ 100.46 m
> ```
> **Subtracting the girl's height first is again essential** — the triangles have their apex at the balloon and their base at her **eye level**, not the ground.

---

From a tower's top a car is seen at a depression of 30°, and six seconds later at 60°. How long until it reaches the tower's foot?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the tower's height be h
>
> at 30°:  tan 30° = h/d₁   →   d₁ = h√3
> at 60°:  tan 60° = h/d₂   →   d₂ = h/√3
>
> distance covered in 6 s = h√3 − h/√3 = (3h − h)/√3 = 2h/√3
> distance still to go     = h/√3
>
> the speed is uniform, so time is proportional to distance:
>
>       time remaining / 6 = (h/√3) / (2h/√3) = 1/2
>
>       time remaining = 3 seconds
> ```
> **The height h CANCELS completely** — which is why the question can be answered without knowing how tall the tower is. **When an unknown cancels, that is a sign the problem was designed not to need it.**

An electrician needs to reach a point 1.3 m below the top of a 5 m pole using a ladder at 60° to the horizontal. Find the ladder's length and where to place its foot. (√3 = 1.73)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> height to reach:  5 − 1.3 = 3.7 m
>
> the LADDER is the hypotenuse  →  use sin
>       sin 60° = 3.7/L
>       √3/2 = 3.7/L
>       L = 7.4/√3 = 4.28 m
>
> for the foot's distance, we want the ADJACENT side  →  use cot (or tan)
>       cot 60° = d/3.7 = 1/√3
>       d = 3.7/√3 = 2.14 m
> ```
> **Ladder 4.28 m; foot 2.14 m from the pole.**
>
> **The subtraction comes first** — she climbs to 3.7 m, not to the 5 m top.

---

An observer 1.5 m tall stands 28.5 m from a chimney; the angle of elevation of the top from her eyes is 45°. Find the chimney's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> work in the triangle ABOVE eye level:
>
>       tan 45° = AE/28.5
>       1 = AE/28.5      →      AE = 28.5 m
>
> chimney height = 28.5 + 1.5 = 30 m
> ```
> **The 1.5 m must be added back at the end.** The right triangle's base is at **eye level**, so it accounts for only the part of the chimney above her eyes.
>
> **A 45° angle always means the height above eye level EQUALS the horizontal distance**, since `tan 45° = 1` — a useful shortcut to recognise.

---

From a point P, the angle of elevation of a 10 m building's top is 30°; with a flag on top it is 45°. Find the flagstaff's length and the distance from P. (√3 = 1.732)

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> FIRST triangle — the known height gives the shared distance:
>       tan 30° = 10/AP
>       1/√3 = 10/AP      →      AP = 10√3 = 17.32 m
>
> SECOND triangle — now use that distance:
>       let the flagstaff be x, so the total height is 10 + x
>       tan 45° = (10 + x)/(10√3)
>       10 + x = 10√3
>       x = 10(√3 − 1) = 7.32 m
> ```
> **Distance 17.32 m; flagstaff 7.32 m.**
>
> **The order matters: the triangle with ENOUGH information goes first**, and the shared side it yields unlocks the other.

---

The shadow of a tower is 40 m longer when the Sun's altitude is 30° than when it is 60°. Find the tower's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the height be h and the SHORTER shadow x, so the longer is x + 40
>
> tan 60° = h/x         →      h = x√3                 …(1)
> tan 30° = h/(x + 40)  →      h = (x + 40)/√3         …(2)
>
> equate:   x√3 = (x + 40)/√3
>           3x = x + 40
>           2x = 40    →    x = 20
>
> h = 20√3 ≈ 34.64 m
> ```
> **The LOWER Sun gives the LONGER shadow** — 30° is the low Sun, so `x + 40` belongs with it. **Getting that assignment backwards gives a negative height and is the usual error here.**

From the top of a multi-storeyed building, the angles of depression of the top and bottom of an 8 m building are 30° and 45°. Find the taller building's height and the distance between them.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> by alternate angles, ∠PBD = 30° and ∠PAC = 45°
>
> in △PBD:   tan 30° = PD/BD    →    BD = PD√3
> in △PAC:   tan 45° = PC/AC    →    PC = AC
>
> now PC = PD + DC, with DC = 8 (the shorter building) and AC = BD
>
> so   PD + 8 = PD√3
>      8 = PD(√3 − 1)
>      PD = 8/(√3 − 1) = 8(√3 + 1)/2 = 4(√3 + 1)
>
> height = PD + 8 = 4√3 + 4 + 8 = 4(3 + √3) ≈ 18.93 m
> distance = BD = PD√3 = 4√3(√3 + 1) = 4(3 + √3) ≈ 18.93 m
> ```
> **The height and the distance come out equal — as they must, since the 45° angle of depression forces `PC = AC`.**

From a bridge 3 m above the banks, the angles of depression of the two banks are 30° and 45°. Find the river's width.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> by alternate angles, the elevations at the banks are 30° and 45°
>
> in △APD:  tan 30° = 3/AD    →    1/√3 = 3/AD    →    AD = 3√3
> in △PBD:  tan 45° = 3/BD    →    BD = 3
>
> width AB = AD + BD = 3√3 + 3 = 3(√3 + 1) ≈ 8.20 m
> ```
> **The observation point is BETWEEN the two banks**, so the two horizontal distances **ADD** rather than subtract. **Compare the two-ships problem, where both were on the same side and the distances subtracted.**

---

How do you tell whether two horizontal distances should be added or subtracted?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Look at where the observer stands relative to the two objects.**
>
> - **Observer BETWEEN them** — as on the bridge, or the point between two poles — **the distances ADD** to give the total separation.
> - **Both objects on the SAME side** — as with two ships from a lighthouse, or a tower's two shadows — **the distances SUBTRACT** to give the gap between them.
>
> **Drawing the figure settles it instantly**, which is exactly why the first step in every problem is to draw it.

---

List the standard steps for tackling any heights-and-distances problem.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **DRAW the figure**, marking the vertical object, the horizontal ground and the line of sight.
> 2. **Mark the given angle at the correct vertex** — always at the **observer**, transferring by **alternate angles** if it is a depression.
> 3. **Label what you KNOW and what you WANT.**
> 4. **Choose the ratio** containing exactly those two sides.
> 5. **Solve**, then **ADD any eye height or base height** the triangle did not include.
> 6. **CHECK the answer is reasonable** — a tower is not 3 cm tall, a low Sun gives a long shadow, and a bigger angle of elevation means a nearer observer.

Why does trigonometry let us measure things a tape never could?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because in a right triangle, ONE side and ONE acute angle determine every other length.**
>
> You can always measure a **horizontal distance on the ground** and an **angle with a simple instrument** — neither requires touching the object. **The ratio then converts those into the vertical height you cannot reach.**
>
> **It is the same principle as the similar-triangle shadow method of Chapter 6, made precise:** there you needed a second object of known height; here the **table of ratios** replaces it, so a single observation suffices.
>
> **This is how the heights of mountains and the distances of the stars and planets were first found** — and it remains the basis of surveying today.
