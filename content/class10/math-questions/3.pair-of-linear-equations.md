Two lines in a plane can do three things. Name them, and give the number of solutions and the correct term for each.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Lines | Solutions | Name |
> |---|---|---|
> | **INTERSECT at one point** | **exactly one — a UNIQUE solution** | **CONSISTENT** |
> | **PARALLEL** | **NO solution** | **INCONSISTENT** |
> | **COINCIDENT** (the same line) | **INFINITELY MANY** — every point on the line | **DEPENDENT**, and also consistent |
>
> - **CONSISTENT** means the pair **has** a solution.
> - **INCONSISTENT** means it has **none**.
> - **DEPENDENT** means the two equations are **equivalent**.
>
> **A dependent pair is ALWAYS consistent** — having infinitely many solutions is still having a solution.

---

State the ratio test for a pair of linear equations.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Write both in the form `a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0` and `a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0`, then:**
> ```
> a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂                →  INTERSECTING  →  unique solution   (consistent)
> a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂        →  COINCIDENT    →  infinitely many   (dependent)
> a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂        →  PARALLEL      →  no solution       (inconsistent)
> ```
> **The converse also holds**, so the test settles the case completely — you never need to draw the graph to know which it is.

---

Explain WHY the ratio test works.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂` means the two lines have the SAME SLOPE.** One equation's x and y coefficients are simply a multiple of the other's, so the lines point the same way — and lines with the same direction either **never meet** or **coincide entirely**.
>
> **The third ratio decides between those two.**
> - If `c₁/c₂` **matches** as well, the *whole* equation is a multiple of the other, so the two describe the **same line** → coincident.
> - If it **doesn't match**, the lines run parallel but sit in **different places** → parallel, never meeting.
>
> **In one sentence: the first two ratios ask "same direction?", and the third asks "same place?"**

Use the ratio test on `5x − 4y + 8 = 0` and `7x + 6y − 9 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a₁/a₂ = 5/7          b₁/b₂ = −4/6 = −2/3
>
> 5/7 ≠ −2/3
> ```
> **The lines INTERSECT, so there is a UNIQUE solution and the pair is CONSISTENT.**
>
> **Once the first two ratios differ, you can stop** — the third is irrelevant.

---

Use the ratio test on `9x + 3y + 12 = 0` and `18x + 6y + 24 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a₁/a₂ = 9/18 = 1/2        b₁/b₂ = 3/6 = 1/2        c₁/c₂ = 12/24 = 1/2
>
> all three are equal
> ```
> **The lines are COINCIDENT — infinitely many solutions, and the pair is DEPENDENT (consistent).**
>
> **Indeed the second equation is exactly twice the first**, so it carries no new information.

---

Use the ratio test on `6x − 3y + 10 = 0` and `2x − y + 9 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a₁/a₂ = 6/2 = 3          b₁/b₂ = −3/−1 = 3          c₁/c₂ = 10/9
>
> 3 = 3  but  10/9 ≠ 3
> ```
> **The lines are PARALLEL — NO solution, and the pair is INCONSISTENT.**
>
> **Same direction, different place.**

Are `3x + 2y = 5` and `2x − 3y = 7` consistent?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> in standard form:  3x + 2y − 5 = 0  and  2x − 3y − 7 = 0
>
> a₁/a₂ = 3/2        b₁/b₂ = 2/(−3) = −2/3
>
> 3/2 ≠ −2/3   →  intersecting
> ```
> **CONSISTENT, with a unique solution.**

---

Are `2x − 3y = 8` and `4x − 6y = 9` consistent?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2x − 3y − 8 = 0   and   4x − 6y − 9 = 0
>
> a₁/a₂ = 2/4 = 1/2        b₁/b₂ = −3/−6 = 1/2        c₁/c₂ = −8/−9 = 8/9
>
> 1/2 = 1/2  but  8/9 ≠ 1/2
> ```
> **INCONSISTENT — the lines are parallel and there is no solution.**

Are `5x − 3y = 11` and `−10x + 6y = −22` consistent?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 5x − 3y − 11 = 0   and   −10x + 6y + 22 = 0
>
> a₁/a₂ = 5/(−10) = −1/2
> b₁/b₂ = −3/6    = −1/2
> c₁/c₂ = −11/22  = −1/2
>
> all three equal
> ```
> **CONSISTENT and DEPENDENT — infinitely many solutions.** The second equation is just **−2 times** the first.

---

Are `(4/3)x + 2y = 8` and `2x + 3y = 12` consistent?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (4/3)x + 2y − 8 = 0   and   2x + 3y − 12 = 0
>
> a₁/a₂ = (4/3)/2 = 2/3
> b₁/b₂ = 2/3
> c₁/c₂ = −8/−12 = 2/3
>
> all three equal
> ```
> **CONSISTENT and DEPENDENT — infinitely many solutions.** Multiply the first by 3/2 and you get the second exactly.

---

Are `x − y = 8` and `3x − 3y = 16` consistent? What about `x + y = 5` and `2x + 2y = 10`?

> [!ANSWER]
> **First pair — INCONSISTENT:**
> ```
> a₁/a₂ = 1/3,   b₁/b₂ = −1/−3 = 1/3,   c₁/c₂ = −8/−16 = 1/2
> 1/3 = 1/3  but  1/2 ≠ 1/3   →  parallel, no solution
> ```
> **Second pair — CONSISTENT and DEPENDENT:**
> ```
> 1/2 = 1/2 = −5/−10 = 1/2   →  coincident, infinitely many solutions
> ```
> **The two look almost identical in form** — the difference is that 16 is not `3 × 8` while 10 **is** `2 × 5`. **The third ratio is doing all the work.**

Are `2x + y − 6 = 0` and `4x − 2y − 4 = 0` consistent? If so, solve them.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a₁/a₂ = 2/4 = 1/2        b₁/b₂ = 1/(−2) = −1/2
>
> not equal  →  intersecting  →  CONSISTENT, unique solution
> ```
> **Solving:**
> ```
> 2x + y = 6          …(1)
> 4x − 2y = 4  →  2x − y = 2   …(2)
>
> (1) + (2):  4x = 8  →  x = 2
> then        y = 6 − 4 = 2
>
> solution: x = 2, y = 2
> ```

Ten students took part in a quiz, and the number of girls was 4 more than the number of boys. Find how many of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let x = boys, y = girls
>
> x + y = 10        …(1)
> y = x + 4         …(2)
>
> substituting:  x + (x + 4) = 10  →  2x = 6  →  x = 3
> then           y = 7
> ```
> **3 boys and 7 girls.**
>
> **Check against the words: 3 + 7 = 10 ✔ and 7 is 4 more than 3 ✔**

5 pencils and 7 pens cost ₹50; 7 pencils and 5 pens cost ₹46. Find the cost of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 5x + 7y = 50        …(1)
> 7x + 5y = 46        …(2)
>
> (1) + (2):  12x + 12y = 96   →   x + y = 8
> (2) − (1):  2x − 2y = −4     →   x − y = −2
>
> adding those two:  2x = 6  →  x = 3,  then y = 5
> ```
> **A pencil costs ₹3 and a pen ₹5.**
>
> **The neat trick here: adding and subtracting the two original equations gives two much simpler ones**, because the coefficients are mirror images (5, 7 and 7, 5).

Half the perimeter of a rectangular garden is 36 m, and its length is 4 m more than its width. Find its dimensions.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let length = x, width = y
>
> HALF the perimeter is x + y, so   x + y = 36        …(1)
>                                   x − y = 4         …(2)
>
> (1) + (2):  2x = 40  →  x = 20
> then        y = 16
> ```
> **Length 20 m, width 16 m.**
>
> **Note the setup: the full perimeter is 2(x + y), so HALF of it is simply x + y** — which is exactly why the problem gives you the half rather than the whole.

Given `2x + 3y − 8 = 0`, write a second equation so that the pair is (i) intersecting, (ii) parallel, (iii) coincident.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i) Intersecting — make the coefficient ratios differ:**
> ```
> 3x + 2y − 7 = 0        since 2/3 ≠ 3/2
> ```
> **(ii) Parallel — match the first two ratios but NOT the third:**
> ```
> 4x + 6y − 10 = 0       since 2/4 = 3/6 = 1/2  but  −8/−10 = 4/5 ≠ 1/2
> ```
> **(iii) Coincident — make the whole equation a multiple:**
> ```
> 4x + 6y − 16 = 0       exactly twice the first
> ```
> **All three answers are non-unique** — any equation meeting the ratio condition works.

Draw `x − y + 1 = 0` and `3x + 2y − 12 = 0` and find the vertices of the triangle they form with the x-axis.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Three vertices come from three pairwise intersections.**
> ```
> line 1 meets the x-axis (y = 0):   x + 1 = 0   →  (−1, 0)
> line 2 meets the x-axis (y = 0):   3x = 12     →  (4, 0)
>
> the two lines meet each other:
>    from line 1:  y = x + 1
>    into line 2:  3x + 2(x + 1) = 12  →  5x = 10  →  x = 2, y = 3
>                                                    →  (2, 3)
> ```
> **Vertices: (−1, 0), (4, 0) and (2, 3).**
>
> **The x-axis is itself the third "line" of the triangle** — which is why its equation `y = 0` is used twice.

---

Why do we need algebraic methods at all, when the graphical method exists?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the graphical method cannot read a non-integral answer accurately.**
>
> If the solution is a point like `(√3, 2√7)`, `(−1.75, 3.3)` or `(4/13, 1/19)`, **there is no way to read those coordinates off graph paper without error.**
>
> **Algebraic methods give the exact answer regardless of how awkward it is** — as in the very first substitution example, where the answer is `x = 49/29, y = 19/29`. No graph would ever have produced that.

---

Set out the three steps of the substitution method.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Step 1.** From **either** equation — whichever is convenient — express **one variable in terms of the other**.
> **Step 2.** **Substitute** that into the **other** equation, reducing it to one equation in one variable. Solve it.
> **Step 3.** Put the value back into the **Step 1** expression to find the other variable.
>
> **"Whichever is convenient" means: pick the equation with a coefficient of 1 or −1**, so the rearrangement produces no fractions.

---

Solve `7x − 15y = 2` and `x + 2y = 3` by substitution.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Step 1  from the second (coefficient of x is 1):   x = 3 − 2y
>
> Step 2  7(3 − 2y) − 15y = 2
>         21 − 14y − 15y = 2
>         −29y = −19
>         y = 19/29
>
> Step 3  x = 3 − 2(19/29) = (87 − 38)/29 = 49/29
>
> solution:  x = 49/29,  y = 19/29
> ```
> **Choosing `x + 2y = 3` to rearrange kept the working clean** — starting from the other equation would have meant dividing by 7 at once.

Solve `x + y = 14` and `x − y = 4` by substitution.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> from the second:  x = y + 4
>
> into the first:   (y + 4) + y = 14   →   2y = 10   →   y = 5
> then              x = 9
> ```
> **x = 9, y = 5.**

Solve `s − t = 3` and `s/3 + t/2 = 6`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> clear the fractions in the second — multiply by 6:
>       2s + 3t = 36
>
> from the first:  s = t + 3
>
> 2(t + 3) + 3t = 36
> 2t + 6 + 3t = 36
> 5t = 30   →   t = 6
> then s = 9
> ```
> **s = 9, t = 6.**
>
> **Always clear fractions first.** It costs one line and prevents a great many slips later.

---

Solve `3x − y = 3` and `9x − 3y = 9` by substitution.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> from the first:  y = 3x − 3
>
> into the second: 9x − 3(3x − 3) = 9
>                  9x − 9x + 9 = 9
>                  9 = 9          ← TRUE, no variable left
> ```
> **INFINITELY MANY SOLUTIONS.**
>
> **Why: the second equation is exactly 3 times the first**, so it adds nothing new — the lines coincide. **A true statement with the variables gone is the algebra telling you exactly that.**

Solve `2x + 3y = 11` and `2x − 4y = −24`, then find m such that `y = mx + 3`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> subtracting the second from the first:  7y = 35  →  y = 5
> then  2x = 11 − 15 = −4  →  x = −2
>
> now put (−2, 5) into y = mx + 3:

Taxi charges are a fixed amount plus a rate per km. 10 km costs ₹105 and 15 km costs ₹155. Find the fixed charge, the rate, and the cost of 25 km.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let f = fixed charge, k = charge per km
>
> f + 10k = 105        …(1)
> f + 15k = 155        …(2)
>
> (2) − (1):  5k = 50   →   k = 10
> then        f = 105 − 100 = 5
>
> for 25 km:  5 + 25(10) = ₹255
> ```
> **Fixed charge ₹5, rate ₹10 per km, and 25 km costs ₹255.**
>
> **Note that the cost is NOT proportional to distance** — 25 km costs less than 2.5 times the 10 km fare, because the fixed ₹5 is paid only once.

A fraction becomes 9/11 when 2 is added to both numerator and denominator, and 5/6 when 3 is added to both. Find the fraction.

>

>            55x − 54x − 27 = −20
>            x = 7
> then       y = (42 + 3)/5 = 9
> ```
> **The fraction is 7/9.**
>
> **Check: 9/11 ✔ and 10/12 = 5/6 ✔**

Five years hence Jacob will be three times as old as his son; five years ago he was seven times as old. Find their present ages.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let J and S be the present ages
>
> J + 5 = 3(S + 5)   →   J = 3S + 10        …(1)
> J − 5 = 7(S − 5)   →   J = 7S − 30        …(2)
>
> 3S + 10 = 7S − 30
> 40 = 4S   →   S = 10
> then J = 40
> ```
> **Jacob is 40 and his son is 10.**
>
> **The commonest error in age problems is adjusting only ONE age.** "Five years hence" means **both** ages go up by 5; "five years ago" means **both** come down by 5.

Set out the four steps of the elimination method, and say when to add and when to subtract.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Step 1.** Multiply both equations by suitable **non-zero constants** so that the coefficients of one variable become **numerically equal**.
> **Step 2.** **Add or subtract** the equations to eliminate that variable.
> **Step 3.** Solve the resulting equation in one variable.
> **Step 4.** Substitute back into either **original** equation to find the other variable.
>
> **SUBTRACT when the matching coefficients have the SAME sign; ADD when they have OPPOSITE signs.** Either way, the aim is to make that term vanish.

Two people's incomes are in the ratio 9 : 7 and their expenditures in the ratio 4 : 3. Each saves ₹2000 a month. Find their incomes.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the incomes be 9x and 7x, the expenditures 4y and 3y
> (income − expenditure = saving)
>
> 9x − 4y = 2000        …(1)
> 7x − 3y = 2000        …(2)
>
> ×3:   27x − 12y = 6000        …(3)
> ×4:   28x − 12y = 8000        …(4)
>
> (4) − (3):  x = 2000
> into (1):   18000 − 4y = 2000  →  y = 4000
>
> incomes = 9x = ₹18,000  and  7x = ₹14,000
> ```
> **Check: 18000 : 14000 = 9 : 7 ✔ and expenditures 16000 : 12000 = 4 : 3 ✔**
>
> **Using "9x and 7x" rather than two separate unknowns builds the ratio into the notation** and leaves fewer unknowns to solve for.

> x + y = 9                                    …(1)
> 9(10x + y) = 2(10y + x)
> 90x + 9y = 20y + 2x
> 88x = 11y     →     y = 8x                   …(2)
>
> from (1):  x + 8x = 9   →   x = 1,  y = 8
> ```
> **The number is 18.**
>
> **Check: 9 × 18 = 162 and 2 × 81 = 162 ✔**
>
> **The essential setup: a two-digit number with digits x and y is `10x + y`, NOT `xy`** — and reversing it gives `10y + x`.

A library charges a fixed amount for the first three days and an extra amount per day after that. Saritha paid ₹27 for 7 days and Susy ₹21 for 5 days. Find both charges.

> [!ANSWER]

>

> Susy kept it 5 days    → 2 extra days:   f + 2d = 21        …(2)
>

> then        f = 21 − 6 = 15
> ```
> **Fixed charge ₹15, and ₹3 for each extra day.**
>
> **The step that must not be rushed: 7 days means only 4 EXTRA days, not 7** — the first three are already covered by the fixed charge.

Two rails are represented by `x + 2y − 4 = 0` and `2x + 4y − 12 = 0`. Will they ever cross?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
> ```
> from the first:  x = 4 − 2y
>
> into the second: 2(4 − 2y) + 4y − 12 = 0
>                  8 − 4y + 4y − 12 = 0
>                  −4 = 0          ← FALSE
> ```
> **No common solution, so the rails never cross.**
>
> **Confirming with the ratio test:** `1/2 = 2/4` but `−4/−12 = 1/3 ≠ 1/2` → **parallel lines** ✔ — which is exactly what you want of railway tracks.
