Define a quadratic equation and its standard form.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A QUADRATIC EQUATION in x is an equation of the form**
> ```
> ax² + bx + c = 0,     where a, b, c are real numbers and a ≠ 0
> ```
> **Any equation `p(x) = 0` where p(x) is a polynomial of DEGREE 2 is a quadratic equation.**
>
> **Writing the terms in DESCENDING order of degree gives the STANDARD FORM.**
>
> **a ≠ 0 is essential** — if a were 0 the x² term would vanish and the equation would be linear.

---

Is `(x + 1)² = 2(x − 3)` a quadratic equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x² + 2x + 1 = 2x − 6
> x² + 7 = 0
> ```
> **YES — quadratic**, with a = 1, b = 0, c = 7.
>
> **Note that b = 0 is perfectly allowed** — only a is required to be non-zero.

---

Is `x² − 2x = (−2)(3 − x)` a quadratic equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x² − 2x = −6 + 2x
> x² − 4x + 6 = 0
> ```
> **YES — quadratic.**

---

Is `(x − 2)(x + 1) = (x − 1)(x + 3)` a quadratic equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x² − x − 2 = x² + 2x − 3
>
> the x² terms CANCEL:
>
> −3x + 1 = 0
> ```
> **NO — it is LINEAR.**
>
> **This is exactly why you must expand and collect before judging.** The equation looks quadratic on both sides, but the x² terms are identical and cancel out.

---

Is `(x − 3)(2x + 1) = x(x + 5)` a quadratic equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2x² − 5x − 3 = x² + 5x
> x² − 10x − 3 = 0
> ```
> **YES — quadratic.**
>
> **Here the x² terms do NOT cancel**, because `2x²` and `x²` are different — one x² survives.

---

Is `(2x − 1)(x − 3) = (x + 5)(x − 1)` a quadratic equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2x² − 7x + 3 = x² + 4x − 5
> x² − 11x + 8 = 0
> ```
> **YES — quadratic.**

---

Is `x² + 3x + 1 = (x − 2)²` a quadratic equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x² + 3x + 1 = x² − 4x + 4
>
> the x² terms CANCEL:
>
> 7x − 3 = 0
> ```
> **NO — it is LINEAR.**

---

Is `(x + 2)³ = 2x(x² − 1)` a quadratic equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x³ + 6x² + 12x + 8 = 2x³ − 2x
> 0 = x³ − 6x² − 14x − 8
> ```
> **NO — it is CUBIC.** The x³ terms do **not** cancel here (`x³` against `2x³`), so a degree-3 term survives.

---

Is `x³ − 4x² − x + 1 = (x − 2)³` a quadratic equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x³ − 4x² − x + 1 = x³ − 6x² + 12x − 8
>
> the x³ terms CANCEL:
>
> 2x² − 13x + 9 = 0
> ```
> **YES — quadratic, despite looking cubic.**
>
> **Together with the earlier examples this makes the point twice over:** an equation can **look quadratic and be linear**, or **look cubic and be quadratic**. **Simplify first, then judge the degree.**

---

The area of a rectangular plot is 528 m² and its length is one more than twice its breadth. Set up the quadratic equation.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the breadth be x metres, so the length is (2x + 1) metres
>
> area:   x(2x + 1) = 528
>         2x² + x = 528
>         2x² + x − 528 = 0
> ```

---

The product of two consecutive positive integers is 306. Set up the equation.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the integers be x and x + 1
>
> x(x + 1) = 306
> x² + x − 306 = 0
> ```
> **"Consecutive" is what lets one unknown do the work of two** — the second integer is forced to be x + 1.

---

Rohan's mother is 26 years older than him, and three years from now the product of their ages will be 360. Set up the equation.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let Rohan's present age be x, so his mother's is x + 26
>
> three years from now:   x + 3   and   x + 29
>
> (x + 3)(x + 29) = 360
> x² + 32x + 87 = 360
> x² + 32x − 273 = 0
> ```
> **Both ages must be advanced by 3** — a very common slip is to advance only one.

---

A train covers 480 km at a uniform speed. Had the speed been 8 km/h less, it would have taken 3 hours more. Set up the equation.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the speed be x km/h;  time = distance / speed
>
> time at speed x:      480/x
> time at speed x − 8:  480/(x − 8)
>
> the slower journey takes 3 hours MORE:
>
>      480/(x − 8) − 480/x = 3
>
> multiply through by x(x − 8):
>
>      480x − 480(x − 8) = 3x(x − 8)
>      3840 = 3x² − 24x
>      x² − 8x − 1280 = 0
> ```
> **Check the direction of the "3 hours more"** — the slower speed gives the **larger** time, so it goes first in the subtraction.

---

What is a root of a quadratic equation, and how does it relate to the zeroes of a polynomial?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A real number α is a ROOT of `ax² + bx + c = 0` if `aα² + bα + c = 0`.** We also say α **satisfies** the equation, or is a **solution**.
>
> **The ZEROES of the polynomial `ax² + bx + c` and the ROOTS of the equation `ax² + bx + c = 0` are exactly the SAME numbers** — two names for one idea.
>
> **Since a quadratic polynomial has at most two zeroes, a quadratic equation has AT MOST TWO ROOTS.**

---

Why does setting each factor to zero give the roots?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a PRODUCT is zero only if at least one FACTOR is zero.** There is no way to multiply two non-zero numbers and get nothing.
>
> So once you have `(2x − 3)(x − 1) = 0`, the only possibilities are `2x − 3 = 0` or `x − 1 = 0`, and each gives a root.
>
> **This is why factorising is worth the effort** — it converts one hard quadratic condition into two easy linear ones.

---

Find the roots of `x² − 3x − 10 = 0` by factorisation.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> need two numbers multiplying to −10 and adding to −3:  −5 and 2
>
> x² − 5x + 2x − 10 = x(x − 5) + 2(x − 5) = (x − 5)(x + 2)
>
> roots: x = 5 and x = −2
> ```
> **Check: 25 − 15 − 10 = 0 ✔ and 4 + 6 − 10 = 0 ✔**

---

Find the roots of `2x² + x − 6 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2 × (−6) = −12, and 4 + (−3) = 1
>
> 2x² + 4x − 3x − 6 = 2x(x + 2) − 3(x + 2) = (2x − 3)(x + 2)
>
> roots: x = 3/2 and x = −2
> ```

---

Find the roots of `√2 x² + 7x + 5√2 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> √2 × 5√2 = 10, and 5 + 2 = 7
>
> √2x² + 5x + 2x + 5√2 = x(√2x + 5) + √2(√2x + 5)
>                       = (√2x + 5)(x + √2)
>
> roots: x = −5/√2 and x = −√2
> ```
> **The method is unchanged by the surds** — `√2 · √2 = 2` is what makes the second grouping work.

---

Find the roots of `2x² − x + 1/8 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> clear the fraction — multiply by 8:
>
> 16x² − 8x + 1 = 0
> (4x − 1)² = 0
>
> 4x − 1 = 0  →  x = 1/4
>
> roots: 1/4 and 1/4  (a REPEATED root)
> ```
> **The factor appears twice, so the root is counted twice** — this is the "two equal roots" case.

---

Find the roots of `100x² − 20x + 1 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 100x² − 10x − 10x + 1 = 10x(10x − 1) − 1(10x − 1) = (10x − 1)²
>
> 10x − 1 = 0  →  x = 1/10
>
> roots: 1/10 and 1/10  (repeated)
> ```
> **Recognising `(10x − 1)²` directly is quicker** — it is a perfect square, since `100x² = (10x)²`, `1 = 1²` and `20x = 2(10x)(1)`.

---

Find the roots of `3x² − 2√6 x + 2 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3 × 2 = 6, and −√6 + (−√6) = −2√6
>
> 3x² − √6x − √6x + 2 = √3x(√3x − √2) − √2(√3x − √2)
>                      = (√3x − √2)(√3x − √2)
>
> √3x − √2 = 0  →  x = √2/√3
>
> roots: √2/√3 and √2/√3  (repeated)
> ```

---

Solve the prayer hall problem: `2x² + x − 300 = 0`, where x is the breadth in metres and the length is `2x + 1`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2 × (−300) = −600, and −24 + 25 = 1
>
> 2x² − 24x + 25x − 300 = 2x(x − 12) + 25(x − 12) = (x − 12)(2x + 25)
>
> roots: x = 12 and x = −12.5
> ```
> **x is a BREADTH, so it cannot be negative — reject −12.5.**
>
> **Breadth = 12 m, length = 2(12) + 1 = 25 m.**
>
> **Check: 12 × 25 = 300 m² ✔ — and always check the AREA, not just the algebra.**

---

John and Jivanti have 45 marbles between them. Each loses 5, and the product of what remains is 124. How many did each start with?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let John have x, so Jivanti has 45 − x
> after losing 5 each:  x − 5  and  40 − x
>
> (x − 5)(40 − x) = 124
> −x² + 45x − 200 = 124
> x² − 45x + 324 = 0
>
> −36 and −9 multiply to 324 and add to −45:
> (x − 36)(x − 9) = 0   →   x = 36 or x = 9
> ```
> **John had 36 and Jivanti 9, or John had 9 and Jivanti 36.**
>
> **Both roots are valid here** — the problem is symmetric in the two people, so neither can be rejected.

---

A cottage industry makes toys whose cost each in rupees is 55 minus the number produced. One day's total cost was ₹750. How many toys were made?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let x toys be made; cost per toy = 55 − x
>
> x(55 − x) = 750
> x² − 55x + 750 = 0
>
> −25 and −30 multiply to 750 and add to −55:
> (x − 25)(x − 30) = 0   →   x = 25 or x = 30
> ```
> **Either 25 toys (at ₹30 each) or 30 toys (at ₹25 each).** Both fit the conditions.

---

Find two numbers whose sum is 27 and product is 182.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let one be x, so the other is 27 − x
>
> x(27 − x) = 182
> x² − 27x + 182 = 0
>
> −13 and −14 multiply to 182 and add to −27:
> (x − 13)(x − 14) = 0   →   x = 13 or 14
> ```
> **The numbers are 13 and 14.**
>
> **The two roots here are the two numbers themselves** — which is what happens whenever the quadratic comes from a sum-and-product pair.

---

Find two consecutive positive integers whose squares sum to 365.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let them be x and x + 1
>
> x² + (x + 1)² = 365
> x² + x² + 2x + 1 = 365
> 2x² + 2x − 364 = 0
> x² + x − 182 = 0
>
> 14 and −13 multiply to −182 and add to 1:
> (x + 14)(x − 13) = 0   →   x = −14 or x = 13
> ```
> **The integers must be POSITIVE, so reject −14.**
>
> **The numbers are 13 and 14.** Check: `169 + 196 = 365` ✔

---

The altitude of a right triangle is 7 cm less than its base, and the hypotenuse is 13 cm. Find the other two sides.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the base be b, so the altitude is b − 7
>
> by Pythagoras:   b² + (b − 7)² = 13²
>                  b² + b² − 14b + 49 = 169
>                  2b² − 14b − 120 = 0
>                  b² − 7b − 60 = 0
>
> −12 and 5 multiply to −60 and add to −7:
> (b − 12)(b + 5) = 0   →   b = 12 or b = −5
> ```
> **A base cannot be negative, so b = 12 and the altitude is 5.**
>
> **The sides are 12 cm and 5 cm.** Check: `144 + 25 = 169 = 13²` ✔

---

A pottery workshop makes articles costing (in rupees) 3 more than twice the number produced. The day's total cost was ₹90. Find the number of articles and the cost of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let x articles be made; cost each = 2x + 3
>
> x(2x + 3) = 90
> 2x² + 3x − 90 = 0
>
> 2 × (−90) = −180, and 15 + (−12) = 3:
> 2x² + 15x − 12x − 90 = x(2x + 15) − 6(2x + 15) = (2x + 15)(x − 6)
>
> x = 6  or  x = −15/2
> ```
> **A number of articles cannot be negative or fractional, so x = 6.**
>
> **6 articles, each costing 2(6) + 3 = ₹15.** Check: `6 × 15 = ₹90` ✔

State the quadratic formula and say where it comes from.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the roots of ax² + bx + c = 0 are
>
>       x = ( −b ± √(b² − 4ac) ) / (2a)          provided b² − 4ac ≥ 0
> ```
> **It is obtained by COMPLETING THE SQUARE on the general equation** — the same method **Sridharacharya (1025 CE)** used, as quoted by Bhaskara II.
>
> **Why it matters: it works for EVERY quadratic**, including those that will not factorise neatly. Factorisation is faster when it works; the formula never fails.

---

What is the discriminant, and what are the three cases?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The DISCRIMINANT is `D = b² − 4ac`** — the expression under the square-root sign in the formula.
>
> | Discriminant | Roots |
> |---|---|
> | **D `>` 0** | **two DISTINCT real roots** |
> | **D = 0** | **two EQUAL real roots**, both `−b/2a` |
> | **D `<` 0** | **NO real roots** |

---

Explain why each of the three cases follows from the formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x = ( −b ± √D ) / (2a)
> ```
> - **D `>` 0:** √D is a **positive** number, so `−b + √D` and `−b − √D` are **different** → **two distinct roots**.
> - **D = 0:** √D is **0**, so both branches collapse to the same value `−b/2a` → **one value, counted twice**.
> - **D `<` 0:** **no real number squares to a negative**, so √D does not exist among the reals → **no real roots**.
>
> **This matches the three parabola pictures from Chapter 2 exactly** — the curve cuts the x-axis at two points, touches it at one, or misses it entirely.

---

Find the nature of the roots of `2x² − 3x + 5 = 0`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a = 2, b = −3, c = 5
>
> D = b² − 4ac = 9 − 40 = −31 < 0
> ```
> **NO REAL ROOTS.** The parabola lies entirely above the x-axis.

---

Find the nature of the roots of `3x² − 4√3 x + 4 = 0`, and find them if real.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a = 3, b = −4√3, c = 4
>
> D = (−4√3)² − 4(3)(4) = 48 − 48 = 0
> ```
> **TWO EQUAL REAL ROOTS.**
> ```
> x = −b/2a = 4√3/6 = 2√3/3 = 2/√3
>
> both roots are 2/√3
> ```

---

Find the nature of the roots of `2x² − 6x + 3 = 0`, and find them if real.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a = 2, b = −6, c = 3
>
> D = 36 − 24 = 12 > 0     →   TWO DISTINCT REAL ROOTS
>
> x = (6 ± √12)/4 = (6 ± 2√3)/4 = (3 ± √3)/2
>
> roots: (3 + √3)/2 and (3 − √3)/2
> ```
> **This one would not have factorised** — the roots are irrational, which is exactly the situation the formula exists for.

---

Find the discriminant of `3x² − 2x + 1/3 = 0` and its roots.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a = 3, b = −2, c = 1/3
>
> D = 4 − 4(3)(1/3) = 4 − 4 = 0     →   two EQUAL real roots
>
> x = −b/2a = 2/6 = 1/3
>
> both roots are 1/3
> ```

---

Find k so that `2x² + kx + 3 = 0` has two equal roots.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> equal roots means D = 0
>
> k² − 4(2)(3) = 0
> k² = 24
> k = ±2√6
> ```
> **Both signs are valid** — `k = 2√6` and `k = −2√6` each give a perfect square, with the repeated root at `−k/4` in each case.

---

Find k so that `kx(x − 2) + 6 = 0` has two equal roots.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> expand first:   kx² − 2kx + 6 = 0
>
> D = (−2k)² − 4(k)(6) = 4k² − 24k = 0
> 4k(k − 6) = 0
> k = 0  or  k = 6
> ```
> **k = 0 must be REJECTED**, because it would make the x² coefficient zero and the equation would not be quadratic at all (it collapses to `6 = 0`).
>
> **So k = 6.**
>
> **The condition a ≠ 0 in the definition is not decoration** — this is exactly the kind of question where forgetting it gives a wrong extra answer.

Is it possible to design a rectangular mango grove of area 800 m² whose length is twice its breadth?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the breadth be b, so the length is 2b
>
> 2b² = 800
> b² = 400
> b = 20   (rejecting −20)
> ```
> **YES — breadth 20 m, length 40 m.**
>
> **Check: 20 × 40 = 800 m² ✔**

---

Two friends' ages sum to 20, and four years ago the product of their ages was 48. Is this possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the ages be x and 20 − x
>
> four years ago:  x − 4  and  16 − x
>
> (x − 4)(16 − x) = 48
> 16x − x² − 64 + 4x = 48
> −x² + 20x − 112 = 0
> x² − 20x + 112 = 0
>
> D = 400 − 448 = −48 < 0
> ```
> **NO — the situation is IMPOSSIBLE.** With a negative discriminant there are no real roots, so no pair of ages satisfies both conditions.
>
> **Notice how little work this took.** You never had to solve the equation — **the sign of the discriminant answered the question by itself.**

---

Is it possible to design a rectangular park with perimeter 80 m and area 400 m²?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the sides be x and y
>
> perimeter:  2(x + y) = 80   →   x + y = 40   →   y = 40 − x
> area:       x(40 − x) = 400
>             x² − 40x + 400 = 0
>
> D = 1600 − 1600 = 0     →   two EQUAL roots
>
> x = 40/2 = 20,  so y = 20
> ```
> **YES — and the "rectangle" turns out to be a 20 m × 20 m SQUARE.**
>
> **The zero discriminant is telling you something real:** for a given perimeter, **the square is the ONLY rectangle achieving the maximum area**, so 400 m² is the largest area possible with an 80 m perimeter — and there is exactly one way to reach it.

---

A pole is to be placed on the boundary of a circular park of diameter 13 m so that the difference of its distances from two diametrically opposite gates is 7 m. Is it possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let BP = x, so AP = x + 7
>
> AB is a DIAMETER, so ∠APB = 90°       (angle in a semicircle)
>
> by Pythagoras:  (x + 7)² + x² = 13²
>                 x² + 14x + 49 + x² = 169
>                 2x² + 14x − 120 = 0
>                 x² + 7x − 60 = 0
>
> D = 49 + 240 = 289 > 0    →   two real roots, so YES it is possible
>
> x = (−7 ± 17)/2   →   x = 5  or  x = −12
> ```
> **A distance cannot be negative, so x = 5.**
>
> **The pole is 5 m from gate B and 12 m from gate A.** Check: `5² + 12² = 169 = 13²` ✔
>
> **The key geometric step is `∠APB = 90°`** — the angle in a semicircle is a right angle, which is what lets Pythagoras be used at all.

---

Why must you always check both roots against the situation in a word problem?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the algebra has no idea what the letters mean.**
>
> `x² − 7x − 60 = 0` gives 12 and −5 whether x is a length, a temperature or a bank balance. **Only YOU know that a base of −5 cm is impossible.**
>
> **Quantities that cannot be negative:** lengths, breadths, areas, ages, speeds, numbers of objects, costs.
> **Quantities that must also be whole numbers:** numbers of toys, articles, people, notes.
>
> **But do not reject roots automatically either** — in the marbles problem BOTH roots were valid, because the problem was symmetric in the two people. **Check each root against the words, and keep every one that fits.**
