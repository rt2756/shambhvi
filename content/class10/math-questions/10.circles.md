A line and a circle are drawn in a plane. What are the three possibilities?

> [!ANSWER]
> | Common points | The line is called |
> |---|---|
> | **NONE** | a **NON-INTERSECTING** line |
> | **TWO** | a **SECANT** |
> | **ONE only** | a **TANGENT** |
>
> **There is no fourth possibility.** A straight line **cannot** meet a circle in three or more points — a circle bends away from any straight line, so once a line has entered and left, it cannot come back.

---

Define a tangent and explain why it is called a special case of a secant.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A TANGENT is a line that intersects a circle at ONLY ONE point** — the **POINT OF CONTACT**, where the tangent is said to **touch** the circle.
>
> **THE TANGENT IS A SPECIAL CASE OF THE SECANT, IN WHICH THE TWO END POINTS OF THE CHORD COINCIDE.**
>
> **You can watch it happen.** Rotate a straight wire about a point P on a circle: for most positions it cuts the circle at P and one other point Q. **As the wire turns towards the tangent position, Q creeps nearer and nearer to P until the two MERGE.** Rotate past that position and a second point immediately reappears on the other side.
>
> **A tangent is a secant whose two intersection points have become one** — which is exactly why it is the boundary case.

---

How many tangents can a circle have?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Infinitely many.**
>
> **There is exactly ONE tangent at each POINT of the circle**, and a circle has **infinitely many points** — so there are infinitely many tangents altogether.
>
> **Distinguish the two counts carefully:** *one tangent per point*, but *infinitely many tangents in all*.

---

Fill in the blanks: a tangent intersects a circle in ___ point(s); a line intersecting a circle in two points is a ___; a circle can have at most ___ parallel tangents.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **A tangent intersects a circle in ONE point.**
> - **A line intersecting a circle in two points is a SECANT.**
> - **A circle can have at most TWO parallel tangents.**
> - **The common point of a tangent and the circle is the POINT OF CONTACT.**
>
> **Why only two parallel tangents: their points of contact must be at opposite ends of a DIAMETER.** Both radii are perpendicular to the same direction, so they lie along one straight line through the centre — and a diameter has just two ends.

---

State and prove Theorem 10.1.

> [!ANSWER]
> **THE TANGENT AT ANY POINT OF A CIRCLE IS PERPENDICULAR TO THE RADIUS THROUGH THE POINT OF CONTACT.**
>
> **Proof.** Let XY be a tangent at P to a circle with centre O.
> ```
> Take any point Q on XY other than P, and join OQ.
>
> Q must lie OUTSIDE the circle — for if Q were inside, the line XY would
> cut the circle TWICE and would be a SECANT, not a tangent.
>
> Therefore OQ > OP (the radius).
>
> This holds for EVERY point of XY except P itself.
>
> So OP is the SHORTEST of all distances from O to points of XY.
>
> The shortest distance from a point to a line is the PERPENDICULAR distance.
>
> Therefore OP ⊥ XY.        ∎
> ```

---

In that proof, which single observation does everything depend on?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That EVERY OTHER POINT of the tangent lies OUTSIDE the circle.**
>
> **That is precisely what "meets the circle only once" means** — and it immediately makes OP shorter than every other distance from O to the line, hence the perpendicular.
>
> **Notice the proof never measures an angle.** It converts the geometric claim into a claim about **which distance is shortest**, which is far easier to settle.

---

What two consequences follow from Theorem 10.1?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **At any point on a circle there can be ONE AND ONLY ONE tangent.**
> **Why: the tangent must be perpendicular to the radius at that point**, and through a given point there is exactly **one** line perpendicular to a given line.
>
> 2. **The line containing the radius through the point of contact is called the NORMAL to the circle at that point.**

---

How does a moving bicycle wheel illustrate Theorem 10.1?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The spokes lie along the RADII, and the ground is a TANGENT to the wheel** — it touches the circle at exactly one point at any instant.
>
> **In EVERY position of the wheel, the spoke pointing at the ground stands at RIGHT ANGLES to the ground.** That is Theorem 10.1, visible as the wheel turns.

---

How many tangents can be drawn through a point inside, on, and outside a circle?

> [!ANSWER]
> | Position of the point | Number of tangents |
> |---|---|
> | **INSIDE** the circle | **NONE** |
> | **ON** the circle | **exactly ONE** |
> | **OUTSIDE** the circle | **exactly TWO** |
>
> **Why an inside point gives none: any line through it MUST come out the other side**, so it necessarily cuts the circle **twice** — every such line is a secant, and none can be a tangent.

---

What is meant by the "length of the tangent" from an external point?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The length of the SEGMENT from the external point P to the POINT OF CONTACT.**
>
> **It is not the length of the whole tangent line — a line has no length.** It is specifically the piece between P and where the line touches the circle.

---

State and prove Theorem 10.2.

> [!ANSWER]
> **THE LENGTHS OF THE TANGENTS DRAWN FROM AN EXTERNAL POINT TO A CIRCLE ARE EQUAL.**
>
> **Proof.** Let PQ and PR be the tangents from an external point P to a circle with centre O.
> ```
> Join OP, OQ and OR.
>
> ∠OQP = ∠ORP = 90°           (Theorem 10.1 — radius ⊥ tangent)
>
> In right triangles OQP and ORP:
>       OQ = OR                (radii of the same circle)
>       OP = OP                (common — the hypotenuse of both)
>
> so    △OQP ≅ △ORP           (RHS congruence)
>
> therefore PQ = PR            (CPCT)        ∎
> ```
> **Notice Theorem 10.1 is used first** — without the right angles there would be no RHS to apply.

---

Prove Theorem 10.2 using Pythagoras instead of congruence.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> both triangles are right-angled at the point of contact, so
>
>       PQ² = OP² − OQ²
>       PR² = OP² − OR²
>
> but OQ = OR (both are radii), and OP is common
>
> so    PQ² = PR²       →       PQ = PR        ∎
> ```
> **Shorter than the congruence proof, and it makes the reason plainer:** both tangent lengths are computed from **the same two numbers** — the distance to the centre and the radius.

---

What else follows from the symmetry of the two-tangent figure?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The whole figure is SYMMETRIC ABOUT THE LINE OP**, so the two tangents are mirror images of each other. Therefore:
> ```
> PQ = PR              the tangent lengths are equal
> ∠OPQ = ∠OPR          OP BISECTS the angle between the tangents
> ∠POQ = ∠POR          OP BISECTS the angle at the centre
> ```
> **Recognising the symmetry gives all three results at once**, and explains why so many problems in this chapter reduce to "half the given angle".

---

Prove that the angle between two tangents from an external point is supplementary to the angle subtended at the centre by the chord of contact.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> consider the quadrilateral OQPR (O the centre, Q and R the points of contact)
>
> ∠OQP = ∠ORP = 90°                  (radius ⊥ tangent)
>
> the four angles of any quadrilateral sum to 360°:
>
>       ∠QPR + ∠QOR + 90° + 90° = 360°
>
>       ∠QPR + ∠QOR = 180°           ∎
> ```
> **They are SUPPLEMENTARY.** Given either angle, the other follows instantly — which is exactly what most numerical questions in this chapter require.

---

A tangent PQ at P meets a line through the centre O at Q, with radius 5 cm and OQ = 12 cm. Find PQ.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> OP ⊥ PQ                (radius ⊥ tangent), so △OPQ is right-angled at P
>
> PQ² = OQ² − OP² = 144 − 25 = 119
>
> PQ = √119 cm
> ```
> **The answer is NOT 13 cm.** 13 would be the **hypotenuse** of a 5–12 right triangle — but here **12 is already the hypotenuse OQ**, because the right angle is at P, not at O.

---

From a point Q the tangent length is 24 cm and the distance to the centre is 25 cm. Find the radius.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the right angle is at the point of contact, so the DISTANCE TO THE CENTRE
> is the hypotenuse
>
> radius² = 25² − 24² = 625 − 576 = 49
>
> radius = 7 cm
> ```
> **Always identify the hypotenuse first: it is the line from the external point to the CENTRE**, since the right angle sits at the point of contact.

---

The tangent from a point A, 5 cm from the centre, is 4 cm long. Find the radius.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> radius² = 5² − 4² = 25 − 16 = 9
>
> radius = 3 cm
> ```
> **A 3–4–5 triangle**, with the 5 cm (distance to the centre) as the hypotenuse.

---

TP and TQ are tangents to a circle with centre O, and ∠POQ = 110°. Find ∠PTQ.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ∠PTQ + ∠POQ = 180°               (supplementary, as proved above)
>
> ∠PTQ = 180° − 110° = 70°
> ```

---

Tangents PA and PB from P are inclined at 80°. Find ∠POA.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ∠AOB = 180° − 80° = 100°         (supplementary)
>
> OP BISECTS ∠AOB (by the symmetry of the figure), so
>
> ∠POA = 100°/2 = 50°
> ```
> **Alternatively, work in the right triangle OAP:**
> ```
> ∠OAP = 90°, and ∠APO = 80°/2 = 40°     (OP bisects the angle at P)
> so ∠POA = 180° − 90° − 40° = 50°        ✔  same answer
> ```

---

Prove that the tangents at the ends of a diameter are parallel.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let AB be a diameter, with tangents at A and at B
>
> the tangent at A ⊥ OA                (Theorem 10.1)
> the tangent at B ⊥ OB                (Theorem 10.1)
>
> but OA and OB lie along the SAME straight line AB (it is a diameter)
>
> so both tangents are perpendicular to the SAME line
>
> two lines perpendicular to the same line are PARALLEL      ∎
> ```
> **This also explains why a circle has AT MOST TWO parallel tangents** — parallel tangents must touch at the ends of a diameter, and a diameter has only two ends.

---

Prove that the perpendicular at the point of contact to a tangent passes through the centre.

> [!ANSWER]
> **By CONTRADICTION.**
> ```
> Let XY be the tangent at P, and suppose the perpendicular to XY at P
> does NOT pass through the centre O.
>
> But by Theorem 10.1, OP ⊥ XY — so OP IS a perpendicular to XY at P.
>
> Through the point P there is only ONE line perpendicular to XY.
>
> So the perpendicular at P must be the line OP itself, which passes
> through O.        ∎
> ```
> **This is the CONVERSE of Theorem 10.1**, and it follows from the uniqueness of the perpendicular through a given point.

---

Two concentric circles have radii 5 cm and 3 cm. Find the length of the chord of the larger circle that touches the smaller one.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the chord TOUCHES the smaller circle, so it is a TANGENT to it —
> hence perpendicular to the 3 cm radius at the point of contact
>
> that point is therefore the MIDPOINT of the chord
>       (the perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects it)
>
> half-chord = √(5² − 3²) = √(25 − 9) = 4
>
> chord = 2 × 4 = 8 cm
> ```
> **Two facts combine here:** *tangent ⊥ radius* (this chapter) and *the perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord* (Class 9). **Together they turn a chord problem into a single Pythagoras calculation.**

---

Quadrilateral ABCD circumscribes a circle. Prove that `AB + CD = AD + BC`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the points of contact on AB, BC, CD, DA be P, Q, R, S
>
> each vertex is an EXTERNAL point with two equal tangent lengths:
>
>       AP = AS = p          BP = BQ = q
>       CQ = CR = r          DR = DS = s
>
> AB + CD = (p + q) + (r + s)
> AD + BC = (p + s) + (q + r)
>
> both equal p + q + r + s        ∎
> ```
> **The sums of the OPPOSITE sides are equal**, and the reason is simply that **every one of the four tangent lengths gets counted exactly once on each side of the equation.**

---

Prove that a parallelogram circumscribing a circle is a rhombus.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> for any circumscribing quadrilateral:   AB + CD = AD + BC
>
> for a PARALLELOGRAM:                    AB = CD  and  AD = BC
>
> substituting:   AB + AB = AD + AD
>                 2AB = 2AD
>                 AB = AD
>
> so all four sides are equal — the parallelogram is a RHOMBUS      ∎
> ```
> **A neat consequence: a rectangle can circumscribe a circle only if it is a square.**

---

XY and X′Y′ are parallel tangents to a circle with centre O, and another tangent AB touches at C, meeting XY at A and X′Y′ at B. Prove ∠AOB = 90°.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the tangent XY touch at P and X′Y′ touch at Q
>
> A is external, with tangents AP and AC, so OA BISECTS ∠PAC
> B is external, with tangents BQ and BC, so OB BISECTS ∠QBC
>
> XY ∥ X′Y′ with AB as a transversal, so the co-interior angles satisfy
>       ∠PAB + ∠QBA = 180°
>
> halving:   ∠OAB + ∠OBA = 90°
>
> in △AOB:   ∠AOB = 180° − 90° = 90°        ∎
> ```
> **The two bisections are what make it work** — and both come from the symmetry of the two-tangent figure at each external point.

---

A triangle ABC circumscribes a circle of radius 4 cm, with BD = 8 cm and DC = 6 cm where D is the point of contact on BC. Find AB and AC.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the points of contact on CA and AB be E and F, and let AE = AF = x
>
> equal tangents:   BD = BF = 8,   CD = CE = 6
>
> so   AB = x + 8,   AC = x + 6,   BC = 8 + 6 = 14
>
> semi-perimeter  s = (x + 8 + x + 6 + 14)/2 = x + 14
>
> by Heron:  area = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)]
>                 = √[(x+14)(x+14−14)(x+14−x−6)(x+14−x−8)]
>                 = √[(x+14)(x)(8)(6)] = √[48x(x+14)]
>
> also, area = r × s = 4(x + 14)
>
> equate:   4(x+14) = √[48x(x+14)]
>           16(x+14)² = 48x(x+14)
>           16(x+14) = 48x
>           x + 14 = 3x
>           x = 7
>
> AB = 15 cm,   AC = 13 cm
> ```
> **The key relation is `area = r × s`** for any triangle with an inscribed circle of radius r — it provides the second expression for the area that makes the equation solvable.

---

Prove that opposite sides of a quadrilateral circumscribing a circle subtend supplementary angles at the centre.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> let the points of contact on AB, BC, CD, DA be P, Q, R, S, with centre O
>
> at each vertex, O lies on the BISECTOR of the angle between the two tangents,
> so the two triangles at each vertex are congruent:
>
>       ∠AOP = ∠AOS,  ∠BOP = ∠BOQ,  ∠COQ = ∠COR,  ∠DOR = ∠DOS
>
> the eight angles round O sum to 360°, and they pair up as four equal pairs
>
> ∠AOB + ∠COD = (∠AOP + ∠BOP) + (∠COR + ∠DOR)
>              = (∠AOS + ∠BOQ) + (∠COQ + ∠DOS)
>              = ∠BOC + ∠AOD
>
> and since all eight add to 360°, each of these two sums is 180°     ∎
> ```
> **Once again everything follows from the bisection at each external point** — the same symmetry that gave equal tangent lengths.

---

Why must the two points of contact of parallel tangents be diametrically opposite?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because each tangent is PERPENDICULAR TO ITS RADIUS at the point of contact.**
>
> **If the two tangents are parallel, both radii are perpendicular to the SAME direction** — so the two radii are parallel to each other. **But both start at the centre**, and two parallel lines through the same point are the **same line**.
>
> **So the two radii lie along one straight line through the centre — a DIAMETER** — and the points of contact are its two ends.

---

A student says "there are exactly two tangents from any point to a circle". Correct them.

> [!ANSWER]
> **That is true only for a point OUTSIDE the circle.**
> ```
> point INSIDE  → NO tangents        (every line through it cuts the circle twice)
> point ON      → exactly ONE        (perpendicular to the radius there)
> point OUTSIDE → exactly TWO
> ```
> **The position of the point relative to the circle decides the count**, and the statement is right in only one of the three cases.
