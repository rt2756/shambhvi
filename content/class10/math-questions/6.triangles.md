Define similar figures, and state the relationship between congruence and similarity.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two figures having the SAME SHAPE (and not necessarily the same size) are SIMILAR.**
>
> **ALL CONGRUENT FIGURES ARE SIMILAR, BUT SIMILAR FIGURES NEED NOT BE CONGRUENT.**
>
> **Congruence is the special case of similarity in which the sizes happen to match too** — same shape *and* same size.

---

Fill in the blanks: all circles are ___; all squares are ___; all ___ triangles are similar.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **All circles are SIMILAR.** (They are congruent only if the radii match.)
> - **All squares are SIMILAR.**
> - **All EQUILATERAL triangles are similar.**
>
> **Why these three: in each case the SHAPE is completely fixed and only the SIZE can vary.** All the angles are determined (90° for squares, 60° for equilateral triangles), and all sides scale together.
>
> **Isosceles triangles are NOT all similar** — one can be tall and narrow, another short and wide.

---

Give two examples of similar figures and two of non-similar figures.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Similar:**
> - Two photographs printed from the same negative at different sizes
> - Any two circles; any two squares; any two equilateral triangles
> - A map and the region it represents
>
> **Non-similar:**
> - A circle and a square (different shapes entirely)
> - A square and a rectangle that is not a square
> - Two photographs of the same person at 10 and at 40, printed the same size — **same size, different shape**

---

State the two conditions for two polygons to be similar, and name the common ratio.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two polygons of the SAME NUMBER OF SIDES are similar if:**
> **(i) their CORRESPONDING ANGLES are EQUAL, and**
> **(ii) their CORRESPONDING SIDES are IN THE SAME RATIO (proportional).**
>
> **That common ratio is the SCALE FACTOR** (or Representative Fraction) — the number used when drawing world maps and building blueprints.

---

Show by example that each condition alone is insufficient for similarity.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A SQUARE and a RECTANGLE (not a square):**
> **All corresponding angles are 90°, so condition (i) HOLDS** — but the sides are not in the same ratio (a 2×2 square against a 2×5 rectangle gives ratios 1 and 2/5). **NOT similar.**
>
> **A SQUARE and a RHOMBUS (not a square):**
> **All four sides of each are equal, so the sides ARE in the same ratio and condition (ii) HOLDS** — but the angles differ (90° against, say, 60° and 120°). **NOT similar.**
>
> **One example satisfies each condition and fails to be similar** — which is exactly why the definition demands both.

---

Use the photograph analogy to explain what similarity means in practice.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Print the same negative at 35 mm and at 45 mm.**
>
> - **Every line segment in the smaller print becomes 45/35 of its length** in the bigger one — all lengths scale by the **same factor**.
> - **Every angle between corresponding line segments stays EXACTLY THE SAME.**
>
> **That is precisely the definition:** equal angles, sides in a fixed ratio. **"Same shape, different size" is not a vague phrase — it means these two things.**

---

Describe the shadow activity and explain why the shadow is similar to the original.

> [!ANSWER]
> Hold a cardboard quadrilateral ABCD **parallel to a table**, between a ceiling bulb at O and the table, and mark the outline of its shadow A′B′C′D′.
>
> **Why it works: LIGHT TRAVELS IN STRAIGHT LINES**, so A′ lies on ray OA, B′ on ray OB, C′ on OC and D′ on OD.
>
> **Measuring gives:**
> ```
> ∠A = ∠A′, ∠B = ∠B′, ∠C = ∠C′, ∠D = ∠D′
> AB/A′B′ = BC/B′C′ = CD/C′D′ = DA/D′A′
> ```
> **Both conditions hold, so the shadow is an ENLARGEMENT — a similar figure.**
>
> **The parallel placement matters** — tilt the cardboard and the shadow is distorted, not merely enlarged.

---

State the Basic Proportionality Theorem, and say who it is named after.

> [!ANSWER]
> **THEOREM 6.1 — the BASIC PROPORTIONALITY THEOREM, or THALES THEOREM:**
>
> **If a line is drawn PARALLEL to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, then the other two sides are divided in the SAME RATIO.**
>
> **In △ABC with DE ∥ BC:**
> ```
> AD/DB = AE/EC
> ```
> **Named after Thales (640–546 BCE)**, who stated that the ratio of any two corresponding sides in two equiangular triangles is always the same, and is believed to have used this result.

---

Prove the Basic Proportionality Theorem.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Given △ABC with DE ∥ BC, D on AB and E on AC. Join BE and CD, and drop DM ⊥ AC and EN ⊥ AB.**
> ```
> ar(ADE) = ½ · AD · EN          ar(BDE) = ½ · DB · EN
> ar(ADE) = ½ · AE · DM          ar(DEC) = ½ · EC · DM
>
> ar(ADE)/ar(BDE) = AD/DB        …(1)   — the height EN is common
> ar(ADE)/ar(DEC) = AE/EC        …(2)   — the height DM is common
>
> △BDE and △DEC are on the SAME BASE DE and between the SAME PARALLELS BC and DE
>
> so   ar(BDE) = ar(DEC)         …(3)
>
> from (1), (2) and (3):    AD/DB = AE/EC        ∎
> ```

---

In that proof, where exactly is the parallel condition used, and why does that matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In step (3) alone** — the claim that `ar(BDE) = ar(DEC)`.
>
> **Two triangles on the SAME BASE and BETWEEN THE SAME PARALLELS have EQUAL AREAS**, because they share a base and have the same height. **DE ∥ BC is exactly what guarantees the same height.**
>
> **Remove the parallelism and the two areas differ, and the whole proof collapses.**
>
> **Why noticing this matters generally: in a sound proof, EVERY hypothesis is used somewhere.** If you can complete a proof without using one of the given conditions, either that condition is unnecessary or your proof has a gap.

---

State the converse of the Basic Proportionality Theorem, and say when each version is used.

> [!ANSWER]
> **THEOREM 6.2: If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the SAME RATIO, then the line is PARALLEL to the third side.**
>
> **Together the two theorems give an "if and only if":**
> ```
> DE ∥ BC        ⟺        AD/DB = AE/EC
> ```
> - **Use 6.1 when you KNOW the lines are parallel and want the RATIO.**
> - **Use 6.2 when you KNOW the ratio and want to PROVE parallelism.**
>
> **Nearly every question in this section is one or the other** — identify which and the method follows.

---

If DE ∥ BC in △ABC, prove that `AD/AB = AE/AC`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> DE ∥ BC   so   AD/DB = AE/EC          (Theorem 6.1)
>
> take reciprocals:   DB/AD = EC/AE
>
> add 1 to both sides:  DB/AD + 1 = EC/AE + 1
>
>                       (DB + AD)/AD = (EC + AE)/AE
>
>                       AB/AD = AC/AE
>
> take reciprocals:     AD/AB = AE/AC        ∎
> ```
> **The trick is "add 1 to both sides"** — it converts a ratio involving the *parts* into one involving the *whole side*, since `DB + AD = AB`.

---

In △ABC with DE ∥ BC, AD = 1.5 cm, DB = 3 cm and AE = 1 cm. Find EC.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> by Theorem 6.1:   AD/DB = AE/EC
>
> 1.5/3 = 1/EC
> 0.5 = 1/EC
> EC = 2 cm
> ```

---

In △ABC with DE ∥ BC, DB = 7.2 cm, AE = 1.8 cm and EC = 5.4 cm. Find AD.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AD/DB = AE/EC
>
> AD/7.2 = 1.8/5.4 = 1/3
>
> AD = 7.2/3 = 2.4 cm
> ```

---

E and F lie on PQ and PR of △PQR with PE = 3.9 cm, EQ = 3 cm, PF = 3.6 cm, FR = 2.4 cm. Is EF ∥ QR?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PE/EQ = 3.9/3   = 1.3
> PF/FR = 3.6/2.4 = 1.5
>
> 1.3 ≠ 1.5
> ```
> **NO — EF is not parallel to QR.**
>
> **By the converse (Theorem 6.2), parallelism would require the two ratios to be EQUAL.** They are not, so the line cannot be parallel.

---

E and F lie on PQ and PR with PE = 4 cm, QE = 4.5 cm, PF = 8 cm, RF = 9 cm. Is EF ∥ QR?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PE/EQ = 4/4.5 = 8/9
> PF/FR = 8/9
>
> equal
> ```
> **YES — EF ∥ QR, by the converse of the Basic Proportionality Theorem.**

---

PQ = 1.28 cm, PR = 2.56 cm, PE = 0.18 cm, PF = 0.36 cm, with E on PQ and F on PR. Is EF ∥ QR?

> [!ANSWER]
> **First find the second parts of each side — the theorem needs the RATIO OF THE PARTS, not part to whole.**
> ```
> EQ = PQ − PE = 1.28 − 0.18 = 1.10
> FR = PR − PF = 2.56 − 0.36 = 2.20
>
> PE/EQ = 0.18/1.10 = 9/55
> PF/FR = 0.36/2.20 = 9/55
>
> equal
> ```
> **YES — EF ∥ QR.**
>
> **The step that is easy to skip is the subtraction.** Being given PQ rather than EQ is a deliberate trap.

---

ABCD is a trapezium with AB ∥ DC. E and F are on the non-parallel sides AD and BC with EF ∥ AB. Show that `AE/ED = BF/FC`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Join AC, meeting EF at G.**
> ```
> AB ∥ DC and EF ∥ AB, so EF ∥ DC
>              (lines parallel to the same line are parallel to each other)
>
> In △ADC, with EG ∥ DC:
>       AE/ED = AG/GC                (Theorem 6.1)        …(1)
>
> In △CAB, with GF ∥ AB:
>       CG/GA = CF/FB
> i.e.  AG/GC = BF/FC                                     …(2)
>
> from (1) and (2):    AE/ED = BF/FC        ∎
> ```
> **The whole method is the DIAGONAL.** Drawing AC splits the trapezium into two triangles, each of which has a line parallel to one side — so the theorem can be applied twice, and AG/GC is the bridge between them.

---

Prove that a line through the midpoint of one side of a triangle, parallel to another side, bisects the third side.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Let D be the midpoint of AB in △ABC, and let DE ∥ BC meet AC at E.**
> ```
> D is the midpoint, so AD = DB, giving AD/DB = 1
>
> DE ∥ BC, so by Theorem 6.1:   AD/DB = AE/EC
>
> therefore AE/EC = 1, so AE = EC
>
> hence E is the MIDPOINT of AC — the line bisects the third side   ∎
> ```
> **This is the Class 9 midpoint theorem, proved here in two lines from Thales.**

---

Prove that the line joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Let D and E be the midpoints of AB and AC in △ABC.**
> ```
> AD = DB  →  AD/DB = 1
> AE = EC  →  AE/EC = 1
>
> so   AD/DB = AE/EC
>
> by Theorem 6.2 (the CONVERSE), DE ∥ BC        ∎
> ```
> **Compare with the previous question: same figure, opposite direction.** There we knew the parallel and deduced the midpoint; here we know the midpoints and deduce the parallel — which is exactly the difference between the theorem and its converse.

---

ABCD is a trapezium with AB ∥ DC whose diagonals meet at O. Show that `AO/BO = CO/DO`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Through O draw a line EO ∥ AB (and hence ∥ DC), meeting AD at E.**
> ```
> In △ADC, with EO ∥ DC:      AE/ED = AO/OC          (Theorem 6.1)     …(1)
>
> In △ABD, with EO ∥ AB:      AE/ED = BO/OD                            …(2)
>
> from (1) and (2):           AO/OC = BO/OD
>
> rearranging:                AO/BO = CO/DO           ∎
> ```
> **Alternatively, by AA similarity:** `∠OAB = ∠OCD` and `∠OBA = ∠ODC` (alternate angles), so `△AOB ~ △COD`, giving the same ratios directly.

---

The diagonals of quadrilateral ABCD meet at O with `AO/BO = CO/DO`. Show that ABCD is a trapezium.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AO/BO = CO/DO   rearranges to   AO/OC = BO/OD
>
> Through O draw EO ∥ DC, meeting AD at E.
>
> In △ADC:   AE/ED = AO/OC                    (Theorem 6.1)
> so         AE/ED = BO/OD
>
> In △ABD, AE/ED = BO/OD means, by Theorem 6.2 (the CONVERSE),
>            EO ∥ AB
>
> But EO ∥ DC by construction, so AB ∥ DC.
>
> A quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides is a TRAPEZIUM.   ∎
> ```
> **This is the converse of the previous question**, and the proof uses Theorem 6.1 in one triangle and Theorem 6.2 in the other — a nice illustration of using both directions in a single argument.

---

In △PQR, `PS/SQ = PT/TR` and `∠PST = ∠PRQ`. Prove that PQR is isosceles.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PS/SQ = PT/TR
> so ST ∥ QR                          (Theorem 6.2, the converse)
>
> therefore ∠PST = ∠PQR               (corresponding angles)      …(1)
>
> given      ∠PST = ∠PRQ                                          …(2)
>
> from (1) and (2):   ∠PQR = ∠PRQ
>
> so PQ = PR                          (sides opposite equal angles are equal)
>
> hence △PQR is ISOSCELES.            ∎
> ```

---

How is similarity of triangles written, and why does the order of letters matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **`△ABC ~ △DEF` means "triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF", where ~ stands for "is similar to"** (as **≅** stands for "is congruent to").
>
> **The order carries the CORRESPONDENCE:** it says A ↔ D, B ↔ E, C ↔ F.
>
> **So `△ABC ~ △EDF` and `△ABC ~ △FED` state something DIFFERENT and are usually false** — though `△BAC ~ △EDF` is fine, because it keeps the same pairing.
>
> **Get the order wrong and every ratio and every angle you write down afterwards will be wrong.**

---

State the AAA criterion and the AA criterion, and explain why AA is enough.

> [!ANSWER]
> **AAA (Theorem 6.3): if in two triangles the CORRESPONDING ANGLES are EQUAL, then their corresponding sides are in the same ratio, and the triangles are similar.**
>
> **AA: if TWO angles of one triangle are respectively equal to two angles of another, the triangles are similar.**
>
> **Why two angles suffice: the ANGLE SUM PROPERTY.** All three angles of any triangle add to 180°, so once two pairs match, **the third pair is forced to match as well** — there is no freedom left.
>
> **AA is the criterion you will use most often**, because two equal angles are far easier to spot than three.

---

State the SSS and SAS similarity criteria, and say what "including" means in SAS.

> [!ANSWER]
> **SSS (Theorem 6.4): if the sides of one triangle are PROPORTIONAL to the sides of the other, then their corresponding angles are equal and the triangles are similar.**
>
> **SAS (Theorem 6.5): if ONE angle of a triangle equals one angle of the other, AND the sides INCLUDING these angles are proportional, then the triangles are similar.**
>
> **"Including" means the equal angle must lie BETWEEN the two proportional sides.** An equal angle elsewhere, with two proportional sides elsewhere, proves nothing at all — exactly as with SAS congruence.

---

For polygons, neither equal angles nor proportional sides alone gives similarity. Why is the situation different for triangles?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because for triangles, EITHER CONDITION IMPLIES THE OTHER.** AAA shows equal angles force proportional sides; SSS shows proportional sides force equal angles.
>
> **The underlying reason is that TRIANGLES ARE RIGID.** Fix the three side lengths of a triangle and the shape is completely determined — there is exactly one triangle with those sides.
>
> **A four-sided frame is not rigid:** a square can be pushed over into a rhombus with the sides unchanged. That is exactly why the square-and-rhombus example defeats condition (ii) for quadrilaterals but has no analogue for triangles.

---

PQ ∥ RS, with PS and QR crossing at O. Prove `△POQ ~ △SOR`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PQ ∥ RS                    (given)
>
> ∠P = ∠S                    (alternate angles)
> ∠Q = ∠R                    (alternate angles)
> ∠POQ = ∠SOR               (vertically opposite angles)
>
> all three pairs equal, so  △POQ ~ △SOR      (AAA similarity)   ∎
> ```
> **AA alone would have sufficed** — the two alternate-angle pairs are enough, and the vertically opposite pair is then automatic.

---

In △ABC and △PQR, AB = 3.8, BC = 6, CA = 3√3 and RQ = 7.6, QP = 12, PR = 6√3. If ∠A = 80° and ∠B = 60°, find ∠P.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AB/RQ = 3.8/7.6  = 1/2
> BC/QP = 6/12     = 1/2
> CA/PR = 3√3/6√3  = 1/2
>
> all three ratios equal, so  △ABC ~ △RQP        (SSS similarity)
>
> the correspondence is A↔R, B↔Q, C↔P
>
> so ∠C = ∠P                (corresponding angles of similar triangles)
>
> ∠C = 180° − 80° − 60° = 40°                    (angle sum property)
>
> ∠P = 40°
> ```
> **The correspondence is the crux: it is `△ABC ~ △RQP`, NOT `△ABC ~ △PQR`.** The ratios matched AB with **RQ** and BC with **QP**, which fixes A↔R, B↔Q, C↔P. **Read it wrongly and you would answer ∠P = 80°.**

---

Two lines cross at O with `OA · OB = OC · OD`. Show that ∠A = ∠C and ∠B = ∠D.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> OA · OB = OC · OD
>
> rearranging into a RATIO:    OA/OC = OD/OB         …(1)
>
> ∠AOD = ∠COB                  (vertically opposite angles)      …(2)
>
> from (1) and (2):   △AOD ~ △COB      (SAS similarity)
>
> therefore ∠A = ∠C  and  ∠D = ∠B      ∎
> ```
> **The key move is turning the PRODUCT into a RATIO.** `OA · OB = OC · OD` looks like a statement about areas; rewritten as `OA/OC = OD/OB` it is exactly the proportionality that SAS needs, with the vertically opposite angles supplying the included angle.

---

△ODC ~ △OBA, with ∠BOC = 125° and ∠CDO = 70°. Find ∠DOC, ∠DCO and ∠OAB.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ∠DOC = 180° − 125° = 55°               (BOD is a straight line, so
>                                          ∠DOC and ∠BOC are a linear pair)
>
> in △ODC:   ∠DCO = 180° − 70° − 55° = 55°      (angle sum property)
>
> △ODC ~ △OBA gives the correspondence O↔O, D↔B, C↔A
>
> so ∠OAB = ∠OCD = 55°
> ```
> **Reading the correspondence off the similarity statement is what gives the last answer** — C pairs with A, so ∠DCO pairs with ∠BAO.

---

D lies on BC of △ABC with ∠ADC = ∠BAC. Show that `CA² = CB · CD`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> In △ADC and △BAC:
>
>       ∠ADC = ∠BAC          (given)
>       ∠ACD = ∠BCA          (the SAME angle — C is common to both)
>
> so    △ADC ~ △BAC          (AA similarity)
>
> corresponding sides:        CA/CB = CD/CA
>
> cross-multiplying:          CA² = CB · CD        ∎
> ```
> **The move that makes it work is spotting the COMMON angle at C.** One given equality plus one shared angle is all AA needs — and the conclusion `CA² = CB · CD` follows from writing the side ratio the right way round.

---

A vertical pole 6 m tall casts a shadow 4 m long. At the same moment a tower casts a shadow 28 m long. Find the tower's height.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the Sun's rays are parallel and both objects are vertical,
> so the two triangles are SIMILAR (AA — a right angle each,
> and equal angles of elevation of the Sun)
>
> height/shadow is the same for both:
>
>       h/28 = 6/4
>       h = 28 × 1.5 = 42 m
> ```
> **The tower is 42 m tall.**
>
> **This is INDIRECT MEASUREMENT in its simplest form** — exactly the principle by which the heights of mountains and the distances of far objects are found, without any tape reaching them.

---

△ABE ≅ △ACD. Show that △ADE ~ △ABC.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> △ABE ≅ △ACD  gives  AB = AC  and  AE = AD          (CPCT)
>
> therefore    AD/AB = AE/AC
>
> ∠DAE = ∠BAC                (the SAME angle, common to both triangles)
>
> so  △ADE ~ △ABC            (SAS similarity)          ∎
> ```
> **Congruence delivers the equal sides; the common angle at A supplies the included angle.** The rest is SAS.

---

Altitudes AD and CE of △ABC meet at P. Show that △AEP ~ △CDP and △ABD ~ △CBE.

> [!ANSWER]
> **△AEP ~ △CDP:**
> ```
> ∠AEP = ∠CDP = 90°                (CE and AD are altitudes)
> ∠APE = ∠CPD                      (vertically opposite angles)
>
> so △AEP ~ △CDP                   (AA similarity)
> ```
> **△ABD ~ △CBE:**
> ```
> ∠ADB = ∠CEB = 90°                (altitudes again)
> ∠ABD = ∠CBE                      (the SAME angle at B)
>
> so △ABD ~ △CBE                   (AA similarity)
> ```
> **In figures full of altitudes, AA is almost always the criterion** — every altitude hands you a right angle for free, so you need find only one more equal angle.

---

ABC and AMP are right triangles, right-angled at B and M. Prove △ABC ~ △AMP and hence `CA/PA = BC/MP`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ∠ABC = ∠AMP = 90°                (given)
> ∠BAC = ∠MAP                      (the SAME angle at A)
>
> so △ABC ~ △AMP                   (AA similarity)
>
> corresponding sides are proportional:
>
>       CA/PA = BC/MP = AB/AM      ∎
> ```

---

CD and GH bisect ∠ACB and ∠EGF, with D on AB and H on FE. If △ABC ~ △FEG, show that `CD/GH = AC/FG`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> △ABC ~ △FEG  gives  ∠A = ∠F  and  ∠ACB = ∠FGE
>
> CD and GH are BISECTORS, so
>       ∠ACD = ½∠ACB  and  ∠FGH = ½∠FGE
>
> since ∠ACB = ∠FGE, halving both gives  ∠ACD = ∠FGH
>
> In △ACD and △FGH:
>       ∠A = ∠F              (from the similarity)
>       ∠ACD = ∠FGH          (just shown)
>
> so △ACD ~ △FGH             (AA similarity)
>
> therefore   CD/GH = AC/FG        ∎
> ```
> **The step worth noting: equal angles have equal halves.** Bisecting two equal angles produces two more equal angles, which is what turns the given similarity into a new one.

---

E is a point on side CB produced of an isosceles triangle ABC with AB = AC. If AD ⊥ BC and EF ⊥ AC, prove △ABD ~ △ECF.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AB = AC, so ∠ABC = ∠ACB          (angles opposite equal sides)
>
> ∠ABD = ∠ABC                       (same angle)
> ∠ECF = ∠ACB                       (vertically opposite / same angle at C)
> so    ∠ABD = ∠ECF
>
> ∠ADB = ∠EFC = 90°                 (AD ⊥ BC and EF ⊥ AC)
>
> so △ABD ~ △ECF                    (AA similarity)     ∎
> ```
> **The isosceles property is what supplies the second pair of equal angles** — without AB = AC there would be only the right angles, and AA would not be satisfied.

---

Sides AB, AC and median AD of △ABC are proportional to sides PQ, PR and median PM of △PQR. Explain the idea behind proving △ABC ~ △PQR.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> given:   AB/PQ = AC/PR = AD/PM
>
> D is the midpoint of BC, so BC = 2·BD
> M is the midpoint of QR, so QR = 2·QM
> ```
> **The strategy: extend AD to double its length (to a point E with AD = DE), and similarly extend PM.** This creates parallelograms in which the median becomes a full side, and lets the given median ratio be converted into a ratio of ordinary sides.
>
> **Once that is done, the three sides of one triangle are proportional to the three of the other, and SSS similarity applies.**
>
> **The general principle worth taking away: a median is awkward because it ends at a midpoint, so DOUBLE it to turn it into a complete side.**

---

If AD and PM are medians of △ABC and △PQR with `△ABC ~ △PQR`, prove that `AB/PQ = AD/PM`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> △ABC ~ △PQR  gives   AB/PQ = BC/QR   and   ∠B = ∠Q
>
> D and M are midpoints, so  BD = ½BC  and  QM = ½QR
>
> therefore  BD/QM = (½BC)/(½QR) = BC/QR = AB/PQ
>
> In △ABD and △PQM:
>       AB/PQ = BD/QM        (just shown)
>       ∠B = ∠Q              (from the similarity)
>
> so △ABD ~ △PQM             (SAS similarity)
>
> therefore  AB/PQ = AD/PM        ∎
> ```
> **The result in words: in similar triangles, the MEDIANS are in the same ratio as the sides.** The same is true of altitudes and angle bisectors, and for the same reason — everything in the figure scales by the one scale factor.

Explain how similarity makes it possible to measure the height of a mountain without climbing it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **By INDIRECT MEASUREMENT.**
>
> **Set up a small triangle you CAN measure that is SIMILAR to the huge triangle you cannot.** Because similar triangles have all sides in the **same ratio**, one unknown length can be found from three known ones.
>
> **The pole-and-shadow problem is exactly this at small scale:** a 6 m pole with a 4 m shadow and a tower with a 28 m shadow form two similar right triangles, and `h/28 = 6/4` gives h = 42 m **without going anywhere near the tower.**
>
> **This principle is how the heights of mountains like Mount Everest and the distances of objects like the Moon were found** — and it is developed further in the trigonometry chapters.
