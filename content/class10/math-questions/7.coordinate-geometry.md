State the distance formula and explain why it is really Pythagoras' theorem.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PQ = √[ (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² ]
> ```
> **It IS Pythagoras, translated into coordinates.** Drop perpendiculars from P and Q to the x-axis and complete the right triangle:
> - the **horizontal** leg is `x₂ − x₁`
> - the **vertical** leg is `y₂ − y₁`
> - the **hypotenuse** is the segment PQ
>
> **`PQ² = (horizontal)² + (vertical)²` is exactly Pythagoras.** Nothing new is assumed; the theorem is simply written in terms of coordinates.
>
> **Take only the POSITIVE square root — distance is never negative.**

---

Does it matter which point you call P and which Q in the distance formula?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
> ```
> (x₂ − x₁)² = (x₁ − x₂)²        because (−5)² = 5²
> ```
> **The differences are SQUARED, so the sign disappears.**
>
> **And this is exactly as it should be** — the distance from P to Q is the same as the distance from Q to P.

Find the distance between (2, 3) and (4, 1); between (−5, 7) and (−1, 3); and between (a, b) and (−a, −b).

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (2,3) and (4,1):
>       √[(4−2)² + (1−3)²] = √(4 + 4) = √8 = 2√2
>
> (−5,7) and (−1,3):
>       √[(−1+5)² + (3−7)²] = √(16 + 16) = √32 = 4√2
>
> (a,b) and (−a,−b):
>       √[(−a−a)² + (−b−b)²] = √(4a² + 4b²) = 2√(a² + b²)
> ```
> **The last one is worth noting: (a, b) and (−a, −b) are opposite points through the origin, so the distance is TWICE the distance of either from the origin** — and `√(a² + b²)` is exactly that distance.

Find the distance between (0, 0) and (36, 15). What does this tell you about towns A and B, where B is 36 km east and 15 km north of A?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> √(36² + 15²) = √(1296 + 225) = √1521 = 39
> ```
> **The towns are 39 km apart in a straight line** — found without measuring anything on the ground.
>
> **A 15-36-39 right triangle is just a 5-12-13 triangle scaled by 3**, which is why the answer comes out whole.

Determine whether (1, 5), (2, 3) and (−2, −11) are collinear.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AB = √[(2−1)² + (3−5)²]     = √(1 + 4)     = √5     ≈ 2.24
> BC = √[(−2−2)² + (−11−3)²]  = √(16 + 196)  = √212   ≈ 14.56
> AC = √[(−2−1)² + (−11−5)²]  = √(9 + 256)   = √265   ≈ 16.28
>
> AB + BC = 2.24 + 14.56 = 16.80        but AC = 16.28
> ```
> **They are NOT collinear**, since the two shorter distances do not add to the longest.
>
> **Why this test works: three points lie on a line exactly when one of them is BETWEEN the other two**, and then the two short journeys must add exactly to the long one. **Any bend makes the direct route shorter than going via the middle point.**

Check whether (5, −2), (6, 4) and (7, −2) are the vertices of an isosceles triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AB = √[(6−5)² + (4+2)²]  = √(1 + 36) = √37
> BC = √[(7−6)² + (−2−4)²] = √(1 + 36) = √37
> AC = √[(7−5)² + (−2+2)²] = √(4 + 0)  = 2
> ```
> **YES — AB = BC = √37, so exactly two sides are equal and the triangle is ISOSCELES.**
>
> **(And it is a genuine triangle, since `2 + √37 ≠ √37`** — the points are not collinear.)

---

How do you name the type of triangle from three computed distances?

> [!ANSWER]
> | Distances | Triangle |
> |---|---|
> | **all three equal** | **EQUILATERAL** |
> | **exactly two equal** | **ISOSCELES** |
> | **(longest)² = sum of the squares of the other two** | **RIGHT-ANGLED** (converse of Pythagoras) |
> | **the two shorter ADD to the longest** | **COLLINEAR — no triangle at all** |
>
> **Always check the collinearity case first** — otherwise you may confidently name a "triangle" that is actually a straight line.
>
> **Keep the distances in surd form and compare their SQUARES.** It avoids decimals and makes the Pythagoras check exact rather than approximate.

Four friends sit at A(3, 4), B(6, 7), C(9, 4) and D(6, 1). Champa says ABCD is a square; Chameli disagrees. Who is right?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AB = √[(6−3)² + (7−4)²] = √(9 + 9) = 3√2
> BC = √[(9−6)² + (4−7)²] = √(9 + 9) = 3√2
> CD = √[(6−9)² + (1−4)²] = √(9 + 9) = 3√2
> DA = √[(3−6)² + (4−1)²] = √(9 + 9) = 3√2
>
> diagonals:
> AC = √[(9−3)² + (4−4)²] = √36 = 6
> BD = √[(6−6)² + (1−7)²] = √36 = 6
> ```
> **CHAMPA is right — it IS a square.**
>
> **Why both checks are needed: four equal sides alone would only prove a RHOMBUS.** The **equal diagonals** are what force the angles to be right angles and make it a square.

Name the quadrilateral formed by (−1, −2), (1, 0), (−1, 2), (−3, 0).

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AB = √[(1+1)² + (0+2)²]  = √8 = 2√2
> BC = √[(−1−1)² + (2−0)²] = √8 = 2√2
> CD = √[(−3+1)² + (0−2)²] = √8 = 2√2
> DA = √[(−1+3)² + (−2−0)²] = √8 = 2√2
>
> diagonals:
> AC = √[(−1+1)² + (2+2)²] = 4
> BD = √[(−3−1)² + (0−0)²] = 4
> ```
> **A SQUARE — all four sides equal AND both diagonals equal.**

Name the quadrilateral formed by (−3, 5), (3, 1), (0, 3), (−1, −4).

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> check the first three points for collinearity:
>
> A(−3,5) to C(0,3):   √(9 + 4) = √13
> C(0,3) to B(3,1):    √(9 + 4) = √13
> A(−3,5) to B(3,1):   √(36 + 16) = √52 = 2√13
>
> √13 + √13 = 2√13     ✔  they ADD exactly
> ```
> **NO QUADRILATERAL IS FORMED — three of the four points are COLLINEAR.**
>
> **A "quadrilateral" needs four vertices in genuinely different directions.** With A, C and B on one straight line, the figure collapses to a triangle with an extra point marked on one side.
>
> **This is why the collinearity check must come first.**

Name the quadrilateral formed by (4, 5), (7, 6), (4, 3), (1, 2).

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> AB = √[(7−4)² + (6−5)²] = √(9 + 1)  = √10
> BC = √[(4−7)² + (3−6)²] = √(9 + 9)  = √18
> CD = √[(1−4)² + (2−3)²] = √(9 + 1)  = √10
> DA = √[(4−1)² + (5−2)²] = √(9 + 9)  = √18
>
> opposite sides EQUAL:  AB = CD  and  BC = DA
>
> diagonals:
> AC = √[(4−4)² + (3−5)²] = 2
> BD = √[(1−7)² + (2−6)²] = √(36 + 16) = √52
>
> diagonals are UNEQUAL
> ```
> **A PARALLELOGRAM** — opposite sides equal, but the diagonals are not, so it is not a rectangle.

Find the point on the x-axis equidistant from (2, −5) and (−2, 9).

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a point on the x-axis has the form (x, 0)
>
> equal distances means equal SQUARED distances:
>
> (x−2)² + (0+5)² = (x+2)² + (0−9)²
> x² − 4x + 4 + 25 = x² + 4x + 4 + 81
> −4x + 25 = 4x + 81
> −8x = 56    →    x = −7
> ```
> **The point is (−7, 0).**
>
> **Squaring both sides at the start is the key simplification** — it removes the roots, and the x² terms then cancel, leaving a linear equation.

Find y so that the distance between P(2, −3) and Q(10, y) is 10 units.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (10 − 2)² + (y + 3)² = 10²
> 64 + (y + 3)² = 100
> (y + 3)² = 36
> y + 3 = ±6
>
> y = 3   or   y = −9
> ```
> **TWO answers, and both are correct.**
>
> **Why: taking a square root gives a ± sign.** Geometrically, there are two points on the vertical line x = 10 at distance 10 from P — one above and one below.

Q(0, 1) is equidistant from P(5, −3) and R(x, 6). Find x, and then QR and PR.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> QP² = QR²
> (5−0)² + (−3−1)² = (x−0)² + (6−1)²
> 25 + 16 = x² + 25
> x² = 16    →    x = 4  or  x = −4
>
> QR = √(16 + 25) = √41            (the same for either x)
>
> PR for x = 4:   √[(4−5)² + (6+3)²] = √(1 + 81) = √82
> PR for x = −4:  √[(−4−5)² + (6+3)²] = √(81 + 81) = 9√2
> ```
> **Both values of x are valid** — R can be on either side of the y-axis, and Q is equidistant from P in each case.

Find a relation between x and y such that (x, y) is equidistant from (3, 6) and (−3, 4).

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (x−3)² + (y−6)² = (x+3)² + (y−4)²
>
> x² − 6x + 9 + y² − 12y + 36 = x² + 6x + 9 + y² − 8y + 16
> −6x − 12y + 36 = 6x − 8y + 16
> −12x − 4y + 20 = 0
>
> 3x + y − 5 = 0
> ```
> **What this equation IS: the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the two points.** Every point equidistant from two fixed points lies on it — which is why the answer is a **line**, not a single point.

State the section formula and explain why the subscripts cross over.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The point P dividing the join of A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂) INTERNALLY in the ratio m₁ : m₂ is**
> ```
> P = ( (m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂) ,  (m₁y₂ + m₂y₁)/(m₁ + m₂) )
> ```
> **Notice m₁ multiplies x₂, and m₂ multiplies x₁ — the subscripts CROSS.**
>
> **Why: m₁ is the part of the journey NEAREST A, so it measures how far you have travelled TOWARDS B** — and therefore it weights **B's** coordinate.
>
> **A quick check: put m₁ = 0.** P should then be at A itself, and the formula gives `m₂x₁/m₂ = x₁` ✔. **Putting m₂ = 0 gives x₂**, i.e. B ✔

---

Give the k : 1 form of the section formula and say when it is preferable.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> P = ( (kx₂ + x₁)/(k + 1) ,  (ky₂ + y₁)/(k + 1) )
> ```
> **Obtained by dividing throughout by m₂ and writing k for m₁/m₂.**
>
> **It is preferable for "FIND THE RATIO" questions**, because it has **one unknown instead of two** — one equation then solves it, with no need to eliminate.

---

Derive the midpoint formula from the section formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A midpoint divides the segment in the ratio 1 : 1**, so put `m₁ = m₂ = 1`:
> ```
> x = (1·x₂ + 1·x₁)/(1 + 1) = (x₁ + x₂)/2
> y = (1·y₂ + 1·y₁)/(1 + 1) = (y₁ + y₂)/2
>
> midpoint = ( (x₁ + x₂)/2 , (y₁ + y₂)/2 )
> ```
> **Simply the AVERAGE of the two x-coordinates and of the two y-coordinates** — which is exactly what "halfway between" ought to mean.

Find the point dividing the join of (4, −3) and (8, 5) in the ratio 3 : 1.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x = (3·8 + 1·4)/(3 + 1)     = (24 + 4)/4  = 7
> y = (3·5 + 1·(−3))/(3 + 1)  = (15 − 3)/4  = 3
>
> the point is (7, 3)
> ```
> **Sanity check: a ratio of 3 : 1 means P is THREE-QUARTERS of the way from A to B.** Three-quarters of the way from 4 to 8 is 7 ✔, and from −3 to 5 is 3 ✔

Find the point dividing the join of (−1, 7) and (4, −3) in the ratio 2 : 3.

> [!ANSWER]

> x = (2·4 + 3·(−1))/(2 + 3)  = (8 − 3)/5   = 1
> y = (2·(−3) + 3·7)/(2 + 3)  = (−6 + 21)/5 = 3
>
> the point is (1, 3)
> ```

Find the points of trisection of the segment joining (4, −1) and (−2, −3).

> [!ANSWER]
> **Trisection means TWO points dividing the segment into three equal parts — in the ratios 1 : 2 and 2 : 1.**
> ```
> P divides 1 : 2
>       x = (1(−2) + 2(4))/3 = (−2 + 8)/3 = 2
>       y = (1(−3) + 2(−1))/3 = (−3 − 2)/3 = −5/3
>       P = (2, −5/3)
>
> Q divides 2 : 1
>       x = (2(−2) + 1(4))/3 = (−4 + 4)/3 = 0
>       y = (2(−3) + 1(−1))/3 = (−6 − 1)/3 = −7/3
>       Q = (0, −7/3)
> ```
> **The commonest error is using the ratio 1 : 3** — but a point one-third of the way along divides the segment into parts of **1 and 2**, so the ratio is **1 : 2**.

Find the points dividing the segment joining A(−2, 2) and B(2, 8) into four equal parts.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Three points, in the ratios 1 : 3, 1 : 1 and 3 : 1.**
> ```
> P₁ (1 : 3)   x = (1·2 + 3(−2))/4 = (2 − 6)/4 = −1
>              y = (1·8 + 3·2)/4   = 14/4 = 7/2
>              P₁ = (−1, 7/2)
>
> P₂ (1 : 1)   the midpoint = ((−2 + 2)/2, (2 + 8)/2) = (0, 5)
>
> P₃ (3 : 1)   x = (3·2 + 1(−2))/4 = 4/4 = 1
>              y = (3·8 + 1·2)/4   = 26/4 = 13/2
>              P₃ = (1, 13/2)
> ```
> **Check the pattern: the x-values −1, 0, 1 are evenly spaced, and so are the y-values 3.5, 5, 6.5** — exactly what equal parts should give.

Find the ratio in which (−1, 6) divides the join of (−3, 10) and (6, −8).

> [!ANSWER]
> **Use the k : 1 form — one unknown.**
> ```
> −1 = (6k + (−3))/(k + 1)
>
> −(k + 1) = 6k − 3
> −k − 1 = 6k − 3
> 2 = 7k    →    k = 2/7
>
> the ratio is 2 : 7
> ```
> **Check with the y-coordinate:**
> ```
> ((2/7)(−8) + 10)/((2/7) + 1) = (−16/7 + 70/7)/(9/7) = (54/7)(7/9) = 6   ✔
> ```
> **Always verify with the other coordinate.** If it does not agree, the point is **not on the segment at all** — which is itself worth discovering.

Find the ratio in which the x-axis divides the join of A(1, −5) and B(−4, 5), and the point of division.

> [!ANSWER]
> **On the x-axis, y = 0 — that is the condition to impose.**
> ```
> 0 = (k·5 + (−5))/(k + 1)
> 5k − 5 = 0    →    k = 1
>
> the ratio is 1 : 1  — the x-axis passes through the MIDPOINT
>
> x = (1(−4) + 1(1))/2 = −3/2
>
> the point of division is (−3/2, 0)
> ```
> **The ratio came out 1 : 1 because A and B are equally far from the x-axis, one 5 below and one 5 above** — so the crossing must be halfway.

(1, 2), (4, y), (x, 6) and (3, 5) are the vertices of a parallelogram taken in order. Find x and y.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Use the property that THE DIAGONALS OF A PARALLELOGRAM BISECT EACH OTHER — so both diagonals have the SAME midpoint.**
> ```
> diagonal AC joins (1,2) and (x,6);  diagonal BD joins (4,y) and (3,5)
>
> midpoints must be equal:
>
> (1 + x)/2 = (4 + 3)/2      →      1 + x = 7      →      x = 6
> (2 + 6)/2 = (y + 5)/2      →      8 = y + 5      →      y = 3
> ```
> **x = 6, y = 3.**
>
> **"Taken in order" is essential** — it tells you that AC and BD are the diagonals rather than sides. Reorder the vertices and the answer changes.

AB is a diameter of a circle with centre (2, −3), and B is (1, 4). Find A.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The centre of a circle is the MIDPOINT of any diameter.**
> ```
> (x + 1)/2 = 2      →      x + 1 = 4      →      x = 3
> (y + 4)/2 = −3     →      y + 4 = −6     →      y = −10
>
> A = (3, −10)
> ```
> **Check: the midpoint of (3, −10) and (1, 4) is (2, −3)** ✔

A is (−2, −2) and B is (2, −4). Find P on AB such that `AP = (3/7)AB`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **First convert the fraction into a RATIO.**
> ```
> AP = (3/7)AB  means AP : AB = 3 : 7,  so PB = AB − AP = (4/7)AB
>
> therefore  AP : PB = 3 : 4
>
> x = (3·2 + 4(−2))/7 = (6 − 8)/7  = −2/7
> y = (3(−4) + 4(−2))/7 = (−12 − 8)/7 = −20/7
>
> P = (−2/7, −20/7)
> ```
> **The trap is using 3 : 7.** The section formula wants **AP : PB**, the ratio of the two PARTS — not the part to the whole.

Niharika runs one-quarter of the 100 m distance AD along the 2nd line and posts a green flag; Preet runs one-fifth of AD along the 8th line and posts a red flag. Find the distance between the flags and where a flag halfway between them should go.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Take the line number as the x-coordinate and the distance along AD as the y-coordinate.**
> ```
> Niharika:  2nd line, ¼ × 100 = 25 m      →  (2, 25)
> Preet:     8th line, ⅕ × 100 = 20 m      →  (8, 20)
>
> distance = √[(8−2)² + (20−25)²] = √(36 + 25) = √61 ≈ 7.81 m
>
> midpoint = ((2 + 8)/2, (25 + 20)/2) = (5, 22.5)
> ```
> **Rashmi should post the blue flag on the 5th line, 22.5 m from A.**
>
> **The whole problem is setting up the coordinates** — once the flags are points, both parts are one formula each.

Find the area of the rhombus with vertices (3, 0), (4, 5), (−1, 4) and (−2, −1) taken in order.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Area of a rhombus = ½ × (product of its diagonals), so find the two diagonals.**
> ```
> diagonal AC joins (3,0) and (−1,4):
>       AC = √[(−1−3)² + (4−0)²] = √(16 + 16) = √32 = 4√2
>
> diagonal BD joins (4,5) and (−2,−1):
>       BD = √[(−2−4)² + (−1−5)²] = √(36 + 36) = √72 = 6√2
>
> area = ½ × 4√2 × 6√2 = ½ × 24 × 2 = 24 square units
> ```
> **"Taken in order" tells you which pairs are the diagonals** — the 1st with the 3rd, and the 2nd with the 4th.

A relay tower is to stand at P between A(0, 0) and B(36, 15) so that its distance from B is twice its distance from A. Where does it go?

> [!ANSWER]
> **"Distance from B is twice that from A" means AP : PB = 1 : 2.**
> ```
> x = (1·36 + 2·0)/(1 + 2) = 36/3 = 12
> y = (1·15 + 2·0)/(1 + 2) = 15/3 = 5
>
> P = (12, 5)
> ```
> **Check: OP = √(144 + 25) = 13, and PB = √(24² + 10²) = √(576 + 100) = √676 = 26** — exactly twice ✔
>
> **Reading the ratio correctly is the whole difficulty:** "twice as far from B" makes AP the SMALLER part, so the ratio is **1 : 2, not 2 : 1**.

---

A student uses the formula `(m₁x₁ + m₂x₂)/(m₁ + m₂)` instead of the correct one. How would you convince them it is wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Test it on a case where the answer is obvious.**
> ```
> take A(0, 0) and B(10, 0), dividing in the ratio 1 : 4
>
> the point should be ONE-FIFTH of the way from A, i.e. at x = 2
>
> correct formula:   (1·10 + 4·0)/5 = 10/5 = 2      ✔
> student's version: (1·0 + 4·10)/5 = 40/5 = 8      ✘  — that is four-fifths of the way
> ```
> **The student's version gives the point on the WRONG SIDE** — it produces the point dividing in the ratio 4 : 1 instead.
>
> **The reason the subscripts cross: m₁ measures the journey ALREADY MADE towards B, so it must weight B's coordinate.**
>
> **Checking a formula against an obvious case is a habit worth keeping** — it catches this kind of slip in seconds.
