Define the degree of a polynomial, and name polynomials of degree 1, 2 and 3 with their general forms.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The DEGREE is the highest power of the variable in the polynomial.**
>
> | Degree | Name | General form |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1** | **LINEAR** | `ax + b`, a ≠ 0 |
> | **2** | **QUADRATIC** | `ax² + bx + c`, a ≠ 0 |
> | **3** | **CUBIC** | `ax³ + bx² + cx + d`, a ≠ 0 |
>
> **'Quadratic' comes from *quadrate*, meaning SQUARE** — degree 2.
>
> **In every case a ≠ 0**, because if the leading coefficient were zero the polynomial would drop to a lower degree.

---

Which of these are polynomials, and why? `x² − 3x + 1`, `1/(x − 1)`, `√x + 2`, `5x³ − 4x² + x − √2`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Polynomials: `x² − 3x + 1` and `5x³ − 4x² + x − √2`.**
>
> **NOT polynomials:**
> - **`1/(x − 1)`** — the **variable is in a DENOMINATOR**, which is the same as a negative power.
> - **`√x + 2`** — the **variable is under a ROOT**, i.e. raised to the power 1/2.
>
> **The rule: a polynomial needs WHOLE-NUMBER powers of the variable.** Note that `−√2` as a **constant** is perfectly fine — irrational **coefficients** are allowed; irrational **powers** are not.

---

Is `2x + 5 − x²` a linear polynomial? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — it is QUADRATIC.**
>
> **Degree is decided by the HIGHEST power present, not by the first term written.** The highest power here is `x²`, so the degree is 2.
>
> Rewritten in the usual order it is `−x² + 2x + 5`, with a = −1, b = 2, c = 5.

---

For `p(x) = x² − 3x − 4`, find p(2), p(0), p(−1) and p(4). What do the last two tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> p(2)  = 4 − 6 − 4   = −6
> p(0)  = 0 − 0 − 4   = −4
> p(−1) = 1 + 3 − 4   = 0
> p(4)  = 16 − 12 − 4 = 0
> ```
> **p(−1) = 0 and p(4) = 0, so −1 and 4 are the ZEROES of x² − 3x − 4.**
>
> **A real number k is a ZERO of p(x) if p(k) = 0** — nothing more than that.

---

Find the zero of the linear polynomial `ax + b`, and apply it to `2x + 3`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> if k is a zero,  ak + b = 0,  so  k = −b/a
>
> zero of a linear polynomial = −(constant term) / (coefficient of x)
> ```
> **For `2x + 3`: k = −3/2.**
>
> **This is the first hint of the chapter's main theme** — the zero is already determined by the coefficients, and the rest of the chapter asks whether the same happens for higher degrees.

---

Why are the zeroes of a polynomial exactly the x-coordinates where its graph meets the x-axis?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the two conditions are the same condition, written differently.**
>
> - A point lies **on the x-axis** exactly when its **y-coordinate is 0**.
> - On the graph of `y = p(x)`, the y-coordinate **is** p(x).
>
> **So "on the x-axis" means p(x) = 0 — which is precisely the definition of a zero.**
>
> **This is why an algebraic idea (solving p(x) = 0) and a geometric one (where does the curve cross?) turn out to be identical.**

---

How many zeroes can a linear polynomial have, and why exactly that many?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Exactly ONE.**
>
> **The graph of `y = ax + b` (a ≠ 0) is a STRAIGHT LINE that is not horizontal**, so it must cross the x-axis — **and, being straight, it can cross only once.**
>
> **The crossing point is `(−b/a, 0)`**, whose x-coordinate is exactly the zero found algebraically.

---

Describe the three possible shapes of a quadratic graph relative to the x-axis, and the number of zeroes in each case.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The graph of `y = ax² + bx + c` is a PARABOLA — opening UPWARDS if a > 0 and DOWNWARDS if a < 0.**
>
> | Case | Graph | Zeroes |
> |---|---|---|
> | **(i)** | **cuts** the x-axis at **two distinct points** | **two distinct zeroes** |
> | **(ii)** | **touches** the x-axis at exactly one point (the two points have coincided) | **two EQUAL zeroes**, i.e. one value |
> | **(iii)** | lies **entirely above or entirely below** the x-axis | **NO zeroes** |
>
> **So a quadratic has AT MOST two zeroes.**

---

Give an example of a quadratic polynomial with no real zeroes, and explain graphically.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`x² + 1`.**
>
> **Algebraically:** `x² + 1 = 0` needs `x² = −1`, and **no real number squares to a negative**.
>
> **Graphically:** a = 1 > 0 so the parabola opens **upwards**, and its lowest point is at `(0, 1)` — **entirely above the x-axis**, so it never crosses. That is case (iii).
>
> **`x² + 4` and `2x² + 3` behave the same way**, and so does any downward parabola lying entirely below the axis, such as `−x² − 5`.

---

State the general result about the number of zeroes of a polynomial of degree n.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A polynomial p(x) of degree n has AT MOST n zeroes**, because the graph of `y = p(x)` meets the x-axis at **at most n points**.
>
> **"At most", NOT "exactly"** — a quadratic can have 2, 1 or 0 zeroes, as the three cases show. The degree gives a **ceiling**, not a count.

---

`y = x³ − 4x` takes the values 0, 3, 0, −3, 0 at x = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2. Find its zeroes and check the degree rule.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The zeroes are −2, 0 and 2** — the x-values where y = 0, and the only points where the curve meets the x-axis.
>
> **Verification by factorising:**
> ```
> x³ − 4x = x(x² − 4) = x(x − 2)(x + 2)
> ```
> giving exactly x = 0, 2, −2.
>
> **A cubic has at most 3 zeroes, and this one attains the maximum.**

---

Find the zeroes of `x³` and of `x³ − x²`, and comment.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x³ = 0        →  the ONLY zero is 0
>
> x³ − x² = x²(x − 1)  →  zeroes are 0 and 1
> ```
> **Both are cubics, yet one has a single zero and the other has two — neither reaches the maximum of three.**
>
> **This is why the rule says "at most".** The graph of `y = x³` meets the x-axis at only one point; `y = x³ − x²` at only two.

---

Find the zeroes of `x² − 2x − 8` and verify the relationships with the coefficients.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x² − 2x − 8 = (x − 4)(x + 2)      →  zeroes 4 and −2
>
> a = 1, b = −2, c = −8
>
> sum     = 4 + (−2) = 2    and  −b/a = −(−2)/1 = 2    ✔
> product = 4 × (−2) = −8   and   c/a =  (−8)/1 = −8   ✔
> ```

---

Find the zeroes of `4s² − 4s + 1` and verify.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 4s² − 4s + 1 = (2s − 1)²      →  zeroes 1/2 and 1/2  (two EQUAL zeroes)
>
> a = 4, b = −4, c = 1
>
> sum     = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1     and  −b/a = 4/4 = 1      ✔
> product = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4   and   c/a = 1/4          ✔
> ```
> **This is case (ii): the parabola TOUCHES the x-axis at s = 1/2 rather than cutting it.**

---

Find the zeroes of `6x² − 3 − 7x` and verify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **First rewrite in standard order:** `6x² − 7x − 3`.
> ```
> split the middle term: 6 × (−3) = −18, and −9 + 2 = −7
>
> 6x² − 9x + 2x − 3 = 3x(2x − 3) + 1(2x − 3) = (3x + 1)(2x − 3)
>
> zeroes: x = −1/3 and x = 3/2
>
> a = 6, b = −7, c = −3
>
> sum     = −1/3 + 3/2 = (−2 + 9)/6 = 7/6   and  −b/a = 7/6    ✔
> product = (−1/3)(3/2) = −1/2              and   c/a = −3/6 = −1/2  ✔
> ```

---

Find the zeroes of `4u² + 8u` and verify.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 4u² + 8u = 4u(u + 2)      →  zeroes 0 and −2
>
> a = 4, b = 8, c = 0
>
> sum     = 0 + (−2) = −2   and  −b/a = −8/4 = −2   ✔
> product = 0 × (−2) = 0    and   c/a = 0/4  = 0    ✔
> ```
> **A missing constant term means c = 0, which forces one zero to be 0** — as the common factor of u makes obvious.

---

Find the zeroes of `t² − 15` and verify.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> use a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b):
>
> t² − 15 = (t − √15)(t + √15)      →  zeroes √15 and −√15
>
> a = 1, b = 0, c = −15
>
> sum     = √15 + (−√15) = 0     and  −b/a = 0     ✔
> product = (√15)(−√15) = −15    and   c/a = −15   ✔
> ```
> **A missing x term means b = 0, which forces the zeroes to be equal and opposite** — their sum has to be zero.

---

Find the zeroes of `3x² − x − 4` and verify.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3 × (−4) = −12, and −4 + 3 = −1
>
> 3x² − 4x + 3x − 4 = x(3x − 4) + 1(3x − 4) = (3x − 4)(x + 1)
>
> zeroes: x = 4/3 and x = −1
>
> a = 3, b = −1, c = −4
>
> sum     = 4/3 − 1 = 1/3    and  −b/a = 1/3    ✔
> product = (4/3)(−1) = −4/3 and   c/a = −4/3   ✔
> ```

---

Derive the relationships between the zeroes and coefficients of `ax² + bx + c`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **If α and β are the zeroes, then `x − α` and `x − β` are factors:**
> ```
> ax² + bx + c = k(x − α)(x − β)              for some constant k
>              = k[x² − (α + β)x + αβ]
>              = kx² − k(α + β)x + kαβ
>
> comparing coefficients on both sides:
>
>       a = k          b = −k(α + β)          c = kαβ
>
> so    α + β = −b/a          αβ = c/a
> ```
> **The derivation is just "multiply out the factored form and match the coefficients"** — which is why it works for any degree.

---

State the quadratic relationships, and say where the minus sign goes.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> sum of zeroes      α + β = −b/a = −(coefficient of x) / (coefficient of x²)
> product of zeroes  αβ    =  c/a =  (constant term)   / (coefficient of x²)
> ```
> **The MINUS SIGN belongs to the SUM only.** The product has no minus.
>
> **That single detail causes most of the mistakes in this chapter** — so check it against a case you know: for `(x−1)(x−2) = x² − 3x + 2`, the sum is 3 and `−b/a = 3` ✔, the product is 2 and `c/a = 2` ✔.

---

Find a quadratic polynomial with sum of zeroes −3 and product 2. Is your answer unique?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> α + β = −b/a = −3          αβ = c/a = 2
>
> take a = 1  →  b = 3,  c = 2
>
> polynomial:  x² + 3x + 2
> ```
> **No, it is NOT unique. Any `k(x² + 3x + 2)` with k real has exactly the same zeroes** — multiplying a polynomial by a non-zero constant scales the whole curve vertically but **does not move where it crosses the x-axis**.

---

State the standard recipe for building a quadratic from its zeroes.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x² − (sum of zeroes)x + (product of zeroes)
> ```
> **Take a = 1 and read the coefficients straight off** — noting the **minus sign in front of the sum**, exactly as in `α + β = −b/a`.
>
> **Multiply through by any constant k if you want to clear fractions** — the zeroes are unchanged.

---

Find a quadratic polynomial whose zeroes have sum 1/4 and product −1.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x² − (1/4)x + (−1) = x² − (1/4)x − 1
>
> multiply by 4 to clear the fraction:
>
>       4x² − x − 4
> ```

---

Find a quadratic polynomial whose zeroes have sum √2 and product 1/3.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x² − √2 x + 1/3
>
> multiply by 3:
>
>       3x² − 3√2 x + 1
> ```
> **Irrational coefficients are perfectly acceptable** — it is irrational *powers* that a polynomial cannot have.

---

Find quadratic polynomials with (i) sum 0, product √5; (ii) sum 1, product 1; (iii) sum −1/4, product 1/4; (iv) sum 4, product 1.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (i)   x² − 0·x + √5      =  x² + √5
> (ii)  x² − x + 1
> (iii) x² + (1/4)x + 1/4  →  ×4  →  4x² + x + 1
> (iv)  x² − 4x + 1
> ```
> **In (iii) note the sign carefully:** the sum is −1/4, so `−(sum) = +1/4` — the minus in the formula and the minus in the sum cancel.

---

State the relationships between the zeroes and coefficients of a cubic, and give the pattern that links them to the quadratic case.

> [!ANSWER]
> **For `ax³ + bx² + cx + d` with zeroes α, β, γ:**
> ```
> α + β + γ            = −b/a          taken ONE at a time
> αβ + βγ + γα         =  c/a          taken TWO at a time
> αβγ                  = −d/a          taken THREE at a time
> ```
> **The pattern: the SIGNS ALTERNATE — minus, plus, minus — and you work down the coefficients b, c, d in order, each divided by a.**
>
> **The quadratic is the same pattern stopped early:** sum (one at a time) `= −b/a`, product (two at a time) `= +c/a`.

---

The zeroes of `2x³ − 5x² − 14x + 8` are 4, −2 and 1/2. Verify all three relationships.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a = 2, b = −5, c = −14, d = 8
>
> sum                 = 4 + (−2) + 1/2 = 5/2
>                     −b/a = 5/2                        ✔
>
> sum of products     = 4(−2) + (−2)(1/2) + (1/2)(4)
> taken two at a time = −8 − 1 + 2 = −7
>                       c/a = −14/2 = −7                ✔
>
> product             = 4 × (−2) × (1/2) = −4
>                     −d/a = −8/2 = −4                  ✔
> ```

---

Verify that 3, −1 and −1/3 are the zeroes of `p(x) = 3x³ − 5x² − 11x − 3`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> p(3)    = 3(27) − 5(9) − 11(3) − 3 = 81 − 45 − 33 − 3 = 0     ✔
>
> p(−1)   = 3(−1) − 5(1) − 11(−1) − 3 = −3 − 5 + 11 − 3 = 0     ✔
>
> p(−1/3) = 3(−1/27) − 5(1/9) − 11(−1/3) − 3
>         = −1/9 − 5/9 + 11/3 − 3
>         = −6/9 + 11/3 − 3 = −2/3 + 11/3 − 9/3 = 0             ✔
> ```
> **All three give 0, so all three are zeroes** — and since a cubic has at most three, these are all of them.

---

For that cubic, verify the three coefficient relationships.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a = 3, b = −5, c = −11, d = −3;   α = 3, β = −1, γ = −1/3
>
> α + β + γ    = 3 − 1 − 1/3 = 5/3
>              −b/a = 5/3                                ✔
>
> αβ + βγ + γα = 3(−1) + (−1)(−1/3) + (−1/3)(3)
>              = −3 + 1/3 − 1 = −11/3
>                c/a = −11/3                             ✔
>
> αβγ          = 3 × (−1) × (−1/3) = 1
>              −d/a = −(−3)/3 = 1                        ✔
> ```

---

If α and β are the zeroes of `x² − 5x + 6`, find `1/α + 1/β` without finding the zeroes.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> α + β = −b/a = 5          αβ = c/a = 6
>
> 1/α + 1/β = (β + α)/(αβ) = 5/6
> ```
> **The whole point of the relationships is that you never need the zeroes themselves.** (Here they happen to be 2 and 3, and `1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6` ✔ — but the method works even when the zeroes are ugly.)

---

If α and β are the zeroes of `x² − 7x + 10`, find `α² + β²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> α + β = 7          αβ = 10
>
> use the identity (α + β)² = α² + 2αβ + β²
>
> α² + β² = (α + β)² − 2αβ = 49 − 20 = 29
> ```
> **The trick worth remembering: rewrite what you want in terms of the SUM and the PRODUCT**, since those are what the coefficients give you directly.

---

One zero of `2x² − 3x + k` is 2. Find k, and then the other zero.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> since 2 is a zero, p(2) = 0:
>
> 2(4) − 3(2) + k = 0
> 8 − 6 + k = 0
> k = −2
> ```
> **The other zero:** with k = −2 the polynomial is `2x² − 3x − 2`, and
> ```
> product of zeroes = c/a = −2/2 = −1
> so  2 × (other) = −1  →  other zero = −1/2
> ```
> **Check with the sum:** `2 + (−1/2) = 3/2` and `−b/a = 3/2` ✔

---

The zeroes of `x² + px + q` are double the zeroes of `2x² − 5x − 3`. Find p and q.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2x² − 5x − 3 = (2x + 1)(x − 3)      →  zeroes −1/2 and 3
>
> doubling them:  −1 and 6
>
> sum     = −1 + 6 = 5           product = (−1)(6) = −6
>
> for x² + px + q:   sum = −p  and  product = q
>
> −p = 5   →   p = −5
>  q = −6
> ```
> **So the polynomial is `x² − 5x − 6`** — and factorising it as `(x − 6)(x + 1)` confirms the zeroes are 6 and −1 ✔

---

Six graphs of `y = p(x)` are shown. How would you read off the number of zeroes in each case?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Count the number of points where the curve MEETS the x-axis** — each such point contributes one zero.
>
> **Points to be careful about:**
> - A curve that **TOUCHES** the axis without crossing still gives a zero there (two equal zeroes counted as one value).
> - A curve **entirely above or entirely below** the axis gives **no zeroes at all**.
> - **Where the curve crosses the y-axis is irrelevant** — that is p(0), not a zero.
> - The count is limited by the degree: **at most n crossings for degree n**.

---

Explain why multiplying a polynomial by 5 does not change its zeroes.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> if p(k) = 0, then 5·p(k) = 5 × 0 = 0
> ```
> **so k is a zero of 5p(x) as well.**
>
> **Graphically:** multiplying by 5 **stretches the curve vertically**, making every y-value five times as far from the axis — **but a point that was AT height 0 stays at height 0.** The crossing points do not move.
>
> **This is exactly why "find a quadratic with these zeroes" has infinitely many answers.**

---

A student says a cubic must have three zeroes because its degree is 3. Correct them.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The degree gives a MAXIMUM, not a count. A cubic has AT MOST three zeroes.**
>
> **Counter-examples:**
> ```
> x³        →  only ONE zero (0)
> x³ − x²   →  only TWO zeroes (0 and 1)
> ```
> **Why: the graph of a cubic must cross the x-axis at least once** (it goes from very negative to very positive), **but it need not cross three times** — it may turn back before reaching the axis again, or touch it and turn away.
>
> **The general statement: a polynomial of degree n meets the x-axis at AT MOST n points, so it has at most n zeroes.**
