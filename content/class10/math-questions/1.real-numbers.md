State the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, and explain which half of it does the real work.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a PRODUCT OF PRIMES, and this factorisation is UNIQUE, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.**
>
> **The theorem says TWO things:**
> 1. **Existence** — every composite number *can* be written as a product of primes.
> 2. **UNIQUENESS** — there is **one and only one** way to do it, order aside.
>
> **Uniqueness is the half that does the work.** It means a number's prime factorisation is a **fingerprint**: if a prime does not appear in the factorisation you found, **it is not there at all**. That single fact settles a whole class of questions.

---

Express each as a product of prime factors: 140, 156, 3825, 5005, 7429.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 140  = 2 × 2 × 5 × 7          = 2² × 5 × 7
> 156  = 2 × 2 × 3 × 13         = 2² × 3 × 13
> 3825 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 5 × 17     = 3² × 5² × 17
> 5005 = 5 × 7 × 11 × 13
> 7429 = 17 × 19 × 23
> ```
> **Method: keep dividing by the smallest prime that goes in, until you reach 1.** For 7429, test primes in order — it survives 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 and finally 17 divides it, giving 437 = 19 × 23.

Find the LCM and HCF of 26 and 91, and verify that LCM × HCF equals their product.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 26 = 2 × 13          91 = 7 × 13
>
> HCF = 13                              (smallest power of the common prime)
> LCM = 2 × 7 × 13 = 182                (greatest power of each prime involved)
>
> check:  13 × 182 = 2366    and    26 × 91 = 2366   ✔
> ```

Find the LCM and HCF of 336 and 54, and verify the product rule.

> 336 = 2⁴ × 3 × 7          54 = 2 × 3³
>
> HCF = 2¹ × 3¹ = 6

>

> ```

---

Why does HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b always hold for two numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because for each prime, the HCF takes the SMALLER exponent and the LCM takes the LARGER one — so together they use BOTH.**
>
> Take a prime appearing as `p^m` in a and `p^n` in b, with m ≤ n:
> ```
> HCF contributes  p^m        LCM contributes  p^n
> HCF × LCM        p^(m+n)
>
> a × b also contributes      p^m × p^n = p^(m+n)
> ```
> **Every prime contributes the same total to both sides**, so the two products are equal.

Find the HCF and LCM of 6, 72 and 120.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 6   = 2 × 3
> 72  = 2³ × 3²
> 120 = 2³ × 3 × 5
>
> HCF = 2¹ × 3¹ = 6        smallest power of each COMMON prime (2 and 3)
> LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5¹ = 360 greatest power of EVERY prime involved
> ```

---

Show that HCF × LCM = product does NOT work for three numbers, using 6, 72 and 120.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 6 × 72 × 120 = 51840
>
> HCF × LCM    = 6 × 360 = 2160        ✘  not equal
> ```
> **The two-number rule is a SPECIAL CASE, not a general one.**
>
> **Why it breaks:** with two numbers each prime has exactly two exponents, and the HCF and LCM take one each. **With three numbers there are three exponents, and the smallest and the largest miss out the middle one entirely.**
>
> **The correct three-number formulas:**
> ```
> LCM(p,q,r) = (p × q × r × HCF(p,q,r)) / (HCF(p,q) × HCF(q,r) × HCF(p,r))
> HCF(p,q,r) = (p × q × r × LCM(p,q,r)) / (LCM(p,q) × LCM(q,r) × LCM(p,r))
> ```

Find the LCM and HCF of 17, 23 and 29.

Find the LCM and HCF of 8, 9 and 25.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 8 = 2³        9 = 3²        25 = 5²
>
> HCF = 1      no prime is common to any two of them
> LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5² = 8 × 9 × 25 = 1800
> ```
> **They are pairwise coprime although none of them is prime** — coprime means *sharing no factor*, not *being prime*.

Given that HCF(306, 657) = 9, find LCM(306, 657).

> HCF × LCM = product

> LCM = (306 × 657) / 9 = 201042 / 9 = 22338

Check whether 6ⁿ can end with the digit 0 for any natural number n.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
> ```
> A number ends in 0 only if it is divisible by 10 = 2 × 5,
> so 5 must appear in its prime factorisation.
>
> 6 = 2 × 3, so 6ⁿ = 2ⁿ × 3ⁿ
> The ONLY primes in the factorisation of 6ⁿ are 2 and 3.
>
> By the UNIQUENESS of prime factorisation, there is no 5 anywhere in it.
> ```
> **So 6ⁿ is never divisible by 5, and can never end in 0.**
>
> **Notice exactly where the theorem is used:** without uniqueness, all we could say is "I didn't find a 5". Uniqueness upgrades that to "**there is no 5**".

---

Can 4ⁿ ever end with the digit zero?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — the same argument.**
> ```
> 4ⁿ = (2²)ⁿ = 2²ⁿ — the only prime present is 2.
> By uniqueness, 5 does not divide 4ⁿ for any n.
> ```
> **Ending in 0 requires a factor of 5, and there is none.**

Explain why 7 × 11 × 13 + 13 and 7 × 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 + 5 are composite.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both have an obvious common factor once you look for it.**
> ```
> 7 × 11 × 13 + 13 = 13 × (7 × 11 + 1) = 13 × 78 = 1014
>     divisible by 13 and by 78, so it has factors besides 1 and itself → COMPOSITE
>
> 7 × 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 + 5 = 5040 + 5 = 5 × (1008 + 1) = 5 × 1009
>     divisible by 5 → COMPOSITE
> ```
> **The trick in both: TAKE OUT THE COMMON FACTOR.** The last term is deliberately chosen to be one of the factors in the product, so it can be pulled out of both terms — and any number with a factor other than 1 and itself is composite.

---

Sonia takes 18 minutes and Ravi 12 minutes to complete one round of a circular field, starting together in the same direction. After how long will they next meet at the starting point?

> [!ANSWER]
> **After the LCM of their times.**
> ```
> 18 = 2 × 3²        12 = 2² × 3
> LCM = 2² × 3² = 36 minutes
> ```
> **They meet at the starting point after 36 minutes.**
>
> **Why LCM and not HCF:** they meet **at the start** only at a moment that is a whole number of rounds for **both**. That means a common multiple of 18 and 12, and the **first** such moment is the **least** common multiple. (Sonia has done 2 rounds, Ravi 3.)

---

Bells at a temple toll at intervals of 9, 12 and 15 minutes. If they toll together at 6 a.m., when do they next toll together?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 9 = 3²        12 = 2² × 3        15 = 3 × 5
> LCM = 2² × 3² × 5 = 180 minutes = 3 hours
> ```
> **They toll together again at 9 a.m.**
>
> **Same reasoning as the circular track:** a shared event happens at a **common multiple** of the intervals, and the next one is the **least**.

Two tankers hold 850 and 680 litres. Find the maximum capacity of a container that can measure both exactly.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The HCF — because the container must go a whole number of times into BOTH.**
> ```
> 850 = 2 × 5² × 17          680 = 2³ × 5 × 17
>
> HCF = 2 × 5 × 17 = 170 litres
> ```
> **Maximum capacity: 170 litres.** (It fits 5 times into 850 and 4 times into 680.)
>
> **How to tell HCF from LCM in word problems:** *dividing up* or *fitting into* → **HCF**; *coming together* or *repeating* → **LCM**.

Find the largest number that divides 245 and 1029 leaving remainder 5 in each case.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Subtract the remainder first, then take the HCF.**
> ```
> 245 − 5 = 240        1029 − 5 = 1024
>
> 240 = 2⁴ × 3 × 5          1024 = 2¹⁰
>
> HCF = 2⁴ = 16
> ```
> **The number is 16.**
>
> **Why subtract first:** "leaves remainder 5" means the number divides **exactly** into (245 − 5) and (1029 − 5). Once the remainders are removed it becomes an ordinary HCF question.
>
> **Check: 16 must be bigger than the remainder 5** — otherwise the remainder would itself be divisible, which is impossible. ✔

---

Two numbers have HCF 9 and LCM 90. Is that possible? What about HCF 18 and LCM 380?

> [!ANSWER]
> **HCF 9, LCM 90 — POSSIBLE.** `90 ÷ 9 = 10`, a whole number.
>
> **HCF 18, LCM 380 — IMPOSSIBLE.** `380 ÷ 18 = 21.1…`, not a whole number.
>
> **Why the test works: the HCF must ALWAYS divide the LCM.**
>
> Every prime appears in the HCF with its **smallest** exponent and in the LCM with its **largest**, and smallest ≤ largest. So every prime power in the HCF also appears (at least as strongly) in the LCM — which is exactly what "HCF divides LCM" means.

State and prove the theorem that if a prime p divides a², then p divides a.

> [!ANSWER]
> **THEOREM: Let p be a PRIME. If p divides a², then p divides a** (a a positive integer).
>
> **Proof.**
> ```
> Write a = p₁p₂…pₙ as a product of primes (not necessarily distinct).
>
> Then a² = (p₁p₂…pₙ)(p₁p₂…pₙ) = p₁² p₂² … pₙ²
>
> p divides a², so by the Fundamental Theorem, p is one of the prime factors of a².
> By UNIQUENESS, the only primes in a² are p₁, p₂, …, pₙ.
> So p is one of p₁, p₂, …, pₙ.
>
> Since a = p₁p₂…pₙ, p divides a.  ∎
> ```

---

Why must p be prime in that theorem? Give a counter-example for a composite divisor.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a composite number can be SPLIT between the two copies of a, while a prime cannot.**
>
> **Counter-example:**
> ```
> 4 divides 6² = 36        but 4 does NOT divide 6
> ```
> **What happened: 36 = 6 × 6 = (2×3)(2×3), and the 4 = 2 × 2 is made of ONE factor of 2 from EACH copy of 6.** Neither copy contains a 4 by itself.
>
> **A prime cannot be assembled from pieces**, which is exactly why the theorem holds for primes and fails for composites.

Prove that √2 is irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> **By CONTRADICTION.**
> ```
> Assume √2 IS rational.
> Then √2 = r/s for integers r, s (s ≠ 0).
> Divide out any common factor, so √2 = a/b with a and b COPRIME.
>
> b√2 = a
> Squaring:       2b² = a²
>
> So 2 divides a²  →  2 divides a.       (theorem above, p = 2)
> Write a = 2c.
>
> Substituting:   2b² = 4c²,  so  b² = 2c²
> So 2 divides b²  →  2 divides b.
>
> Therefore a and b share the factor 2.
> But a and b were taken COPRIME.        ← CONTRADICTION
> ```
> **The contradiction arose from the assumption that √2 is rational. Therefore √2 is IRRATIONAL.**

---

In that proof, why is the step "divide out any common factor" essential?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because it sets the trap that the proof later springs.**
>
> The whole argument ends by showing that **a and b share the factor 2**. That is only a contradiction if we have already insisted they share **nothing**.
>
> **Without that step there is no contradiction at all** — "a and b are both even" would be an unremarkable fact about some fraction, not an impossibility.
>
> **And the step is always legitimate:** any fraction can be reduced to lowest terms without changing its value.

Prove that √3 is irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Assume √3 = a/b with a, b coprime, b ≠ 0.
>
> b√3 = a
> Squaring:      3b² = a²
>
> So 3 divides a²  →  3 divides a.  Write a = 3c.
>
> 3b² = 9c²  →  b² = 3c²
> So 3 divides b²  →  3 divides b.
>
> a and b share the factor 3 — contradicting coprimality.
>
> Therefore √3 is irrational.
> ```

---

Prove that √5 is irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Assume √5 = a/b with a, b coprime, b ≠ 0.
>
> b√5 = a  →  squaring:  5b² = a²
> So 5 divides a²  →  5 divides a.  Write a = 5c.
>
> 5b² = 25c²  →  b² = 5c²
> So 5 divides b²  →  5 divides b.
>
> a and b share the factor 5 — contradiction.
>
> Therefore √5 is irrational.
> ```
> **The proof is identical in shape for 2, 3 and 5 — and works for √p for ANY prime p**, because the only tool it needs (if p divides a² then p divides a) holds for every prime.

---

Show that 5 − √3 is irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Assume 5 − √3 IS rational, so 5 − √3 = a/b with a, b integers, b ≠ 0.
>
> Rearranging:    √3 = 5 − a/b = (5b − a)/b
>
> a and b are integers, so (5b − a)/b is a ratio of integers — RATIONAL.
> So √3 would be rational.
>
> But √3 is irrational (proved above).   ← CONTRADICTION
>
> Therefore 5 − √3 is irrational.
> ```

Show that 3√2 is irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Assume 3√2 = a/b with a, b integers, b ≠ 0.
>
> Rearranging:    √2 = a/(3b)
>
> 3, a and b are integers, so a/(3b) is RATIONAL.
> So √2 would be rational — contradicting its known irrationality.
>
> Therefore 3√2 is irrational.
> ```

---

Prove that 3 + 2√5 is irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Assume 3 + 2√5 = a/b with a, b integers, b ≠ 0.
>
> 2√5 = a/b − 3 = (a − 3b)/b
>
>  √5 = (a − 3b)/(2b)
>
> a and b are integers, so (a − 3b)/(2b) is RATIONAL.
> So √5 would be rational — contradicting its irrationality.
>
> Therefore 3 + 2√5 is irrational.
> ```
> **Note the two operations undone in order: first the addition of 3, then the multiplication by 2** — each one leaves the right-hand side built only from integers.

---

Prove that 1/√2 is irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Assume 1/√2 = a/b with a, b integers, b ≠ 0 (and a ≠ 0, since 1/√2 ≠ 0).
>
> Taking reciprocals:   √2 = b/a
>
> b and a are integers, so b/a is RATIONAL.
> So √2 would be rational — contradiction.
>
> Therefore 1/√2 is irrational.
> ```
> **Alternatively:** `1/√2 = √2/2`, which is a **non-zero rational (1/2) times an irrational (√2)** — and that is always irrational.

---

Prove that 7√5 and 6 + √2 are irrational.

> [!ANSWER]
> **7√5:**
> ```
> Assume 7√5 = a/b with a, b integers, b ≠ 0.
> Then √5 = a/(7b), which is rational — contradicting √5 irrational.
> ```
> **6 + √2:**
> ```
> Assume 6 + √2 = a/b with a, b integers, b ≠ 0.
> Then √2 = a/b − 6 = (a − 6b)/b, which is rational — contradicting √2 irrational.
> ```
> **Both are irrational.**

---

Name the general shape of all these proofs, in four steps.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Assume the combination IS rational**, equal to a/b with a, b integers.
> 2. **Rearrange to isolate the known irrational number** on one side.
> 3. **Observe the other side is built only from INTEGERS**, so it is rational.
> 4. **That contradicts the known irrationality** — so the assumption was wrong.
>
> **The point worth noticing: you never prove irrationality from scratch again.** √2 and √3 were proved the hard way, using the prime-divisibility theorem. Everything after that is proved by **breaking the assumption against a result you already have**.

---

State the two rules about combining rationals and irrationals.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **The SUM or DIFFERENCE of a rational and an irrational number is IRRATIONAL.**
> - **The PRODUCT or QUOTIENT of a NON-ZERO rational and an irrational number is IRRATIONAL.**
>
> **Why "non-zero" is in the second rule:** `0 × √2 = 0`, which is perfectly rational. **Zero destroys the irrationality**, so it must be excluded.

---

Is the sum of two irrational numbers always irrational? Is the product?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neither — both can be rational.**
> ```
> SUM:      (2 + √3) + (2 − √3) = 4            both terms irrational, sum rational
> PRODUCT:  √2 × √2 = 2                        both factors irrational, product rational
>           (3 + √5)(3 − √5) = 9 − 5 = 4
> ```
> **Why the rules in the previous question don't apply:** they concern a **rational combined with an irrational**. When BOTH numbers are irrational, the irrational parts **can cancel** — and there is no theorem to prevent it.
>
> **The lesson: read the conditions of a rule before using it.**

---

A student writes: "√2 = 1.41421356…, and the decimals go on forever, so √2 is irrational." What is wrong with this argument?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Going on forever is not enough — RATIONAL numbers can do that too.**
> ```
> 1/3 = 0.3333333…        1/7 = 0.142857142857…
> ```
> Both are perfectly rational and both have non-terminating decimals.
>
> **What actually distinguishes them is REPEATING.** A rational number's decimal either terminates or **repeats a fixed block for ever**; an irrational number's does neither.
>
> **And the deeper problem: you can never CHECK "never repeats" by looking at digits.** No matter how many you compute, a repeat could begin later. **That is precisely why the proof by contradiction is needed** — it settles in a few lines what no amount of computation could.

Verify the Fundamental Theorem for 32760, and use it to write the number as a product of powers of primes.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 32760 ÷ 2 = 16380
> 16380 ÷ 2 = 8190
>  8190 ÷ 2 = 4095
>  4095 ÷ 3 = 1365
>  1365 ÷ 3 = 455
>   455 ÷ 5 = 91
>    91 ÷ 7 = 13
>    13 ÷ 13 = 1
>
> 32760 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 13 = 2³ × 3² × 5 × 7 × 13
> ```
> **The theorem guarantees that ANY route through the factor tree ends at the same answer.** Start by dividing by 5 or by 13 instead, and the primes you finish with are identical — only the order of discovery changes.

The HCF of two numbers is 27 and their LCM is 162. If one number is 54, find the other.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> HCF × LCM = product of the two numbers
>
> 27 × 162 = 54 × x
>
> 4374 = 54x
>
> x = 4374 / 54 = 81
> ```
> **The other number is 81.**
>
> **Sanity check: does 27 divide both 54 and 81?** 54 = 27 × 2 and 81 = 27 × 3 ✔ — and since 2 and 3 are coprime, 27 really is the **highest** common factor.

Explain how the prime factorisation of the denominator decides whether a fraction's decimal expansion terminates.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The decimal expansion of a rational number p/q (in lowest terms) TERMINATES exactly when the prime factorisation of q contains only 2s and 5s** — that is, `q = 2ⁿ 5ᵐ`. Otherwise it is **non-terminating repeating**.
>
> **Why 2 and 5 specifically:** a terminating decimal is a fraction whose denominator is a power of **10**, and `10 = 2 × 5`. A denominator built only from 2s and 5s can always be multiplied up to a power of 10; one containing any other prime never can.
> ```
> 3/8   = 3/2³      → multiply by 5³/5³ → 375/1000 = 0.375        TERMINATES
> 7/40  = 7/(2³×5)  → 175/1000 = 0.175                            TERMINATES
> 2/3               → 3 is neither 2 nor 5 → 0.6666…              REPEATS
> 5/12  = 5/(2²×3)  → the 3 spoils it → 0.41666…                  REPEATS
> ```
> **Note "in lowest terms" matters:** 6/15 looks bad but reduces to 2/5, which terminates.
