## 2.1 Indicators — how you tell an acid from a base

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An INDICATOR is a dye whose colour (or smell) changes in acid and in base.**

| Indicator | In ACID | In BASE |
|---|---|---|
| **Litmus** (purple when neutral) | **red** | **blue** |
| **Methyl orange** | **red** | **yellow** |
| **Phenolphthalein** | **colourless** | **pink** |
| **Turmeric** | stays yellow | **red** |

**Litmus is a purple dye extracted from LICHEN**, a plant of the division Thallophyta. Red cabbage, turmeric and the petals of Hydrangea, Petunia and Geranium work too.

> [!NOTE]
> **OLFACTORY INDICATORS change their SMELL rather than their colour** — useful for a visually impaired student.
>
> **Onion and vanilla essence lose their smell in a BASE. Clove oil does the same.** So a strip of cloth kept overnight with chopped onions can test a solution just as litmus paper does.

> [!TIP]
> **With only RED litmus paper you can still identify acid, base and water.**
> - the solution that turns it **blue** is the **base**
> - the other two leave it red — **so take the paper turned blue by the base** and touch it with each of the remaining two
> - **whichever turns it back to red is the ACID**; the one that leaves it blue is **water**
>
> **The trick is that an indicator works in both directions**, so a used strip is still a tool.

## 2.2 The chemical properties of acids and bases

### With metals

```
Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas

Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2
Mg + 2HCl  → MgCl2 + H2
```
**The metal DISPLACES hydrogen from the acid.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Bases usually do not do this — but some do.**
> ```
> 2NaOH(aq) + Zn(s) → Na2ZnO2(s) + H2(g)
>                     (sodium zincate)
> ```
> **Hydrogen is released again**, but the salt has a negative ion made of the **metal and oxygen**. **Not all metals behave this way with bases.**

**Testing the gas: bring a burning candle to a soap bubble filled with it — it burns with a POP.** That pop is the hydrogen test.

### With metal carbonates and hydrogencarbonates

```
Metal carbonate / hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + Carbon dioxide + Water

Na2CO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
NaHCO3(s) +  HCl(aq) →  NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
```

**Testing the gas — pass it through LIME WATER:**
```
Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)
(lime water)           (white precipitate — the milkiness)
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Lime water turning MILKY is the test for carbon dioxide** — the milkiness is a fine white precipitate of calcium carbonate.
>
> **But pass EXCESS CO2 and the milkiness disappears again:**
> ```
> CaCO3(s) + H2O(l) + CO2(g) → Ca(HCO3)2(aq)      soluble!
> ```
> **Why: the insoluble carbonate is converted into soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate**, which dissolves and the solution goes clear. **So the test must be done with a limited amount of gas.**

**Limestone, chalk and marble are all calcium carbonate, CaCO3.**

### Acid + base = neutralisation

```
Base + Acid → Salt + Water

NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)
```
**Add phenolphthalein to NaOH and it turns pink. Add HCl drop by drop and the pink DISAPPEARS — the base has been cancelled. Add NaOH again and the pink returns.**

### Metal oxides and non-metal oxides

```
Metal oxide + Acid → Salt + Water
CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl2(aq) + H2O(l)
                    the solution turns BLUE-GREEN

Non-metal oxide + Base → Salt + Water
CO2(g) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **METAL OXIDES ARE BASIC. NON-METAL OXIDES ARE ACIDIC.**
>
> **How we know: they behave like bases and acids respectively.** A metal oxide reacts with an acid to give salt and water — exactly what a base does. A non-metal oxide reacts with a base to give salt and water — exactly what an acid does.
>
> **The classification comes from behaviour, not from a rule to be memorised.**

## 2.3 What all acids and all bases have in common

**Acids all release hydrogen with metals — so hydrogen seems to be the common ingredient. But glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen too, and they are not acids. So having hydrogen is not enough.**

**The bulb experiment settles it:** two nails in a beaker connected to a battery and a bulb.
```
dilute HCl, dilute H2SO4   →  the bulb GLOWS
glucose, alcohol           →  the bulb does NOT glow
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A solution conducts electricity only if it contains free IONS.**
>
> **Acids conduct because they release H⁺(aq) ions in water. Glucose and alcohol have hydrogen, but they do not release it as ions** — so no current flows and they are not acids.
>
> **This is the real definition:**
> - **ACID — a substance that produces H⁺(aq) ions in water**
> - **BASE — a substance that produces OH⁻(aq) ions in water**

### Water is essential

**Dry HCl gas does NOT turn dry blue litmus red. The same gas passed over WET litmus turns it red at once.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The H⁺ ion cannot separate from the HCl molecule without water.**
> ```
> HCl + H2O → H3O⁺ + Cl⁻
> ```
> **A bare H⁺ ion cannot exist on its own** — it is a naked proton and immediately attaches to a water molecule, giving the **HYDRONIUM ion H3O⁺**.
> ```
> H⁺ + H2O → H3O⁺
> ```
> **So we always write H⁺(aq) or H3O⁺, never a lonely H⁺.** No water, no ions, no acidity.

```
             H2O
NaOH(s)   --------→  Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
KOH(s)    --------→  K⁺(aq)  + OH⁻(aq)
Mg(OH)2(s)--------→  Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)
```

> [!NOTE]
> **An ALKALI is a base that DISSOLVES in water.** All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis — many simply do not dissolve. Alkalis feel soapy, taste bitter and are corrosive. **Never taste or touch them.**

**Neutralisation, seen at ion level:**
```
H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l)
```
**That single equation IS neutralisation.** The salt's ions were spectators; the real event is H⁺ and OH⁻ combining to make water.

### Diluting an acid — the safety rule

> [!WARNING]
> **ALWAYS ADD ACID TO WATER, NEVER WATER TO ACID.**
>
> **Why: dissolving a concentrated acid or base in water is HIGHLY EXOTHERMIC.**
>
> **Add acid slowly to a large volume of water** and the heat is spread through all that water, which warms gently. **Add a little water to concentrated acid** and all that heat is released in a tiny volume — the mixture can **splash out and burn you**, and the glass can crack from local heating.
>
> **This is the one safety rule in the chapter that can genuinely injure you.**

**DILUTION reduces the number of H3O⁺ or OH⁻ ions per unit volume** — the acid is not destroyed, only spread out.

## 2.4 The pH scale

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **pH measures the HYDROGEN ION CONCENTRATION of a solution.** The **p** is from the German *potenz*, meaning **power**.
> ```
> 0 ---------- 7 ---------- 14
> very acidic  NEUTRAL   very alkaline
>
> pH < 7   acidic       more H⁺
> pH = 7   neutral
> pH > 7   basic        more OH⁻
> ```
> **HIGHER hydrogen-ion concentration means LOWER pH** — the scale runs backwards, which is the single thing most often got wrong.

**A UNIVERSAL INDICATOR is a mixture of several indicators**, showing a different colour at each pH. Paper soaked in it is pH paper.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **STRONG vs WEAK is not the same as CONCENTRATED vs DILUTE.**
>
> **A STRONG acid gives MORE H⁺ ions; a WEAK acid gives fewer** — even at the same concentration. One molar HCl and one molar acetic acid have the same concentration, but HCl releases far more H⁺, so HCl is strong and acetic acid weak.
>
> **Concentration is how much acid you put in; strength is how much of it ionises.**

> [!NOTE]
> **A basic solution still contains SOME H⁺ ions** — it is basic because the OH⁻ ions vastly outnumber them, not because H⁺ has vanished.

### Why pH matters in everyday life

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Our bodies work between pH 7.0 and 7.8.** Living things survive only in a narrow pH band.
> - **ACID RAIN — rain with pH below 5.6.** It lowers the pH of rivers and makes survival hard for aquatic life.
> - **The STOMACH makes hydrochloric acid** to digest food. Too much causes pain, and an **ANTACID** — a mild base like **magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia)** — neutralises the excess.
> - **TOOTH DECAY begins below pH 5.5.** Enamel is calcium hydroxyapatite, the hardest substance in the body; it does not dissolve in water but **corrodes below 5.5**. Bacteria make acid from leftover sugar, so **cleaning the mouth after eating** and using a **basic toothpaste** prevents decay.
> - **A BEE STING injects an acid** — rubbing on **baking soda**, a mild base, relieves it. **Nettle stings inject methanoic acid**, and rubbing on a **dock leaf** (which grows beside nettles) neutralises it.
> - **SOIL that is too acidic is treated with quick lime, slaked lime or chalk** — all basic — to raise its pH.

**Some naturally occurring acids:**
```
vinegar — acetic acid          curd — lactic acid
lemon, orange — citric acid    tamarind — tartaric acid
tomato — oxalic acid           ant and nettle stings — methanoic acid
```

## 2.5 More about salts

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A SALT is what is left when the H⁺ of an acid is replaced by a metal ion** — every salt therefore has a **parent acid and a parent base**.
> ```
> NaCl      from HCl        + NaOH
> Na2SO4    from H2SO4      + NaOH
> CH3COONa  from CH3COOH    + NaOH
> NH4Cl     from HCl        + NH4OH
> ```
> **Salts sharing a positive or a negative radical form a FAMILY** — NaCl and Na2SO4 are both sodium salts; NaCl and KCl are both chloride salts.

### The pH of a salt

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Salt of | pH | Example |
> |---|---|---|
> | **strong acid + strong base** | **7 — neutral** | NaCl |
> | **strong acid + weak base** | **less than 7 — acidic** | NH4Cl |
> | **strong base + weak acid** | **more than 7 — basic** | Na2CO3 |
>
> **Why: the stronger parent wins.** In sodium carbonate the base (NaOH) is strong and the acid (carbonic acid) is weak, so the base's character survives in the salt and the solution comes out basic.

### Chemicals made from common salt

**Sodium chloride comes from seawater, and as ROCK SALT mined like coal** — brown crystals from seas that dried up long ago. From it come several industrial chemicals.

**THE CHLOR-ALKALI PROCESS — electricity through brine (concentrated NaCl solution):**
```
2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl2(g) + H2(g)
```
> [!NOTE]
> **The name says what comes out: "chlor" for chlorine and "alkali" for sodium hydroxide.**
> ```
> chlorine  Cl2  — at the ANODE     → bleaching powder, PVC, disinfectants
> hydrogen  H2   — at the CATHODE   → fuels, margarine, ammonia
> sodium hydroxide — near the cathode → soaps, detergents, paper
> ```
> **All three products are useful, which is what makes the process worth running.**

**BLEACHING POWDER — chlorine on DRY slaked lime:**
```
2Ca(OH)2 + 2Cl2 → Ca(ClO)2 + CaCl2 + 2H2O
```
**Uses: bleaching cotton, linen and wood pulp; an oxidising agent in industry; and disinfecting drinking water.**

**BAKING SODA — sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO3:**
```
NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3 → NH4Cl + NaHCO3

             Heat
2NaHCO3   ---------→  Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why cakes rise: baking POWDER is baking soda plus a mild edible acid (tartaric acid).** On heating or wetting,
> ```
> NaHCO3 + H⁺ → CO2 + H2O + sodium salt of the acid
> ```
> **The CO2 bubbles trapped in the dough make it soft and spongy.**
>
> **Why the acid is included: without it, the soda alone leaves sodium carbonate behind, which tastes bitter.** The acid uses it up.
>
> **Other uses — an ingredient in antacids** (being mildly basic, it neutralises stomach acid) **and in soda-acid fire extinguishers.**

**WASHING SODA — Na2CO3·10H2O**, made by recrystallising sodium carbonate:
```
Na2CO3 + 10H2O → Na2CO3·10H2O
```
**Uses: glass, soap and paper industries; making borax; a domestic cleaning agent; and REMOVING PERMANENT HARDNESS OF WATER.**

## 2.6 Water of crystallisation

**Heat blue copper sulphate crystals and they turn WHITE, with water droplets appearing in the tube. Add a few drops of water and the BLUE RETURNS.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WATER OF CRYSTALLISATION is the fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt.**
> ```
> CuSO4·5H2O    hydrated copper sulphate — 5 water molecules
> Na2CO3·10H2O  washing soda — 10
> CaSO4·2H2O    gypsum — 2
> ```
> **The crystals are NOT wet.** The water is built into the crystal structure, in a fixed ratio — which is exactly why the formula can name a precise number. **The blue colour itself belongs to the hydrated form**, which is why it vanishes when the water is driven off and returns when water is added back.

**PLASTER OF PARIS — gypsum heated to 373 K:**
```
                373 K
CaSO4·2H2O  ----------→  CaSO4·½H2O  +  1½H2O
(gypsum)                 (Plaster of Paris)

and back again:
CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O
                     sets into a hard solid mass
```
> [!TIP]
> **How can half a water molecule exist? It cannot — and it doesn't.** The formula means **two formula units of CaSO4 SHARE one molecule of water**, so per unit it averages a half.
>
> **Uses: doctors' plaster casts for holding fractured bones in place, toys, decorative material, and smoothing surfaces.** It works because adding water turns the powder back into gypsum, which sets hard.

> [!WARNING]
> **Plaster of Paris must be stored in a moisture-proof container** — otherwise the damp in the air sets it into a useless hard lump before it is ever used.

       hydrogencarbonate                               excess CO2 clears it again)

   base — gives OH⁻(aq);  an ALKALI is a base that dissolves
