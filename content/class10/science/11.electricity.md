## 11.1 Electric current

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ELECTRIC CURRENT is the RATE OF FLOW of electric charge.**
> ```
> I = Q/t
>
> SI unit: the AMPERE (A).      1 A = 1 C/s
> ```
> **An ELECTRIC CIRCUIT is a continuous and CLOSED path for the current.** Break it anywhere — turn a switch off — and the current stops.

```
1 coulomb ≈ the charge of 6 × 10¹⁸ electrons
one electron carries −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

1 mA = 10⁻³ A          1 µA = 10⁻⁶ A
```

> [!WARNING]
> **THE CONVENTIONAL DIRECTION OF CURRENT IS OPPOSITE TO THE FLOW OF ELECTRONS.**
>
> **Why this awkward convention exists: ELECTRONS WERE NOT KNOWN when electricity was first studied.** Current was assumed to be a flow of **positive** charge, and the direction of that flow was defined as the direction of the current.
>
> **When electrons were discovered and found to be negative, the convention was already in universal use** — so it was kept. **It is a historical accident, not a physical fact.**

**An AMMETER measures current, and is always connected IN SERIES** in the circuit whose current is being measured.

## 11.2 Potential difference

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Charges do not flow by themselves.** Just as water in a perfectly horizontal tube does not flow until there is a **pressure difference**, electrons move only if there is a difference of **electric pressure** — the **POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE** — along the conductor.
> ```
> V = W/Q          potential difference = work done per unit charge
>
> SI unit: the VOLT (V).        1 V = 1 J/C
> ```
> **1 volt is the potential difference between two points when 1 joule of work is done to move a charge of 1 coulomb from one to the other.**

**A CELL generates a potential difference across its terminals by CHEMICAL ACTION — even when no current is being drawn. To MAINTAIN a current, the cell must keep spending its stored chemical energy.**

**A VOLTMETER measures potential difference, and is always connected IN PARALLEL across the two points concerned.**

> [!TIP]
> **Why ammeter in series but voltmeter in parallel.**
>
> **An ammeter must have the whole current pass THROUGH it — so it goes in the path.**
>
> **A voltmeter must compare two points, so it must be connected ACROSS them.** Putting them the wrong way round measures nothing useful, and an ammeter across a battery will short it out.

**Worked:**
```
Work done in moving 2 C across a potential difference of 12 V:
   W = VQ = 12 × 2 = 24 J
```

## 11.3 Ohm's law

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **OHM'S LAW: the potential difference across a conductor is DIRECTLY PROPORTIONAL to the current through it, PROVIDED ITS TEMPERATURE REMAINS THE SAME.**
> ```
> V ∝ I        so       V = IR
>
> R = V/I      RESISTANCE, in OHMS (Ω)
>
> 1 Ω = 1 V/A
> ```
> **The V–I graph is a STRAIGHT LINE THROUGH THE ORIGIN, and its slope is the resistance.**

> [!WARNING]
> **The condition "provided the temperature remains the same" is part of the law, not a footnote.**
>
> **Resistance CHANGES with temperature** — so a conductor that gets hot as current rises will not give a straight line. **A bulb filament is exactly such a case.**

**RESISTANCE is the property that OPPOSES the flow of electrons and controls the size of the current.**

## 11.4 What resistance depends on

**The experiment: replace the wire in a circuit and watch the ammeter.**
```
double the LENGTH (same thickness)  → the current HALVES
use a THICKER wire (same length)    → the current INCREASES
use a DIFFERENT MATERIAL (same size)→ the current CHANGES
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> R ∝ l           directly proportional to LENGTH
> R ∝ 1/A         inversely proportional to AREA OF CROSS-SECTION
>
> combining:      R = ρ l/A
>
> ρ (rho) is the RESISTIVITY of the material.   SI unit: Ω m
> ```
> **RESISTIVITY is a CHARACTERISTIC PROPERTY OF THE MATERIAL** — it does not depend on the size or shape of the piece, whereas resistance does.

> [!TIP]
> **Why R rises with length and falls with area.**
>
> **A longer wire means the electrons must push through more obstruction** — like a longer stretch of rough road. Twice the length, twice the opposition.
>
> **A thicker wire gives more room to flow through** — like widening the road. More area, less opposition per electron.
>
> **This is why R = ρl/A has l on top and A underneath.** The formula is a statement about traffic.

```
CONDUCTORS  (metals and alloys)   ρ = 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁶ Ω m
INSULATORS  (rubber, glass)       ρ = 10¹² to 10¹⁷ Ω m

silver     1.60 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m       the best conductor
copper     1.62 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m
aluminium  2.63 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m
```
**Both resistance and resistivity VARY WITH TEMPERATURE.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why particular materials are chosen for particular jobs:**
> ```
> COPPER and ALUMINIUM — transmission lines
>    very LOW resistivity, so little energy is wasted as heat on the way
>
> ALLOYS (nichrome etc.) — electric irons, toasters, heaters
>    resistivity is generally HIGHER than that of their constituent metals,
>    AND they do not OXIDISE (burn) readily at high temperature
>
> TUNGSTEN — bulb filaments, almost exclusively
>    a very high melting point (3380 °C), so it can glow white-hot
>    without melting
> ```
> **Each choice follows from what the material has to do** — carry current without loss, or get hot without being destroyed.

## 11.5 Resistors in series

```
the SAME CURRENT flows through every resistor
   (the ammeter reads the same wherever it is placed in the loop)

the potential differences ADD:   V = V1 + V2 + V3

EQUIVALENT RESISTANCE            Rs = R1 + R2 + R3
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the resistances simply add: joining resistors end to end is exactly like making ONE LONGER WIRE**, and resistance is proportional to length.
>
> **So the equivalent resistance is always GREATER than the largest individual resistance.** Adding a resistor in series can only make things harder for the current.

## 11.6 Resistors in parallel

```
the SAME POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE V is across every resistor

the currents ADD:                I = I1 + I2 + I3

EQUIVALENT RESISTANCE            1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3
```

**The derivation, which is worth being able to reproduce:**
```
I = I1 + I2 + I3

by Ohm's law on the combination:   I = V/Rp
by Ohm's law on each resistor:     I1 = V/R1,  I2 = V/R2,  I3 = V/R3

so     V/Rp = V/R1 + V/R2 + V/R3

divide through by V:

       1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The equivalent resistance in parallel is always LESS than the SMALLEST individual resistance.**
>
> **Why: adding another branch gives the current an EXTRA PATH.** More paths means more total current for the same voltage — and more current at the same voltage means less resistance.
>
> **The extreme case makes it obvious:** 1 Ω in parallel with 10⁶ Ω has an equivalent resistance of just under 1 Ω. **The huge resistor carries almost no current, so it barely matters** — but it cannot make the combination worse than the good path alone.

### Why household wiring is parallel, not series

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Three fatal problems with a series circuit for domestic use:**
>
> **1. The same current flows through everything.** But an electric bulb and an electric heater need **currents of widely different values** to work properly, so they cannot be run in series.
>
> **2. If one component fails, the circuit BREAKS and NOTHING works.** This is the miserable experience of fairy lights — one dead bulb kills the whole string, and each must be tested to find it.
>
> **3. Every appliance would share the supply voltage**, so none would get the 220 V it is designed for.
>
> **A parallel circuit solves all three: it DIVIDES the current between the gadgets, gives each the FULL voltage, and lets each be switched on or off independently.**

## 11.7 Heating effect of electric current

**A source spends energy to maintain a current. Some may do useful work — turning a fan — but in a PURELY RESISTIVE circuit ALL of it is dissipated as HEAT.**

**Derivation of Joule's law:**
```
work done in moving charge Q through potential difference V   =  VQ
so the source supplies energy VQ in time t

power input   P = VQ/t = VI

energy supplied in time t   = VIt

all of it appears as heat:   H = VIt

and using V = IR:            H = I²Rt
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **JOULE'S LAW OF HEATING: `H = I²Rt`**
>
> **Heat produced in a resistor is:**
> - **directly proportional to the SQUARE of the current**, for a given resistance
> - **directly proportional to the RESISTANCE**, for a given current
> - **directly proportional to the TIME** the current flows
>
> **The square on the current matters enormously in practice.** Double the current and you get **FOUR TIMES** the heat — which is why an overloaded wire heats up so alarmingly fast.

### Useful applications

```
ELECTRIC IRON · TOASTER · OVEN · KETTLE · HEATER   — direct use of the heat

ELECTRIC BULB
   the filament must RETAIN as much heat as possible so it gets white-hot
      and emits light, and must NOT MELT
   → TUNGSTEN, melting point 3380 °C
   → thermally ISOLATED using insulating supports
   → the bulb is filled with chemically INACTIVE nitrogen and argon
      to prolong the filament's life
   most of the power still appears as HEAT; only a small part as light

FUSE — protection against unduly high current
   placed IN SERIES with the device
   a wire of metal or alloy of appropriate MELTING POINT
      (aluminium, copper, iron, lead)
   too much current → the wire heats → it MELTS → the circuit BREAKS
   encased in a porcelain cartridge with metal ends
   domestic ratings: 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A …
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How to choose a fuse — worked.** An electric iron consuming 1 kW at 220 V draws
> ```
> I = P/V = 1000/220 = 4.54 A
> ```
> **so a 5 A fuse must be used** — the next rating above the working current.
>
> **Why it must be ABOVE, but not far above: a fuse rated below 4.54 A would blow during normal use; one rated far above would never blow when it should.** The fuse must tolerate normal working and fail on an overload.

> [!TIP]
> **Why the fuse goes in SERIES.** In series it carries the **whole** current of the circuit, so it can sense an overload — and when it melts, it **breaks the only path**, cutting everything off. **In parallel it would carry only part of the current and, on melting, would cut off nothing.**

## 11.8 Electric power

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> P = VI = I²R = V²/R
>
> SI unit: the WATT (W).      1 W = 1 V × 1 A
> ```
> **1 watt is the power consumed by a device carrying 1 A at a potential difference of 1 V.**
>
> **Which form to use depends on what you know** — all three are the same statement rearranged with Ohm's law.

```
1 kilowatt = 1000 watts

COMMERCIAL UNIT of electrical energy:  the KILOWATT HOUR (kW h), the "unit"

1 kW h = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J
```

> [!WARNING]
> **A common misunderstanding: electrons are NOT consumed in a circuit.**
>
> **What you pay the electricity company for is the ENERGY used to move electrons through your bulbs, fans and motors** — not the electrons themselves. The same electrons stay in the wires.

**Worked examples:**
```
BULB — 220 V generator, current 0.50 A
   P = VI = 220 × 0.50 = 110 W

ELECTRIC IRON — 840 W maximum, 360 W minimum, at 220 V
   maximum:  I = P/V = 840/220 = 3.82 A;  R = V/I = 220/3.82 = 57.60 Ω
   minimum:  I = P/V = 360/220 = 1.64 A;  R = V/I = 220/1.64 = 134.15 Ω

REFRIGERATOR — 400 W, 8 hours a day, 30 days, at ₹3.00 per kW h
   energy = 400 × 8 × 30 = 96000 W h = 96 kW h
   cost   = 96 × 3.00 = ₹288
```

> [!TIP]
> **Notice what changes between maximum and minimum heating in the iron: the RESISTANCE.**
>
> **The supply voltage is fixed at 220 V, so the only way to change the power is to change the resistance** — and since `P = V²/R`, a **higher** resistance gives a **lower** power. **That is exactly what the setting dial on an iron does.**

## Summary

```
CURRENT   I = Q/t ;  unit AMPERE, 1 A = 1 C/s
   conventional current flows OPPOSITE to the electrons — a historical
   accident, since electrons were unknown when the convention was fixed
   AMMETER in SERIES · VOLTMETER in PARALLEL

POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE   V = W/Q ;  unit VOLT, 1 V = 1 J/C
   charges flow only if there is a difference of electric "pressure"
   a cell must keep spending chemical energy to MAINTAIN a current

OHM'S LAW   V = IR, provided the TEMPERATURE stays the same
   the V–I graph is a straight line through the origin; its slope is R

RESISTANCE   R = ρ l/A
   longer wire → more opposition;  thicker wire → more room to flow
   RESISTIVITY ρ is a property of the MATERIAL, not of the piece
   copper/aluminium for transmission (low ρ) · alloys for heaters
   (high ρ and no oxidation) · tungsten for filaments (m.p. 3380 °C)

SERIES     same CURRENT everywhere;  voltages add;  Rs = R1 + R2 + R3
   greater than the largest resistor — it is like one longer wire
PARALLEL   same VOLTAGE across each; currents add;  1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3
   less than the smallest resistor — every extra branch is an extra path
   household wiring is PARALLEL: each gadget gets the full voltage, draws
   the current it needs, and keeps working if another fails

HEATING   H = VIt = I²Rt      JOULE'S LAW
   heat ∝ I², so DOUBLING the current gives FOUR times the heat
   bulb: tungsten filament, thermally isolated, nitrogen/argon filling
   FUSE: in SERIES, melts and breaks the circuit on overload;
      1 kW at 220 V draws 4.54 A → use a 5 A fuse

POWER   P = VI = I²R = V²/R ;  unit WATT
   commercial unit of ENERGY = kilowatt hour;  1 kW h = 3.6 × 10⁶ J
   you pay for ENERGY, not for electrons — electrons are not consumed
```
