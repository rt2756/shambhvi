## 3.1 Physical properties

| Property | METALS | NON-METALS |
|---|---|---|
| **Lustre** | shiny | dull |
| **Malleable** (beaten into sheets) | yes | no |
| **Ductile** (drawn into wires) | yes | no |
| **Conducts heat and electricity** | yes | no |
| **Sonorous** (rings when struck) | yes | no |
| **State at room temperature** | solid | solid or gas |
| **Hardness** | generally hard | soft and brittle |

> [!WARNING]
> **Physical properties alone cannot classify an element — there are too many exceptions.**
>
> - **MERCURY is a metal but a LIQUID** at room temperature. **BROMINE is a non-metal and also a liquid.**
> - **Gallium and caesium melt in your palm** — metals with very low melting points.
> - **IODINE is a non-metal but LUSTROUS.**
> - **Lithium, sodium and potassium are so soft they can be cut with a knife.**
> - **GRAPHITE is a non-metal that CONDUCTS electricity.**
>
> **This is why elements are properly classified by their CHEMICAL properties** — which have no such exceptions.

> [!NOTE]
> **ALLOTROPES are different forms of the same element.** Carbon has two famous ones: **DIAMOND**, the hardest natural substance known, and **GRAPHITE**, soft and a conductor. **Same element, utterly different properties** — because the atoms are arranged differently.

## 3.2 Metals with oxygen

```
Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide

2Cu + O2 → 2CuO           black copper(II) oxide
4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3
2Mg + O2 → 2MgO           dazzling white flame
```

**Reactivity towards oxygen varies enormously:**
```
K, Na       — catch fire in open air, so they are stored under KEROSENE
Mg, Al, Zn, Pb — covered by a thin protective oxide layer at room temperature
Fe          — does not burn as a lump, but iron FILINGS burn vigorously in a flame
Cu          — does not burn; the hot metal gets a black coating of CuO
Ag, Au      — do not react with oxygen even at high temperature
```

> [!TIP]
> **Why sodium is kept under kerosene:** it reacts so vigorously with the oxygen and moisture of the air that it **catches fire on its own**. Kerosene keeps air away from the metal, and sodium does not react with kerosene.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why aluminium doesn't corrode away although it is a reactive metal:** the thin layer of Al2O3 that forms **seals the surface** and stops any more oxygen getting through. **A reactive metal protected by its own product.**
>
> **ANODISING makes that layer deliberately thicker.** The aluminium article is made the **anode** and electrolysed in dilute sulphuric acid; the oxygen released at the anode thickens the oxide coat, which can then be dyed for an attractive finish.

### Basic, acidic and amphoteric oxides

```
METAL oxides are BASIC:
   Na2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq)
   K2O(s)  + H2O(l) → 2KOH(aq)         these dissolve to give ALKALIS

NON-METAL oxides are ACIDIC
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **AMPHOTERIC OXIDES react with BOTH acids and bases to give salt and water.** Aluminium oxide and zinc oxide are the two to know.
> ```
> Al2O3 + 6HCl  → 2AlCl3   + 3H2O          behaving as a BASE
> Al2O3 + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO2  +  H2O          behaving as an ACID
>                 (sodium aluminate)
> ```
> **They sit on the boundary between metal and non-metal behaviour, so they can play either role.**

**Most metal oxides are insoluble in water; only a few (Na2O, K2O) dissolve to form alkalis.**

## 3.3 Metals with water

```
Metal + Water → Metal oxide + Hydrogen
Metal oxide + Water → Metal hydroxide      (if the oxide is soluble)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A ladder of reactivity, visible in one experiment:**
> ```
> K, Na    — react VIOLENTLY with COLD water; the hydrogen catches fire
>            2K(s)  + 2H2O(l) → 2KOH(aq)  + H2(g) + heat
>            2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g) + heat
>
> Ca       — reacts with cold water, less violently — the heat is NOT
>            enough to ignite the hydrogen
>            Ca(s) + 2H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)
>
> Mg       — no reaction with cold water; reacts with HOT water
>
> Al,Zn,Fe — no reaction with cold or hot water; react with STEAM
>            2Al(s) + 3H2O(g) → Al2O3(s) + 3H2(g)
>            3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) → Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g)
>
> Pb,Cu,Ag,Au — no reaction with water at all
> ```
> **The pattern: the more reactive the metal, the milder the form of water it can attack.** Potassium manages cold water; iron needs steam; gold cannot manage even that.

> [!TIP]
> **Why calcium and magnesium start to FLOAT:** bubbles of hydrogen gas stick to the metal's surface and **carry it up like tiny floats**. It is not that the metal has become lighter — the trapped gas has made the whole assembly less dense than water.

## 3.4 Metals with acids

```
Metal + Dilute acid → Salt + Hydrogen

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2
Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2
Fe + H2SO4 → FeSO4 + H2
```
**Order of vigour observed: `Mg > Al > Zn > Fe`. Copper gives NO bubbles and no temperature rise — it does not react with dilute HCl at all.**

> [!WARNING]
> **NITRIC ACID is the exception — it does NOT give hydrogen with metals.**
>
> **Why: HNO3 is a strong OXIDISING AGENT.** It oxidises the hydrogen produced straight into water, while itself being reduced to one of the nitrogen oxides (N2O, NO, NO2).
>
> **So the hydrogen is made and then immediately destroyed** — which is why you never collect it. **Magnesium and manganese with VERY dilute HNO3 are the only exceptions.**

> [!NOTE]
> **AQUA REGIA — Latin for "royal water" — is 3 parts concentrated HCl to 1 part concentrated HNO3, freshly mixed.** It dissolves **gold and platinum**, which neither acid can touch on its own. A highly corrosive, fuming liquid.

## 3.5 The reactivity series

**Displacement gives the cleanest test of all: if metal A displaces metal B from B's salt solution, then A is more reactive.**
```
Metal A + salt solution of B → salt solution of A + Metal B

Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s)        happens — Fe beats Cu
Cu(s) + FeSO4(aq) → no reaction              Cu cannot beat Fe
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE ACTIVITY (REACTIVITY) SERIES — metals in order of DECREASING reactivity:**
> ```
> K    potassium     MOST reactive
> Na   sodium
> Ca   calcium
> Mg   magnesium
> Al   aluminium
> Zn   zinc
> Fe   iron
> Pb   lead
> ------------------------------  [H]  hydrogen
> Cu   copper
> Hg   mercury
> Ag   silver
> Au   gold          LEAST reactive
> ```
> **Metals ABOVE hydrogen can displace hydrogen from dilute acids. Metals BELOW it cannot** — which is exactly why copper gives no bubbles with dilute HCl.

> [!TIP]
> **The series is not a list to memorise blindly — it is a summary of experiments.** Every position in it was decided by watching one metal displace another, or fail to.

## 3.6 How metals and non-metals react — ionic bonding

**Noble gases have a completely filled outermost shell and are almost inert. Every other element behaves as though it is TRYING to reach that arrangement.**

```
Na (2,8,1)  →  Na⁺ (2,8) + e⁻        loses 1 electron — now like neon
Cl (2,8,7)  + e⁻ →  Cl⁻ (2,8,8)      gains 1 electron — now like argon

Mg (2,8,2)  →  Mg²⁺ (2,8) + 2e⁻      loses 2
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **METALS LOSE electrons to form POSITIVE ions (cations). NON-METALS GAIN electrons to form NEGATIVE ions (anions).**
>
> **Why the charge appears: the nucleus does not change.** Sodium still has 11 protons but now only 10 electrons — so a net **+1**. Chlorine still has 17 protons but now 18 electrons — a net **−1**.
>
> **The oppositely charged ions then attract each other by strong ELECTROSTATIC force** — and that attraction is the IONIC (electrovalent) BOND.

> [!NOTE]
> **Sodium chloride does NOT exist as molecules.** There is no "NaCl molecule" — only a huge three-dimensional aggregate of alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. **The formula gives the RATIO, not a molecule.**

### Properties of ionic compounds — and why

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Property | Reason |
> |---|---|
> | **Solid and hard** | strong force of attraction between opposite ions |
> | **BRITTLE — break under pressure** | a small shift lines up like charges, which repel |
> | **High melting and boiling points** | a lot of energy is needed to break the strong inter-ionic attraction |
> | **Soluble in water, insoluble in petrol and kerosene** | water can pull the ions apart; organic solvents cannot |
> | **Do NOT conduct as SOLIDS** | the ions are locked in a rigid structure and cannot move |
> | **DO conduct when MOLTEN or DISSOLVED** | heat or water overcomes the attraction, so the ions are free to move to the electrodes |
>
> **The last two are one idea, not two.** Conduction needs **moving charges**; ions carry charge but can only move once they are freed. **NaCl melts at 1074 K — which is a direct measure of how strong that attraction is.**

## 3.7 Occurrence and extraction of metals

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **MINERAL — any element or compound occurring naturally in the earth's crust.**
> - **ORE — a mineral containing enough of a metal to extract PROFITABLY.**
> - **GANGUE — the soil, sand and other impurities mined along with the ore.**
>
> **Every ore is a mineral, but not every mineral is an ore.** The difference is economic, not chemical.

**METALLURGY = extracting a metal from its ore and refining it.**

### Where in the earth a metal is found depends on where it sits in the series

```
BOTTOM of the series (Au, Ag, Pt, Cu)
   — so unreactive they are found FREE (native)

MIDDLE (Zn, Fe, Pb)
   — found as OXIDES, SULPHIDES or CARBONATES

TOP (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al)
   — so reactive they are NEVER found free
```
**Most ores are oxides — because oxygen is abundant and very reactive.**

### The three extraction routes

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Step 0 for all of them: ENRICHMENT — remove the gangue**, using a difference in physical or chemical properties between gangue and ore.

**LOW in the series — heating alone is enough:**
```
                Heat
2HgS(s) + 3O2 ------→ 2HgO(s) + 2SO2(g)
                Heat
2HgO(s)      ------→ 2Hg(l) + O2(g)

copper from Cu2S, likewise, by heating in air
```

**MIDDLE of the series — convert to the oxide, then REDUCE with carbon:**
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ROASTING — heating a SULPHIDE ore strongly in EXCESS AIR:**
> ```
> 2ZnS(s) + 3O2(g) → 2ZnO(s) + 2SO2(g)
> ```
> **CALCINATION — heating a CARBONATE ore strongly in LIMITED AIR:**
> ```
> ZnCO3(s) → ZnO(s) + CO2(g)
> ```
> **Both make the same thing — the OXIDE — because it is far easier to reduce an oxide than a sulphide or a carbonate.**
>
> **How to remember which is which: roasting needs oxygen** (it is adding oxygen to a sulphide); **calcination is just heating** (it drives CO2 off a carbonate).

```
then reduce:   ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g)
```

**Sometimes a very reactive metal is the reducing agent instead:**
```
3MnO2(s) + 4Al(s) → 3Mn(l) + 2Al2O3(s) + Heat
```
> [!NOTE]
> **THE THERMIT REACTION — used to weld railway tracks and cracked machine parts:**
> ```
> Fe2O3(s) + 2Al(s) → 2Fe(l) + Al2O3(s) + Heat
> ```
> **So much heat is released that the iron is produced MOLTEN** — and molten iron poured into the gap is exactly what a weld needs. **The reaction supplies both the metal and the heat to melt it.**

**TOP of the series — ELECTROLYTIC REDUCTION:**
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Carbon cannot reduce the oxides of Na, Mg, Ca or Al.**
>
> **Why: these metals have a GREATER AFFINITY for oxygen than carbon does**, so carbon cannot take the oxygen away from them.
>
> **So electricity does the job instead** — molten chlorides are electrolysed:
> ```
> at the CATHODE   Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na
> at the ANODE     2Cl⁻ → Cl2 + 2e⁻
> ```
> **The metal is deposited at the cathode** — the negative electrode — because the metal ion is positive. **Aluminium is obtained by electrolytic reduction of aluminium oxide.**

### Refining

**ELECTROLYTIC REFINING** — used for copper, zinc, tin, nickel, silver and gold:
```
ANODE     = the IMPURE metal
CATHODE   = a thin strip of PURE metal
ELECTROLYTE = a solution of the metal's salt

pure metal dissolves from the anode into the electrolyte,
and an equal amount is deposited on the cathode

soluble impurities stay in solution;
insoluble ones settle below the anode as ANODE MUD
```
> [!TIP]
> **The anode mud is not waste — it often contains silver, gold and platinum**, and recovering them can pay for the whole refining operation.

## 3.8 Corrosion

```
iron   + moist air  →  RUST, brown and flaky
silver + sulphur in air → black silver sulphide
copper + moist CO2  →  green basic copper carbonate
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE THREE-TEST-TUBE EXPERIMENT settles what rusting needs:**
> ```
> A  nail + ordinary water, corked            →  RUSTS
> B  nail + boiled distilled water + oil layer →  no rust
>       (boiling removes dissolved air; oil keeps more out)
> C  nail + dry air + anhydrous CaCl2, corked  →  no rust
>       (CaCl2 absorbs any moisture)
> ```
> **Conclusion: rusting needs BOTH air (oxygen) AND water. Either one alone is harmless.**
>
> **This is a properly designed experiment — each tube removes exactly one factor**, so the result points at the cause and nothing else.

### Prevention

```
painting · oiling · greasing · galvanising · chrome plating ·
anodising · alloying
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **GALVANISATION — coating iron or steel with a thin layer of ZINC.**
>
> **The remarkable part: a galvanised article stays protected EVEN IF THE ZINC COATING IS SCRATCHED THROUGH.**
>
> **Why: zinc is MORE REACTIVE than iron.** Wherever both are exposed, the oxygen attacks the zinc in preference to the iron. **The zinc is sacrificed to save the iron** — so a scratch does not start the rust the way a scratch in paint does.

## 3.9 Alloys

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An ALLOY is a HOMOGENEOUS MIXTURE of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal.**
>
> **Made by melting the main metal and dissolving the others in it in definite proportions, then cooling.**

```
STEEL           iron + about 0.05% carbon    hard and strong
                (pure iron is very soft and stretches when hot)
STAINLESS STEEL iron + nickel + chromium     hard and does not rust
BRASS           copper + zinc
BRONZE          copper + tin
SOLDER          lead + tin — LOW melting point, so it welds wires
AMALGAM         any alloy containing MERCURY
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An alloy has LOWER electrical conductivity and a LOWER melting point than the pure metals in it.**
>
> **This is why brass and bronze are not used for electrical circuits, though copper is** — and it is exactly why **solder** is useful: its low melting point lets you join wires without melting the wires.
>
> **The point of alloying is that you can DESIGN the properties you want.** Pure iron is too soft to build with; add a trace of carbon and it becomes steel.

> [!NOTE]
> **Pure gold is 24 carat and too soft for jewellery.** It is alloyed with silver or copper to harden it. **22-carat gold, common in India, means 22 parts gold to 2 parts copper or silver.**

> [!NOTE]
> **The IRON PILLAR near the Qutub Minar in Delhi is over 1600 years old and has not rusted.** Indian iron workers had developed a rust-resistant process; the pillar is 8 m high and weighs 6 tonnes, and scientists from around the world have come to study it.

## 3.10 Chemical properties of non-metals

```
non-metals GAIN electrons → negative ions
non-metal oxides are ACIDIC or NEUTRAL
non-metals do NOT displace hydrogen from dilute acids
non-metals react with hydrogen to form HYDRIDES
```
> [!TIP]
> **Why non-metals cannot displace hydrogen from an acid:** displacement means **giving away** electrons to take a metal's place — and non-metals are electron **takers**, not givers. **The whole of a non-metal's chemistry runs the opposite way to a metal's.**

## Summary

```
PHYSICAL   metals: lustrous, malleable, ductile, sonorous, conduct
           non-metals: the opposite
   EXCEPTIONS matter — mercury (liquid metal), bromine (liquid non-metal),
   iodine (lustrous non-metal), graphite (conducting non-metal),
   sodium (metal soft enough to cut) — which is why we classify CHEMICALLY

METAL + oxygen → metal OXIDE (basic; Al2O3 and ZnO are AMPHOTERIC)
METAL + water  → oxide/hydroxide + hydrogen — K,Na violent in cold water;
                 Ca less so; Mg needs hot water; Al,Zn,Fe need STEAM;
                 Pb,Cu,Ag,Au not at all
METAL + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen    Mg > Al > Zn > Fe;  Cu: no reaction
   NITRIC ACID gives no hydrogen — it is an oxidising agent
   AQUA REGIA (3 HCl : 1 HNO3) dissolves even gold

ACTIVITY SERIES   K Na Ca Mg Al Zn Fe Pb [H] Cu Hg Ag Au
   above hydrogen → displaces H2 from dilute acids
   a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its salt

IONIC BOND   metal LOSES electrons (cation), non-metal GAINS (anion);
   opposite charges held by strong electrostatic force
   ionic compounds: hard, brittle, high m.p., soluble in water,
   conduct only when MOLTEN or DISSOLVED — ions must be free to move

METALLURGY   mineral ⊃ ore; gangue = impurities
   bottom of series → found FREE, or extracted by heating alone
   middle → ROASTING (sulphide + excess air) or CALCINATION (carbonate,
            limited air) to the oxide, then reduce with CARBON
            or with a reactive metal — THERMIT welds railway tracks
   top → ELECTROLYTIC reduction; carbon cannot reduce them because
         they hold oxygen more strongly than carbon does
   ELECTROLYTIC REFINING — impure anode, pure cathode, anode mud below

CORROSION   rusting needs BOTH air AND water (the three-tube experiment)
   GALVANISING works even when scratched, because zinc is more reactive
   than iron and is sacrificed in its place

ALLOYS   steel · stainless steel · brass · bronze · solder · amalgam
   lower conductivity and lower melting point than the pure metals
```
