## 10.1 A line and a circle

**From Class 9: a CIRCLE is the collection of all points in a plane at a constant distance (the RADIUS) from a fixed point (the CENTRE).**

**Put a line PQ and a circle together and there are exactly THREE possibilities:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Common points | The line is called |
> |---|---|
> | **NONE** | a **NON-INTERSECTING** line |
> | **TWO** | a **SECANT** |
> | **ONE only** | a **TANGENT** |
>
> **There is no fourth possibility.** A straight line cannot meet a circle in three or more points.

> [!NOTE]
> **A pulley over a well:** the rope on either side of the pulley, thought of as a ray, is a **tangent** to the circle of the pulley.

## 10.2 The tangent

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A TANGENT to a circle is a line that intersects the circle at ONLY ONE point.** That point is the **POINT OF CONTACT**, and the tangent is said to **TOUCH** the circle there.

**Activity.** Attach a straight wire AB to a circular wire at a point P, so it can rotate about P. In most positions the wire cuts the circle at **P and one other point** (Q₁, Q₂, R₁, R₂ …). **In exactly ONE position it meets the circle at P alone** — that is the tangent.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE TANGENT IS A SPECIAL CASE OF THE SECANT, IN WHICH THE TWO END POINTS OF THE CHORD COINCIDE.**
>
> **Watch the activity carefully and you can see it happen:** as AB rotates towards the tangent position, the second common point Q₁ **creeps nearer and nearer to P and finally coincides with it**. Rotate past that position and a new second point immediately appears on the other side.
>
> **So a tangent is a secant whose two intersection points have merged into one** — which is exactly why it is a boundary case, and why there is only **one** tangent at each point of a circle.

### Theorem 10.1

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 10.1: THE TANGENT AT ANY POINT OF A CIRCLE IS PERPENDICULAR TO THE RADIUS THROUGH THE POINT OF CONTACT.**

**Proof.** Let XY be a tangent at P to a circle with centre O.
```
Take any point Q on XY other than P, and join OQ.

Q must lie OUTSIDE the circle —
      if Q were inside, XY would cut the circle twice and be a SECANT, not a tangent.

Therefore  OQ > OP  (the radius).

This is true for EVERY point of XY except P.

So OP is the SHORTEST of all the distances from O to points of XY.

The shortest distance from a point to a line is the PERPENDICULAR distance.

Therefore OP ⊥ XY.        ∎
```

> [!TIP]
> **The whole proof rests on one observation: every other point of the tangent lies outside the circle.** That is precisely what "meets the circle only once" means — and it immediately makes OP the shortest distance, hence the perpendicular.

**Two consequences:**
- **At any point on a circle there can be ONE AND ONLY ONE tangent.**
- The line containing the radius through the point of contact is sometimes called the **NORMAL** to the circle at that point.

> [!NOTE]
> **A bicycle wheel makes the theorem visible.** The spokes lie along the radii, and the ground is a **tangent** to the wheel. **In every position, the radius through the point of contact stands at right angles to the ground.**

## 10.3 How many tangents from a point?

**Activity.** Take a point P and try to draw tangents through it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Position of P | Number of tangents |
> |---|---|
> | **INSIDE** the circle | **NONE** |
> | **ON** the circle | **exactly ONE** |
> | **OUTSIDE** the circle | **exactly TWO** |

> [!TIP]
> **Why no tangent exists from an inside point:** any line through a point inside the circle **must come out the other side**, so it necessarily cuts the circle **twice**. A line through it can only ever be a secant.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The LENGTH OF THE TANGENT from an external point P is the length of the segment from P to the point of contact.**
>
> **It is NOT the whole line — a line has no length.** It is the piece between P and where it touches.

### Theorem 10.2

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 10.2: THE LENGTHS OF THE TANGENTS DRAWN FROM AN EXTERNAL POINT TO A CIRCLE ARE EQUAL.**

**Proof.** Let PQ and PR be the two tangents from P to a circle with centre O.
```
Join OP, OQ and OR.

∠OQP = ∠ORP = 90°           (Theorem 10.1 — radius ⊥ tangent)

In right triangles OQP and ORP:
      OQ = OR                (radii of the same circle)
      OP = OP                (common — the hypotenuse of both)

so    △OQP ≅ △ORP           (RHS congruence)

therefore  PQ = PR           (CPCT)         ∎
```

**Alternatively, by Pythagoras:**
```
PQ² = OP² − OQ²      and      PR² = OP² − OR²

since OQ = OR (both radii), PQ² = PR², so PQ = PR
```

> [!TIP]
> **The whole figure is symmetric about the line OP** — which is why the two tangents are mirror images. **Everything else in the chapter follows from that symmetry:**
> ```
> PQ = PR                (equal tangent lengths)
> ∠OPQ = ∠OPR            (OP bisects the angle between the tangents)
> ∠POQ = ∠POR            (OP bisects the angle at the centre)
> ```

### The angle relationship

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In the quadrilateral OQPR, the angles at Q and R are both 90°, and the four angles of any quadrilateral sum to 360°. Therefore**
> ```
> ∠QPR + ∠QOR = 360° − 90° − 90° = 180°
> ```
> **THE ANGLE BETWEEN THE TWO TANGENTS FROM AN EXTERNAL POINT IS SUPPLEMENTARY TO THE ANGLE SUBTENDED AT THE CENTRE BY THE LINE JOINING THE POINTS OF CONTACT.**
>
> **This one relation answers a great many questions in this chapter** — given either angle, the other follows at once.

## Circumscribing figures

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When a quadrilateral ABCD CIRCUMSCRIBES a circle (every side is a tangent):**
> ```
> AB + CD = AD + BC
> ```
> **The sums of the OPPOSITE sides are equal.**
>
> **Why: each vertex is an external point with two equal tangent lengths.** Label the tangent lengths from A, B, C, D as p, q, r, s. Then every side is a sum of two of them, and adding opposite pairs gives `p + q + r + s` **both times**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A PARALLELOGRAM circumscribing a circle must be a RHOMBUS.**
>
> **Why:** for a parallelogram, `AB = CD` and `AD = BC`. Combined with `AB + CD = AD + BC` this gives `2AB = 2AD`, so **all four sides are equal.**

## Summary

```
A LINE AND A CIRCLE — exactly three possibilities
      no common point   → NON-INTERSECTING line
      two common points → SECANT
      one common point  → TANGENT (the point of contact)

A TANGENT IS A SECANT whose two intersection points have MERGED
      — which is why there is exactly ONE tangent at each point of a circle

THEOREM 10.1  the tangent is PERPENDICULAR to the radius at the point of contact
      proof: every other point of the tangent lies OUTSIDE the circle, so the
      radius to the point of contact is the SHORTEST distance from the centre
      to the line — and the shortest distance is the perpendicular

NUMBER OF TANGENTS from a point
      inside  → none    (any line through it must cut the circle twice)
      on      → exactly one
      outside → exactly two

THEOREM 10.2  the two tangent LENGTHS from an external point are EQUAL
      proof: RHS congruence of the two right triangles (equal radii, common
      hypotenuse OP) — or Pythagoras
      the figure is symmetric about OP, so OP also bisects both angles

ANGLE RELATION  ∠QPR + ∠QOR = 180°
      from the quadrilateral OQPR with two right angles

CIRCUMSCRIBING QUADRILATERAL   AB + CD = AD + BC
      because each vertex contributes two EQUAL tangent lengths
      a circumscribing PARALLELOGRAM must therefore be a RHOMBUS
```
