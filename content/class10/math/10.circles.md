## What this chapter is about

In Class 9 you met the parts of a circle — centre, radius, chord, arc, sector, segment. This chapter looks at **a circle and a straight line together**, and in particular at the line that just **touches** the circle: the **tangent**. Two theorems carry the whole chapter:

1. A tangent is **perpendicular to the radius** at the point where it touches.
2. The two tangents from an outside point are **equal in length**.

## 10.1 A circle and a line — three possibilities

Draw a circle and slide a ruler across it.

| Picture | Common points | Name |
|---|---|---|
| the line misses the circle | **0** | **non-intersecting line** |
| the line cuts through | **2** (A and B) | **secant** |
| the line just touches | **1** (A) | **tangent** |

There's no fourth option — a straight line can't meet a circle in three points.

> [!NOTE]
> **Everyday tangents:** the rope on either side of a pulley over a well; the road under a rolling bicycle wheel. The word comes from the Latin *tangere*, "to touch", introduced by the Danish mathematician **Thomas Fineke** in 1583.

## 10.2 Tangent to a circle

### A tangent is a limiting secant

**Activity 1.** Pin a straight wire AB to a circular wire at a point P and rotate it. In most positions it cuts the circle at P **and** at a second point Q. As you rotate it, Q slides closer and closer to P — and in **exactly one** position, Q reaches P and the wire touches at P only. That position is the tangent.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A TANGENT is a line that meets the circle at exactly one point**, called the **point of contact**. You can think of it as a **secant whose two end points have come together**.
>
> **At each point of a circle there is one and only one tangent.**

**Activity 2.** Draw a secant PQ and lines parallel to it. Moving away from the centre, the chords get shorter until the chord length becomes zero — once on each side. So **a circle has exactly two tangents parallel to any given line** (and so at most two parallel tangents).

### Theorem 10.1 — tangent ⊥ radius

Look at a rolling wheel: the spoke pointing straight down to the ground always looks **at right angles** to the road. That's no accident.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 10.1: The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.**
>
> **Proof.** Let XY touch the circle (centre O) at P. Take any other point Q on XY and join OQ.
> - Q must lie **outside** the circle — if it were inside or on it, XY would meet the circle twice and be a secant, not a tangent.
> - So OQ is **longer** than the radius: **OQ > OP**.
> - This holds for every point of XY except P, so **OP is the shortest distance** from O to the line XY.
> - The shortest segment from a point to a line is the **perpendicular**. So **OP ⊥ XY**. ∎

> [!NOTE]
> - The line through the point of contact along the radius is called the **normal** to the circle at that point.
> - The theorem also explains why there's **only one tangent at P**: only one line through P can be perpendicular to OP.

> [!TIP]
> **Whenever you see a tangent and a radius meeting, mark a right angle.** It almost always turns the problem into a Pythagoras problem.

### Exercise 10.1, worked

**1. How many tangents can a circle have?** **Infinitely many** — one at every point, and a circle has infinitely many points.

**2. Fill in the blanks.** (i) A tangent meets a circle in **one** point. (ii) A line meeting a circle in two points is a **secant**. (iii) A circle can have **two** parallel tangents at most. (iv) The common point of a tangent and the circle is the **point of contact**.

**3. Tangent PQ at P, radius 5 cm, OQ = 12 cm. PQ = ?** ∠OPQ = 90° (Theorem 10.1), so

```
PQ = √(OQ² − OP²) = √(144 − 25) = √119 cm         → (D)
```

**4. Draw a circle and two lines parallel to a given line, one a tangent and one a secant.** Draw the given line ℓ. Draw a diameter perpendicular to ℓ; at one end of it, draw the line parallel to ℓ — that's the tangent (it's perpendicular to the radius there). Any parallel line between the centre and that tangent cuts the circle twice — a secant.

## 10.3 Number of tangents from a point

**Activity 3.** Try drawing tangents through a point P:

| Where P is | Tangents through P | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **inside** the circle | **none** | every line through an inside point crosses the circle twice |
| **on** the circle | **exactly one** | Theorem 10.1 — the line ⊥ to OP |
| **outside** the circle | **exactly two** | one touching on each "side" |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The LENGTH OF A TANGENT** from an external point P is the length of the segment from P to the point of contact (PT₁ or PT₂).

### Theorem 10.2 — tangents from a point are equal

Measure PT₁ and PT₂ in your drawing — they come out equal. Always.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 10.2: The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.**
>
> **Proof.** Tangents PQ and PR from P touch the circle (centre O) at Q and R. Join OP, OQ, OR.
> ```
> ∠OQP = ∠ORP = 90°        (radius ⊥ tangent, Theorem 10.1)
> OQ = OR                  (radii)
> OP = OP                  (common hypotenuse)
> △OQP ≅ △ORP              (RHS congruence)
> PQ = PR                  (CPCT)  ∎
> ```
> **Or by Pythagoras:** PQ² = OP² − OQ² = OP² − OR² = PR².

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Bonus from the same congruence:** ∠OPQ = ∠OPR, so **OP bisects the angle between the two tangents**, and ∠POQ = ∠POR. The centre always lies on the bisector of the angle between the tangents.

### Worked examples

**Example 1 — in two concentric circles, a chord of the bigger circle that touches the smaller one is bisected at the point of contact.**

Chord AB of the big circle touches the small circle at P. OP is a radius of the small circle and AB is a tangent to it, so **OP ⊥ AB** (Theorem 10.1). But AB is also a chord of the big circle, and **the perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects it** (Class 9). So **AP = BP**.

**Example 2 — tangents TP and TQ from T. Prove ∠PTQ = 2∠OPQ.** Let ∠PTQ = θ.

```
TP = TQ (Theorem 10.2)  →  △TPQ is isosceles
∠TPQ = ∠TQP = (180° − θ)/2 = 90° − θ/2
∠OPT = 90° (Theorem 10.1)
∠OPQ = ∠OPT − ∠TPQ = 90° − (90° − θ/2) = θ/2
so ∠PTQ = θ = 2∠OPQ  ∎
```

**Example 3 — chord PQ = 8 cm in a circle of radius 5 cm; tangents at P and Q meet at T. Find TP.**

```
OT bisects PQ at right angles at R  →  PR = 4
OR = √(5² − 4²) = 3
In right △TRP and △PRO, ∠RPO = ∠PTR (both = 90° − ∠TPR)  →  similar (AA)
TP/PO = RP/RO   →   TP/5 = 4/3   →   TP = 20/3 cm
```
*Check with Pythagoras:* let TR = y. TP² = y² + 16 and TP² + 25 = (y + 3)². Subtracting: 25 = 6y − 7 → y = 16/3, TP² = 256/9 + 16 = 400/9, **TP = 20/3** ✔.

### Exercise 10.2, worked

**1. Tangent from Q is 24 cm; Q is 25 cm from the centre. Radius?**

```
r = √(25² − 24²) = √(625 − 576) = √49 = 7 cm          → (A)
```

**2. Tangents TP, TQ with ∠POQ = 110°. ∠PTQ = ?** In quadrilateral OPTQ, ∠OPT = ∠OQT = 90°, and the angles add to 360°:

```
∠PTQ = 360° − 90° − 90° − 110° = 70°                  → (B)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The tangent-angle rule:** the angle between two tangents and the angle at the centre between the radii to the points of contact are **supplementary** (add to 180°). Two right angles take up 180° of the quadrilateral's 360°. (This is Q10.)

**3. Tangents PA, PB inclined at 80°. ∠POA = ?** ∠AOB = 180° − 80° = 100°, and OP bisects it: **∠POA = 50°** → **(A)**. (Or: in △OAP, 90° + 40° + ∠POA = 180°.)

**4. Tangents at the ends of a diameter are parallel.** Let AB be a diameter, with tangents PQ at A and RS at B. Then ∠OAP = 90° and ∠OBS = 90°. These are **alternate angles** made by line AB with PQ and RS, and they're equal → **PQ ∥ RS**.

**5. The perpendicular to a tangent at the point of contact passes through the centre.** The radius OP is perpendicular to the tangent at P (Theorem 10.1). Through P there is only **one** line perpendicular to the tangent — so the perpendicular *is* the line OP, which contains O.

**6. Tangent from A is 4 cm; A is 5 cm from the centre. Radius?** r = √(25 − 16) = **3 cm**.

**7. Concentric circles of radii 5 cm and 3 cm; chord of the big one touching the small one.** By Example 1, the point of contact bisects the chord, and OP ⊥ chord:

```
half-chord = √(5² − 3²) = 4   →   chord = 8 cm
```

**8. A quadrilateral ABCD circumscribes a circle. Prove AB + CD = AD + BC.** The sides touch at P (on AB), Q (BC), R (CD), S (DA). By Theorem 10.2, tangents from each corner are equal:

```
AP = AS,   BP = BQ,   CR = CQ,   DR = DS
AB + CD = (AP + BP) + (CR + DR) = AS + BQ + CQ + DS = (AS + DS) + (BQ + CQ) = AD + BC  ∎
```

**9. XY ∥ X′Y′ are tangents; tangent AB touches at C, meeting them at A and B. Prove ∠AOB = 90°.** Let XY touch at P and X′Y′ at Q. From A, the tangents AP and AC are equal, so OA bisects ∠PAC; similarly OB bisects ∠QBC. Since XY ∥ X′Y′, ∠PAB + ∠QBA = 180° (co-interior). So

```
∠OAB + ∠OBA = ½(∠PAB + ∠QBA) = 90°   →   ∠AOB = 180° − 90° = 90°  ∎
```

**10. The angle between two tangents is supplementary to the angle the contact chord subtends at the centre.** In quadrilateral OAPB: ∠OAP = ∠OBP = 90°, so ∠APB + ∠AOB = 360° − 180° = **180°** ∎.

**11. A parallelogram circumscribing a circle is a rhombus.** By Q8, AB + CD = AD + BC. In a parallelogram AB = CD and AD = BC, so 2AB = 2BC → AB = BC. Adjacent sides equal → all sides equal → **rhombus** ∎.

**12. △ABC circumscribes a circle of radius 4 cm; BD = 8, DC = 6 (D on BC). Find AB and AC.** Let the tangents from A be x (AF = AE = x). Tangents from B: BD = BF = 8. From C: CD = CE = 6.

```
AB = x + 8,   AC = x + 6,   BC = 14,   semi-perimeter s = x + 14

area as three triangles with height r = 4:  ½ × 4 × (AB + BC + CA) = 4(x + 14)
Heron's formula:  area = √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)] = √[(x + 14)(x)(6)(8)]

16(x + 14)² = 48x(x + 14)   →   x + 14 = 3x   →   x = 7
AB = 15 cm,   AC = 13 cm
```

> [!TIP]
> **Splitting a triangle round its incircle** into three triangles, each with height r, gives **area = r × s** (radius × semi-perimeter). It's a handy fact whenever a circle is drawn inside a triangle.

**13. Opposite sides of a circumscribing quadrilateral subtend supplementary angles at the centre.** Join O to the four corners and four contact points. Each corner's two tangents make congruent triangles, so the angles at O come in four equal pairs: 2(a + b + c + d) = 360°, giving a + b + c + d = 180°. The angle AB subtends is a + b, the angle CD subtends is c + d — they add to **180°** ∎.

## The two big results in one picture

From an external point P with tangents PA and PB to a circle with centre O:

```
∠OAP = ∠OBP = 90°               (radius ⊥ tangent)
PA = PB                         (equal tangents)
OP bisects ∠APB and ∠AOB
∠APB + ∠AOB = 180°
OP² = OA² + PA²                 (Pythagoras: r² + tangent²)
```

## In a nutshell

- A line and a circle: **no** common point, **two** (secant), or **one** (tangent).
- One tangent at each point of the circle; at most **two parallel** tangents.
- **Tangent ⊥ radius** at the point of contact (the radius is the shortest distance from the centre).
- Tangents from a point: **inside → 0, on → 1, outside → 2**.
- **Tangents from an external point are equal** (RHS congruence); the centre lies on the angle bisector.
- **Circumscribed quadrilateral:** AB + CD = AD + BC.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** the right angle is between the tangent and the RADIUS, at the point of contact — not at the external point · in OP² = r² + tangent², OP is the hypotenuse · angle between tangents + angle at centre = 180°, not 90° · a tangent from a point inside the circle doesn't exist · when tangents from a vertex are equal, label them with the same letter (x) straight away.
