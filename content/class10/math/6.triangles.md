## What this chapter is about

**How tall is Mount Everest? How far away is the Moon?** Nobody climbed up with a tape measure. They used **similar figures** — shapes that are the same *shape* but a different *size*. If you know one small triangle well, you know every triangle of the same shape, just scaled up.

In Class 9 you studied **congruent** figures — same shape **and** same size. Now we relax that to **same shape, any size**.

## 6.2 Similar figures

**Everyday examples:**
- A photo and its enlargement.
- A map and the real land.
- Two circles of different radii.
- Two squares of different side.
- Two equilateral triangles.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two figures with the same shape (but not necessarily the same size) are called SIMILAR.**
> **All congruent figures are similar, but similar figures need not be congruent.**

**Why are all circles similar?** A circle has only one measurement — its radius. Scale a circle of radius 2 by 3 and you get a circle of radius 6. Every circle can be scaled into every other. The same goes for squares (one side) and equilateral triangles (one side, all angles 60°).

> [!WARNING]
> **Not every pair of rectangles is similar** — a long thin rectangle and a square are both rectangles but different shapes. Same for isosceles triangles, rhombuses, and so on.

### When are two polygons similar?

Think of a photo enlarged to twice its size. **Every length doubles** (sides in the same ratio), but **every angle stays the same** — a right angle is still a right angle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two polygons with the same number of sides are similar if:**
> 1. their **corresponding angles are equal**, AND
> 2. their **corresponding sides are in the same ratio** (proportional).
>
> The common ratio is the **scale factor** (or representative fraction, for maps).

> [!WARNING]
> **Both conditions are needed for polygons.**
> - A **square** and a **rectangle**: all angles 90° (equal), but sides not in proportion → **not similar**.
> - A **square** and a **rhombus**: sides in proportion (all equal), but angles differ → **not similar**.

### Exercise 6.1, worked

**1.** (i) All circles are **similar**. (ii) All squares are **similar**. (iii) All **equilateral** triangles are similar. (iv) Polygons with the same number of sides are similar if corresponding angles are **equal** and corresponding sides are **proportional**.

**2.** (i) Similar: two photos of the same scene in different sizes; two ₹1 coins of different series *(circles)*; any two squares. (ii) Not similar: a square and a rectangle; a square and a rhombus; an equilateral and a right triangle.

**3.** A square and a rhombus (Fig. 6.8) → **not similar**: sides in proportion, but the rhombus's angles aren't 90°.

## 6.3 Similarity of triangles

For triangles the same two conditions apply. Write **△ABC ~ △DEF** ("is similar to") to mean

```
∠A = ∠D,  ∠B = ∠E,  ∠C = ∠F      and      AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The ORDER of letters matters.** △ABC ~ △DEF says A ↔ D, B ↔ E, C ↔ F. So AB matches DE, not EF. Write the letters so that matching angles sit in the same position.

### The Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales)

**Try it.** Draw any angle with arms through A. Mark equal steps along one arm: P, Q, D, R, B (each 1 cm, say). Draw a line through B to any point C on the other arm. Through D draw a line **parallel to BC**, meeting AC at E. Measure: AD/DB = 3/2, and you'll find AE/EC is also 3/2.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.1 — BASIC PROPORTIONALITY THEOREM (BPT, or THALES' THEOREM)**
>
> **If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, the other two sides are divided in the same ratio.**
> ```
> In △ABC, if DE ∥ BC (D on AB, E on AC), then   AD/DB = AE/EC
> ```

**Proof (using areas).** Join BE and CD. Draw EN ⊥ AB and DM ⊥ AC.

```
area(ADE) = ½ × AD × EN        area(BDE) = ½ × DB × EN       (same height EN)
so  area(ADE)/area(BDE) = AD/DB                                      (1)

area(ADE) = ½ × AE × DM        area(DEC) = ½ × EC × DM       (same height DM)
so  area(ADE)/area(DEC) = AE/EC                                      (2)

△BDE and △DEC stand on the SAME base DE and lie BETWEEN THE SAME PARALLELS DE and BC,
so they have equal areas:  area(BDE) = area(DEC)                     (3)

From (1), (2), (3):   AD/DB = AE/EC   ∎
```

> [!TIP]
> **Why the parallel line matters:** it's what makes area(BDE) = area(DEC). Two triangles on the same base between the same parallels have the same height, hence the same area (a Class 9 fact). Without "parallel", step (3) fails.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.2 — CONVERSE OF BPT.** **If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side.**
>
> **Why:** suppose AD/DB = AE/EC but DE is *not* parallel to BC. Draw DE′ ∥ BC instead. By BPT, AD/DB = AE′/E′C. So AE/EC = AE′/E′C, and adding 1 to both: AC/EC = AC/E′C, so EC = E′C — E and E′ are the same point. So DE ∥ BC after all.

**Useful forms of BPT** (they all follow by adding 1 or flipping): if DE ∥ BC,

```
AD/DB = AE/EC      AD/AB = AE/AC      DB/AB = EC/AC
```

**Example 1 — show AD/AB = AE/AC.** From BPT, DB/AD = EC/AE. Add 1: (DB + AD)/AD = (EC + AE)/AE, i.e. AB/AD = AC/AE. Flip: **AD/AB = AE/AC**.

**Example 2 — trapezium ABCD, AB ∥ DC, EF ∥ AB with E on AD, F on BC. Show AE/ED = BF/FC.** Join AC, cutting EF at G. In △ADC, EG ∥ DC, so AE/ED = AG/GC. In △CAB, GF ∥ AB, so AG/GC = BF/FC. Together: **AE/ED = BF/FC**.

**Example 3 — PS/SQ = PT/TR and ∠PST = ∠PRQ. Show △PQR is isosceles.** By the converse, ST ∥ QR, so ∠PST = ∠PQR (corresponding angles). Given ∠PST = ∠PRQ, so ∠PQR = ∠PRQ, so **PQ = PR** (sides opposite equal angles).

### Exercise 6.2, worked

**1. DE ∥ BC.** (i) AD = 1.5, DB = 3, AE = 1: 1.5/3 = 1/EC → **EC = 2 cm**. (ii) DB = 7.2, AE = 1.8, EC = 5.4: AD/7.2 = 1.8/5.4 = 1/3 → **AD = 2.4 cm**.

**2. Is EF ∥ QR?** Compare PE/EQ with PF/FR (converse of BPT).

```
(i)   3.9/3 = 1.3     3.6/2.4 = 1.5     unequal → NO
(ii)  4/4.5 = 8/9     8/9               equal   → YES
(iii) EQ = 1.28 − 0.18 = 1.10, FR = 2.56 − 0.36 = 2.20
      0.18/1.10 = 9/55   0.36/2.20 = 9/55  equal → YES
```

**3. LM ∥ CB and LN ∥ CD. Prove AM/AB = AN/AD.** In △ABC, LM ∥ CB → AM/AB = AL/AC. In △ADC, LN ∥ CD → AN/AD = AL/AC. Both equal AL/AC, so **AM/AB = AN/AD**.

**4. DE ∥ AC and DF ∥ AE. Prove BF/FE = BE/EC.** In △BAE, DF ∥ AE → BD/DA = BF/FE. In △BAC, DE ∥ AC → BD/DA = BE/EC. So **BF/FE = BE/EC**.

**5. DE ∥ OQ and DF ∥ OR. Show EF ∥ QR.** In △POQ: PE/EQ = PD/DO. In △POR: PF/FR = PD/DO. So PE/EQ = PF/FR, and by the converse in △PQR, **EF ∥ QR**.

**6. AB ∥ PQ and AC ∥ PR. Show BC ∥ QR.** In △OPQ: OA/AP = OB/BQ. In △OPR: OA/AP = OC/CR. So OB/BQ = OC/CR → by the converse in △OQR, **BC ∥ QR**.

**7. A line through the mid-point of one side, parallel to another, bisects the third.** D is the mid-point of AB (AD/DB = 1) and DE ∥ BC. BPT: AE/EC = AD/DB = 1 → AE = EC, so **E is the mid-point**.

**8. The line joining the mid-points of two sides is parallel to the third.** AD/DB = 1 = AE/EC → by the converse, **DE ∥ BC**.

**9. Trapezium ABCD, AB ∥ DC, diagonals meet at O. Show AO/BO = CO/DO.** Through O draw EF ∥ DC (E on AD, F on BC). In △ADC, EO ∥ DC → AE/ED = AO/OC. In △DAB, EO ∥ AB → AE/ED = BO/OD. So AO/OC = BO/OD, which rearranges to **AO/BO = CO/DO**.

**10. If AO/BO = CO/DO, show ABCD is a trapezium.** Draw OE ∥ AB meeting AD at E. In △DAB, AE/ED = BO/OD. Given AO/OC = BO/OD, so AE/ED = AO/OC. By the converse in △ADC, EO ∥ DC. Since EO ∥ AB too, **AB ∥ DC** → trapezium.

## 6.4 Criteria for similarity

Checking all three angles **and** all three ratios is a lot of work. For triangles, fewer facts are enough.

### AAA (and AA)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.3 — AAA SIMILARITY.** **If the corresponding angles of two triangles are equal, their corresponding sides are in the same ratio, and the triangles are similar.**
>
> **Why (sketch):** with ∠A = ∠D, ∠B = ∠E, ∠C = ∠F, place △DEF on △ABC so D sits on A: P on AB with AP = DE, Q on AC with AQ = DF. Then △APQ ≅ △DEF (SAS), so ∠APQ = ∠E = ∠B. Equal corresponding angles mean PQ ∥ BC, and BPT gives AP/AB = AQ/AC — that is, DE/AB = DF/AC. Repeat for the other pair.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **AA SIMILARITY.** **Two equal angles are enough.** If ∠A = ∠D and ∠B = ∠E, then ∠C = 180° − ∠A − ∠B = 180° − ∠D − ∠E = ∠F automatically (angle sum of a triangle).

> [!WARNING]
> **AAA works for triangles only.** A square and a 1 × 2 rectangle have all angles equal but aren't similar. And **SSS needs only proportional sides for triangles** — not for quadrilaterals (a square and a rhombus). Triangles are special: they're **rigid**.

### SSS

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.4 — SSS SIMILARITY.** **If the sides of one triangle are proportional to the sides of the other, their corresponding angles are equal and the triangles are similar.**
> ```
> AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD   ⇒   △ABC ~ △DEF
> ```
> **Why:** mark P, Q on DE, DF with DP = AB, DQ = AC. Then DP/PE = DQ/QF, so PQ ∥ EF (converse of BPT), △DPQ ~ △DEF, and PQ/EF = DP/DE = BC/EF, so PQ = BC. Now △DPQ ≅ △ABC (SSS congruence), so the angles match.

### SAS

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.5 — SAS SIMILARITY.** **If one angle of a triangle equals one angle of the other, and the sides INCLUDING these angles are proportional, the triangles are similar.**
> ```
> AB/DE = AC/DF  and  ∠A = ∠D   ⇒   △ABC ~ △DEF
> ```
> The angle must sit **between** the two sides.

| Criterion | What you check |
|---|---|
| **AA** | two pairs of equal angles |
| **SSS** | all three side ratios equal |
| **SAS** | one equal angle + the two sides around it in the same ratio |

### Worked examples

**Example 4 — PQ ∥ RS (lines through O). Prove △POQ ~ △SOR.** PQ ∥ RS gives ∠P = ∠S and ∠Q = ∠R (alternate angles). By **AA**, △POQ ~ △SOR.

**Example 5 — find ∠P.** △ABC has AB = 3.8, BC = 6, CA = 3√3, ∠A = 80°, ∠B = 60°. In △PQR, RQ = 7.6, QP = 12, PR = 6√3.

```
AB/RQ = 3.8/7.6 = 1/2     BC/QP = 6/12 = 1/2     CA/PR = 3√3/6√3 = 1/2
```
All equal, so by **SSS**, **△ABC ~ △RQP** (A ↔ R, B ↔ Q, C ↔ P). So ∠P = ∠C = 180° − 80° − 60° = **40°**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Match vertices by the sides, not by the letters.** AB pairs with RQ, so A ↔ R and B ↔ Q, which leaves C ↔ P. Guessing "P goes with A" would give the wrong answer, 80°.

**Example 6 — OA·OB = OC·OD. Show ∠A = ∠C and ∠B = ∠D.** Rewrite as OA/OC = OD/OB. ∠AOD = ∠COB (vertically opposite). By **SAS**, △AOD ~ △COB, so **∠A = ∠C, ∠D = ∠B**.

**Example 7 — a girl 90 cm tall walks away from a 3.6 m lamp-post at 1.2 m/s. Her shadow after 4 s?**

In 4 s she walks 4.8 m. Let the shadow be x m. The lamp-post and the girl both stand vertically, and the light ray makes the same angle with the ground, so the big triangle (lamp, tip of shadow) and small triangle (girl, tip of shadow) are similar by **AA**:

```
x / (4.8 + x) = 0.9 / 3.6 = 1/4
4x = 4.8 + x   →   x = 1.6 m
```

**Example 8 — CM, RN medians of △ABC ~ △PQR. Prove △AMC ~ △PNR, CM/RN = AB/PQ, △CMB ~ △RNQ.**
(i) AB/PQ = AC/PR and ∠A = ∠P; since AM = AB/2 and PN = PQ/2, AM/PN = AC/PR → **SAS**: △AMC ~ △PNR. (ii) So CM/RN = CA/RP = AB/PQ. (iii) Similarly with MB = AB/2, NQ = PQ/2 and ∠B = ∠Q.

**3. Trapezium ABCD, AB ∥ DC, diagonals meet at O. Show OA/OC = OB/OD.** ∠OAB = ∠OCD and ∠OBA = ∠ODC (alternate angles, AB ∥ DC). By **AA**, △OAB ~ △OCD, so **OA/OC = OB/OD**.

**4. QR/QS = QT/PR and ∠1 = ∠2. Show △PQS ~ △TQR.** ∠1 = ∠2 means ∠PQR = ∠PRQ, so PQ = PR. Then QR/QS = QT/PQ, i.e. QS/QR = PQ/QT, with the common angle ∠Q → **SAS**.

**5. ∠P = ∠RTS. Show △RPQ ~ △RTS.** ∠R is common and ∠P = ∠RTS → **AA**.

**6. △ABE ≅ △ACD. Show △ADE ~ △ABC.** Congruence gives AB = AC and AE = AD, so AD/AB = AE/AC, with common ∠A → **SAS**.

**7. Altitudes AD and CE meet at P.** Each pair has a right angle and one shared or vertically-opposite angle → **AA** each time: (i) △AEP ~ △CDP (∠E = ∠D = 90°, ∠APE = ∠CPD) · (ii) △ABD ~ △CBE (90° and common ∠B) · (iii) △AEP ~ △ADB (90° and common ∠A) · (iv) △PDC ~ △BEC (90° and common ∠C).

**8. Parallelogram ABCD, E on AD produced, BE meets CD at F. Show △ABE ~ △CFB.** ∠A = ∠C (opposite angles of a parallelogram); ∠AEB = ∠CBF (alternate, AE ∥ BC) → **AA**.

**9. Right triangles ABC and AMP (right angles at B and M) with common ∠A.** By **AA**, (i) △ABC ~ △AMP, so (ii) **CA/PA = BC/MP**.

**10. CD, GH bisect ∠ACB and ∠EGF; △ABC ~ △FEG.** ∠ACB = ∠FGE, so their halves are equal: ∠ACD = ∠FGH and ∠DCB = ∠HGE. With ∠A = ∠F → (iii) △DCA ~ △HGF (AA), giving (i) CD/GH = AC/FG; with ∠B = ∠E → (ii) △DCB ~ △HGE.

**11. Isosceles △ABC (AB = AC), AD ⊥ BC, EF ⊥ AC, E on CB produced. Show △ABD ~ △ECF.** ∠ABD = ∠ACB = ∠ECF (equal base angles); ∠ADB = ∠EFC = 90° → **AA**.

**12. AB/PQ = BC/QR = AD/PM (AD, PM medians). Show △ABC ~ △PQR.** BD = BC/2 and QM = QR/2, so AB/PQ = BD/QM = AD/PM → △ABD ~ △PQM (SSS), so ∠B = ∠Q. Then AB/PQ = BC/QR with ∠B = ∠Q → **SAS**.

**13. ∠ADC = ∠BAC. Show CA² = CB·CD.** In △ADC and △BAC: ∠ADC = ∠BAC and ∠C is common → AA → CA/CB = CD/CA → **CA² = CB·CD**.

**14. AB/PQ = AC/PR = AD/PM (medians). Show △ABC ~ △PQR.** Extend AD to E with DE = AD, and PM to N with MN = PM. ABEC and PQNR are parallelograms (diagonals bisect each other), so BE = AC and QN = PR. Then AB/PQ = BE/QN = AE/PN → △ABE ~ △PQN (SSS), so ∠BAE = ∠QPN; similarly ∠CAE = ∠RPN. Adding, ∠A = ∠P, and **SAS** finishes it.

**15. A 6 m pole casts a 4 m shadow; a tower casts 28 m at the same time.** The sun's rays make the same angle for both, so the triangles are similar (AA):

```
h / 28 = 6 / 4   →   h = 42 m
```

**16. △ABC ~ △PQR with medians AD, PM. Prove AB/PQ = AD/PM.** AB/PQ = BC/QR = BD/QM (halves) and ∠B = ∠Q → △ABD ~ △PQM (SAS) → **AB/PQ = AD/PM**.

> [!TIP]
> **The proof recipe for "show these triangles are similar":**
> 1. Name the two triangles in **matching order**.
> 2. Hunt for equal angles: **common** angles, **vertically opposite** angles, **alternate/corresponding** angles from parallels, **right angles**.
> 3. If you have two, it's **AA**. If you have one plus ratios of the sides around it, it's **SAS**.
> 4. Then read off whatever ratio the question wants.

## In a nutshell

- **Similar** = same shape; **congruent** = same shape and size. All circles, squares, equilateral triangles are similar.
- **Polygons are similar** when angles are equal AND sides are proportional — both needed.
- **BPT:** a line parallel to one side divides the other two in the same ratio (proof via equal areas between parallels). **Converse:** same ratio ⇒ parallel.
- **Triangle criteria:** **AA** (AAA), **SSS** (all sides proportional), **SAS** (included angle + proportional sides).
- Write the similarity statement in **matching order**; shadows and heights are solved with similar triangles.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** keep the vertex order in △ABC ~ △DEF · in BPT compare AD/DB with AE/EC (part to part), or AD/AB with AE/AC (part to whole) — don't mix them · SAS needs the angle BETWEEN the two sides · AAA alone doesn't make quadrilaterals similar · when a side is given as a whole (PQ), subtract to get the part (EQ = PQ − PE) · shadow problems: add the distance walked to the shadow for the big triangle's base.
