## 6.1 Congruent vs similar

**Class 9 studied CONGRUENCE — same shape AND same size.** This chapter studies figures with the **same shape but not necessarily the same size**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two figures having the SAME SHAPE (and not necessarily the same size) are called SIMILAR figures.**
>
> **ALL CONGRUENT FIGURES ARE SIMILAR, BUT SIMILAR FIGURES NEED NOT BE CONGRUENT.**
>
> Congruence is the special case of similarity where the sizes happen to match too.

**Why it matters:** the heights of mountains like Mount Everest and the distances of far-off objects like the Moon **were never measured with a tape**. They were found by **INDIRECT MEASUREMENT**, based on the principle of similarity.

## 6.2 Similarity of polygons

**All circles are similar. All squares are similar. All equilateral triangles are similar** — in each case the shape is fixed and only the size varies.

**But a circle and a square are not similar, and neither are a triangle and a square** — the shapes themselves differ.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **TWO POLYGONS OF THE SAME NUMBER OF SIDES ARE SIMILAR IF:**
> **(i) their CORRESPONDING ANGLES are EQUAL, and**
> **(ii) their CORRESPONDING SIDES are IN THE SAME RATIO (proportional).**
>
> **That common ratio is called the SCALE FACTOR** (or Representative Fraction). World maps and building blueprints are drawn using a suitable scale factor.

> [!WARNING]
> **BOTH conditions are needed — neither one alone is enough.**
>
> - **A SQUARE and a RECTANGLE:** all corresponding angles are 90°, **equal** — but the sides are **not** in the same ratio. **NOT similar.**
> - **A SQUARE and a RHOMBUS:** all four sides in each are equal, so the sides **are** in the same ratio — but the angles are **not** equal. **NOT similar.**
>
> **Each example satisfies exactly one condition and fails to be similar** — which is precisely why the definition insists on both.

> [!TIP]
> **The photograph analogy makes the definition obvious.** Print the same negative at 35 mm and 45 mm and every length is multiplied by 45/35, while **every angle stays exactly the same**. That is what "same shape, different size" means in practice.
>
> **Two photographs of the same person at 10 and at 40, printed the same size, are NOT similar** — same size, different shape.

> [!NOTE]
> **The shadow activity.** Hold a cardboard quadrilateral ABCD parallel to a table between a ceiling bulb at O and the table, and mark the shadow A′B′C′D′. Because **light travels in straight lines**, A′ lies on ray OA, B′ on ray OB, and so on — and measuring shows
> ```
> ∠A = ∠A′, ∠B = ∠B′, ∠C = ∠C′, ∠D = ∠D′
> and    AB/A′B′ = BC/B′C′ = CD/C′D′ = DA/D′A′
> ```
> **The shadow is an ENLARGEMENT of the original — a similar figure.**

**Similarity is transitive:** if polygon 1 is similar to polygon 2 and polygon 2 to polygon 3, then **polygon 1 is similar to polygon 3**.

## 6.3 The Basic Proportionality (Thales) Theorem

**Triangles are polygons, so the same definition applies:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two triangles are SIMILAR if (i) their corresponding angles are equal and (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio.**
>
> Triangles with equal corresponding angles are called **EQUIANGULAR**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Thales (640–546 BCE)** stated an important truth about equiangular triangles: **the ratio of any two corresponding sides in two equiangular triangles is always the same.** He is believed to have used the result now called the **Basic Proportionality Theorem** — or the **Thales Theorem**.

**Activity.** On one arm of an angle mark `AP = PQ = QD = DR = RB` (five equal steps). Draw any line through B meeting the other arm at C, then draw a line through D **parallel to BC** meeting AC at E.
```
AD/DB = 3/2       and measuring gives       AE/EC = 3/2   too
```
**Not a coincidence:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.1 — BASIC PROPORTIONALITY THEOREM (THALES THEOREM)**
>
> **If a line is drawn PARALLEL to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, then the other two sides are divided in the SAME RATIO.**
>
> **In △ABC with DE ∥ BC:**
> ```
> AD/DB = AE/EC
> ```

### The proof, and why it works

**Join BE and CD, and drop DM ⊥ AC and EN ⊥ AB.**

```
ar(ADE) = ½ · AD · EN          ar(BDE) = ½ · DB · EN
ar(ADE) = ½ · AE · DM          ar(DEC) = ½ · EC · DM

so   ar(ADE)/ar(BDE) = AD/DB              …(1)   (same height EN)
and  ar(ADE)/ar(DEC) = AE/EC              …(2)   (same height DM)

△BDE and △DEC are on the SAME BASE DE and between the SAME PARALLELS BC and DE

so   ar(BDE) = ar(DEC)                    …(3)

from (1), (2) and (3):     AD/DB = AE/EC          ∎
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The whole proof turns on step (3), and that is where the PARALLEL condition is used.**
>
> Two triangles on the **same base** and **between the same parallels** have **equal areas**, because they have the same base and the same height. **Remove the parallelism and the areas differ, and the theorem collapses.**
>
> **This is worth noticing generally: in a good proof, every hypothesis is used somewhere.** If you can prove a result without using one of its conditions, either the condition is unnecessary or your proof is wrong.

### The converse

**Activity.** Mark five equal steps `AB₁ = B₁B₂ = B₂B₃ = B₃B₄ = B₄B` on one arm and five on the other. Joining B₁C₁, B₂C₂, … you find
```
AB₁/B₁B = AC₁/C₁C = 1/4      and B₁C₁ ∥ BC
AB₂/B₂B = AC₂/C₂C = 2/3      and B₂C₂ ∥ BC
AB₃/B₃B = AC₃/C₃C = 3/2      and B₃C₃ ∥ BC
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.2 — THE CONVERSE**
>
> **If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the SAME RATIO, then the line is PARALLEL to the third side.**
>
> **So the two theorems together give an "if and only if":**
> ```
> DE ∥ BC        ⟺        AD/DB = AE/EC
> ```
> **Use Theorem 6.1 when you KNOW the lines are parallel and want the ratio; use Theorem 6.2 when you KNOW the ratio and want to prove parallelism.**

## 6.4 Criteria for similarity of triangles

**Writing similarity:** `△ABC ~ △DEF` means "triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF", where **~ stands for "is similar to"** (as **≅** stood for "is congruent to").

> [!WARNING]
> **The ORDER OF THE VERTICES MATTERS.** `△ABC ~ △DEF` says specifically that A ↔ D, B ↔ E and C ↔ F.
>
> **You cannot write `△ABC ~ △EDF` or `△ABC ~ △FED`** for the same triangles — though `△BAC ~ △EDF` is fine, because it keeps the pairing.
>
> **The letters carry the correspondence**, so a careless reordering states something different and usually false.

**Checking all six pairs (three angles and three side ratios) would be tedious. As with congruence, fewer facts suffice.**

### AAA and AA

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.3 — AAA SIMILARITY**
>
> **If in two triangles the CORRESPONDING ANGLES are EQUAL, then their corresponding sides are in the same ratio, and hence the triangles are similar.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE AA CRITERION.** By the **angle sum property**, if two angles of one triangle equal two angles of another, the **third pair must be equal too** — the angles of every triangle add to 180°.
>
> **So: if TWO angles of one triangle are respectively equal to two angles of another, the triangles are SIMILAR.**
>
> **This is the criterion you will use most often**, because two angles are usually far easier to find than three.

### SSS

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.4 — SSS SIMILARITY**
>
> **If in two triangles the sides of one are PROPORTIONAL to the sides of the other, then their corresponding angles are equal and the triangles are similar.**

**Activity check:** `AB = 3, BC = 6, CA = 8` against `DE = 4.5, EF = 9, FD = 12` gives all three ratios equal to **2/3** — and measuring confirms `∠A = ∠D`, `∠B = ∠E`, `∠C = ∠F`.

### SAS

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 6.5 — SAS SIMILARITY**
>
> **If ONE angle of a triangle equals one angle of the other, AND the sides INCLUDING these angles are proportional, then the two triangles are similar.**

> [!WARNING]
> **"Including" is essential — the equal angle must be BETWEEN the two proportional sides.** An equal angle somewhere else with two proportional sides elsewhere proves nothing.

### The remarkable simplification

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For POLYGONS in general, equal angles alone or proportional sides alone is NOT enough** — the square/rectangle and square/rhombus examples showed that.
>
> **But for TRIANGLES, Theorems 6.3 and 6.4 show that EITHER CONDITION IMPLIES THE OTHER.** Equal angles force proportional sides (AAA); proportional sides force equal angles (SSS).
>
> **Triangles are RIGID in a way other polygons are not.** A four-sided frame can be pushed out of shape while keeping its side lengths; a triangular frame cannot. That rigidity is exactly why the two conditions collapse into one.

## Worked examples

**PQ ∥ RS, with the lines crossing at O. Prove `△POQ ~ △SOR`.**
```
PQ ∥ RS                    (given)
∠P = ∠S                    (alternate angles)
∠Q = ∠R                    (alternate angles)
∠POQ = ∠SOR                (vertically opposite angles)

therefore △POQ ~ △SOR      (AAA similarity)
```

**In △ABC and △PQR, `AB = 3.8, BC = 6, CA = 3√3` and `RQ = 7.6, QP = 12, PR = 6√3`. Given `∠A = 80°` and `∠B = 60°`, find ∠P.**
```
AB/RQ = 3.8/7.6  = 1/2
BC/QP = 6/12     = 1/2
CA/PR = 3√3/6√3  = 1/2

all three equal, so  △ABC ~ △RQP        (SSS similarity)

therefore ∠C = ∠P                        (corresponding angles of similar triangles)

∠C = 180° − 80° − 60° = 40°              (angle sum property)

so ∠P = 40°
```

> [!TIP]
> **Notice the correspondence carefully: it is `△ABC ~ △RQP`, not `△ABC ~ △PQR`.** The ratios matched AB with RQ and BC with QP, so A ↔ R, B ↔ Q, C ↔ P. **Get that wrong and you would conclude ∠P = 80°.**

**Given `OA · OB = OC · OD` for two lines crossing at O, show `∠A = ∠C` and `∠B = ∠D`.**
```
OA · OB = OC · OD
so   OA/OC = OD/OB                       …(1)

∠AOD = ∠COB                              (vertically opposite angles)   …(2)

from (1) and (2):  △AOD ~ △COB           (SAS similarity)

therefore ∠A = ∠C  and  ∠D = ∠B
```

> [!TIP]
> **The key move is rearranging the product into a RATIO.** `OA · OB = OC · OD` looks like a statement about areas; rewritten as `OA/OC = OD/OB` it is exactly the proportionality SAS needs — with the vertically opposite angles supplying the included angle.

## Summary

```
SIMILAR    same shape, not necessarily same size
   all congruent figures are similar; similar figures need not be congruent
   all circles similar · all squares similar · all equilateral triangles similar

POLYGONS of the same number of sides are similar if BOTH
   (i) corresponding ANGLES are equal, and
   (ii) corresponding SIDES are in the same ratio (the SCALE FACTOR)
   neither alone suffices:  square vs rectangle (angles only),
                            square vs rhombus (sides only)

BASIC PROPORTIONALITY (THALES) THEOREM
   DE ∥ BC in △ABC   ⟹   AD/DB = AE/EC
   proved by equal areas of triangles on the same base between the same parallels
CONVERSE
   AD/DB = AE/EC     ⟹   DE ∥ BC
   together they give an "if and only if"

SIMILARITY CRITERIA FOR TRIANGLES
   AAA   corresponding angles equal        ⟹ sides proportional ⟹ similar
   AA    two angles equal is enough (angle sum forces the third)
   SSS   sides proportional                ⟹ angles equal ⟹ similar
   SAS   one angle equal AND the sides INCLUDING it proportional ⟹ similar

FOR TRIANGLES ONLY, each condition implies the other — triangles are rigid

NOTATION  △ABC ~ △DEF means A↔D, B↔E, C↔F.  The order of the letters
          carries the correspondence and must not be rearranged carelessly
```
