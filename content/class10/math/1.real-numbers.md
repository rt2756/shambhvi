## What this chapter is about

**Class 9 introduced irrational numbers. This chapter proves things about them.**

Two big ideas, both about **positive integers**:
- **The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic** — every composite number has one and only one prime "fingerprint"
- **Proof by contradiction** — the technique used to show `√2`, `√3` and `√5` really are irrational

## 1.1 The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

**Multiply primes together in any combination and you get composite numbers:**
```
7 × 11 × 23           = 1771
3 × 7 × 11 × 23       = 5313
2 × 3 × 7 × 11 × 23   = 10626
2³ × 3 × 7³           = 8232
```
**There are infinitely many primes, so this produces infinitely many numbers. The question is — can we produce EVERY composite number this way?**

**Factorise from the other direction, with a factor tree:**
```
32760 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 13 = 2³ × 3² × 5 × 7 × 13
123456789 = 3² × 3803 × 3607          (3803 and 3607 are both prime)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 1.1 — THE FUNDAMENTAL THEOREM OF ARITHMETIC**
>
> **Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a PRODUCT OF PRIMES, and this factorisation is UNIQUE, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.**
>
> Equivalently: **the prime factorisation of a natural number is unique, except for the order of its factors.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The theorem says TWO things, and the second is the powerful one.**
>
> **1. Existence** — every composite number *can* be written as a product of primes.
> **2. UNIQUENESS** — there is **one and only one** way to do it (order aside).
>
> **Why uniqueness matters so much:** it means a number's prime factorisation is a **fingerprint**. If a prime does not appear in the factorisation you found, it is **not there at all** — and that single fact settles a whole class of questions.

**Standard form:** write `x = p₁ p₂ … pₙ` with the primes in **ascending order**, then combine repeats into powers. **Once the order is fixed as ascending, the factorisation is unique.**

> [!NOTE]
> An equivalent version was probably first recorded as **Proposition 14 of Book IX in Euclid's *Elements***, but the **first correct proof was given by Carl Friedrich Gauss** in his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*.
>
> **Gauss (1777–1855)**, often called the **"Prince of Mathematicians"**, is considered one of the three greatest mathematicians of all time, with **Archimedes and Newton**.

### Using uniqueness

**Worked — can 4ⁿ ever end in 0?**
```
if 4ⁿ ended in 0 it would be divisible by 5,
so 5 would appear in its prime factorisation

but 4ⁿ = (2²)ⁿ = 2²ⁿ — the ONLY prime in the factorisation is 2

by UNIQUENESS, there are no other primes there
```
**So there is NO natural number n for which 4ⁿ ends with the digit zero.**

> [!TIP]
> **This is the whole method in one example.** Uniqueness turns "I can't find a 5" into "there IS no 5" — which is the difference between failing to prove something and proving it false.

## 1.2 HCF and LCM by prime factorisation

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> HCF = product of the SMALLEST power of each COMMON prime factor
> LCM = product of the GREATEST power of EACH prime factor involved
> ```

**Worked — 6 and 20:**
```
6  = 2¹ × 3¹
20 = 2² × 5¹

HCF = 2¹                = 2
LCM = 2² × 3¹ × 5¹      = 60
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For any TWO positive integers a and b:**
> ```
> HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
> ```
> **Check: HCF(6,20) × LCM(6,20) = 2 × 60 = 120 = 6 × 20 ✔**
>
> **Why it works:** every prime appears in the pair with two exponents, a smaller and a larger. The HCF takes the smaller, the LCM takes the larger — **and together they use both, which is exactly what multiplying a by b does.**

**Worked — HCF of 96 and 404, then the LCM:**
```
96  = 2⁵ × 3
404 = 2² × 101

HCF = 2² = 4

LCM = (96 × 404) / HCF = 38784 / 4 = 9696
```

**Worked — 6, 72 and 120:**
```
6   = 2 × 3
72  = 2³ × 3²
120 = 2³ × 3 × 5

HCF = 2¹ × 3¹           = 6      (smallest powers of the common primes 2 and 3)
LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5¹      = 360    (greatest powers of every prime involved)
```

> [!WARNING]
> **The product rule FAILS for three numbers.**
> ```
> 6 × 72 × 120 = 51840        but      HCF × LCM = 6 × 360 = 2160
> ```
> **So HCF(p,q,r) × LCM(p,q,r) ≠ p × q × r.** The two-number rule is a special case, not a general one.
>
> **The correct three-number formulas are:**
> ```
> LCM(p,q,r) = (p × q × r × HCF(p,q,r)) / (HCF(p,q) × HCF(q,r) × HCF(p,r))
>
> HCF(p,q,r) = (p × q × r × LCM(p,q,r)) / (LCM(p,q) × LCM(q,r) × LCM(p,r))
> ```

## 1.3 Revisiting irrational numbers

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A number s is IRRATIONAL if it CANNOT be written as p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.**
>
> Familiar examples: `√2, √3, √15, π, 2/√3, 0.10110111011110…`

**Class 9 said these numbers are irrational. This chapter PROVES it.**

### The lemma the proof needs

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 1.2: Let p be a PRIME. If p divides a², then p divides a** (a a positive integer).
>
> **Proof.** Write `a = p₁p₂…pₙ` as a product of primes. Then
> ```
> a² = (p₁p₂…pₙ)(p₁p₂…pₙ) = p₁² p₂² … pₙ²
> ```
> Since p divides a², **the Fundamental Theorem says p must be one of the prime factors of a²**. But by **uniqueness**, the only primes in a² are p₁, p₂, …, pₙ. **So p is one of them — and since a = p₁p₂…pₙ, p divides a.**

> [!WARNING]
> **The word PRIME is essential.** The statement fails for composite divisors: **4 divides 6² = 36, but 4 does NOT divide 6.**
>
> **This is exactly where the Fundamental Theorem is doing the work** — a prime cannot be split among the factors, but a composite number can.

### The proof that √2 is irrational

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 1.3: √2 is irrational.**
>
> **The method is PROOF BY CONTRADICTION** — assume the opposite, and show it leads to an impossibility.
> ```
> Assume √2 IS rational.
>
> Then √2 = r/s for integers r and s (s ≠ 0).
> Divide out any common factor, so √2 = a/b with a and b COPRIME.
>
> b√2 = a
> Squaring:      2b² = a²
>
> So 2 divides a²  →  by Theorem 1.2, 2 divides a.
> Write a = 2c.
>
> Substituting:  2b² = 4c²,  so  b² = 2c²
> So 2 divides b²  →  by Theorem 1.2, 2 divides b.
>
> Therefore a and b have 2 as a common factor.
> But we said a and b are COPRIME.        ← CONTRADICTION
>
> The contradiction arose from assuming √2 is rational.
> Therefore √2 is IRRATIONAL.
> ```

> [!TIP]
> **Where every step earns its place:**
> - **"Divide out the common factor"** sets the trap — it is the coprimality that will be contradicted.
> - **Theorem 1.2 is used TWICE** — once to get from a² to a, once to get from b² to b.
> - **The contradiction is not that 2b² = a² is false** — it is that a and b turn out to be both coprime and not coprime.

**The same proof for √3:**
```
Assume √3 = a/b with a, b coprime.
b√3 = a  →  3b² = a²
So 3 divides a²  →  3 divides a.  Write a = 3c.
3b² = 9c²  →  b² = 3c²
So 3 divides b²  →  3 divides b.
a and b share the factor 3 — contradicting coprimality.
Therefore √3 is irrational.
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The same argument works for √p for ANY prime p.** Only Theorem 1.2 is needed, and that holds for every prime.

### Two rules, and how to prove particular cases

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **The SUM or DIFFERENCE of a rational and an irrational number is IRRATIONAL.**
> - **The PRODUCT or QUOTIENT of a NON-ZERO rational and an irrational number is IRRATIONAL.**

**Worked — show 5 − √3 is irrational:**
```
Assume 5 − √3 is rational, so 5 − √3 = a/b with a, b integers, b ≠ 0.

Rearranging:   √3 = 5 − a/b = (5b − a)/b

a and b are integers, so (5b − a)/b is RATIONAL.
So √3 would be rational — contradicting the fact that √3 is irrational.

Therefore 5 − √3 is irrational.
```

**Worked — show 3√2 is irrational:**
```
Assume 3√2 = a/b with a, b integers, b ≠ 0.

Rearranging:   √2 = a/(3b)

3, a and b are integers, so a/(3b) is RATIONAL.
So √2 would be rational — contradiction.

Therefore 3√2 is irrational.
```

> [!TIP]
> **Both proofs have the same shape, and it is worth naming it:**
> 1. Assume the combination is rational.
> 2. **Rearrange to isolate the known irrational number on one side.**
> 3. Observe that the other side is built only from **integers**, so it is rational.
> 4. That contradicts the known irrationality — done.
>
> **The known result (√2 or √3 irrational) is the anvil you break the assumption against.** Notice you never have to prove irrationality from scratch again.

## Summary

```
FUNDAMENTAL THEOREM OF ARITHMETIC
   every composite number = a product of primes, UNIQUELY, apart from order
   uniqueness is the useful half: a prime absent from the factorisation isn't there

HCF = product of the SMALLEST power of each COMMON prime
LCM = product of the GREATEST power of EACH prime involved

HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a × b            TWO numbers only
HCF(p,q,r) × LCM(p,q,r) ≠ p × q × r    the rule does NOT extend

THEOREM: if a PRIME p divides a², then p divides a
   (false for composites: 4 | 36 but 4 ∤ 6)

PROOF BY CONTRADICTION for √2, √3, √p
   assume rational = a/b with a, b COPRIME
   → p divides a → p divides b → they share p → contradicts coprime

rational ± irrational          = irrational
non-zero rational × irrational = irrational
   proved by isolating the irrational and showing the rest is built from integers
```
