## What this chapter is about

In Class 9 you met **irrational numbers** like √2 and π and were *told* they can't be written as a fraction. This chapter does two things:

1. It proves a fact you have used since Class 6 without noticing — **every whole number above 1 breaks into primes in exactly one way.** This is the **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic**.
2. It uses that fact to **prove** that √2, √3, √5 (and √p for any prime p) really are irrational.

Both ideas are about **positive integers**, and the second is built on the first.

## 1.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

### Start with an experiment

Take a few primes and multiply them in different combinations:

```
7 × 11 × 23           = 1771
3 × 7 × 11 × 23       = 5313
2 × 3 × 7 × 11 × 23   = 10626
2³ × 3 × 7³           = 8232
```

Every answer is a **composite** number (it has factors other than 1 and itself). Since there are infinitely many primes, you can make infinitely many composites this way.

**Now go backwards.** Can *every* composite number be built like this? Try 32760 with a factor tree — keep splitting until every branch ends in a prime:

```
32760
= 2 × 16380
= 2 × 2 × 8190
= 2 × 2 × 2 × 4095
= 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 1365
= 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 455
= 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 91
= 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 13
= 2³ × 3² × 5 × 7 × 13
```

**Why must the splitting always end?** Each time you split a composite number, the two pieces are *smaller* than it. Whole numbers can't keep getting smaller forever, so eventually every piece is a number that can't be split — a prime.

> [!NOTE]
> **Try it a different way and you get the same primes.** Start 32760 = 8 × 4095 or 32760 = 10 × 3276 — however you begin, the bottom of the tree is always 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 5, 7, 13. That is the surprising part, and it is what the theorem promises.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 1.1 — FUNDAMENTAL THEOREM OF ARITHMETIC**
>
> **Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is UNIQUE, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.**
>
> It says **two** things:
> 1. **Existence** — you *can* always break a composite number into primes (the factor tree always finishes).
> 2. **Uniqueness** — there is **only one** set of primes that works. The order can change (2 × 3 or 3 × 2), but the primes and how many times each appears cannot.

**Writing it in standard form:** list the primes in **ascending order** and gather repeats into powers — `32760 = 2³ × 3² × 5 × 7 × 13`. Once the order is fixed, there is only one way to write it.

> [!TIP]
> **Think of the prime factorisation as a number's fingerprint.** No two different numbers have the same one, and a number has only one. The most useful consequence: **if a prime is not in the fingerprint, it does not divide the number at all.** You'll use this again and again.

> [!NOTE]
> A version of this theorem appears as Proposition 14 of Book IX in **Euclid's *Elements*** (about 300 BC), but the first correct proof was given by **Carl Friedrich Gauss** in 1801. Gauss is often called the "Prince of Mathematicians".

### Using uniqueness — Example 1: can 4ⁿ end in 0?

**Think first:** a number ends in 0 exactly when it is divisible by 10, i.e. by **both 2 and 5**.

```
4ⁿ = (2 × 2)ⁿ = 2²ⁿ
```

The only prime in the fingerprint of 4ⁿ is **2**. By uniqueness, there is **no 5** hiding in there — so 4ⁿ is never divisible by 5, so it **never ends in 0**, for any natural number n.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why "uniqueness" is doing the work here:** without it, someone could say "maybe 4ⁿ has *another* factorisation that includes a 5". Uniqueness rules that out. "I can't see a 5" becomes "there **is** no 5".

### HCF and LCM by prime factorisation

You already know HCF (highest common factor) and LCM (lowest common multiple). The fingerprint makes them mechanical.

**Example 2 — 6 and 20:**

```
6  = 2¹ × 3¹
20 = 2² × 5¹
```

- **HCF:** a common factor can only use primes that **both** numbers have, and no more copies than the number with fewer copies. Both have 2; 6 has only one 2. So HCF = 2¹ = **2**.
- **LCM:** a common multiple must contain **everything** each number needs — two 2s (for 20), one 3 (for 6), one 5 (for 20). So LCM = 2² × 3 × 5 = **60**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ```
> HCF = product of the SMALLEST power of each COMMON prime factor
> LCM = product of the GREATEST power of EACH prime factor involved
> ```
> **Why it works:** to *divide* both numbers, a factor can't use more of any prime than either number has → take the smaller power, and only primes both share. To be *divisible by* both, a multiple must contain all the primes of each, as many times as the hungrier number needs → take the greater power of every prime.

**Notice:** HCF × LCM = 2 × 60 = 120 = 6 × 20. That's not luck.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For any two positive integers a and b:**
> ```
> HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
> ```
> **Why it works:** look at one prime, say 2. It appears in a with some power and in b with some power — one smaller, one bigger. The HCF takes the smaller power, the LCM takes the bigger. Together HCF × LCM uses **both** powers, which is exactly what a × b uses. This happens for every prime, so the products match.

**Example 3 — HCF of 96 and 404, then the LCM:**

```
96  = 2⁵ × 3
404 = 2² × 101

Common prime: only 2. Smaller power: 2²  →  HCF = 4

LCM = (96 × 404) ÷ HCF = 38784 ÷ 4 = 9696
```

**Example 4 — HCF and LCM of 6, 72 and 120:**

```
6   = 2  × 3
72  = 2³ × 3²
120 = 2³ × 3 × 5

HCF = 2¹ × 3¹       = 6      (2 and 3 are common; smallest powers)
LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5   = 360    (every prime; greatest powers)
```

> [!WARNING]
> **The product rule is for TWO numbers only.** Check: 6 × 72 × 120 = 51840, but HCF × LCM = 6 × 360 = 2160. Not equal.
>
> **Why it breaks:** with three numbers a prime has *three* powers. HCF takes the smallest, LCM the largest — the **middle** one is never used, so the product comes out short.

> [!NOTE]
> Numbers with HCF 1 are called **co-prime**. For co-prime numbers the LCM is just their product — there's no overlap to remove.

**4. Given HCF(306, 657) = 9, find LCM.**

```
LCM = (306 × 657) ÷ 9 = 306 × 73 = 22338
```

**5. Can 6ⁿ end in 0?** 6ⁿ = (2 × 3)ⁿ = 2ⁿ × 3ⁿ. Ending in 0 needs a factor 5. By uniqueness the only primes are 2 and 3 — **no 5** — so **6ⁿ never ends in 0**.

**6. Why are these composite?**

```
7 × 11 × 13 + 13 = 13 × (7 × 11 + 1) = 13 × 78 = 1014
7 × 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 + 5 = 5 × (7 × 6 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 + 1) = 5 × 1009 = 5045
```

Each has a factor (13, or 5) other than 1 and itself, so each is **composite**. The trick: **spot the common factor in both terms and take it out**.

**7. Sonia takes 18 min a round, Ravi 12 min. When do they meet at the start again?** Sonia is at the start at 18, 36, 54, … minutes; Ravi at 12, 24, 36, … The first time on **both** lists is the LCM.

```
18 = 2 × 3²,   12 = 2² × 3
LCM = 2² × 3² = 36  →  after 36 minutes
```

## 1.3 Revisiting irrational numbers

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A number s is IRRATIONAL if it cannot be written as p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.**
> Examples: √2, √3, √15, π, −√2/√3, 0.10110111011110…

To prove √2 is irrational we need one small fact first.

### Theorem 1.2 — if a prime divides a², it divides a

**See it with numbers.** 3 divides 36 = 6², and 3 divides 6. 5 divides 225 = 15², and 5 divides 15. Is that always true?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THEOREM 1.2: Let p be a prime. If p divides a², then p divides a** (a is a positive integer).
>
> **Why it works:** write a in primes: a = p₁ × p₂ × … × pₙ. Then
> ```
> a² = p₁ × p₁ × p₂ × p₂ × … × pₙ × pₙ
> ```
> a² uses **exactly the same primes** as a — just each one twice. If the prime p divides a², then p must be in a²'s fingerprint (uniqueness). But a²'s fingerprint contains only p₁, …, pₙ — the primes of a. So p is one of the primes of a, and therefore p divides a.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Prime" is essential.** 4 divides 6² = 36, but 4 does **not** divide 6. A composite like 4 = 2 × 2 can borrow one 2 from each copy of 6. A prime can't be split, so it has to sit wholly inside one copy of a.

### Theorem 1.3 — √2 is irrational

The method is **proof by contradiction**: pretend the opposite is true, follow the logic carefully, and arrive at something impossible. Then the pretence must have been false.

```
Suppose √2 IS rational.
Then √2 = r/s for integers r, s (s ≠ 0).
Cancel all common factors, so √2 = a/b where a and b are CO-PRIME
(no common factor except 1).

Multiply up:        b√2 = a
Square both sides:  2b² = a²

So 2 divides a².  By Theorem 1.2, 2 divides a.
So a = 2c for some integer c.

Put that back:      2b² = (2c)² = 4c²
Divide by 2:        b² = 2c²

So 2 divides b².  By Theorem 1.2, 2 divides b.

Now 2 divides a AND 2 divides b — they share the factor 2.
But we chose a and b with NO common factor.   ← contradiction

So the assumption was wrong: √2 is irrational.
```

> [!TIP]
> **Why each step is there:**
> - **"Cancel all common factors"** — any fraction can be reduced to lowest terms, so this costs nothing. It sets the trap the proof will spring.
> - **Squaring** gets rid of the root, leaving only whole numbers, where Theorem 1.2 can work.
> - **Theorem 1.2 is used twice** — once on a, once on b.
> - **The contradiction:** a and b turned out to be both co-prime and *not* co-prime. Both can't be true.

**Example 5 — √3 is irrational** (exactly the same shape, with 3 in place of 2):

```
Suppose √3 = a/b, a and b co-prime.
b√3 = a  →  3b² = a²  →  3 divides a²  →  3 divides a.   Let a = 3c.
3b² = 9c²  →  b² = 3c²  →  3 divides b²  →  3 divides b.
a and b share 3 — contradicts co-prime. So √3 is irrational.
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The same proof works for √p for any prime p**, because Theorem 1.2 holds for every prime. (It does NOT prove √4 is irrational — 4 isn't prime, and indeed √4 = 2.)

### Mixing rationals and irrationals

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **rational ± irrational = irrational**
> - **non-zero rational × irrational = irrational**, and the same for ÷
>
> **Why:** if 5 − √3 were rational, then √3 = 5 − (that rational) would be rational too, because rationals subtracted from rationals stay rational. That's false. The same "move everything else to the other side" trick works for products.

**Example 6 — 5 − √3 is irrational.**

```
Suppose 5 − √3 = a/b  (a, b integers, b ≠ 0).
Then √3 = 5 − a/b = (5b − a)/b.
5b − a and b are integers, so (5b − a)/b is rational.
So √3 would be rational — but √3 is irrational. Contradiction.
Hence 5 − √3 is irrational.
```

**Example 7 — 3√2 is irrational.**

```
Suppose 3√2 = a/b.  Then √2 = a/(3b), which is rational (a, 3b integers).
Contradiction, since √2 is irrational. So 3√2 is irrational.
```

> [!TIP]
> **The recipe for "show X is irrational" when X is built from √2, √3, √5:**
> 1. Suppose X = a/b.
> 2. **Rearrange to get the root alone** on one side.
> 3. The other side is made only of integers → rational.
> 4. That contradicts the root being irrational. Done.

### Exercise 1.2, worked

**1. Prove √5 is irrational.** Same as Theorem 1.3 with 5:

```
Suppose √5 = a/b, a and b co-prime.  Then 5b² = a², so 5 divides a² → 5 divides a.
Let a = 5c: 5b² = 25c² → b² = 5c² → 5 divides b² → 5 divides b.
a and b share 5 — contradiction. So √5 is irrational.
```

**2. Prove 3 + 2√5 is irrational.**

```
Suppose 3 + 2√5 = a/b.
2√5 = a/b − 3 = (a − 3b)/b
√5 = (a − 3b)/(2b)       ← integers over integers: rational
But √5 is irrational (Q1). Contradiction. So 3 + 2√5 is irrational.
```

**3. Prove these are irrational:**

```
(i)  1/√2.  Suppose 1/√2 = a/b (a ≠ 0, since 1/√2 isn't 0).
     Flip both sides: √2 = b/a — rational. Contradiction.

(ii) 7√5.  Suppose 7√5 = a/b.  Then √5 = a/(7b) — rational. Contradiction.

(iii) 6 + √2.  Suppose 6 + √2 = a/b.  Then √2 = a/b − 6 = (a − 6b)/b — rational. Contradiction.
```

> [!NOTE]
> **Rationalising also works for (i):** 1/√2 = √2/2 = ½ × √2, a non-zero rational times an irrational, so it's irrational.

## Practice — test yourself

1. **Is 2ⁿ × 5ⁿ ever ending in 0?** Yes — it equals 10ⁿ, which always ends in 0. (Contrast with 4ⁿ and 6ⁿ: what's different is that **5 is in the fingerprint**.)
2. **HCF of two numbers is 9 and LCM is 459. One is 27. The other?** 9 × 459 ÷ 27 = **153**.
3. **Can two numbers have HCF 16 and LCM 380?** No — the HCF must divide the LCM (every common factor divides every common multiple), and 380 ÷ 16 isn't whole.
4. **Smallest number divisible by 8, 9 and 25?** LCM = **1800** (Exercise 1.1 Q3).
5. **Is √2 × √8 irrational?** No — √2 × √8 = √16 = 4. *The product of two irrationals can be rational*, so the "rational × irrational" rule doesn't extend to two irrationals.

## In a nutshell

- **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic:** every composite number is a product of primes in **exactly one way** (order aside). Uniqueness means a prime missing from the factorisation doesn't divide the number.
- **HCF** = smallest power of each **common** prime; **LCM** = greatest power of **every** prime.
- **HCF × LCM = a × b** for two numbers (each prime's two powers get used once each). Not for three.
- **If a prime p divides a², it divides a** (false for composites: 4 | 36 but 4 ∤ 6).
- **√2, √3, √5, √p (p prime) are irrational** — proved by contradiction using co-prime a/b.
- **rational ± irrational** and **non-zero rational × or ÷ irrational** are irrational — prove by isolating the root.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 1 is neither prime nor composite · HCF × LCM = product works for two numbers only · the HCF must divide the LCM · Theorem 1.2 needs a PRIME · in the √2 proof, say "a and b co-prime" at the start or there's nothing to contradict · irrational × irrational can be rational (√2 × √2 = 2) · a number ends in 0 only if both 2 and 5 are in its prime factorisation.
