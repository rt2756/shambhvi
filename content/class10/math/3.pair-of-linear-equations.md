## 3.1 What the chapter is about

**A situation to fix ideas.** Akhila goes to a fair. **The number of Hoopla games is half the number of Giant Wheel rides.** A ride costs ₹3 and a Hoopla game ₹4, and she spends ₹20 in all.

```
let x = number of rides,  y = number of Hoopla games

y = x/2                 …(1)
3x + 4y = 20            …(2)
```

**You could guess and check — one ride? two rides? — but that is slow and gives no guarantee.** This chapter gives methods that always work.

## 3.2 The graphical method

**A pair of linear equations is represented by TWO LINES, and two lines in a plane can do exactly three things.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Lines | Solutions | Name |
> |---|---|---|
> | **INTERSECT at a single point** | **exactly one — a UNIQUE solution** | **CONSISTENT** |
> | **PARALLEL** | **NO solution** | **INCONSISTENT** |
> | **COINCIDENT** (the same line) | **INFINITELY MANY** — every point on the line | **DEPENDENT** (and consistent) |
>
> - **CONSISTENT** = has a solution.
> - **INCONSISTENT** = has no solution.
> - **DEPENDENT** = the two equations are **equivalent**, so there are infinitely many common solutions.
>
> **A dependent pair is ALWAYS consistent** — infinitely many solutions is still "having a solution".

### The ratio test

**Write both equations in the form `a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0` and `a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0`, then compare the three ratios.**

| Pair | Ratios | Lines | Solutions |
|---|---|---|---|
| `x − 2y = 0`, `3x + 4y − 20 = 0` | `1/3`, `−2/4`, `0/−20` → **a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂** | **intersecting** | **exactly one (unique)** |
| `2x + 3y − 9 = 0`, `4x + 6y − 18 = 0` | `2/4 = 3/6 = −9/−18` → **all three equal** | **coincident** | **infinitely many** |
| `x + 2y − 4 = 0`, `2x + 4y − 12 = 0` | `1/2 = 2/4` but `≠ −4/−12` | **parallel** | **none** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE RATIO TEST**
> ```
> a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂                  →  INTERSECTING   →  unique solution     (consistent)
> a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂          →  COINCIDENT     →  infinitely many     (dependent)
> a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂          →  PARALLEL       →  no solution         (inconsistent)
> ```
> **The converse is also true**, so the test decides the case completely.

> [!TIP]
> **Why the ratios say what they say.**
>
> **`a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂` means the two lines have the SAME SLOPE** — one equation's x and y coefficients are just a multiple of the other's. Same slope means they never meet, unless they are the same line.
>
> **The third ratio then decides between "same line" and "parallel":** if `c₁/c₂` matches too, the whole equation is a multiple of the other, so the lines coincide. **If it doesn't match, the lines are parallel but shifted apart.**
>
> **So the first two ratios ask "are they going the same way?" and the third asks "are they in the same place?"**

**Worked — are `x + 3y = 6` and `2x − 3y = 12` consistent?**

| x | 0 | 6 | | x | 0 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `y = (6 − x)/3` | 2 | 0 | | `y = (2x − 12)/3` | −4 | −2 |

**Plot A(0, 2), B(6, 0), P(0, −4), Q(3, −2). The lines AB and PQ meet at B(6, 0).**
**So x = 6, y = 0 — the pair is CONSISTENT with a unique solution.**

**Worked — `5x − 8y + 1 = 0` and `3x − (24/5)y + 3/5 = 0`.**
```
multiply the second by 5/3:   5x − 8y + 1 = 0
```
**That IS the first equation, so the lines are COINCIDENT and there are infinitely many solutions.**

> [!TIP]
> **Spotting a multiple saves all the plotting.** Whenever one equation looks like a fraction-heavy version of the other, try scaling it — if it becomes identical, you are done.

**Worked — Champa's pants and skirts.** "The number of skirts is **two less than twice** the number of pants" and also "**four less than four times** the number of pants".
```
y = 2x − 2        …(1)
y = 4x − 4        …(2)

the lines meet at (1, 0)

so x = 1, y = 0 — she bought ONE pair of pants and NO skirts
```

> [!TIP]
> **Always check the answer against the words, not just the algebra.** "1 pant and 0 skirts" satisfies both sentences — and it is exactly the kind of answer that guessing would have skipped over.

## 3.3 Algebraic methods

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why we need algebra at all.** The graphical method fails when the solution has **non-integral coordinates** — points like `(√3, 2√7)`, `(−1.75, 3.3)` or `(4/13, 1/19)` **cannot be read accurately off graph paper.**
>
> **Algebraic methods give the exact answer regardless.**

### 3.3.1 Substitution

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE METHOD**
> **Step 1.** From **either** equation — whichever is convenient — express **one variable in terms of the other**.
> **Step 2.** **Substitute** that into the **other** equation, giving one equation in one variable. Solve it.
> **Step 3.** Put the value back into the **Step 1** expression to get the other variable.

**Worked — `7x − 15y = 2` and `x + 2y = 3`:**
```
Step 1  from the second:   x = 3 − 2y

Step 2  substitute into the first:
        7(3 − 2y) − 15y = 2
        21 − 14y − 15y  = 2
        −29y = −19
        y = 19/29

Step 3  x = 3 − 2(19/29) = (87 − 38)/29 = 49/29

solution:  x = 49/29,  y = 19/29
```

> [!TIP]
> **Choose the equation with a coefficient of 1 (or −1) to rearrange.** Here `x + 2y = 3` gives `x = 3 − 2y` with no fractions. Starting from the other equation would have meant dividing by 7 immediately.
>
> **And notice the answer: 49/29 and 19/29.** No amount of careful graph-reading would have produced that — which is exactly the point of the method.

**Worked — Aftab and his daughter.** "Seven years ago I was **seven times** as old as you were; three years from now I shall be **three times** as old as you will be."
```
let s = Aftab's age, t = daughter's age

s − 7 = 7(t − 7)   →   s − 7t + 42 = 0     …(1)
s + 3 = 3(t + 3)   →   s − 3t = 6          …(2)

from (2):  s = 3t + 6
into (1):  (3t + 6) − 7t + 42 = 0
           −4t + 48 = 0   →   t = 12
then       s = 3(12) + 6 = 42
```
**Aftab is 42 and his daughter 12.**

> [!WARNING]
> **Set up the equations carefully with ages.** "Seven years ago" means **both** ages must be reduced by 7 — a very common slip is to reduce only one.

### When substitution produces no variable at all

**Worked — 2 pencils and 3 erasers cost ₹9; 4 pencils and 6 erasers cost ₹18.**
```
2x + 3y = 9        …(1)
4x + 6y = 18       …(2)

from (1):  x = (9 − 3y)/2

into (2):  4(9 − 3y)/2 + 6y = 18
           18 − 6y + 6y = 18
           18 = 18          ← TRUE, and no variable left
```
**A TRUE statement with no variable means INFINITELY MANY solutions** — the two equations say exactly the same thing. **So the cost of a pencil cannot be determined uniquely.**

**Worked — will two rails `x + 2y − 4 = 0` and `2x + 4y − 12 = 0` ever cross?**
```
from (1):  x = 4 − 2y

into (2):  2(4 − 2y) + 4y − 12 = 0
           8 − 4y + 4y − 12 = 0
           −4 = 0           ← FALSE, and no variable left
```
**A FALSE statement means NO solution — the pair is inconsistent, and the rails never cross.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **What to do when the variables vanish:**
> ```
> statement is TRUE  (e.g. 18 = 18)  →  INFINITELY MANY solutions (dependent)
> statement is FALSE (e.g. −4 = 0)   →  NO solution (inconsistent)
> ```
> **The variables disappearing is not an error — it is the algebra telling you the lines are coincident or parallel.**

### 3.3.2 Elimination

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE METHOD**
> **Step 1.** Multiply both equations by suitable **non-zero constants** to make the coefficients of one variable **numerically equal**.
> **Step 2.** **Add or subtract** to eliminate that variable.
> **Step 3.** Solve the resulting equation in one variable.
> **Step 4.** Substitute back into either **original** equation for the other variable.
>
> **Step 2 rule: SUBTRACT when the signs are the same, ADD when they are opposite.**

**Worked — incomes in the ratio 9 : 7, expenditures 4 : 3, each saves ₹2000 a month.**
```
incomes 9x and 7x; expenditures 4y and 3y

9x − 4y = 2000        …(1)
7x − 3y = 2000        …(2)

×3:   27x − 12y = 6000        …(3)
×4:   28x − 12y = 8000        …(4)

(4) − (3):   x = 2000

into (1):  9(2000) − 4y = 2000  →  18000 − 2000 = 4y  →  y = 4000

incomes = 9x = ₹18,000  and  7x = ₹14,000
```
**Check: 18000 : 14000 = 9 : 7 ✔ and expenditures 16000 : 12000 = 4 : 3 ✔**

> [!TIP]
> **Why "9x and 7x" rather than two separate unknowns:** using the ratio directly builds one condition into the notation and leaves fewer unknowns to chase.

**Worked — `2x + 3y = 8` and `4x + 6y = 7`:**
```
×2:   4x + 6y = 16        …(3)
      4x + 6y = 7         …(4)

(3) − (4):   0 = 9        ← FALSE

so the pair has NO SOLUTION
```

**Worked — a two-digit number.** Its sum with the reversed number is 66, and its digits differ by 2.
```
let the tens digit be x and the units digit y, so the number is 10x + y
the reversed number is 10y + x

(10x + y) + (10y + x) = 66
11(x + y) = 66
x + y = 6          …(1)

the digits differ by 2, so EITHER  x − y = 2  …(2)   OR   y − x = 2  …(3)

with (1) and (2):  x = 4, y = 2  →  the number 42
with (1) and (3):  x = 2, y = 4  →  the number 24

there are TWO such numbers: 42 and 24
```
**Check: 42 + 24 = 66 ✔ and the digits differ by 2 ✔**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two lessons from this problem:**
> 1. **A two-digit number with tens digit x and units digit y is `10x + y`, not `xy`.** Reversing gives `10y + x`.
> 2. **"Differ by 2" does NOT say which is larger**, so both cases must be solved. **A question can have more than one valid answer, and the algebra will not warn you — the wording must.**

## Summary

```
GRAPHICAL   two lines can intersect, be parallel, or coincide
   intersect  → unique solution     CONSISTENT
   parallel   → no solution         INCONSISTENT
   coincident → infinitely many     DEPENDENT (and consistent)

RATIO TEST for a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0
   a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂                intersecting, unique solution
   a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂        coincident, infinitely many
   a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂        parallel, none
   first two ratios: same direction?   third ratio: same place?

SUBSTITUTION   express one variable from the easier equation,
               substitute into the other, solve, then back-substitute
ELIMINATION    scale both equations to match one coefficient,
               add or subtract to remove it, solve, then back-substitute
               subtract if the signs match, add if they are opposite

IF THE VARIABLES VANISH
   a TRUE statement  (18 = 18)  → infinitely many solutions
   a FALSE statement (−4 = 0)   → no solution

use ALGEBRA rather than a graph whenever the answer may be
non-integral — 49/29 cannot be read off graph paper

two-digit number with digits x (tens) and y (units)  =  10x + y
reversed = 10y + x;  "digits differ by 2" means BOTH cases must be checked
```
