## What this chapter is about

A polynomial like x² − 3x − 4 is a machine: put a number in for x, get a number out. The inputs that give **0** out are special — they're called **zeroes**. This chapter asks two questions:

1. **What do zeroes look like on a graph?** (Answer: where the curve crosses the x-axis.)
2. **Can you read the zeroes' sum and product straight off the coefficients, without solving?** (Answer: yes — and that's the powerful part.)

## Quick recap from Class 9

- The **degree** is the highest power of x. 4x + 2 has degree 1; 2y² − 3y + 4 has degree 2; 5x³ − 4x² + x − √2 has degree 3.
- Things like 1/(x − 1), √x + 2 or 1/(x² + 2x + 3) are **not** polynomials — x appears under a root or in a denominator, i.e. with a power that isn't a whole number.

| Degree | Name | General form |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | linear | ax + b, a ≠ 0 |
| 2 | quadratic ("quadrate" = square) | ax² + bx + c, a ≠ 0 |
| 3 | cubic | ax³ + bx² + cx + d, a ≠ 0 |

> [!NOTE]
> **Why "a ≠ 0" every time?** If a were 0, the top term would vanish and the degree would drop. 0x² + 3x + 1 is really just the linear 3x + 1.

### Values and zeroes

Let p(x) = x² − 3x − 4.

```
p(2)  = 4 − 6 − 4   = −6
p(0)  = 0 − 0 − 4   = −4
p(−1) = 1 + 3 − 4   = 0     ←
p(4)  = 16 − 12 − 4 = 0     ←
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **p(k)** means "the value of p(x) when x = k".
> **A real number k is a ZERO of p(x) if p(k) = 0.** So −1 and 4 are the zeroes of x² − 3x − 4.

**The linear case is easy.** For p(x) = 2x + 3, set 2k + 3 = 0, so k = −3/2. In general:

```
zero of ax + b  =  −b/a  =  −(constant term) / (coefficient of x)
```

So for a linear polynomial, the zero is **tied to its coefficients**. The big question of the chapter: does the same happen for quadratics?

## 2.2 Geometrical meaning of the zeroes

### Linear: a straight line

The graph of y = 2x + 3 is a straight line through (−2, −1) and (2, 7). It crosses the x-axis at (−3/2, 0) — and −3/2 is exactly the zero.

**Why must that be so?** Every point on the x-axis has y = 0. So where the graph meets the x-axis, y = 2x + 3 = 0 — which is precisely the equation for the zero.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The zeroes of p(x) are the x-coordinates of the points where the graph of y = p(x) meets the x-axis.**
> A straight line (not horizontal) meets the x-axis exactly once, so a **linear polynomial has exactly one zero**.

### Quadratic: a parabola

Make a table for y = x² − 3x − 4:

| x | −2 | −1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 6 | **0** | −4 | −6 | −6 | −4 | **0** | 6 |

Plot these and join them smoothly — you get a U-shaped curve called a **parabola**. It crosses the x-axis at x = −1 and x = 4, the two zeroes we found.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The graph of y = ax² + bx + c is a parabola.**
> - **a > 0** → opens **upwards** (∪). *Why:* for big x (positive or negative), ax² is huge and positive and swamps the other terms, so both arms shoot up.
> - **a < 0** → opens **downwards** (∩), for the same reason with a negative sign.

A parabola can meet the x-axis in **three** ways:

| Case | Picture | Number of zeroes |
|---|---|---|
| (i) cuts the x-axis at two different points A and A′ | crosses twice | **2 distinct zeroes** |
| (ii) just touches the x-axis at one point | the two points coincide | **1 zero** (two equal zeroes) |
| (iii) stays completely above or completely below the x-axis | never meets it | **no zero** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **So a quadratic polynomial has at most 2 zeroes.** A ∪ or ∩ shape simply can't cross a straight line more than twice.

### Cubic: up to three crossings

For y = x³ − 4x:

| x | −2 | −1 | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | **0** | 3 | **0** | −3 | **0** |

The curve snakes up and down, crossing the x-axis at −2, 0 and 2 — three zeroes. Check by factorising: x³ − 4x = x(x − 2)(x + 2). ✔

Other cubics cross fewer times:
- y = x³ meets the x-axis only at 0 → **one** zero.
- y = x³ − x² = x²(x − 1) meets it at 0 and 1 → **two** zeroes.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A polynomial of degree n has AT MOST n zeroes**, because its graph meets the x-axis at most n times.
>
> **Why (in short):** each zero k gives a factor (x − k). A degree-n polynomial can't contain more than n such factors multiplied together — that would make the degree bigger than n.

> [!NOTE]
> You won't be asked to *plot* quadratic or cubic graphs — the book says so. You **will** be asked to **read** them: count the crossings.

### Example 1 and Exercise 2.1 — counting zeroes from a graph

**The rule:** count the points where the curve **meets** the x-axis — crossings *and* touchings. Ignore where it meets the y-axis.

- A curve meeting the x-axis at one point → **1** zero.
- Two points → **2**. Three → **3**. Four → **4**.
- A curve that never touches the x-axis (e.g. a horizontal-ish line above it, or a parabola floating above it) → **no** zeroes.

> [!WARNING]
> **A curve that touches the x-axis and bounces back still counts** as meeting it — that point is a zero. And a **straight line parallel to the x-axis** (not on it) never meets it: **no** zero.

The answers in the book's key for Fig. 2.10 are: (i) no zeroes, (ii) 1, (iii) 3, (iv) 2, (v) 4, (vi) 3 — each is just the number of meeting points in that picture.

## 2.3 Zeroes and coefficients

### Spot the pattern

**Example 2 — x² + 7x + 10.** Factorise by splitting the middle term (find two numbers that multiply to 10 and add to 7: 2 and 5):

```
x² + 7x + 10 = (x + 2)(x + 5)      → zeroes −2 and −5

sum of zeroes     = −2 + (−5) = −7     and   −(coefficient of x)/(coefficient of x²) = −7/1 ✔
product of zeroes = (−2)(−5)  = 10     and   (constant term)/(coefficient of x²) = 10/1 ✔
```

**x² − 3x − 4** had zeroes −1 and 4: sum 3 = −(−3)/1, product −4 = −4/1. Same pattern!

### Why it always works

Suppose α and β are the zeroes of ax² + bx + c. Then (x − α) and (x − β) are factors, so

```
ax² + bx + c = a(x − α)(x − β)
             = a[x² − (α + β)x + αβ]
             = ax² − a(α + β)x + aαβ
```

Two polynomials are equal only if their **matching coefficients** are equal. Compare the x terms and the constants:

```
b = −a(α + β)     →   α + β = −b/a
c = aαβ           →   αβ    =  c/a
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **If α and β are the zeroes of ax² + bx + c (a ≠ 0):**
> ```
> α + β = −b/a = −(coefficient of x) / (coefficient of x²)
> αβ    =  c/a =  (constant term)    / (coefficient of x²)
> ```

> [!TIP]
> **Memory hook:** sum has a **minus**, product doesn't. Both are "something over a".

**Example 3 — x² − 3.** Use a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b):

```
x² − 3 = (x − √3)(x + √3)      → zeroes √3 and −√3

sum     = √3 + (−√3) = 0     and   −(coefficient of x)/1 = −0/1 = 0 ✔
product = (√3)(−√3)  = −3    and   constant/1 = −3 ✔
```

> [!NOTE]
> **No x term means b = 0, so the zeroes add to 0** — they're a number and its negative. That's why x² − 3 has ±√3.

### Building a quadratic from its zeroes

Run the formula backwards. If the sum is S and the product is P, then with a = 1: b = −S and c = P.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A quadratic whose zeroes have sum S and product P:**
> ```
> x² − Sx + P        (or any k(x² − Sx + P), k ≠ 0)
> ```
> **Why "any k":** multiplying the whole polynomial by k doesn't change where it is zero — k × 0 is still 0.

**Example 4 — sum −3, product 2.** x² − (−3)x + 2 = **x² + 3x + 2**. (Check: (x + 1)(x + 2), zeroes −1, −2, sum −3, product 2 ✔.)

### Cubics have three relationships

For p(x) = 2x³ − 5x² − 14x + 8, you can check p(4) = 0, p(−2) = 0, p(½) = 0, and a cubic has at most three zeroes, so these are all of them.

```
sum             = 4 + (−2) + ½            = 5/2   = −(−5)/2   = −b/a
pairs           = 4(−2) + (−2)(½) + (½)(4) = −8 − 1 + 2 = −7 = −14/2 = c/a
product         = 4 × (−2) × ½            = −4    = −8/2      = −d/a
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **If α, β, γ are the zeroes of ax³ + bx² + cx + d:**
> ```
> α + β + γ        = −b/a
> αβ + βγ + γα     =  c/a
> αβγ              = −d/a
> ```
> **Why:** the same comparison trick — a(x − α)(x − β)(x − γ) = a[x³ − (α+β+γ)x² + (αβ+βγ+γα)x − αβγ]. The signs alternate −, +, −.

> [!NOTE]
> The cubic relationships (Example 5 in the book) are marked "not from the examination point of view". Know they exist; the quadratic ones are the exam ones.

## Exercise 2.2, worked

**1. Find the zeroes and verify the relationships.**

**(i) x² − 2x − 8.** Two numbers with product −8 and sum −2: −4 and 2.

```
x² − 2x − 8 = (x − 4)(x + 2)     → zeroes 4, −2
sum 4 + (−2) = 2  = −(−2)/1 ✔       product 4 × (−2) = −8 = −8/1 ✔
```

**(ii) 4s² − 4s + 1.** This is a perfect square: (2s)² − 2(2s)(1) + 1².

```
4s² − 4s + 1 = (2s − 1)²          → zeroes ½, ½
sum ½ + ½ = 1 = −(−4)/4 ✔          product ¼ = 1/4 ✔
```

**(iii) 6x² − 3 − 7x.** First **rearrange into standard order**: 6x² − 7x − 3. Product of a and c = 6 × (−3) = −18; two numbers with product −18 and sum −7: −9 and 2.

```
6x² − 9x + 2x − 3 = 3x(2x − 3) + 1(2x − 3) = (3x + 1)(2x − 3)
zeroes −1/3 and 3/2
sum   −1/3 + 3/2 = −2/6 + 9/6 = 7/6  = −(−7)/6 ✔
product (−1/3)(3/2) = −1/2 = −3/6 ✔
```

**(iv) 4u² + 8u.** No constant, so u is a common factor.

```
4u² + 8u = 4u(u + 2)     → zeroes 0, −2
sum −2 = −8/4 ✔           product 0 = 0/4 ✔
```

**(v) t² − 15** = (t − √15)(t + √15) → zeroes **√15, −√15**. Sum 0 = −0/1 ✔, product −15 = −15/1 ✔.

**(vi) 3x² − x − 4.** a × c = −12; numbers with product −12, sum −1: −4 and 3.

```
3x² − 4x + 3x − 4 = x(3x − 4) + 1(3x − 4) = (x + 1)(3x − 4)
zeroes −1 and 4/3
sum −1 + 4/3 = 1/3 = −(−1)/3 ✔        product −4/3 ✔
```

**2. Find a quadratic with the given sum and product.** Use x² − Sx + P, then multiply through to clear fractions (allowed — "any k").

| Sum, product | x² − Sx + P | Tidy answer |
|---|---|---|
| (i) ¼, −1 | x² − ¼x − 1 | ×4 → **4x² − x − 4** |
| (ii) √2, ⅓ | x² − √2x + ⅓ | ×3 → **3x² − 3√2x + 1** |
| (iii) 0, √5 | x² − 0x + √5 | **x² + √5** |
| (iv) 1, 1 | **x² − x + 1** | |
| (v) −¼, ¼ | x² + ¼x + ¼ | ×4 → **4x² + x + 1** |
| (vi) 4, 1 | **x² − 4x + 1** | |

> [!WARNING]
> **Sign slip alert:** the middle coefficient is **minus** the sum. Sum −¼ gives **+**¼x.

## More worked examples

**A. Without solving, find the sum and product of the zeroes of 2x² + 5x − 3.**

```
sum = −5/2         product = −3/2
```
(Check: 2x² + 5x − 3 = (2x − 1)(x + 3), zeroes ½ and −3: sum −5/2 ✔, product −3/2 ✔.)

**B. If one zero of x² − 6x + k is 2, find k and the other zero.**

```
put x = 2:   4 − 12 + k = 0   →  k = 8
sum of zeroes = 6, so the other zero = 6 − 2 = 4
check product: 2 × 4 = 8 = k ✔
```

**C. If α and β are the zeroes of x² − 5x + 6, find α² + β² without finding α and β.**

The trick: (α + β)² = α² + 2αβ + β², so α² + β² = (α + β)² − 2αβ.

```
α + β = 5,  αβ = 6
α² + β² = 25 − 12 = 13
```
(Check: zeroes are 2 and 3; 4 + 9 = 13 ✔.)

**D. Same α, β — find 1/α + 1/β.**

```
1/α + 1/β = (β + α)/(αβ) = 5/6
```

> [!TIP]
> **Symmetric expressions** (ones that don't change if you swap α and β) can always be rewritten using just α + β and αβ. That's why the two relationships are so useful.

**E. Find k if the zeroes of kx² + 3x − 6 add to 3/2... then check.** Sum = −3/k = 3/2 → k = −2. Polynomial −2x² + 3x − 6 has sum −3/(−2) = 3/2 ✔.

## In a nutshell

- **Zero of p(x):** a number k with p(k) = 0. **Graphically:** the x-coordinate where y = p(x) meets the x-axis.
- **Linear** → a line → exactly **1** zero (−b/a). **Quadratic** → a parabola (∪ if a > 0, ∩ if a < 0) → **2, 1 or 0** zeroes. **Degree n** → at most **n** zeroes.
- **Quadratic ax² + bx + c:** **α + β = −b/a**, **αβ = c/a** — from ax² + bx + c = a(x − α)(x − β).
- **Quadratic with sum S, product P:** **k(x² − Sx + P)**.
- **Cubic:** α+β+γ = −b/a, αβ+βγ+γα = c/a, αβγ = −d/a (not for the exam).

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** rearrange to ax² + bx + c before reading a, b, c (6x² − 3 − 7x has b = −7, c = −3) · sum is −b/a, NOT b/a · a "touching" point on a graph is still a zero · count meetings with the x-axis, not the y-axis · when forming a polynomial, the middle term is −(sum)x · a² + b² ≠ (a + b)²; use (α + β)² − 2αβ.
