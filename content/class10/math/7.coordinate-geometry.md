## 7.1 What coordinate geometry is for

**From Class 9:** a point's position needs a pair of **coordinate axes**. The distance from the y-axis is the **x-coordinate (abscissa)**; from the x-axis, the **y-coordinate (ordinate)**. Points on the x-axis are **(x, 0)**; on the y-axis, **(0, y)**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **COORDINATE GEOMETRY IS AN ALGEBRAIC TOOL FOR STUDYING GEOMETRY.**
>
> **It lets us study geometry using algebra, and understand algebra with the help of geometry.** A linear equation becomes a straight line; `y = ax² + bx + c` becomes a parabola.
>
> **Because of this it is used in physics, engineering, navigation, seismology and art.**

**This chapter adds two tools: the DISTANCE between two given points, and the coordinates of the point DIVIDING a segment in a given ratio.**

## 7.2 The distance formula

**A town B is 36 km east and 15 km north of town A. How far is B from A, without measuring?**

### Building up from simple cases

```
two points on the x-axis:   A(4, 0), B(6, 0)
    AB = OB − OA = 6 − 4 = 2 units

two points on the y-axis:   C(0, 3), D(0, 8)
    CD = 8 − 3 = 5 units

one on each axis:           A(4, 0), C(0, 3)
    right-angled at the origin, so  AC = √(3² + 4²) = 5 units
```

**Two points not on the axes — P(4, 6) and Q(6, 8):**
```
drop PR and QS perpendicular to the x-axis, and PT perpendicular to QS

RS = 2, so PT = 2
QS = 8 and TS = PR = 6, so QT = 2

by Pythagoras:   PQ² = PT² + QT² = 2² + 2² = 8
                 PQ = 2√2 units
```

**Points in different quadrants — P(6, 4) and Q(−5, −3):**
```
PT = 11 units,  QT = 7 units

PQ = √(11² + 7²) = √170 units
```

### The general formula

```
for P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂), drop PR and QS to the x-axis
and PT perpendicular to QS

OR = x₁, OS = x₂,  so  RS = x₂ − x₁ = PT
SQ = y₂, ST = PR = y₁,  so  QT = y₂ − y₁

by Pythagoras in △PTQ:
      PQ² = PT² + QT² = (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE DISTANCE FORMULA**
> ```
> PQ = √[ (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² ]
> ```
> **We take only the POSITIVE square root, because distance is never negative.**
>
> **Distance from the ORIGIN:**
> ```
> OP = √(x² + y²)
> ```

> [!TIP]
> **The order of subtraction does not matter.** `PQ = √[(x₁ − x₂)² + (y₁ − y₂)²]` gives the same answer, because **the differences are SQUARED** and `(−5)² = 5²`.
>
> **This makes sense: the distance from P to Q is the same as from Q to P.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The formula IS Pythagoras' theorem, dressed for coordinates.** The horizontal gap `x₂ − x₁` and the vertical gap `y₂ − y₁` are the two legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the segment PQ. **Nothing new is being assumed — only translated.**

## Uses of the distance formula

**Worked — do (3, 2), (−2, −3) and (2, 3) form a triangle? Of what type?**
```
PQ = √[(3+2)² + (2+3)²] = √(25 + 25) = √50 = 5√2
QR = √[(−2−2)² + (−3−3)²] = √(16 + 36) = √52 = 2√13
PR = √[(3−2)² + (2−3)²] = √(1 + 1) = √2
```
**Check whether they form a triangle at all:** three points are **COLLINEAR** if the two shorter distances add to the longest. Here `√2 + 5√2 = 6√2 ≈ 8.49` while `2√13 ≈ 7.21` — **not equal, so they do form a triangle.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How to name the type of triangle from the three distances:**
> - **all three equal → EQUILATERAL**
> - **exactly two equal → ISOSCELES**
> - **the square of the longest = the sum of the squares of the other two → RIGHT-ANGLED** (the converse of Pythagoras)
> - **the two shorter distances ADD to the longest → the points are COLLINEAR, and there is no triangle at all**

> [!TIP]
> **Keep the distances in surd form and compare their SQUARES** — it avoids decimals and makes the Pythagoras check exact.

## 7.3 The section formula

**A telephone relay tower is to stand at P between A and B, so that its distance from B is TWICE its distance from A — that is, P divides AB in the ratio 1 : 2.** With A as origin, B is at (36, 15). Where is P?

```
drop perpendiculars from P and B to the x-axis, and PC perpendicular to BE

by AA similarity, △POD ~ △BPC

OD/PC = OP/PB = 1/2       and       PD/BC = OP/PB = 1/2

so    x/(36 − x) = 1/2      and     y/(15 − y) = 1/2

giving  x = 12  and  y = 5
```
**The tower stands at P(12, 5).**

### The general derivation

```
let P(x, y) divide the join of A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂) internally with PA/PB = m₁/m₂

drop AR, PS, BT perpendicular to the x-axis; draw AQ and PC parallel to it

by AA similarity:   △PAQ ~ △BPC

      PA/BP = AQ/PC = PQ/BC

AQ = x − x₁      PC = x₂ − x
PQ = y − y₁      BC = y₂ − y

so    m₁/m₂ = (x − x₁)/(x₂ − x)      →      x = (m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂)
      m₁/m₂ = (y − y₁)/(y₂ − y)      →      y = (m₁y₂ + m₂y₁)/(m₁ + m₂)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE SECTION FORMULA** — the point P dividing the join of A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂) **internally** in the ratio **m₁ : m₂** is
> ```
> P = ( (m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂) ,  (m₁y₂ + m₂y₁)/(m₁ + m₂) )
> ```
> **In the k : 1 form** (dividing throughout by m₂ and writing k for m₁/m₂):
> ```
> P = ( (kx₂ + x₁)/(k + 1) ,  (ky₂ + y₁)/(k + 1) )
> ```

> [!WARNING]
> **Watch the CROSSING of the subscripts: m₁ multiplies x₂, and m₂ multiplies x₁.**
>
> **Why it crosses: m₁ is the part of the journey NEAREST A, so it measures how far you have gone TOWARDS B** — and therefore it weights B's coordinate. **Getting this backwards is the single commonest error in the chapter.**
>
> **A quick check: put m₁ = 0.** Then P should be at A itself, and the formula gives `(m₂x₁)/(m₂) = x₁` ✔

### The midpoint — a special case

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A MIDPOINT divides the segment in the ratio 1 : 1.** Putting `m₁ = m₂ = 1`:
> ```
> midpoint of A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂)  =  ( (x₁ + x₂)/2 ,  (y₁ + y₂)/2 )
> ```
> **Just the AVERAGE of the two x's and of the two y's** — which is exactly what "halfway" should mean.

**Worked — the point dividing (4, −3) and (8, 5) in the ratio 3 : 1:**
```
x = (3·8 + 1·4)/(3 + 1) = (24 + 4)/4 = 7
y = (3·5 + 1·(−3))/(3 + 1) = (15 − 3)/4 = 3

the point is (7, 3)
```
**Sanity check: the ratio 3 : 1 means P is three-quarters of the way from A to B**, and 7 is indeed three-quarters of the way from 4 to 8 ✔

## Finding the ratio

**Worked — in what ratio does (−4, 6) divide the join of A(−6, 10) and B(3, −8)?**

```
let the ratio be m₁ : m₂. By the section formula:

      −4 = (3m₁ − 6m₂)/(m₁ + m₂)

      −4m₁ − 4m₂ = 3m₁ − 6m₂
      2m₂ = 7m₁
      m₁ : m₂ = 2 : 7

check with the y-coordinate:
      (−8(2) + 10(7))/(2 + 7) = (−16 + 70)/9 = 54/9 = 6      ✔
```

**Alternative — use the k : 1 form**, which involves one unknown instead of two:
```
      −4 = (3k − 6)/(k + 1)

      −4k − 4 = 3k − 6
      7k = 2      →      k = 2/7

so the ratio is 2/7 : 1, i.e.  2 : 7
```

> [!TIP]
> **The k : 1 form is almost always quicker for "find the ratio" questions** — one unknown, one equation.
>
> **And always CHECK with the other coordinate.** If the point really lies on the segment, the same ratio must satisfy both x and y. **If it doesn't, the point is not on the line at all** — which is itself a useful thing to discover.
