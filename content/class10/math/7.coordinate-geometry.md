## What this chapter is about

**Two towns:** B is 36 km east and 15 km north of town A. How far apart are they in a straight line? And a telephone company wants a tower on the road between them, twice as far from B as from A — where exactly?

Coordinate geometry answers both by turning geometry into **arithmetic on coordinates**. Two formulas do almost all the work:

1. **Distance formula** — how far apart two points are.
2. **Section formula** — the point that splits a segment in a given ratio (including the mid-point).

### Recap from Class 9

- A point is written **(x, y)**. **x (abscissa)** = distance from the y-axis; **y (ordinate)** = distance from the x-axis.
- A point **on the x-axis** is **(x, 0)**; a point **on the y-axis** is **(0, y)**.

## 7.2 The distance formula

### Start with easy cases

- **Same y:** A(4, 0) and B(6, 0) lie on a horizontal line → AB = 6 − 4 = **2**.
- **Same x:** C(−3, 2) and D(−3, 7) lie on a vertical line → CD = 7 − 2 = **5**.

**Now a slanted one: P(4, 3) and Q(6, 3)?** Same y, so 2. But what about **P(4, 6) and Q(6, 3)?** Draw lines through P and Q parallel to the axes to make a right triangle: the horizontal leg is 6 − 4 = 2, the vertical leg is 6 − 3 = 3. By **Pythagoras**, PQ² = 2² + 3² = 13, so PQ = **√13**.

### The general formula

For any P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂), the same right triangle has legs **(x₂ − x₁)** across and **(y₂ − y₁)** up. So

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **DISTANCE FORMULA**
> ```
> PQ = √[ (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² ]
> ```
> **Distance of P(x, y) from the origin O(0, 0):**  OP = √(x² + y²)
>
> **Why it works:** it's just Pythagoras. The horizontal gap and the vertical gap are the two legs of a right triangle; the distance is the hypotenuse.

> [!TIP]
> **The order doesn't matter.** (x₂ − x₁)² = (x₁ − x₂)², because squaring removes the sign. So you can subtract either way round — each bracket is squared on its own.

**The two towns:** A = (0, 0), B = (36, 15). AB = √(36² + 15²) = √(1296 + 225) = √1521 = **39 km** — about the real-world straight-line distance. (That's Exercise 7.1 Q2.)

### What you can prove with distances

| To show … | Check that … |
|---|---|
| three points are **collinear** | the two shorter distances **add up** to the longest |
| a triangle is **isosceles** | two sides are equal |
| a triangle is **equilateral** | all three sides are equal |
| a triangle is **right-angled** | the sides satisfy Pythagoras: a² + b² = c² |
| a quadrilateral is a **square** | all 4 sides equal **and** both diagonals equal |
| a **rhombus** | all 4 sides equal, diagonals **not** equal |
| a **rectangle** | opposite sides equal **and** diagonals equal |
| a **parallelogram** | opposite sides equal (diagonals not equal) |

> [!WARNING]
> **Four equal sides is not enough for a square** — a rhombus has that too. You need equal diagonals to force the right angles.

**Example 1 — do P(3, 2), Q(−2, −3), R(2, 3) form a triangle? What kind?**

```
PQ = √[(−5)² + (−5)²] = √50 ≈ 7.07
QR = √[4² + 6²]       = √52 ≈ 7.21
PR = √[(−1)² + 1²]    = √2  ≈ 1.41
```
The sum of any two sides is more than the third (PQ + PR ≈ 8.48 > 7.21), so they **do** form a triangle. Also PQ² + PR² = 50 + 2 = 52 = QR², so it's a **right triangle** (right angle at P).

**Example 2 — A(1, 7), B(4, 2), C(−1, −1), D(−4, 4) form a square.**

```
AB = √(9 + 25) = √34      BC = √(25 + 9) = √34
CD = √(9 + 25) = √34      DA = √(25 + 9) = √34      all sides equal
AC = √(4 + 64) = √68      BD = √(64 + 4) = √68      diagonals equal  → SQUARE
```

**Example 3 — are Ashima A(3, 1), Bharti B(6, 4) and Camella C(8, 6) sitting in a line?**

```
AB = √(9 + 9)   = 3√2
BC = √(4 + 4)   = 2√2
AC = √(25 + 25) = 5√2
AB + BC = 5√2 = AC   → collinear: yes, in a line
```

**Example 4 — a relation between x and y if (x, y) is equidistant from A(7, 1) and B(3, 5).** Equal distances → equal **squares** of distances (avoids roots):

```
(x − 7)² + (y − 1)² = (x − 3)² + (y − 5)²
x² − 14x + 49 + y² − 2y + 1 = x² − 6x + 9 + y² − 10y + 25
−8x + 8y = −16     →     x − y = 2
```

> [!NOTE]
> **x − y = 2 is a straight line** — it's the **perpendicular bisector** of AB. Every point equidistant from A and B lies on it (a Class 9 fact).

**Example 5 — a point on the y-axis equidistant from A(6, 5) and B(−4, 3).** On the y-axis means **(0, y)**:

```
36 + (5 − y)² = 16 + (3 − y)²
61 − 10y = 25 − 6y   →   4y = 36   →   y = 9     the point is (0, 9)
check: AP = √(36 + 16) = √52,  BP = √(16 + 36) = √52 ✔
```

## 7.3 The section formula

### The idea

**P divides AB in the ratio m₁ : m₂** means AP : PB = m₁ : m₂. For the telephone tower, "twice as far from B as from A" means AP : PB = 1 : 2.

**Start with numbers.** A(0, 0) and B(36, 15), ratio 1 : 2. P is ⅓ of the way from A to B, so it's ⅓ of the way across and ⅓ of the way up: P = (12, 5).

### The general formula — and why

Take A(x₁, y₁), B(x₂, y₂), and P(x, y) with AP : PB = m₁ : m₂. Drop perpendiculars to the x-axis and draw horizontals to form two small right triangles, **AQP** and **PCB**. Their sides are parallel, so they are **similar (AA)**:

```
AP/PB = AQ/PC = PQ/BC
m₁/m₂ = (x − x₁)/(x₂ − x)
m₁x₂ − m₁x = m₂x − m₂x₁
x = (m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂)
```
The same works vertically for y.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SECTION FORMULA** — P divides A(x₁, y₁)–B(x₂, y₂) internally in the ratio m₁ : m₂:
> ```
> P = ( (m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂) ,  (m₁y₂ + m₂y₁)/(m₁ + m₂) )
> ```
> **Memory hook — "cross-multiply":** m₁ goes with B (the far end), m₂ goes with A. Each point is "pulled" toward the end it's closer to.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **MID-POINT** (ratio 1 : 1):
> ```
> M = ( (x₁ + x₂)/2 ,  (y₁ + y₂)/2 )
> ```
> **Why:** put m₁ = m₂ = 1 — the mid-point is just the **average** of the two ends.

> [!TIP]
> **Ratio as k : 1.** When the ratio is unknown, call it **k : 1**. Then P = ((kx₂ + x₁)/(k + 1), (ky₂ + y₁)/(k + 1)) — one unknown instead of two.

**Example 6 — divide (4, −3)–(8, 5) in the ratio 3 : 1.**

```
x = (3 × 8 + 1 × 4)/4 = 28/4 = 7
y = (3 × 5 + 1 × (−3))/4 = 12/4 = 3         P = (7, 3)
```

**Example 7 — in what ratio does (−4, 6) divide A(−6, 10)–B(3, −8)?** Let the ratio be m₁ : m₂:

```
−4 = (3m₁ − 6m₂)/(m₁ + m₂)   →   −4m₁ − 4m₂ = 3m₁ − 6m₂   →   7m₁ = 2m₂   →   2 : 7
check with y: (2 × (−8) + 7 × 10)/9 = 54/9 = 6 ✔
```

> [!NOTE]
> **Always check with the other coordinate.** If the y-value doesn't match, the point isn't on the segment at all.

**Example 8 — points of trisection of A(2, −2)–B(−7, 4).** Trisection points split AB into three equal parts, so P divides it 1 : 2 and Q divides it 2 : 1.

```
P = ( (−7 + 4)/3 , (4 − 4)/3 )   = (−1, 0)
Q = ( (−14 + 2)/3 , (8 − 2)/3 )  = (−4, 2)
```

**Example 9 — the ratio in which the y-axis divides (5, −6)–(−1, −4).** On the y-axis, **x = 0**. Ratio k : 1:

```
x = (−k + 5)/(k + 1) = 0   →   k = 5   →   ratio 5 : 1
y = (5 × (−4) + (−6))/6 = −26/6 = −13/3        point (0, −13/3)
```

**Example 10 — A(6, 1), B(8, 2), C(9, 4), D(p, 3) form a parallelogram (in order). Find p.** The **diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other**, so AC and BD have the **same mid-point**:

```
mid-point of AC = (15/2, 5/2)      mid-point of BD = ((8 + p)/2, 5/2)
(8 + p)/2 = 15/2   →   p = 7
```

## In a nutshell

- **Distance:** PQ = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²] (Pythagoras). From the origin: √(x² + y²).
- **Collinear:** two smaller distances add to the largest. **Square:** 4 equal sides + equal diagonals.
- **Equidistant:** set the **squared** distances equal.
- **Section formula (m₁ : m₂):** ((m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂), (m₁y₂ + m₂y₁)/(m₁ + m₂)) — from similar triangles.
- **Mid-point:** the average of the ends. **Unknown ratio:** call it k : 1.
- Parallelogram: diagonals share a mid-point.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** square the differences, don't square then subtract the wrong way — (−3)² = 9 · m₁ multiplies x₂ (the FAR end), not x₁ · on the x-axis y = 0, on the y-axis x = 0 · four equal sides could be a rhombus — check the diagonals · AP = (3/7)AB means ratio 3 : 4, not 3 : 7 · always check an unknown ratio with the other coordinate.
