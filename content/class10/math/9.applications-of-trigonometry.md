## 9.1 Heights and distances

**Chapter 8 defined the ratios. This chapter uses them to measure things you cannot reach.**

### The three key terms

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **LINE OF SIGHT — the line drawn from the eye of an observer to the point being viewed.**
>
> **ANGLE OF ELEVATION — the angle the line of sight makes with the HORIZONTAL when the point viewed is ABOVE the horizontal level.** You **raise** your head to look.
>
> **ANGLE OF DEPRESSION — the angle the line of sight makes with the HORIZONTAL when the point viewed is BELOW the horizontal level.** You **lower** your head to look.

> [!WARNING]
> **Both angles are measured from the HORIZONTAL, never from the vertical.**
>
> **And an angle of depression is measured at the OBSERVER'S position**, between the horizontal through the observer's eye and the downward line of sight — **not at the object.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE ALTERNATE-ANGLES TRICK — used in almost every depression problem.**
>
> The horizontal at the observer's eye and the horizontal at the ground are **PARALLEL**, and the line of sight is a **TRANSVERSAL**.
>
> **So the angle of DEPRESSION at the top equals the angle of ELEVATION at the bottom** — they are **alternate angles**.
>
> **This is what lets you transfer the given angle into the right triangle where you can actually use it.**

### What you need to know to find a height

**To find the height of a minar from where a student stands:**
1. the **distance** from the foot of the minar
2. the **angle of elevation** of the top
3. the **height of the student's eye** above the ground

**Then `height of minar = BC + BD`, where BD is the eye height and BC is found from the right triangle** — using **tan** or **cot**, since those are the ratios connecting the opposite and adjacent sides.

> [!TIP]
> **How to choose the ratio: list what you HAVE and what you WANT, then pick the ratio containing exactly those two sides.**
> ```
> have adjacent, want opposite      →  tan
> have opposite, want adjacent      →  tan (or cot)
> have hypotenuse, want opposite    →  sin
> have hypotenuse, want adjacent    →  cos
> have a side, want the hypotenuse  →  sin or cos
> ```
> **Never start calculating before you have decided this** — it is where most of the work in these problems actually lies.

## Worked examples

### One triangle, one unknown

**A tower stands vertically. From a point 15 m from its foot the angle of elevation of the top is 60°. Find its height.**
```
tan 60° = AB/BC

√3 = AB/15

AB = 15√3 m
```

**An electrician must reach a point 1.3 m below the top of a 5 m pole, with a ladder at 60° to the horizontal. Find the ladder's length and where to place its foot. (√3 = 1.73)**
```
height to reach:  BD = 5 − 1.3 = 3.7 m

the LADDER is the hypotenuse, so use sin:

sin 60° = BD/BC
√3/2 = 3.7/BC
BC = (3.7 × 2)/√3 = 4.28 m       ← the ladder's length

for the distance of the foot, use cot (adjacent over opposite):

cot 60° = DC/BD = 1/√3
DC = 3.7/√3 = 2.14 m
```

**An observer 1.5 m tall stands 28.5 m from a chimney; the angle of elevation of the top from her eyes is 45°. Find the chimney's height.**
```
work in the triangle above EYE level:

tan 45° = AE/DE
1 = AE/28.5      →      AE = 28.5 m

height of chimney = AE + eye height = 28.5 + 1.5 = 30 m
```

> [!WARNING]
> **When the observer's height is given, the triangle sits ABOVE eye level — so the trigonometry gives only PART of the height.** The observer's height must be **added at the end**.
>
> **Forgetting to add it is the commonest error in this chapter.**

### Two triangles sharing a side

**From point P the angle of elevation of the top of a 10 m building is 30°; with a flag on top it is 45°. Find the flagstaff's length and the distance of the building from P. (√3 = 1.732)**
```
FIRST triangle — use the KNOWN height to find the shared distance:

tan 30° = AB/AP
1/√3 = 10/AP      →      AP = 10√3 = 17.32 m

SECOND triangle — now AP is known, so the total height follows:

let the flagstaff be x, so AD = 10 + x

tan 45° = AD/AP
1 = (10 + x)/(10√3)
10 + x = 10√3
x = 10(√3 − 1) = 7.32 m
```
**Distance 17.32 m; flagstaff 7.32 m.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE STRATEGY FOR TWO-TRIANGLE PROBLEMS: find the SHARED side first.**
>
> The two right triangles here have the **same base AP**. Whichever triangle has enough information goes first; **AP is then the bridge into the second triangle.**

**The shadow of a tower is 40 m longer when the Sun's altitude is 30° than when it is 60°. Find the tower's height.**
```
let the height be h and the shorter shadow x, so the longer shadow is x + 40

from the 60° triangle:   tan 60° = h/x        →     h = x√3        …(1)
from the 30° triangle:   tan 30° = h/(x + 40) →     h = (x + 40)/√3 …(2)

equate:   x√3 = (x + 40)/√3
          3x = x + 40
          2x = 40   →   x = 20

then      h = 20√3 m
```

> [!TIP]
> **When no length is given at all, name the unknowns and get TWO equations — one per triangle — then eliminate.** The chapter's hardest problems are all of this form.

### Angles of depression

**From the top of a multi-storeyed building, the angles of depression of the top and bottom of an 8 m building are 30° and 45°. Find the taller building's height and the distance between them.**
```
by ALTERNATE ANGLES, ∠PBD = 30° and ∠PAC = 45°

in △PBD:   tan 30° = PD/BD    →    BD = PD√3

in △PAC:   tan 45° = PC/AC    →    PC = AC

now  PC = PD + DC,  with DC = AB = 8  and  AC = BD

so   PD + 8 = PD√3
     8 = PD(√3 − 1)
     PD = 8/(√3 − 1) = 8(√3 + 1)/2 = 4(√3 + 1)

height PC = PD + 8 = 4√3 + 4 + 8 = 4(3 + √3) m
distance AC = BD = PD√3 = 4√3(√3 + 1) = 4(3 + √3) m
```

**From a bridge 3 m above the banks, the angles of depression of the two banks are 30° and 45°. Find the river's width.**
```
by alternate angles, ∠A = 30° and ∠B = 45°

in △APD:  tan 30° = PD/AD  →  1/√3 = 3/AD  →  AD = 3√3

in △PBD:  tan 45° = PD/BD  →  BD = PD = 3

width AB = AD + BD = 3√3 + 3 = 3(√3 + 1) m
```

> [!TIP]
> **Rationalise surd denominators as you go** — `8/(√3 − 1)` becomes `4(√3 + 1)` by multiplying above and below by `(√3 + 1)`, since `(√3 − 1)(√3 + 1) = 2`. **Leaving a surd in the denominator makes the final arithmetic far harder.**

## How to approach any problem in this chapter

> [!IMPORTANT]
> 1. **DRAW the figure**, marking the vertical object, the horizontal ground and the line of sight.
> 2. **Mark the given angle at the correct vertex** — elevation at the observer's eye, depression at the observer's eye, transferring by alternate angles where needed.
> 3. **Label what you KNOW and what you WANT.**
> 4. **Choose the ratio** containing exactly those two sides.
> 5. **Solve**, and **add any eye height or base height** the triangle did not include.
> 6. **Check the answer is reasonable** — a tower is not 3 mm tall, and the shadow of a tall object at a small Sun angle should be long.

## Summary

```
LINE OF SIGHT      from the observer's eye to the point viewed
ANGLE OF ELEVATION line of sight ABOVE the horizontal — you raise your head
ANGLE OF DEPRESSION line of sight BELOW the horizontal — you lower your head
   BOTH are measured from the HORIZONTAL, and both at the OBSERVER

ALTERNATE ANGLES  the angle of DEPRESSION from the top equals the angle of
   ELEVATION from the bottom — the two horizontals are parallel and the
   line of sight is a transversal.  This transfers a given angle into a
   usable triangle, and is needed in nearly every depression problem

CHOOSING THE RATIO — list what you HAVE and what you WANT
   adjacent ↔ opposite     tan or cot
   hypotenuse ↔ opposite   sin or cosec
   hypotenuse ↔ adjacent   cos or sec

WHEN THE OBSERVER'S HEIGHT IS GIVEN the triangle sits ABOVE eye level,
   so the trigonometry gives only PART of the height — ADD the eye height

TWO-TRIANGLE PROBLEMS  find the SHARED side first; it bridges the two
   if no length is given at all, name the unknowns, write one equation per
   triangle, and eliminate

standard values used constantly:
   tan 30° = 1/√3     tan 45° = 1     tan 60° = √3
   rationalise surd denominators as you go
```
