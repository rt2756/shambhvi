## 8.1 Weaving — the oldest way of making a fabric

**Nature was weaving long before people were.**

> [!NOTE]
> **The male BAYA WEAVER bird builds a hanging pouch-shaped nest out of grass**, threading strands over and under to make it strong. **An expert bird's nest is beautifully fine; a young bird's first attempts are rough.** Even weaving has to be learnt.

```
WEAVING = crossing two sets of threads or strips over and under
          each other until they lock into a single fabric

    one set runs VERTICALLY, the other HORIZONTALLY
    each thread goes OVER, UNDER, OVER, UNDER …
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY OVER-AND-UNDER HOLDS TOGETHER AND STACKING DOES NOT.**
>
> **Lay strips on top of each other and they slide apart the moment you lift them.** Nothing is holding anything.
>
> **Weave them, and every strip is TRAPPED — held down by the strips it goes under and holding down the ones it goes over.**
>
> **Each thread is doing two jobs at once: being gripped and gripping.** That mutual grip is the whole of weaving.
>
> **And each row must be the OPPOSITE of the one above it.** If two rows went over and under in the same places, nothing would be trapped and the mat would fall apart.

> [!TIP]
> **You can test this with paper strips in five minutes.** Tape 5–6 strips down at the top and weave others through — over, under, over, under. **Then look at your own shirt through a magnifying glass or a phone camera zoom.** **The criss-cross you see is exactly what you just made, only much finer.**

**People have woven** coconut fibre, palm reeds, bamboo, grass, jute, cotton and silk into mats, baskets and cloth.

## 8.2 Weaving traditions of India

**People in India knew how to weave 4,000 years ago.** Traditional weaving is done by hand on an instrument called a **LOOM**, and the cloth is called **HANDLOOM fabric**.

| Tradition | From |
|---|---|
| **Kanjeevaram** | Tamil Nadu |
| **Pashmina** | Kashmir (wool from the Changthangi goat of Ladakh) |
| **Ikat** | Odisha and Gujarat |
| **Patola** | Patan, Gujarat — one saree takes 6 months to a year |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY HANDLOOM MATTERS TO INDIA BEYOND THE CLOTH ITSELF.**
>
> **It employs over 45 lakh people**, especially women and rural artisans — one of the largest cottage industries in the world.
>
> **It uses NO ELECTRICITY**, so it is genuinely eco-friendly and can be done anywhere, including in a home.
>
> **And each tradition carries designs and techniques that exist nowhere else.** If the last weaver of a pattern stops, the pattern is gone — there is no book that contains it.
>
> **Textile mills using machines make cloth far faster and far cheaper.** Both exist; they are good at different things.

> [!NOTE]
> **Indian muslin was so fine it was called "woven air"** — a whole saree could pass through a ring. **India was the first country to cultivate cotton and make cloth from it**, which changed textiles worldwide.

## 8.3 From fibre to thread — spinning

```
FIBRE   a single thin hair-like strand
THREAD  many fibres TWISTED together  (also called yarn)
CLOTH   threads WOVEN together
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY TWISTING LOOSE COTTON MAKES SOMETHING STRONG — the key idea of the chapter.**
>
> **Pull a strand out of a ball of cotton and it snaps at once.** The fibres are short and simply slide past one another.
>
> **Now twist it as you pull. It becomes noticeably stronger.**
>
> **Why: twisting presses the fibres against each other, and the tighter they are pressed the harder it is for one to slide.** Pull on the thread and the twist tightens further, gripping harder still.
>
> **So a thread is not a long fibre.** It is thousands of SHORT fibres, held together by nothing but friction — and friction is enough because the twist keeps renewing it.
>
> **This is why a rope of any length can be made from fibres a few centimetres long.**

**A CHARKHA (spinning wheel) does exactly what your fingers and a pencil do**, only faster and more evenly.

> [!NOTE]
> **Gandhiji made spinning a symbol of the freedom struggle.** Knowing how to make your own cloth — spinning the thread and weaving the fabric — meant not depending on anyone else for it. **That is what *atmanirbhar* (self-reliant) means, and the cloth made this way is KHADI.**

## 8.4 Natural and synthetic fibres

```
NATURAL     from plants or animals
   cotton (plant) · linen (plant) · bamboo (plant) · jute (plant)
   wool (sheep, goat) · silk (silk moth)

SYNTHETIC   made by people from artificial materials
   nylon · polyester · rayon · terylene
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE REAL DIFFERENCE, AND WHY BOTH ARE USED.**
>
> **Natural fibres come from something that GREW, so they take land, water and time — and they eventually rot away when discarded.**
>
> **Synthetic fibres are manufactured, so they can be made in any quantity, in any colour, with chosen properties: strong, stretchy, waterproof, cheap.** But most do not rot, so they stay as waste for a very long time.
>
> **Neither is simply better.** Cotton breathes and is comfortable in heat; nylon makes a rope that will not rot in the rain.
>
> **The sensible question is not "which is good?" but "which suits this job?"** — and, increasingly, "what happens to it afterwards?"

**How to guess what a cloth is made of:** feel whether it is smooth or rough, thick or thin, shiny or dull, stretchy or not.
> [!TIP]
> ```
> cotton    soft, not shiny, not stretchy, comfortable in heat
> silk      very smooth and SHINY, light
> wool      thick, slightly rough, very warm
> jute      coarse and rough — sacks and ropes
> polyester smooth, slightly shiny, does not crease, often stretchy
> ```
> **Why shininess is a good clue: a shiny fibre has a very smooth surface that reflects light evenly.** Silk and many synthetics do; cotton and wool have rougher surfaces that scatter it.

## 8.5 Silk and the life cycle of the silk moth

```
1. the silk moth LAYS EGGS
2. the eggs hatch into tiny CATERPILLARS (silkworms)
3. the caterpillars eat mulberry leaves and grow big
4. each spins a COCOON of silk thread around itself
5. an adult MOTH emerges from the cocoon
6. and the cycle begins again
```
**To get silk, the cocoons are put in hot water and the thread is gently unwound, then made into yarn.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY A COCOON GIVES ONE ENORMOUSLY LONG THREAD — and why that makes silk special.**
>
> **The caterpillar builds its cocoon by winding a SINGLE continuous strand round and round itself**, sometimes hundreds of metres long.
>
> **So silk does not have to be spun from short fibres at all.** It arrives already long, already smooth, already strong.
>
> **That is why silk is so fine and so shiny** — there are no short fibre-ends sticking out to scatter the light or weaken the thread.
>
> **Cotton must be twisted to make up for being short. Silk never needed to be.**

> [!TIP]
> **A LIFE CYCLE is the pattern of growth and change every living thing goes through** — born, grows, reproduces, dies, and the next generation begins. **A butterfly, a mango tree and a person all have one; they differ in the stages, not in the idea.**

## 8.6 Stitching

> [!NOTE]
> **The TAILORBIRD** — a tiny green bird — **sews its own nest.** It pokes holes along the edge of a big leaf with its beak and pulls plant fibre or spider silk through, exactly as a tailor uses needle and thread, **making a green sleeve which it pads to hold its eggs.**

**The RUNNING STITCH** — the simplest stitch there is:
```
knot the thread · come up at A · go down at B
              · come up at C · go down at D
              · keep going: up, down, up, down, in a straight line
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY ONE BROKEN THREAD MATTERS SO MUCH MORE IN STITCHING THAN IN WEAVING.**
>
> **A running stitch is ONE thread doing the whole job.** Cut it anywhere and it can pull out from that point in both directions — the seam opens.
>
> **A woven cloth is thousands of threads, each held by its neighbours.** One broken thread leaves a small flaw; the cloth holds.
>
> **This is the difference between a chain and a net.** A chain fails at its weakest link; a net loses one knot and carries on.
>
> **It is also why a seam is knotted at both ends**, and why stronger seams use stitches that lock rather than simply run.

## 8.7 Decorating cloth — the embroideries of India

| Embroidery | From |
|---|---|
| **Chikan / Chikankari** | Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh |
| **Banjara** | Rajasthan |
| **Kantha** | West Bengal, Odisha, Tripura |
| **Gota** | Rajasthan |
| **Phulkari** | Punjab |
| **Toda** | Tamil Nadu |
| **Kashmiri** | Kashmir |
| **Khneng** | Meghalaya |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY EVERY REGION DEVELOPED ITS OWN — the same reason as the drums in Chapter 5.**
>
> **Each style grew from what was locally available: the cloth woven there, the threads that could be got, the dyes from local plants, and the images that mattered to those people.**
>
> **So an embroidery is a record of a place** — its plants, its beliefs, its festivals, its way of life.
>
> **KANTHA is a particularly good example**, because it began as a way of stitching worn-out cloths together into a quilt. **Its beauty grew directly out of thrift.**

> [!NOTE]
> **BANDHANI** is a tie-dye done entirely by hand: **small parts of the cloth are tied tightly with thread before dyeing, so the dye cannot reach them** — and each tied spot becomes a dot in the pattern. **No machine involved, just fingers and thread.**

## 8.8 Reusing and recycling cloth

**In India clothes are rarely thrown away.**
```
passed to a younger sibling or to anyone who can use them
cut down or restitched into something smaller
old cloths joined into a QUILT — the kantha tradition
worn-out cloth used for cleaning
scraps sold to a recycler
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY CLOTH IS TOO VALUABLE TO THROW AWAY — think about what went into it.**
>
> **A cotton shirt began as a plant that took land, water, months of growing and a harvest.** Then the cotton was cleaned, spun into thread, woven into cloth, dyed, cut and stitched.
>
> **All of that work is still sitting in the shirt when it stops fitting you.** Nothing about it has been used up except its size.
>
> **So passing it on is not a small kindness. It is refusing to throw away everything that was spent making it.**
>
> **And synthetic cloth thrown away does not rot** — it stays as waste for decades, which makes the reasoning stronger, not weaker.
