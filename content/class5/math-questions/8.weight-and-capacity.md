A shopkeeper writes "Cupboard — 45 g" on his stock list. What is wrong, and what should it say?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It should be 45 kg, not 45 g.**
>
> **Why: 45 g is about the weight of a small packet of biscuits.** No cupboard is that light.
>
> **The unit has to match the object, because a measurement is a COUNT of that unit.** Counting a cupboard in grams would give about 45,000 — an enormous, useless number.
>
> **Sense-check:** things you can carry in one hand are grams; things you need both hands or another person for are kilograms.

---

Convert 6 kg 40 g into grams. A friend answers 6,400 g. Is the friend right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No. The correct answer is 6,040 g.**
>
> **Why 6,400 g is wrong: 6,400 g means 6 kg 400 g** — ten times too many grams.
>
> **Why 6,040 g is right: the gram part must fill the LAST THREE places**, because 1 kg = 1,000 g and 1,000 has three zeros.
> ```
> 6 kg   40 g
>   ↓     ↓
>   6    040     →  6,040 g
> ```
> **40 must be padded to 040.** That leading zero is holding the hundreds-of-grams place empty — it is not decoration.

---

Convert 9,006 g into kilograms and grams.

> [!ANSWER]
> **9 kg 6 g.**
>
> **Why: cover the last three digits with your finger.**
> ```
> 9,006  →   9 | 006   →  9 kg 6 g
> ```
> **What is left is the kilograms; what is hidden is the grams.**
>
> **No arithmetic is needed at all** — that is the payoff of understanding that the kilogram is simply the thousands column with a name.

---

Which is heavier: 3 kg 90 g or 3,900 g? By how much?

> [!ANSWER]
> **3,900 g is heavier, by 810 g.**
>
> **Why: convert BOTH to the same unit first.**
> ```
> 3 kg 90 g  =  3,090 g
> 3,900 g    =  3,900 g
> 3,900 − 3,090 = 810 g
> ```
> **They look almost identical written down, which is exactly the danger.** 90 g padded to 090 sits in the tens place; 900 g sits in the hundreds place.
>
> **Never judge two mixed quantities by eye — convert, then compare as ordinary numbers.**

---

Why can't you compare 45 mg and 8 g just by looking at the numbers 45 and 8?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the two numbers are counting DIFFERENT THINGS.**
>
> **45 counts milligrams; 8 counts grams — and a gram is a thousand times bigger.**
> ```
> 8 g = 8,000 mg,  which beats 45 mg easily
> ```
> **So 8 g is far heavier, even though 8 is a much smaller number than 45.**
>
> **Number-order is only trustworthy when both numbers count the same unit.** It is the same reason you cannot add 3 apples to 4 oranges and call it 7 apples.

---

Fill each blank with `<`, `>` or `=`: (a) 7 kg 8 g ___ 7,800 g   (b) 2,050 mg ___ 2 g 50 mg   (c) 4 l 60 ml ___ 4,600 ml

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (a) 7 kg 8 g  = 7,008 g,  and 7,008 < 7,800     →   <
> (b) 2,050 mg  and 2 g 50 mg = 2,050 mg          →   =
> (c) 4 l 60 ml = 4,060 ml,  and 4,060 < 4,600    →   <
> ```
> **All three turn on the same idea: the small-unit part fills the last THREE places, padded with zeros.**
>
> **Part (b) is worth staring at** — the two sides look completely different and are exactly equal. **That is why converting first is not optional.**

---

An ornament weighs 12 g 75 mg. Give its weight in milligrams, and explain why the rule is the same one used for kilograms and grams.

> [!ANSWER]
> **12,075 mg.**
> ```
> 12 g   75 mg
>   ↓     ↓
>  12    075    →  12,075 mg
> ```
> **Why the rule is the same: 1 g = 1,000 mg, and 1 kg = 1,000 g — both steps are ×1,000, with three zeros.**
>
> **So the small-unit part gets the last three places in BOTH cases.**
>
> **Learn the move once and it works three times over** — mg→g, g→kg, and ml→l.

---

A lorry carries 7,000 kg of grain. Express this in quintals and in tonnes.

> [!ANSWER]
> **70 quintals, which is 7 tonnes.**
> ```
> 100 kg = 1 quintal   →  7,000 ÷ 100 = 70 quintals
> 10 quintals = 1 tonne →      70 ÷ 10 = 7 tonnes
> ```
> **Why we divide: a quintal is BIGGER than a kilogram, so it takes fewer of them** to make the same load.
>
> **Check by walking back up the ladder: 7 × 1,000 = 7,000 kg ✔** If the round trip does not return your starting number, one of the two steps was wrong.

---

Fill in: ______ kg = 12 tonnes.

> [!ANSWER]
> **12,000 kg.**
>
> **Why: 1 tonne = 1,000 kg, so going from the big unit down to the small unit we MULTIPLY.**
> ```
> 12 × 1,000 = 12,000 kg
> ```
> **The check is the question "should the number get bigger or smaller?"** Kilograms are small, so it takes more of them — the number must grow. **You never have to remember the direction; it is forced.**

---

Add 6 kg 850 g and 3 kg 400 g using the kg | g column method, and say why the carry is 1.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>      kg |  g
>       1 |          ← the carry
>       6 | 8 5 0
>    +  3 | 4 0 0
>    -----------
>      10 | 2 5 0     →   10 kg 250 g
> ```
> **850 + 400 = 1,250 g. Write 250 and carry 1 kg.**
>
> **Why the carry happens at 1,000 and not at 10: the gram column holds up to 999 grams**, and it takes exactly 1,000 g to make one more kilogram.
>
> **That is all "1 kg = 1,000 g" is telling you** — where the column overflows.

---

Subtract: 8 l − 3 l 450 ml.

> [!ANSWER]
> **4 l 550 ml.**
> ```
>       l  |   ml
>       8  | 0 0 0     borrow 1 l  →  7 l and 1,000 ml
>    −  3  | 4 5 0
>    ------------
>       4  | 5 5 0
> ```
> **Why we borrow 1,000 and not 10: one litre IS a thousand millilitres**, so that is what arrives in the ml column when you break a litre open.
>
> **Check by adding back: 4 l 550 ml + 3 l 450 ml = 7 l + 1,000 ml = 8 l ✔** Adding back is the fastest way to test any subtraction.

---

A shop sells 14 kg 300 g of tomatoes on Monday and 9 kg 850 g on Tuesday. How much more was sold on Monday?

> [!ANSWER]
> **4 kg 450 g more.**
> ```
> convert:  14,300 g − 9,850 g = 4,450 g = 4 kg 450 g
> ```
> **Why converting is the smart choice here: 300 − 850 needs a borrow anyway**, and once you are borrowing, working in a single unit removes all chance of confusing the two columns.
>
> **Mental check:** 9 kg 850 g is nearly 10 kg, and 14 kg 300 g is a bit over 14 kg — so the gap should be a bit under 4½ kg. **4 kg 450 g fits ✔**

---

A hostel kitchen buys 80 kg of flour, uses 36 kg 750 g, then buys 45 kg 500 g more. How much flour is in store now?

> [!ANSWER]
> **88 kg 750 g.**
> ```
> 80 kg − 36 kg 750 g  =  80,000 − 36,750 = 43,250 g = 43 kg 250 g
> 43 kg 250 g + 45 kg 500 g = 43,250 + 45,500 = 88,750 g = 88 kg 750 g
> ```
> **Why the order matters: the flour was used BEFORE the new flour arrived**, so you must subtract first and add second.
>
> **A multi-step problem is a ladder** — each rung hands you the number the next rung needs. **Doing the steps out of order gives a number that never existed.**

---

Five sacks of onions each weigh 12 kg 400 g. Find the total weight, working the kg and the g separately.

> [!ANSWER]
> **62 kg.**
> ```
> 5 × 12 kg 400 g
> = (5 × 12 kg) + (5 × 400 g)
> = 60 kg + 2,000 g
> = 60 kg + 2 kg  =  62 kg
> ```
> **Why splitting is allowed: 12 kg 400 g MEANS 12 kg PLUS 400 g** — the two parts are added, not glued together.
>
> **And multiplying a sum means multiplying each part.** It is the distributive idea you already use with plain numbers, applied to a quantity.
>
> **Don't forget the last step:** 2,000 g is not left as grams — it regroups into 2 kg.

---

7 kg 700 g of rice is shared equally among 7 families. How much does each get? Now try the same with 5 kg 300 g among 4 families.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 7 kg 700 g ÷ 7 :  7 kg ÷ 7 = 1 kg,  700 g ÷ 7 = 100 g   →  1 kg 100 g
> 5 kg 300 g ÷ 4 :  5 kg ÷ 4 is NOT a whole number of kg — splitting fails
>                   convert:  5,300 ÷ 4 = 1,325 g = 1 kg 325 g
> ```
> **Why the first one splits and the second one doesn't: splitting is only safe when BOTH parts divide exactly.**
>
> **When either part leaves a remainder, that remainder has to travel into the other column** — and the split method has no way to carry it across.
>
> **When in doubt, convert to a single unit and divide once.** That method never fails.

---

A tin holds 8 l 400 ml of oil, poured equally into 7 bottles. How much is in each bottle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1 l 200 ml.**
> ```
> 8 l ÷ 7 does not divide evenly, so convert:
> 8,400 ml ÷ 7 = 1,200 ml = 1 l 200 ml
> ```
> **Why converting was necessary: 8 does not split into 7 equal whole litres**, so the leftover litre must be broken into millilitres and shared out too.
>
> **Converting first does that automatically** — the 8 litres become 8,000 ml, and every millilitre is then available to be shared.
>
> **Check: 7 × 1,200 = 8,400 ml ✔**

---

If 6 kg of apples cost ₹270, use a double number line to find the cost of 18 kg.

> [!ANSWER]
> **₹810.**
> ```
>          ×3
>   6 kg ─────→ 18 kg
>    |            |
>  ₹270 ─────→  ₹810
>          ×3
> ```
> **Why both lines get the same ×3: if three times as much is bought, three times as much is paid.** The two quantities move together, in step.
>
> **So whatever you do to the top line you must do to the bottom** — that lock is what makes the picture answer the question with no formula at all.

---

A scrap dealer pays ₹45 for 3 kg of plastic. What does he pay for 11 kg?

> [!ANSWER]
> **₹165.**
> ```
> find the cost of 1 kg first:   ₹45 ÷ 3 = ₹15 per kg
> then                         11 × ₹15 = ₹165
> ```
> **Why go via 1 kg: 3 does not step neatly up to 11 by whole-number multiplying**, so the ×3, ×2 shortcut has nothing to grab.
>
> **The "cost of one" is the universal stepping stone** — once you know it, ANY number of kilograms is one multiplication away.
>
> **Sense-check: 11 kg is a bit under 4 lots of 3 kg, so the cost should be a bit under 4 × ₹45 = ₹180. ₹165 fits ✔**

---

Is it sensible to say "the bucket holds 20 ml of water"? What is the right figure and unit?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — a bucket holds about 20 LITRES.**
>
> **Why 20 ml is absurd: 20 ml is about four teaspoons.** You could not wash a hand with it.
>
> **The number 20 was fine; the unit was a thousand times too small.**
>
> **This is the capacity version of "refrigerator — 50 g".** The same sense-check applies: pick the unit that makes the count an ordinary, comfortable size.

---

Convert 3 l 8 ml into millilitres, and 20,045 ml into litres and millilitres.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3 l 8 ml  →  3 | 008   →   3,008 ml
> 20,045 ml →  20 | 045  →  20 l 45 ml
> ```
> **Why 3 l 8 ml is NOT 3,800 or 3,080: the ml part takes the last three places**, so 8 has to be padded to 008.
>
> **Two zeros are needed here, not one** — one for the hundreds place and one for the tens place, both of which are genuinely empty.
>
> **Going back the other way, cover three digits with your finger.** The method is identical to kg and g because 1 l = 1,000 ml has the same three zeros.

---

Ramiz drinks two 500 ml bottles at school. Muskaan drinks 3 l a day. How many 500 ml refills does Muskaan need, and who drinks more at school?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Ramiz  : 500 + 500 = 1,000 ml = 1 l at school
> Muskaan: 3 l = 3,000 ml,  3,000 ÷ 500 = 6 refills a day
> ```
> **Muskaan drinks more over the day (3 l against 1 l).**
>
> **Why the division works: each refill delivers exactly 500 ml**, so asking "how many refills?" is asking "how many 500s fit inside 3,000?"
>
> **That is what division IS — repeated equal groups counted out of a total.**

---

Riya fills one 2 l bottle and four 500 ml bottles. Aarav fills three 750 ml bottles. Who filled more, and by how much?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Riya, by 1 l 750 ml.**
> ```
> Riya : 2,000 + (4 × 500) = 2,000 + 2,000 = 4,000 ml = 4 l
> Aarav: 3 × 750 = 2,250 ml = 2 l 250 ml
> 4,000 − 2,250 = 1,750 ml = 1 l 750 ml
> ```
> **Why everything was converted to ml before comparing: Riya's total mixes litres with millilitres**, and you cannot compare a mixture against a mixture directly.
>
> **One unit throughout turns a fiddly comparison into an ordinary subtraction.**

---

A juice seller has 5 l of juice and serves 250 ml glasses. How many full glasses can he serve? If he has served 10, how much is left? At ₹25 a glass, what will the whole container earn?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (a) 5,000 ÷ 250 = 20 full glasses
> (b) 10 × 250 = 2,500 ml served, so 5,000 − 2,500 = 2,500 ml = 2 l 500 ml left
> (c) 20 × ₹25 = ₹500
> ```
> **Notice that part (a) had to be answered before part (c) could be** — you cannot price the container without first knowing it holds 20 glasses.
>
> **In a multi-part problem the parts are usually rungs of a ladder.** Solve them in order and each answer hands you the next.

---

Milk is poured equally into 8 glasses of 360 ml each, and 120 ml is left in the bottle. How much milk was in the bottle to start with?

> [!ANSWER]
> **3 l.**
> ```
> 8 × 360 = 2,880 ml poured out
> 2,880 + 120 = 3,000 ml = 3 l
> ```
> **Why we ADD the leftover: the original amount is everything that came out PLUS everything still inside.**
>
> **Nothing vanishes when you pour** — so working backwards means putting every part back together.
>
> **A neat sanity check: the answer landed exactly on 3 l**, which is the sort of tidy number a question is usually built around.

---

A shop sells rice at ₹60 per kg. What is the cost of 12 kg 500 g?

> [!ANSWER]
> **₹750.**
> ```
> 12 kg    →  12 × 60 = ₹720
> 500 g    →  half a kg  →  half of ₹60 = ₹30
> total    →  ₹720 + ₹30 = ₹750
> ```
> **Why splitting into 12 kg and 500 g works: the price is per kilogram, so each whole kilogram costs the same ₹60** and any part-kilogram costs that same fraction of ₹60.
>
> **500 g is exactly half a kilogram**, which is why the second step is so easy — spotting friendly fractions like ½, ¼ and ¾ of a kilogram saves a great deal of arithmetic.

---

A king donates wheat equal to 10 times his weight. This year he donated 800 kg; last year 780 kg. What are the two weights, and how much did he gain?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> this year : 800 ÷ 10 = 80 kg
> last year : 780 ÷ 10 = 78 kg
> gain      : 80 − 78 = 2 kg
> ```
> **Why we DIVIDE to get his weight: the donation is 10 times the weight, so the weight is the donation shared into 10 equal parts.**
>
> **Multiplication and division undo each other** — that is the only reason working backwards is possible at all.
>
> **You could also find the gain directly:** the donations differ by 20 kg, and since every 10 kg of wheat stands for 1 kg of king, **20 kg of wheat means 2 kg of king ✔** Two routes, same answer — a good sign.

---

An ant weighs about 3 mg. About how many ants would balance a 6 g biscuit?

> [!ANSWER]
> **About 2,000 ants.**
> ```
> 6 g = 6,000 mg
> 6,000 ÷ 3 = 2,000
> ```
> **Why we had to convert first: you cannot divide grams by milligrams** — the two numbers count different things, so the division would be meaningless.
>
> **Once both are in milligrams, the question becomes "how many 3s in 6,000?"** — an ordinary division.
>
> **This is the same rule as comparing:** get everything into ONE unit before doing anything at all with the numbers.

---

Two shops are 850 g and 1 kg 200 g of sugar short. A supplier can send only whole kilograms. How many kilograms must he send, and how much will be spare?

> [!ANSWER]
> **He must send 3 kg, leaving 950 g spare.**
> ```
> total needed: 850 g + 1,200 g = 2,050 g = 2 kg 50 g
> 2 kg is not enough (50 g short), so 3 kg must go
> spare: 3,000 − 2,050 = 950 g
> ```
> **Why the answer rounds UP, not to the nearest: 2 kg would leave one shop 50 g short**, and a shortage is not an acceptable answer to "how much must he send?"
>
> **Whenever a question says "must", ask what happens if you send one less.** If the answer is "someone goes without", you round up.

---

Which is greater: 800 mg + 88 mg, or 880 mg + 8 mg? Explain without doing much arithmetic.

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are EQUAL — both come to 888 mg.**
> ```
> 800 + 88 = 888        880 + 8 = 888
> ```
> **The quick way to see it without adding: the second sum takes 80 from the first number and gives 80 to the second.**
> ```
> 800 → 880   (+80)
>  88 →   8   (−80)
> ```
> **A total is unchanged when you move an amount from one part to the other** — it has only been shifted, not created or destroyed.
>
> **Spotting that shift is faster and safer than adding twice.**
