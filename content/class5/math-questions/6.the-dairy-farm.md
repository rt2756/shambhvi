Butter packets are arranged in 4 rows of 6, then rearranged into 6 rows of 4. Does the total change?

> [!ANSWER]
> **NO — both give 24 packets.**
> ```
> 4 × 6 = 24        6 × 4 = 24
> ```
> **Why: turning the arrangement sideways does not ADD or REMOVE any packets.** The rows become columns and the columns become rows, but nothing has been touched.
>
> **YOU CAN MULTIPLY NUMBERS IN ANY ORDER.**
>
> **This is genuinely useful, not just true.** If you know 6 × 4 but stumble over 4 × 6, simply swap them — **there is only ever one fact to learn, not two.**

---

Find: 4 × 10 · 3 × 100 · 20 × 1,000. Explain what happens to the digits.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 4 × 10     = 40           every digit moves ONE place LEFT
> 3 × 100    = 300          every digit moves TWO places LEFT
> 20 × 1,000 = 20,000       every digit moves THREE places LEFT
> ```
> **Why the digits move: multiplying by 10 makes the number TEN TIMES bigger, and the next place to the left is worth exactly ten times as much.**
>
> **Why zeros appear: the places the digits LEFT BEHIND are now empty, and a zero holds each one open.**
>
> **The zeros are not "stuck on" as a trick — they are PLACEHOLDERS**, doing exactly the job zeros always do.

Find 30 × 20 by splitting off the tens, and explain the shortcut it justifies.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 30 × 20 = (3 × 10) × (2 × 10)
>         = 3 × 2 × 10 × 10
>         = 6 × 100
>         = 600
> ```
> **This is why you may "multiply the front digits and count the zeros":**
> ```
> 60 × 50  → 6 × 5 = 30, and TWO zeros   → 3,000
> 700 × 8  → 7 × 8 = 56, and TWO zeros   → 5,600
> 400 × 10 → 4 × 1 = 4,  and THREE zeros → 4,000
> ```
> **The shortcut works because ORDER DOES NOT MATTER** — you are allowed to gather all the tens together and deal with them at the end.
>
> **So it is not a rule to memorise; it is a consequence of a rule you already know.**

Show five different ways to work out 18 × 5.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> HALVING     half of 18 is 9;  9 × 5 = 45;  45 + 45 = 90
>
> DOUBLING    18 → 36 → 72  (that is 4 lots);  72 + 18 = 90  (the 5th lot)
>
> SPLITTING   18 = 10 + 8;  10 × 5 = 50, 8 × 5 = 40;  50 + 40 = 90
>
> REARRANGING 18 × 5 = 9 × 10 = 90
>
> NEAREST TEN 20 × 5 = 100, but that is 2 lots too many;  100 − 10 = 90
> ```
> **All five give 90, and every one of them is correct.**
>
> **Why knowing several is worth it: DIFFERENT NUMBERS SUIT DIFFERENT METHODS.** There is no single "proper" way to multiply — **the skill is looking at the numbers first and choosing the easiest route.**

Explain the halving-and-doubling strategy and why it works.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3 × 18                      22 × 5
> double ↓  ↓ half            half ↓   ↓ double
> 6 ×  9  = 54                11 × 10 = 110
> ```
> **HALVE ONE NUMBER AND DOUBLE THE OTHER — the answer does not change.**
>
> **Why: you have taken away half of one side and given exactly that much back to the other.**
>
> **Picture it with packets:**
> ```
> 3 × 18 is 3 rows of 18
> cut each row in half and lay the halves out separately
>    → now 6 rows of 9
> the packets have been REARRANGED, not removed
> ```

Use halving and doubling for: 22 × 5 · 38 × 5 · 12 × 25.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 22 × 5  → 11 × 10  = 110
> 38 × 5  → 19 × 10  = 190
> 12 × 25 →  6 × 50  = 300      →  or again:  3 × 100 = 300
> ```
> **Why the method suits 5s and 25s especially: DOUBLING A 5 GIVES 10, and doubling a 25 twice gives 100** — the easiest numbers there are.
>
> **In the last one you can halve TWICE**, turning a 25 into a 100 and a 12 into a 3. **A hard-looking sum becomes 3 × 100.**
>
> **The strategy is at its best when one number is close to a "round" partner.**

Use halving and doubling for: 14 × 3 · 50 × 28 · 15 × 22.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 14 × 3  →  7 × 6   = 42
> 50 × 28 → 100 × 14 = 1,400
> 15 × 22 →  30 × 11 = 330
> ```
> **Notice that in each one you halve the number that HALVES EASILY and double the other.**
>
> **In 50 × 28, doubling the 50 gives 100** — so the whole sum collapses to 100 × 14.
>
> **In 15 × 22, halving the 22 gives 11**, which is very easy to multiply by.
>
> **Deciding WHICH number to halve is the actual skill here** — always the one that makes the pair friendlier.

Find 4 × 19 using the nearest multiple.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 4 × 19 = 4 × 20 − 4
>        = 80 − 4
>        = 76
> ```
> **Why you must SUBTRACT 4: 19 is ONE LESS than 20.**
>
> **By working out 4 × 20 you counted TWENTY lots of 4, when only NINETEEN were wanted** — so you have one extra lot of 4 to give back.
>
> **The correction is always ONE LOT OF THE OTHER NUMBER** when you are one away from the round number.

---

Find 14 × 21 using the nearest multiple, and explain why the correction goes the other way.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 14 × 21 = 14 × 20 + 14
>         = 280 + 14
>         = 294
> ```
> **Here you must ADD 14, because 21 is ONE MORE than 20.**
>
> **Working out 14 × 20 gave only TWENTY lots when TWENTY-ONE were wanted** — so one lot of 14 is still missing.
>
> **The direction rule, and the one real danger of this method:**
> ```
> rounded UP   → you counted too many → SUBTRACT
> rounded DOWN → you counted too few  → ADD
> ```
> **Getting this backwards gives an answer that is wrong by exactly twice the correction** — so it is worth pausing to check each time.

Find 12 × 35 by splitting both numbers into their place values.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 35 = 30 + 5          12 = 10 + 2
>
>        ×    |   30      5
>       ------|-------------
>        10   |  300     50
>         2   |   60     10
>       ------|-------------
>             |  360  +  60   =  420
> ```
> **12 × 35 = 420.**
>
> **Why the splitting is allowed: 12 LOTS of 35 is the same as 10 LOTS plus 2 LOTS.** You are simply taking the 12 in two convenient pieces.
>
> **And the 35 can be split too — into 30 and 5 — which is why FOUR small products appear** rather than two.

Write out 12 × 35 as a column sum showing all four products, then as a shorter version with two.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ALL FOUR PARTS               GROUPED TWO AT A TIME
>
>       35  (30 + 5)                 35
>    ×  12  (10 + 2)              ×  12
>    --------------               ----------
>       10     (2 × 5)               70    (2 × 35)
>       60     (2 × 30)             350   (10 × 35)
>       50    (10 × 5)             ----------
>      300   (10 × 30)              420
>    --------------
>      420
> ```
> **They are THE SAME CALCULATION, written out in more or fewer steps.**
>
> **The short version simply does 2 × 35 in one go instead of 2 × 5 and 2 × 30 separately** — 10 + 60 = 70, the same number.
>
> **The short column method you may already know goes further still and hides the parts entirely.** Seeing them written out is what makes it understandable rather than magic.

Find 32 × 8 by splitting.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 32 = 30 + 2
>
>    8 × 30 = 240
>    8 ×  2 =  16
>    ---------------
>             256
> ```
> **32 × 8 = 256.**
>
> **A check with a different method — the nearest multiple:**
> ```
> 32 × 8 ≈ 30 × 8 = 240,  plus 2 × 8 = 16  →  256   ✔
> ```
> **When two different methods agree, you can trust the answer.** That is a better check than doing the same method twice.

Find 69 × 45 by splitting both numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 69 = 60 + 9          45 = 40 + 5
>
>        ×    |   60      9
>       ------|--------------
>        40   | 2,400    360
>         5   |   300     45
>       ------|--------------
>             | 2,700  + 405  =  3,105
> ```
> **69 × 45 = 3,105.**
>
> **A check by the nearest multiple: 70 × 45 = 3,150, then take away one lot of 45**
> ```
> 3,150 − 45 = 3,105    ✔
> ```
> **The second method is quicker here, because 69 is so close to 70** — another reminder to look at the numbers before choosing a route.

Gollappa has 29 cows, each producing 5 litres of milk a day. How much milk in a day?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 29 × 5
>
> by the nearest multiple:  30 × 5 = 150, then take away one lot of 5
>                           150 − 5 = 145
> ```
> **145 litres a day.**
>
> **Why the nearest multiple suits this one perfectly: 29 is ONE LESS than 30**, and 30 × 5 is a fact you can see at once.
>
> **Halving and doubling would also work here:** 29 does not halve neatly, but you could do 29 × 5 → the same 145 by splitting into 20 × 5 + 9 × 5 = 100 + 45.

Maska Cow Farm has 297 cows, each needing 18 kg of fodder a day. How much fodder is needed daily?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 297 × 18
>
> by the nearest multiple, working on the 18:
>    297 × 20 = 5,940
>    297 ×  2 =   594
>    5,940 − 594 = 5,346
> ```
> **5,346 kg of fodder a day.**
>
> **Why round the 18 rather than the 297: 20 is a much friendlier multiplier than 18**, and 297 × 20 is just 297 × 2 with a zero after it.
>
> **The correction here is TWO lots, not one, because 18 is TWO away from 20** — so you subtract 2 × 297.
>
> **Choosing WHICH number to round is part of the skill.**

Find 123 × 84 by splitting.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 84 = 80 + 4
>
>    123 × 80 = 9,840
>    123 ×  4 =   492
>    -----------------
>              10,332
> ```
> **123 × 84 = 10,332.**
>
> **A sense-check before trusting it: 123 is about 120 and 84 is about 80, so the answer should be near 120 × 80 = 9,600.**
>
> **Our answer, 10,332, is comfortably in that region** ✔ — whereas a slip that gave 1,033 or 103,320 would be caught at once.

A family produces 35 kg of kitchen waste a month. How much in a year?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 12 months × 35 kg
>
>    10 × 35 = 350
>     2 × 35 =  70
>    ------------
>              420
> ```
> **420 kg of kitchen waste in a year.**
>
> **Why splitting the 12 into 10 + 2 is the natural move: multiplying by 10 is instant**, and 2 × 35 is a fact you know.
>
> **The alternative — 12 × 35 by halving and doubling — gives 6 × 70 = 420** ✔, which is even quicker if you spot it.

The family composts that waste and gets 150 kg of compost a year. Compost sells at ₹24 per kg. How much do they save?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 150 × 24
>
>        ×    |   100     50
>       ------|--------------
>        20   | 2,000  1,000
>         4   |   400    200
>       ------|--------------
>             | 2,400 +1,200  = 3,600
> ```
> **They save ₹3,600 a year.**
>
> **A quicker route if you spot it — halving and doubling:**
> ```
> 150 × 24  →  300 × 12  →  600 × 6 = 3,600     ✔
> ```
> **Three easy doublings and halvings replace four multiplications.**

Place the digits 2, 5 and 3 to make a product as close to 100 as possible.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 53 × 2 = 106      only 6 away    ← CLOSEST
> 23 × 5 = 115      15 away
> 35 × 2 =  70      30 away
> 25 × 3 =  75      25 away
> 32 × 5 = 160      60 away
> 52 × 3 = 156      56 away
> ```
> **The best is 53 × 2 = 106.**
>
> **How to search sensibly rather than trying everything: a two-digit number times a one-digit number reaches 100 only if the pair is roughly right.**
>
> **Pairing the LARGEST two-digit number (53) with the SMALLEST single digit (2)** keeps the product from overshooting — and it lands just past 100.

List four different ways to arrange 60 buttermilk pouches in equal rows.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>  2 × 30 = 60
>  3 × 20 = 60
>  4 × 15 = 60
>  5 × 12 = 60
>  6 × 10 = 60
> ```
> **Each pair is a way of splitting 60 into equal rows — and each can be turned round as well** (30 × 2, 20 × 3 and so on), since order does not matter.
>
> **What the pairs really are: the FACTORS of 60**, taken two at a time.
>
> **How to be sure you have them all: work upwards from 1 and stop when the two numbers cross over.** After 6 × 10 comes 10 × 6, which is one you already have.

Why is there no single "correct" method for multiplying?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because every method is the same multiplication rearranged, so they all give the same answer — and DIFFERENT NUMBERS SUIT DIFFERENT REARRANGEMENTS.**
> ```
> 22 × 5   → halving and doubling is easiest    (5 becomes 10)
> 4 × 19   → the nearest multiple is easiest    (19 is next to 20)
> 123 × 84 → splitting by place value is easiest (no shortcut fits)
> ```
> **The real skill is not knowing one procedure but LOOKING AT THE NUMBERS FIRST** and asking which route they make short.
>
> **And whichever route you take, a second method makes an excellent check** — two different paths reaching the same answer is strong evidence it is right.
