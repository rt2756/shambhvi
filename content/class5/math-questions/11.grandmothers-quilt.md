A square tablecloth has sides of 7 cm on a plan. Find its perimeter, and explain why you may multiply instead of adding four times.

> [!ANSWER]
> **28 cm.**
> ```
> 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 = 28        or        4 × 7 = 28
> ```
> **Why multiplying is allowed: adding the SAME number over and over IS multiplication.** 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 is four sevens.
>
> **So for any shape with all its sides equal:** perimeter = (number of sides) × (one side).
>
> **This is not a new formula to learn** — it is the one you already have, wearing a geometry hat.

---

A regular hexagon has sides of 9 cm. What is its perimeter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **54 cm.**
> ```
> 6 sides × 9 cm = 54 cm
> ```
> **Why "regular" is the word that makes this easy: it tells you all six sides are EQUAL**, so one multiplication replaces six additions.
>
> **If the hexagon were irregular you would have to measure and add all six separately** — no shortcut exists, because there is no repeated number to multiply.

---

Draw three different rectangles with a perimeter of 24 cm. How many are there altogether (whole-number sides)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Six: 1×11, 2×10, 3×9, 4×8, 5×7, 6×6.**
>
> **Why: the border is L + B + L + B, which is TWO copies of (L + B).**
> ```
> perimeter 24  →  L + B = 12
> ```
> **So any two whole numbers adding to 12 will do.**
>
> **Why there are many and not one: the perimeter fixes the TOTAL of L and B, not the two numbers themselves.** That single fact explains this whole chapter's biggest surprise.

Why can't you use circles to measure the area of a table top?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because circles leave gaps between them, however tightly you pack them.**
>
> **Those little curved gaps are part of the table but are counted by nothing.** So "12 circles" leaves out an unknown amount of table.
>
> **A measuring unit must account for EVERY bit of the thing being measured.** That is exactly why the tiling shape has to leave no gaps and no overlaps.
>
> **This is Chapter 7's tessellation idea doing a real job** — a shape that fails to tessellate cannot serve as a unit of area.

---

Triangles tile perfectly well. So why is the SQUARE chosen as the unit of area?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because squares have two advantages nothing else matches.**
>
> **First: all four sides are equal, so it does not matter which way round you lay one down.** A triangle has to be pointed the right way, and half of them upside down, to tile.
>
> **Second — and this is the big one: squares line up in ROWS AND COLUMNS.** That turns counting into multiplying.
>
> **Measuring area in triangles would be perfectly honest** — it just wouldn't hand you the formula Area = Length × Breadth for free.

---

Explain why the area of a 7 cm by 5 cm rectangle is 35 square cm, without simply quoting the formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because 35 unit squares fit inside it, in an array.**
> ```
> lay 1-cm squares along the length  →  7 fit in one row
> the breadth is 5 cm                →  5 such rows stack up
> 5 rows of 7  =  35 squares
> ```
> **Why multiplying two LENGTHS gives an AREA: the length tells you how many squares fit in a row and the breadth tells you how many rows there are.**
>
> **So the formula is not a rule about lengths at all — it is a shortcut for counting squares.**
>
> **It is the same array you met with the coconuts.** Rows × columns, again.

---

Why is area measured in square cm but perimeter in plain cm?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because they are counting different things.**
>
> **Perimeter is a LENGTH — how far you walk round the edge.** Lengths are measured in cm.
>
> **Area is a COUNT OF SQUARES — how many 1-cm squares fill the inside.** Each one is a square centimetre, so the answer is so many square centimetres.
>
> **Writing "area = 35 cm" is not a small slip; it says the wrong kind of thing.** A quick test: if the answer came from multiplying two lengths together, it must be a square unit.

A rectangular field is 42 m long and 34 m wide. Find its area and its perimeter.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> area      = 42 × 34 = 1,428 square m
> perimeter = (2 × 42) + (2 × 34) = 84 + 68 = 152 m
> ```
> **Why the perimeter uses 2 × each: opposite sides of a rectangle are equal**, so there are two 42s and two 34s in the border.
>
> **Sense-check on the area: 42 × 34 is a bit more than 40 × 34 = 1,360, and a bit less than 42 × 35 = 1,470.** 1,428 sits between them ✔
>
> **Note the units carefully — square m for one, m for the other.**

---

A garden's area is 96 square m and its length is 12 m. Find the breadth.

> [!ANSWER]
> **8 m.**
> ```
> 12 × B = 96      →      B = 96 ÷ 12 = 8 m
> ```
> **Why you may run the formula backwards: Area = L × B is a fact family, exactly like N = D × Q.**
>
> **The same relationship can be read three ways** — Area = L × B, L = Area ÷ B, B = Area ÷ L — depending on which number is missing.
>
> **Check: 12 × 8 = 96 ✔** and the perimeter, if you want it, is 2(12 + 8) = 40 m.

---

Find the perimeter and the area of three rectangles: 1 by 12, 2 by 6, and 3 by 4. What do you notice?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>            area          perimeter
> 1 by 12    12 sq cm       26 cm
> 2 by 6     12 sq cm       16 cm
> 3 by 4     12 sq cm       14 cm
> ```
> **All three have the SAME area but very different perimeters.**
>
> **Why they can differ so much: area counts the INSIDE, perimeter measures the EDGE.** They answer different questions, so there is no reason they should move together.
>
> **Picture the 12 squares as 12 children.** In single file almost every child is on the outside — lots of edge. **Huddled in a 3 by 4 block, the middle children are surrounded and contribute no edge at all.**

---

Trisha says "I made my rectangle bigger in area, so its perimeter went up too." Is she always right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — and one counter-example settles it.**
> ```
> a 1 by 12 rectangle : area 12 sq cm, perimeter 26 cm
> a 4 by 4 square     : area 16 sq cm, perimeter 16 cm
> ```
> **The square has MORE area and LESS perimeter.**
>
> **Why one example is enough: her claim was an "always" claim.** A single case where it fails proves it is not always true — you never have to check any others.
>
> **This is the always/sometimes/never test from Chapter 9 doing real work.** Her statement is *sometimes* true, which is a completely different thing.

---

Find the areas of all the rectangles with perimeter 20 cm. Which has the biggest area?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The 5 by 5 square, with area 25 square cm.**
> ```
> L + B = 10
> 1 by 9  →  9 sq cm        4 by 6  →  24 sq cm
> 2 by 8  → 16 sq cm        5 by 5  →  25 sq cm   ← biggest
> 3 by 7  →  21 sq cm
> ```
> **Why the squarest shape wins: a long thin rectangle is nearly all edge** — every square inside it touches the outside.
>
> **Squashing it towards a square tucks more squares into the MIDDLE, where they add area but no edge.**
>
> **This is why a farmer fencing a field wants it square-ish** — the same length of fence encloses far more land.

---

Two rooms have the same floor area, but one needs much more skirting board round the walls. How is that possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because skirting board measures the PERIMETER, and equal areas can have very different perimeters.**
> ```
> a 6 m by 6 m room : area 36 sq m, perimeter 24 m
> a 2 m by 18 m room: area 36 sq m, perimeter 40 m
> ```
> **The long thin room needs two-thirds more skirting for exactly the same floor space.**
>
> **Why: the long room is nearly all wall.** Its floor squares almost all touch an edge.
>
> **Practical moral: floor area tells you about carpet; perimeter tells you about skirting, fencing and lace.** Ask which one the question is really about.

---

Four unit squares are arranged (a) in a 2 by 2 block and (b) in a straight line. Find the area and perimeter of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (a) 2 by 2 block : area 4 sq units, perimeter 8 units
> (b) 1 by 4 line  : area 4 sq units, perimeter 10 units
> ```
> **Why the areas match: four squares are four squares, however you arrange them.** Moving a square changes where it is, not how much surface there is.
>
> **Why the perimeters differ: in the block, each square hides two of its edges against neighbours; in the line, the two end squares hide only one each.**
>
> **Count the EXPOSED edges — the little edges with nothing next to them — and you have the perimeter directly.**

On a grid, a shape is made of 5 unit squares in an L: three in a row, with two more stacked above the left-hand one. Find its area and perimeter.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Area 5 square units; perimeter 12 units.**
> ```
> ■
> ■
> ■ ■ ■
> ```
> **Area: just count the squares — 5.**
>
> **Perimeter: walk round the outside** — up 3, right 1, down 2, right 2, down 1, left 3. That is 3+1+2+2+1+3 = 12.
>
> **Why walking round is safer than adding pieces: the edges where two squares touch are inside the shape and belong to no border at all.** Counting exposed edges only, you get the same 12 ✔

An L-shape is made of one rectangle 10 cm by 4 cm joined to another 5 cm by 6 cm. Find its area. Can you find its perimeter by adding the two perimeters?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Area 70 square cm. And NO — you cannot add the perimeters.**
> ```
> area:  (10 × 4) + (5 × 6) = 40 + 30 = 70 square cm
> ```
> **Why areas ADD: the squares in one piece and the squares in the other are all different squares.** None is counted twice, none is missed.
>
> **Why perimeters DO NOT add: the join between the two pieces is an edge of each piece, but it is NOT part of the finished shape's border.** Adding the two perimeters counts that inside join twice when it should be counted zero times.
>
> **For perimeter, always walk round the OUTSIDE of the finished shape.**

---

A rectangle of paper 8 cm by 3 cm is cut into pieces and rearranged into a new shape. Has the area changed? Has the perimeter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The area is unchanged (24 square cm). The perimeter almost certainly HAS changed.**
>
> **Why the area cannot change: the same paper is still there.** Cutting and moving does not create or destroy surface — the same 24 unit squares are simply in new places.
>
> **Why the perimeter can change: cutting creates NEW edges, and rearranging decides which of them end up on the outside.**
>
> **This is a good way to remember which quantity is the sturdy one.** Area survives being rearranged; perimeter does not.

---

Grandmother traces her hand on a 1-cm grid and counts 38 whole squares plus about 12 half-squares. What is the approximate area of her hand?

> [!ANSWER]
> **About 44 square cm.**
> ```
> 38 whole squares                    = 38
> 12 half-squares, paired up = 6 more =  6
> total                               ≈ 44 square cm
> ```
> **Why "about" is the honest word: a hand has curved edges, and no whole number of little squares fits a curve exactly.**
>
> **Two people tracing the same hand will get slightly different answers, and neither is wrong.**
>
> **A finer grid gives a closer answer** — half-centimetre squares would trap the curve more tightly. **Saying "about 44" is more truthful than pretending it is exactly 44.**

---

A square and a rectangle both have a perimeter of 36 cm. The rectangle is 12 cm long. Which encloses more area, and by how much?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The square, by 9 square cm.**
> ```
> square    : side 36 ÷ 4 = 9 cm   →   area 9 × 9 = 81 sq cm
> rectangle : L + B = 18, L = 12, so B = 6   →   area 12 × 6 = 72 sq cm
> difference: 81 − 72 = 9 square cm
> ```
> **Why the square wins: for a FIXED perimeter, the squarest shape always has the biggest area.**
>
> **The rectangle is stretched, so more of its squares sit against an edge and fewer are tucked in the middle.**
>
> **Notice you were given the perimeter, not the sides — so the first job in each case was to work out the sides.** Reading which quantity you have been handed is half of every area question.

A path of square tiles runs round the edge of a 5 by 5 square courtyard, one tile wide. How many tiles are in the path?

> [!ANSWER]
> **16 tiles.**
> ```
> the whole 5 by 5 square      = 25 tiles
> the inner 3 by 3 square      =  9 tiles
> the border                   = 25 − 9 = 16 tiles
> ```
> **Why subtracting is safer than counting round: counting round the edge tempts you to count each CORNER twice.**
>
> **Four sides of 5 gives 20 — wrong, because the four corner tiles have each been counted in two sides.** 20 − 4 = 16 ✔ — the same answer, once the double-count is repaired.
>
> **Two independent routes agreeing is the strongest check there is.**

Which needs more lace: the border of a 10 cm by 2 cm rectangle, or the border of a 5 cm by 4 cm rectangle? Which holds more patchwork?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The long thin one needs more lace; both hold the same patchwork.**
> ```
> 10 by 2 : perimeter 24 cm, area 20 sq cm
> 5 by 4  : perimeter 18 cm, area 20 sq cm
> ```
> **Why: equal areas, unequal perimeters — the shapes hold the same amount of cloth but one has far more edge.**
>
> **Grandmother should choose the 5 by 4 if lace is expensive**, and it makes no difference at all to how much old cloth she uses up.
>
> **This is exactly the trade-off the chapter is about**, met in the situation it started from.

Draw two DIFFERENT shapes (not both rectangles) that each have an area of 6 square units on a grid. Do they have the same perimeter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Almost certainly not.**
> ```
> a 2 by 3 rectangle        →  area 6, perimeter 10
> six squares in a line     →  area 6, perimeter 14
> an L: 4 in a row + 2 above one end → area 6, perimeter 12
> ```
> **Why the perimeter keeps changing while the area stays put: every time two squares are placed side by side, TWO edges get hidden inside the shape.**
>
> **A compact arrangement hides many edges; a straggly one hides few.**
>
> **The area only counts squares, and there are always six.** Nothing you do to their arrangement can change that number.

In the tile game, a player has a figure with perimeter 10 and adds one more square. Can the perimeter go DOWN?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — if the new square is tucked into a notch where it touches two or three existing squares.**
> ```
> adding a square that touches on 1 side  → perimeter +2
> adding a square that touches on 2 sides → perimeter unchanged
> adding a square that touches on 3 sides → perimeter −2
> ```
> **Why: the new square brings 4 edges, but every side where it touches a neighbour hides TWO edges — one of its own and one of the neighbour's.**
> ```
> 4 new edges − 2 hidden per touch
> ```
> **So area always goes up when you add a tile, but perimeter can go up, stay put, or come down.** **That single fact makes this a game rather than a race.**

---

Prove that "a bigger perimeter means a bigger area" is false.

> [!ANSWER]
> **One counter-example is enough.**
> ```
> 1 by 8 rectangle : perimeter 18 cm, area  8 sq cm
> 4 by 4 square    : perimeter 16 cm, area 16 sq cm
> ```
> **The first has the BIGGER perimeter and the SMALLER area.**
>
> **Why a single example finishes the job: the claim says "always".** To break an "always" you need only one case where it fails — you do not have to say anything about the others.
>
> **Note that the claim is not silly — it is often true.** It is *sometimes* true, and the whole point of this chapter is that "sometimes" and "always" are worlds apart.

A wall 4 m long and 3 m high is to be covered with square tiles of side 50 cm. How many tiles are needed?

> [!ANSWER]
> **48 tiles.**
> ```
> work in centimetres:  400 cm by 300 cm
> tiles along the length : 400 ÷ 50 = 8
> tiles up the height    : 300 ÷ 50 = 6
> total                  : 8 × 6 = 48
> ```
> **Why converting to one unit first was essential: the wall is in metres and the tile in centimetres**, and dividing a metre by a centimetre would be meaningless — the same rule as Chapter 8's comparisons.
>
> **Why counting rows and columns beats dividing the areas here:** it also tells you the tiles fit **exactly**, with none needing to be cut. **Area ÷ area would give 48 too, but would not warn you if the tiles didn't fit neatly.**
