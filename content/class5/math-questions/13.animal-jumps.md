Draw all the arrays possible for 18 counters, and list the factors of 18.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Factors: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18.**
> ```
> 1 × 18       2 × 9       3 × 6
> ```
> **Why each array gives two factors: an array of 3 rows of 6 says 3 × 6 = 18**, so both 3 and 6 divide 18 exactly, with nothing left over. That is precisely what "factor" means.
>
> **And why 4, 5, 7 and 8 are missing: try 4 rows of 18 counters and the last row comes up short.** The leftovers are the remainder.
>
> **"Make a rectangle" and "divides exactly" are the same test**, done with counters instead of arithmetic.

---

Find all the factors of 36 without missing any. How do you know when to stop?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.**
> ```
> 1 × 36      2 × 18      3 × 12      4 × 9      6 × 6
> ```
> **Hunt in PAIRS, because that is how factors come.** 1 pairs with 36, 2 with 18, and so on.
>
> **You stop when the pair meets in the middle — here at 6 × 6.** After that, trying 7 would need a partner smaller than 7, and every such partner has already been checked.
>
> **This is why pairing is better than guessing: it tells you when you are FINISHED.** Guessing never does.

---

Why does every number have at least two factors?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because 1 × N = N is true for every number N.**
>
> **That single array — one long row of N counters — always exists**, so 1 and N are always factors.
>
> **So every number starts with two factors before you look at all.** The interesting question is whether it has any *others*.
>
> **The one exception worth noticing: 1 itself.** Its two "factors" are 1 and 1 — the same number — so it really has only one factor, which is why 1 is not counted as prime.

---

Explain, using counters, why 23 is prime but 24 is not.

> [!ANSWER]
> **23 counters can only be laid out as one long line of 23; 24 can be laid out several ways.**
> ```
> 23 → 1 × 23 only
> 24 → 1 × 24, 2 × 12, 3 × 8, 4 × 6
> ```
> **Why 23 refuses: try 2 rows and one row is longer; try 3, 4, 5 … and every attempt leaves a ragged edge.** There is no pair of whole numbers whose product is 23 except 1 and 23.
>
> **A prime is a number that refuses to make a proper rectangle.**
>
> **That is not a definition to memorise — it is a thing you can see on the table in front of you.**

---

A rabbit jumps 6 at a time from 0. Can it land on 45? On 48?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not on 45; yes on 48.**
> ```
> after n jumps the rabbit is at 6 × n
> 48 = 6 × 8 ✔        45 ÷ 6 = 7 remainder 3 ✘  (it jumps over 45)
> ```
> **Why the jumper can only reach multiples of 6: after n jumps it has travelled 6 + 6 + … + 6, which is 6 × n.** There is no way to be anywhere else.
>
> **So "can the rabbit reach 48?", "is 48 a multiple of 6?" and "is 6 a factor of 48?" are the SAME question asked three ways.**

---

A frog jumps 4 and a spider jumps 6, both from 0. Where do they first meet, and where after that?

> [!ANSWER]
> **First at 12, then at 24, 36, 48 …**
> ```
> frog   : 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24 …
> spider : 6, 12, 18, 24 …
> ```
> **Why the meetings repeat every 12: when they meet at 12, they are both standing on a landing spot with the whole road ahead** — exactly the situation they were in at 0.
>
> **So whatever happened between 0 and 12 must happen again between 12 and 24, and again after that.**
>
> **This is why you only ever need the FIRST common multiple.** Every other one is a multiple of it.

---

Is the first common multiple of two numbers always found by multiplying them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — only when the two numbers share no factor bigger than 1.**
> ```
> 3 and 5 : 3 × 5 = 15, and they DO first meet at 15 ✔
> 4 and 6 : 4 × 6 = 24, but they first meet at 12
> 6 and 8 : 6 × 8 = 48, but they first meet at 24
> ```
> **Why multiplying always gives *a* common multiple: 4 × 6 contains a whole number of 4s and a whole number of 6s.**
>
> **But when the two numbers share a factor, they meet sooner** — 4 and 6 both contain a 2, so half of 24 is already enough.
>
> **Multiplying is a safe way to find a common multiple. Listing is the way to find the FIRST one.**

---

A spider jumps 5 and a grasshopper jumps 10. Where do they meet?

> [!ANSWER]
> **At 10, 20, 30, 40 … — every landing of the grasshopper.**
>
> **Why: 10 is a multiple of 5, so the spider simply takes TWO hops wherever the grasshopper takes one.**
>
> **Everywhere the grasshopper lands, the spider is already landing too.**
>
> **The general rule: if the bigger jump is a multiple of the smaller, the first common multiple is the bigger number itself.** No listing needed.

---

Sher Khan hunts every 3rd day and Bagheera every 5th day, both starting on day 0. When do they hunt together?

> [!ANSWER]
> **On days 15, 30, 45, 60 …**
> ```
> Sher Khan : 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 …
> Bagheera  : 5, 10, 15, 20 …
> ```
> **Why 15 and not something smaller: 3 and 5 share no factor except 1**, so nothing before their product can contain a whole number of both.
>
> **Why the meetings then repeat every 15 days: after day 15 both are back at a starting position**, and the pattern begins again.
>
> **A calendar version of the number line** — the mathematics is identical.

---

Find all the common factors of 30 and 45.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1, 3, 5 and 15.**
> ```
> 30 : 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30
> 45 : 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 45
> common : 1, 3, 5, 15
> ```
> **Why 2 fails: it divides 30 but not 45 (45 is odd).** A common factor has to serve BOTH numbers.
>
> **Notice the common factors are exactly the factors of 15**, the biggest of them. **That is not a coincidence** — every common factor divides the biggest common factor, in the same way every common multiple is a multiple of the smallest one.

---

What are the common factors of 8 and 9? What does your answer tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only 1.**
> ```
> 8 : 1, 2, 4, 8          9 : 1, 3, 9
> ```
> **They share nothing but 1** — and 1 is shared by every pair, since it divides everything.
>
> **Why this had to happen: 8 and 9 are CONSECUTIVE.** Any number bigger than 1 that divided both would have to land on 8 and on 9 — but those are only one step apart, and a stride bigger than 1 cannot land twice within a single step.
>
> **The argument works for any two neighbours** — 14 and 15, 99 and 100. **It is a proof, not a pattern noticed in examples.**

---

Mowgli wants to reach Baloo at 30 but must avoid Sher Khan's house at 25. What jump sizes can he use?

> [!ANSWER]
> **2, 3, 6, 10, 15 or 30.**
> ```
> reaches 30 : 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30    (the factors of 30)
> reaches 25 : 1, 5, 25                      (the factors of 25)
> must avoid : 1 and 5   (they land on BOTH)
> safe       : 2, 3, 6, 10, 15, 30
> ```
> **Why a jump of 1 is banned: it lands on every number, including 25.**
>
> **Why a jump of 5 is banned: 5 divides 25, so he would touch down right at the tiger's door.**
>
> **"Which jump reaches this but not that?" is always a factor question in disguise.**

---

What jump size lets Mowgli meet Kaa at 21 and Akela at 35?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A stride of 7 (or 1, but that visits everybody).**
> ```
> 21 : 1, 3, 7, 21          35 : 1, 5, 7, 35
> common : 1, 7
> ```
> **Why it must be a COMMON factor: the jump has to divide 21 exactly, so he lands on Kaa, AND divide 35 exactly, so he lands on Akela.**
>
> **Only a number in both lists can do both jobs.**
>
> **7 is the useful answer** — 1 works but is no answer at all, since a jumper of 1 visits every single house.

---

Which of 10, 16, 27, 36, 48 can be reached by jumps of 4 from 0?

> [!ANSWER]
> **16, 36 and 48.**
> ```
> 16 = 4 × 4 ✔     36 = 4 × 9 ✔     48 = 4 × 12 ✔
> 10 ÷ 4 = 2 r 2 ✘   (the jumper goes 8 then 12, straight over 10)
> 27 ÷ 4 = 6 r 3 ✘
> ```
> **Why the remainder tells you instantly: a remainder means the jumper overshoots.** It lands just before and just after, but never on.
>
> **A zero remainder means it lands exactly.**
>
> **So the whole question is "does 4 divide it?" — asked five times.**

---

Sort these into "divisible by 2 only", "by 5 only", and "by 2, 5 and 10": 44, 65, 70, 25, 92, 80.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> by 2 only        : 44  92
> by 5 only        : 65  25
> by 2, 5 and 10   : 70  80
> ```
> **Why you only need to look at the LAST DIGIT: every number splits into "a whole lot of tens" plus its ones digit** — 92 = 90 + 2, 65 = 60 + 5.
>
> **And 10 is divisible by both 2 and 5, so every ten is already divisible by 2 and 5.** The tens part can never spoil things.
>
> **Everything therefore hangs on the leftover ones digit** — which is why the rules feel like magic and are nothing of the kind.

---

Why is "divisible by 10 but not by 2 or 5" an impossible category?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because 10 = 2 × 5.**
>
> **A number made of whole tens is made of whole 2s and whole 5s at the same time.** 70 is seven tens, which is fourteen 2s and also fourteen 5s.
>
> **So anything divisible by 10 is automatically divisible by both 2 and 5.** There is nothing to put in that box.
>
> **Finding that a box must stay EMPTY is a real result, not a failure to fill it in.** It tells you something true about every number there will ever be.

---

Is every number divisible by 2 also divisible by 10? Give a reason.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
> ```
> 34 is divisible by 2 (34 = 2 × 17) but not by 10 (34 ÷ 10 = 3 r 4)
> ```
> **Why the implication only runs one way: 10 contains a 2, so every ten carries a 2 with it — but a 2 does not carry a 10.**
>
> **A number needs BOTH a 2 and a 5 to be divisible by 10**, and 34 has no 5 in it.
>
> **The general shape of this: if A is a factor of B, then everything divisible by B is divisible by A — but not the reverse.** Bigger factors are harder to satisfy.

---

True or false: "Every factor of an even number is even." Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
> ```
> 3 is a factor of 6, and 3 is odd
> 5 is a factor of 20, and 5 is odd
> ```
> **Why an even number can have odd factors: 6 = 2 × 3.** The 2 makes the number even all by itself; the 3 is free to be odd.
>
> **Every even number has at least one odd factor, namely 1.**
>
> **One counter-example is enough to destroy an "every" claim** — you do not have to say anything about the other cases.

---

True or false: "An odd number can never have an even factor." Prove your answer.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.**
>
> **Suppose it could — suppose some even number were a factor of an odd number N.**
>
> **Then N would be that even number multiplied by something. But an even number times ANYTHING is even**, because it is made of 2s and so is the answer.
>
> **So N would have to be even — contradicting the fact that it is odd.** The assumption is impossible, so no even factor can exist.
>
> **Notice the shape of this argument: assume it happened, and show something impossible follows.** It is one of the most powerful moves in mathematics, and you have just used it.

---

True or false: "Multiples of odd numbers are always odd."

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
> ```
> 6 is a multiple of 3, and 6 is even
> 20 is a multiple of 5, and 20 is even
> ```
> **Why: multiplying an odd number by an EVEN number gives an even answer.** 3 × 2 = 6.
>
> **The pattern is worth knowing:** an odd number's multiples alternate — odd, even, odd, even — as you multiply by 1, 2, 3, 4 …
>
> **Compare with the previous question and notice the asymmetry.** Factors of an odd number must all be odd; multiples of an odd number need not be.

---

Show that the product of any two numbers is always a common multiple of them.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Take 7 and 9. Their product is 63.**
> ```
> 63 = 7 × 9   so 63 is a multiple of 7
> 63 = 9 × 7   so 63 is a multiple of 9
> ```
> **Why this always works: the product literally CONTAINS both numbers as factors** — that is what writing it as 7 × 9 means.
>
> **So it qualifies as a multiple of each, no matter what the two numbers are.**
>
> **This gives you a common multiple for any pair, instantly.** It just may not be the smallest one — 4 and 6 give 24, but meet first at 12.

Two bells ring together at 6 a.m. One rings every 12 minutes, the other every 18. When do they next ring together?

> [!ANSWER]
> **At 6:36 a.m.**
> ```
> 12 : 12, 24, 36, 48 …
> 18 : 18, 36, 54 …
> first common multiple = 36 minutes
> ```
> **Why not 12 × 18 = 216 minutes: 12 and 18 share the factor 6**, so they meet long before their product.
>
> **After 6:36 they ring together every 36 minutes** — at 7:12, 7:48, and so on — because the situation at 6:36 is identical to the one at 6:00.
>
> **A real-world common-multiple question**, and the same reasoning as two animals jumping.

---

A box of sweets can be shared exactly among 4 children or exactly among 6 children, with none left over. What is the smallest possible number of sweets?

> [!ANSWER]
> **12.**
> ```
> shares among 4 → the number is a multiple of 4 : 4, 8, 12, 16 …
> shares among 6 → a multiple of 6              : 6, 12, 18 …
> smallest in both lists = 12
> ```
> **Why "shares exactly" means "is a multiple of": no remainder is left, which is precisely the definition.**
>
> **Why not 24: 24 works too, but the question asked for the SMALLEST** — and every other answer (24, 36, 48) is a multiple of 12.
>
> **Once you have the first common multiple, you have described every possible answer.**

---

Ribbon is to be cut into equal pieces with none left over. One length is 24 cm and another is 40 cm. What are the possible piece lengths?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1, 2, 4 or 8 cm.**
> ```
> 24 : 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24
> 40 : 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 20, 40
> common : 1, 2, 4, 8
> ```
> **Why this is a COMMON FACTOR question and the bell question was a common multiple one:** here the piece length must **divide** both ribbons; there the ringing time had to be a **multiple** of both gaps.
>
> **Ask yourself: does the answer go INTO the given numbers, or do the given numbers go into the answer?** That single question tells you which tool to reach for.
>
> **The longest useful piece is 8 cm** — giving 3 pieces from one ribbon and 5 from the other.

---

Which numbers below 30 have exactly three factors?

> [!ANSWER]
> **4, 9 and 25.**
> ```
> 4  : 1, 2, 4          9  : 1, 3, 9          25 : 1, 5, 25
> ```
> **Why exactly three and not an even number: factors normally come in PAIRS**, so most numbers have an even count.
>
> **These three break the pattern because their middle pair is a number times ITSELF** — 2 × 2, 3 × 3, 5 × 5 — so the pair collapses into one factor.
>
> **And why only these: the square must be of a PRIME.** 36 = 6 × 6 but has nine factors, because 6 itself splits further. **4, 9 and 25 are the squares of 2, 3 and 5 — the primes small enough to fit.**

---

A number leaves no remainder when divided by 2, 5 and 10. What must its last digit be?

> [!ANSWER]
> **0.**
>
> **Why: divisibility by 10 alone already forces the last digit to be 0**, and every such number is automatically divisible by 2 and 5 as well, since 10 = 2 × 5.
>
> **So the three conditions are not really three conditions.** The 10 does all the work and the other two come free.
>
> **A useful habit: before answering a question with several conditions, check whether some of them are already implied by the others.** Often only one is doing anything.
