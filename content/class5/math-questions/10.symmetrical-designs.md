You want a paper cutout of the letter M. Where should you fold, and why does the trick work at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fold once, vertically, then draw half the M with its straight edge on the fold.**
>
> **Why the trick works: the scissors cut through BOTH layers at once**, so the two halves are not carefully drawn to match — **they ARE the same cut.**
>
> **No skill is involved; the paper does the matching.**
>
> **And the crease becomes the line of symmetry.** Fold the finished M back along it and the halves land exactly on each other — which is precisely what "reflection symmetry" means.

---

Sunil tries to cut the letter A using a quarter fold (two folds). What does he get, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **He gets a strange four-armed shape, not an A.**
>
> **Why: two folds make FOUR layers, so the scissors produce four identical pieces** — the cutout is forced to have both a vertical AND a horizontal line of symmetry.
>
> **But A only has a vertical line.** Its top is a point and its bottom is two legs; those cannot be mirror images of each other.
>
> **The rule: use as many folds as the letter has lines of symmetry, no more.** One line → one fold. Two lines → two folds.

---

Sort these letters by their lines of symmetry: E, T, X, K, V, O, N.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> vertical only    :  T   V
> horizontal only  :  E   K
> both             :  X   O
> neither          :  N
> ```
> **Why E is horizontal and not vertical: fold E left-to-right and the three arms all stick out on one side only.** Fold it top-to-bottom and the top arm lands on the bottom arm ✔
>
> **X and O can be made with a quarter fold; T, V, E and K need a half fold; N must be drawn in full.**

---

N looks perfectly balanced. Explain carefully why it has no line of symmetry.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the diagonal stroke goes the wrong way when you fold it.**
>
> **Fold N down the middle: the left half's slash runs from top-left downwards; the right half needs one running from top-RIGHT downwards.** They point in opposite directions and do not land on top of each other.
>
> **Fold it across instead and the same thing happens.**
>
> **The test is always the fold, never the feeling.** N *feels* balanced because it does have symmetry — but of the turning kind, not the folding kind. **That is exactly why it deceives.**

---

Which of these have a line of symmetry: the letters S, W, Z, Y?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> W  — vertical line ✔
> Y  — vertical line ✔
> S  — none
> Z  — none
> ```
> **Why S and Z fail for the same reason N does: both are built from strokes that reverse direction.** A mirror turns the top curve of S the wrong way round.
>
> **But watch what happens if you TURN them instead** — S and Z both look the same after a half turn. **They are in the same family as N: rotational symmetry without reflection symmetry.**
>
> **W and Y are the opposite family** — they fold but do not turn.

---

Why doesn't "it looks the same after a full turn" count as rotational symmetry?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because EVERY shape in the world looks the same after a full turn.**
>
> **A full turn puts the shape back exactly where it started** — of course it matches. Nothing has been tested.
>
> **A property that everything possesses cannot tell things apart**, which is the whole job of a property.
>
> **So rotational symmetry means matching after turning LESS than a full circle.** It is the same reasoning as "every number is divisible by 1" — true, and useless for sorting numbers.

---

A firki looks the same after a quarter turn. Show that it must therefore also look the same after a half turn and a three-quarter turn.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a quarter turn is a move that already works, and you may simply do it again.**
> ```
> ¼ turn works
> ¼ then ¼   = ½ turn      — works, it is the same move twice
> ¼ ¼ ¼      = ¾ turn      — works
> ¼ ¼ ¼ ¼    = full turn   — back to the start
> ```
> **Why this is a proof and not an observation: nothing new is being assumed at any step.** Each turn only repeats a move that succeeded.
>
> **Four quarter turns fill the circle, so there are exactly FOUR matching positions — the firki has order 4.**
>
> **This is why you only ever need to find the SMALLEST turn that works.** Everything else follows for free.

---

A design looks the same after a one-third turn. What is its order, and where else does it match?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Order 3 — it also matches at a two-thirds turn.**
> ```
> ⅓ works  →  ⅔ works (do it twice)  →  full turn (back to start)
> three equal turns fill the circle → 3 matching positions
> ```
> **Why the order equals the bottom number of the fraction: 1/n of a turn repeated n times is one whole turn**, and every step along the way is a matching position.
>
> **So "smallest turn = 1/n" and "order n" are two ways of saying the same thing.**
>
> **A shape with order 3 will never match at a quarter turn** — quarters and thirds do not line up.

---

Give the order of rotational symmetry of: a square, an equilateral triangle, a regular hexagon, and the letter A.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> square              — order 4   (matches at ¼, ½, ¾)
> equilateral triangle— order 3   (matches at ⅓, ⅔)
> regular hexagon     — order 6   (matches every ⅙ turn)
> letter A            — order 1   (matches nowhere before the full turn)
> ```
> **Why a regular shape's order equals its number of sides: turning it by one side's worth of angle moves each corner onto the next corner's place.** Everything looks unchanged because all the sides and corners are identical.
>
> **Order 1 is the polite way of saying "no rotational symmetry"** — the only turn that works is the full turn every shape manages.

---

Give one shape with reflection symmetry only, one with rotational only, one with both, and one with neither.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> reflection only  :  letter A  ·  letter V  ·  an isosceles triangle
> rotational only  :  letter N  ·  letter S  ·  letter Z
> both             :  letter H  ·  letter X  ·  a square
> neither          :  letter F  ·  letter R  ·  letter P
> ```
> **Why this matters: all four boxes can be filled, which PROVES the two symmetries are independent.**
>
> **Neither one implies the other.** If reflection always brought rotation with it, the "reflection only" box would be impossible to fill — and A fills it easily.
>
> **Reflection asks "can I fold it onto itself?"; rotation asks "can I turn it onto itself?"** Two different questions, two independent answers.

---

Which digits have reflection symmetry, which have rotational symmetry, and which have both?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> REFLECTION   0  8  (vertical and horizontal)     3  (horizontal only)
>              1  (vertical, if drawn as a plain stroke)
> ROTATIONAL   0  8  (half turn)    1 (plain stroke)
> BOTH         0  8  (and the plain-stroke 1)
> NEITHER      2  4  5  6  7  9
> ```
> **Why 3 folds only horizontally: its two bumps sit one above the other**, so a horizontal crease lands them on each other. A vertical crease leaves both bumps on the same side.
>
> **Important honesty: the answer depends on HOW the digit is written.** A 1 as a bare stroke has both symmetries; a 1 with a flag and a base has neither.
>
> **Symmetry belongs to the shape on the page, not to the number it stands for.**

---

Does the number 609 have rotational symmetry? Does it have reflection symmetry?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Rotational: YES (half turn). Reflection: NO.**
> ```
> turn 609 upside down:
>    the 6 lands where the 9 was, and becomes a 9
>    the 9 lands where the 6 was, and becomes a 6
>    the 0 stays a 0
> you read 609 again ✔
> ```
> **Why the mirror fails: a mirror reverses the ORDER of the digits, just as the half turn does — but it does NOT flip each digit top-to-bottom.**
>
> **6 in a mirror is a backwards 6, not a 9.**
>
> **609 is the cleanest proof that the two tests really do ask different questions.**

---

Build a 4-digit number with a vertical line of symmetry, and a different one with half-turn symmetry.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> vertical mirror : 1881  or  8008  or  1001
>    use only mirror-safe digits (0, 1, 8) and read the same both ways
> half turn       : 6889  or  1691  or  8698
>    pair 6 with 9 at OPPOSITE ends; 0, 1, 8 may sit anywhere
> ```
> **Why 6 and 9 must be paired at opposite ends for the turn: turning the number brings the last digit to the front.** A 6 at the front becomes a 9 at the back, so a 9 must have been waiting there.
>
> **And why 6 and 9 are banned from the mirror version: neither survives a mirror as a readable digit.**
>
> **Building one is a far better test than spotting one** — you have to know exactly what the rule demands.

---

A design has a flower in the top-left corner. What must be added so the design looks the same after a half turn?

> [!ANSWER]
> **An identical flower in the BOTTOM-RIGHT corner, the same distance from the centre.**
>
> **Why: turn the design half way and the top-left flower travels to the bottom-right.** For the design to look unchanged, something identical must ALREADY be sitting there.
>
> **The added copy must also be turned upside down**, because the original arrives that way — not merely slid across.
>
> **A useful picture: draw the line from the flower through the centre and continue it the same distance beyond.** That is where the copy goes.

---

The same design is to look the same after a QUARTER turn. How many flowers are needed, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Four flowers — one at each quarter position round the centre.**
>
> **Why four, and why it is forced rather than chosen: turn a quarter and the flower moves to a new spot, which must already hold an identical flower.**
>
> **Turn again and the same argument applies. And again.** By the time you are back at the start, you have demanded four copies.
>
> **"Order 4 means four of everything" is not a rule someone made up — the turning itself insists on it.**

---

Explain why block-printed cloth from Rajasthan is so often rotationally symmetric.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the symmetry comes from the METHOD, not from the artist's care.**
>
> **One carved wooden block is stamped four times round a centre, at quarter-turn positions.** Every stamp lays down an identical picture, so the finished print CANNOT come out any other way.
>
> **The craftsman does not have to draw four matching flowers** — the block guarantees they match, exactly as folded paper guarantees two matching halves.
>
> **The same is true of rangoli made round a centre, of paper snowflakes, and of the firki.** In each case a repeated physical action does the mathematics for you.

---

Colour a square using two colours so that it looks the same after every quarter turn. How many times does it match in a full turn?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Divide the square into four quarter-sections round the centre and colour them in a pattern that repeats: each quarter looks exactly like the one before it, rotated.** For example, colour the top-left half red and half blue, then copy that same arrangement into each of the other three quarters, turned a quarter each time.
>
> **It matches 4 times in a full turn** (at ¼, ½, ¾ and the full turn).
>
> **Why every quarter must be an exact rotated copy: a quarter turn slides each quarter-section onto the next one.** If they are not identical, the colours will not agree after the turn.
>
> **Notice this design usually has NO line of symmetry** — the quarters spin round rather than mirroring, which is how pinwheel patterns get their sense of movement.

---

Two shapes are stuck together to make a design. Shape A has order 4 and shape B has order 2, both about the same centre. Can the finished design have order 4?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — the best it can manage is order 2.**
>
> **Why: for the WHOLE design to survive a quarter turn, every part of it must.** Shape B does not, so after a quarter turn shape B is out of place and the design has changed.
>
> **A design is only as symmetric as its least symmetric part.**
>
> **But a half turn works for both** (B by assumption, A because half is two quarters), **so the design has order 2** — which is why 2 is the largest order that divides into both.

---

Is a rectangle's diagonal a line of symmetry? Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> **Why: fold a rectangle along its diagonal and the two triangles do NOT land on each other** — one is long-and-low, the other tall-and-narrow. The corners miss.
>
> **A rectangle has exactly two lines of symmetry: the vertical and the horizontal through the centre.**
>
> **A SQUARE is different — its diagonals ARE lines of symmetry**, because its sides are all equal so the two triangles are identical. **That is one of the things that makes a square special among rectangles.**
>
> **The lesson: never assume a line is a line of symmetry because it looks central.** Fold, trace, or hold a mirror to it.

---

A regular hexagon: how many lines of symmetry, and what order of rotational symmetry?

> [!ANSWER]
> **6 lines of symmetry and order 6.**
> ```
> lines : 3 through opposite corners + 3 through opposite side-midpoints
> turns : matches every ⅙ of a turn — 6 matching positions
> ```
> **Why both numbers are 6: all six sides and all six corners are identical**, so every way of mapping the hexagon onto itself just relabels which corner is which.
>
> **This equality is not a coincidence — for any REGULAR shape, the number of lines of symmetry equals the number of sides equals the order.** A square: 4, 4, 4. An equilateral triangle: 3, 3, 3.
>
> **It breaks the moment the shape stops being regular:** a rectangle has 2 lines and order 2, but 4 sides.

---

A rangoli pattern matches after a one-sixth turn. Name every turn (less than a full turn) at which it matches.

> [!ANSWER]
> **At ⅙, ²⁄₆ (= ⅓), ³⁄₆ (= ½), ⁴⁄₆ (= ⅔) and ⁵⁄₆ of a turn — five positions, order 6.**
>
> **Why: the one-sixth turn is a move that works, so repeating it must keep working** — right round until you have done all six and arrived back at the start.
>
> **Notice that ⅓ and ½ appear inside the list.** A shape with order 6 automatically has orders 3 and 2 hidden inside it, because 3 and 2 both divide 6.
>
> **So higher order means MORE symmetry, not different symmetry** — it contains the smaller ones.

---

Ravi says "my design has rotational symmetry at a one-fifth turn and also at a one-quarter turn". Why is this impossible for a simple design?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because fifths and quarters do not fit together in a circle.**
>
> **Why: if both turns work, then combining them must work too — and a ¼ turn followed by a ⅕ turn gives 9/20 of a turn**, then more and more combinations, ending with matches at turns smaller than any drawn detail could survive.
>
> **The only figure that can match at absolutely every angle is a circle** (or a set of plain rings), which has no distinguishable design on it at all.
>
> **So for any design with actual features, the matching turns must all be multiples of ONE smallest turn.** Fifths and quarters have no common smallest turn other than the whole circle.

---

Make a shape with exactly one line of symmetry and no rotational symmetry. Explain why your shape works.

> [!ANSWER]
> **An isosceles triangle (two equal sides, one different).**
>
> **Why it has exactly one line: fold it down the middle, from the top point to the middle of the base, and the two equal sides land on each other ✔** No other fold works — the base is a different length from the sides, so it can never land on one.
>
> **Why it has no rotational symmetry: the top point and the two base corners are all different from each other.** Any turn moves the point away from the top, and the shape looks different at once.
>
> **Other good answers: the letter A, a kite, a plain arrow.** All share the same feature — a single mirror line and three distinguishable corners.

---

A wooden block is stamped 4 times round a point to make a print. The finished print has order 4. Now the block is stamped only 3 times, leaving one quarter empty. What is the order now?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Order 1 — no rotational symmetry at all.**
>
> **Why: turn the print a quarter and the empty gap moves to a place where a stamp is sitting.** The two do not match, so the print has changed.
>
> **The same failure happens at a half turn and a three-quarter turn** — the gap is always somewhere new.
>
> **One missing copy destroys the whole symmetry.** This is the strongest possible illustration of why order 4 demands four of everything: not "usually four", but exactly four, with none left out.

---

Fold a square of paper twice and cut a notch out of the folded corner. When you open it out, where are the holes and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Four identical holes, arranged symmetrically round the centre of the square.**
>
> **Why: two folds make FOUR layers, and one cut passes through all four at once.** Each layer becomes a separate hole when the paper opens.
>
> **Why they are placed symmetrically: the folds are the lines of symmetry, and opening out reflects the notch across each of them in turn.**
>
> **Cutting the corner that sits at the CENTRE gives a hole in the middle; cutting a corner on the outer edge gives four holes round the rim.** The fold's geometry decides everything — this is exactly how paper snowflakes are made.
