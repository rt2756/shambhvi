What do you get when you add one thousand to 9,000? How is it written?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You get TEN THOUSAND, written 10,000.**
>
> ```
> 10 Thousands = 1 Ten thousand = 10,000
> ```
> **Why a new place is needed: the thousands column can only hold up to 9.** Ten thousands will not fit in it, so a new column — **TTh** — opens up on the left.
>
> **This is the same thing that happened at 9 + 1 = 10 and at 99 + 1 = 100.** The rule never changes: **when a place fills up, the next place to the left takes over.**

---

Complete: 10 Ones = ___, 10 Tens = ___, 10 Hundreds = ___, 10 Thousands = ___.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 10 Ones      = 1 Ten          = 10
> 10 Tens      = 1 Hundred      = 100
> 10 Hundreds  = 1 Thousand     = 1,000
> 10 Thousands = 1 Ten thousand = 10,000
> ```
> **The one pattern behind all four: EVERY PLACE IS TEN TIMES THE PLACE ON ITS RIGHT.**
>
> **This is why we never need new digits.** The same ten digits 0–9 do all the work — what changes is only **where** a digit sits.

Write these in words: 10,024 · 13,520 · 45,867.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 10,024 — Ten thousand twenty-four
> 13,520 — Thirteen thousand five hundred twenty
> 45,867 — Forty-five thousand eight hundred sixty-seven
> ```
> **The method: SPLIT THE NUMBER AT THE COMMA.**
> ```
> 45,867  →  "45"  |  "867"
>         →  forty-five THOUSAND | eight hundred sixty-seven
> ```
> **Read the part before the comma, say "thousand", then read the rest.**
>
> **That is exactly what the comma is for** — it marks the place where you pause.

Write these in figures: Ten thousand seven hundred forty-three · Fifty-six thousand four hundred ninety-one · Ninety thousand nine.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Ten thousand seven hundred forty-three     = 10,743
> Fifty-six thousand four hundred ninety-one = 56,491
> Ninety thousand nine                       = 90,009
> ```
> **Watch "ninety thousand NINE" carefully — it is 90,009, not 90,900 or 90,090.**
>
> **Why: the "nine" comes right at the end with no "hundred" or "ty" after it, so it is nine ONES.** The empty hundreds and tens must be filled with **zeros to hold the places open.**
>
> **Zeros are not nothing — they are placeholders.** Without them the 9 would slide left into the wrong column.

Write 9,123 as a sum of its place values.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 9,123 = 9 Thousands + 1 Hundred + 2 Tens + 3 Ones
>       = 9,000 + 100 + 20 + 3
> ```
> **Each digit is worth its face value MULTIPLIED BY its place.**
>
> **The 9 is not just "nine" — it is nine THOUSANDS**, which is why it contributes 9,000 to the total. **A digit's worth depends entirely on where it stands.**

Continue these patterns: (a) 456, 567, 678, ___, ___ (b) 5,501, 6,401, 7,301, ___, ___ (c) 10,794, 10,796, 10,798, ___

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (a) 456, 567, 678, 789, 890          the step is +111
> (b) 5,501, 6,401, 7,301, 8,201, 9,101   the step is +900
> (c) 10,794, 10,796, 10,798, 10,800      the step is +2
> ```
> **How to find any pattern: SUBTRACT one term from the next.**
> ```
> 567 − 456 = 111        so the step is 111
> 6,401 − 5,501 = 900    so the step is 900
> ```
> **Check the step works for the SECOND gap too before trusting it** — one gap could be a coincidence, two almost never is.

---

Continue: 73,005, 72,004, ___ and 82,350, 83,350, ___

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 73,005, 72,004, 71,003        the step is −1,001 (going DOWN)
> 82,350, 83,350, 84,350        the step is +1,000
> ```
> **The first one is a decreasing pattern**, which is easy to miss if you assume every sequence grows.
>
> **Always check the DIRECTION first: is the second term bigger or smaller than the first?** Only then work out the size of the step.

A student says 9,990 is greater than 49,014 because 9 is greater than 4. Is the student right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **NO — the student is wrong.**
>
> **The mistake: comparing the leading digits BEFORE counting how many digits each number has.**
> ```
> 9,990  has FOUR digits
> 49,014 has FIVE digits
> ```
> **A FIVE-digit number is ALWAYS bigger than a FOUR-digit number, whatever digits are used.**
>
> **The proof: the SMALLEST five-digit number is 10,000, and the BIGGEST four-digit number is 9,999.** Even at their closest, the five-digit number wins.
>
> **So: 9,990 is SMALLER than 49,014.**

---

State the correct method for comparing two numbers, in order.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1. COUNT THE DIGITS. More digits → bigger number. Stop here if they differ.
>
> 2. If the digit count is the SAME, compare from the LEFT, one place at a
>    time, and stop at the FIRST place where they differ.
> ```
> **Why start from the LEFT: the leftmost digit is worth the MOST.**
>
> **A difference in the ten-thousands column outweighs everything to its right put together** — so once you find a difference there, nothing further can change the answer.
>
> **And once you find the first difference, STOP.** The remaining digits are irrelevant.

---

Compare 40,347 and 40,473 using this method.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> both have FIVE digits, so go to step 2
>
> TTh    4 = 4      same — look further
> Th     0 = 0      same — look further
> H      3 < 4      DIFFERENT — stop here
>
> so    40,347 < 40,473
> ```
> **The 47 and 73 at the end never had to be looked at.**
>
> **Once the hundreds differ, no arrangement of the tens and ones could make up the gap** — a hundred is worth more than all the tens and ones together.

---

Arrange in increasing order: 40,347 · 34,407 · 40,473 · 34,740 · 73,404 · 74,430 · 47,340 · 18,926.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 18,926 · 34,407 · 34,740 · 40,347 · 40,473 · 47,340 · 73,404 · 74,430
> ```
> **How to do it without getting lost: SORT BY THE FIRST DIGIT FIRST.**
> ```
> 1…  → 18,926
> 3…  → 34,407 and 34,740
> 4…  → 40,347, 40,473, 47,340
> 7…  → 73,404, 74,430
> ```
> **Then sort within each group by the second digit, and so on.**
>
> **Working left to right in stages keeps eight numbers manageable** — trying to compare all eight at once is where mistakes happen.

---

A rabbit is at 2,346 and its food is at a neighbouring TEN. Which ten does it reach in the fewest jumps?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2,340  ←  2,346  →  2,350
>
> back to 2,340:  6 jumps
> forward to 2,350: 4 jumps
> ```
> **2,350 is the NEAREST TEN — only 4 jumps.**
>
> **This is what "rounding" really means: finding the round number you can reach in the FEWEST steps.**
>
> **The shortcut you can use instead of counting: look at the ONES digit.** It is 6, which is 5 or more, so round **UP**.

---

Find the nearest hundred and nearest thousand of 2,346.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> NEAREST HUNDRED
>    2,300 ← 2,346 → 2,400
>    46 back, 54 forward → 2,300 is nearer
>
> NEAREST THOUSAND
>    2,000 ← 2,346 → 3,000
>    346 back, 654 forward → 2,000 is nearer
> ```
> **By the shortcut:**
> - **for the nearest hundred, look at the TENS digit — 4, which is less than 5, so round DOWN**
> - **for the nearest thousand, look at the HUNDREDS digit — 3, less than 5, so round DOWN**
>
> **Both agree with the counting** — which is the point of a shortcut.

---

State the rounding shortcut and explain why you look at that particular digit.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> NEAREST TEN      — look at the ONES digit
> NEAREST HUNDRED  — look at the TENS digit
> NEAREST THOUSAND — look at the HUNDREDS digit
>
> the digit is 5 or more → round UP
> the digit is 4 or less → round DOWN
> ```
> **Why THAT digit: it tells you which HALF of the gap you are standing in.**
>
> **Halfway between two tens is 5.** If the ones digit is above 5 you are past the middle and nearer the top; if below 5, you are nearer the bottom.
>
> **The same logic works at every level** — halfway between two hundreds is 50, so the tens digit decides; halfway between two thousands is 500, so the hundreds digit decides.

Round 3,176 and 5,789 to the nearest ten, hundred and thousand.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Number | Nearest ten | Nearest hundred | Nearest thousand |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **3,176** | 3,180 | 3,200 | 3,000 |
> | **5,789** | 5,790 | 5,800 | 6,000 |
>
> **Working through 3,176:**
> ```
> nearest TEN      — ones digit 6, ≥5, round UP    → 3,180
> nearest HUNDRED  — tens digit 7, ≥5, round UP    → 3,200
> nearest THOUSAND — hundreds digit 1, <5, round DOWN → 3,000
> ```
> **Notice that 3,176 rounds UP to the nearest hundred but DOWN to the nearest thousand.** That is not a contradiction — **each level asks a different question**, and you must look at a different digit for each.

---

Round 4,017 to the nearest hundred and to the nearest thousand. Is anything odd about the answers?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> nearest HUNDRED  — tens digit is 1, less than 5, round DOWN → 4,000
> nearest THOUSAND — hundreds digit is 0, less than 5, round DOWN → 4,000
> ```
> **Both answers are 4,000 — and there is nothing wrong with that.**
>
> **Why it happens: 4,017 is genuinely very close to 4,000 in BOTH senses.** It is only 17 away, which is a tiny distance whether you are measuring in hundreds or in thousands.
>
> **A round number like 4,000 IS a hundred and IS a thousand at the same time**, so it can serve as the answer to both questions.

Vijay rounds a number to the nearest hundred and Suma rounds the same number to the nearest thousand. They get the same answer. Which of these could they have used: 7,126 · 7,835 · 7,030 · 6,999?

> [!ANSWER]
> **7,030 and 6,999.**
> ```
> 7,126 → hundred 7,100 · thousand 7,000     DIFFERENT ✘
> 7,835 → hundred 7,800 · thousand 8,000     DIFFERENT ✘
> 7,030 → hundred 7,000 · thousand 7,000     SAME ✔
> 6,999 → hundred 7,000 · thousand 7,000     SAME ✔
> ```
> **What the two winners have in common: each is VERY CLOSE to a whole thousand.**
>
> **7,030 is just 30 above 7,000, and 6,999 is just 1 below it.** When a number is that close to a thousand, rounding to the nearest hundred lands you on the thousand as well.
>
> **6,999 is the pleasing case: it rounds UP past 7,000 both times**, even though it starts with a 6.

---

In the number 1,478, interchange two digits to make a number larger than 5,500.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Swap the 1 and the 7 to get 7,418.**
>
> **Why that swap: to make a number BIG, put the BIGGEST digit in the LEFTMOST place.**
>
> **The digits available are 1, 4, 7, 8. The leading digit is currently 1 — the smallest of them — which is wasting the most valuable position.**
>
> **Moving the 7 there gives 7,418, which is comfortably above 5,500.** Swapping the 1 and the 8 would work too, giving 8,471.
>
> **The general lesson: the LEFTMOST place is where a change makes the biggest difference.**

Interchange two digits of 48,247 to make (i) the smallest possible number, (ii) the largest possible number.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the digits are  4  8  2  4  7
>
> (i) SMALLEST — put the smallest digit (2) at the front
>     swap the leading 4 with the 2  →  28,447
>
> (ii) LARGEST — put the largest digit (8) at the front
>     swap the leading 4 with the 8  →  84,247
> ```
> **Both answers use the same idea: THE LEFTMOST PLACE MATTERS MOST.**
>
> **Changing the ten-thousands digit by 2 changes the number by 20,000. Changing the ones digit by 2 changes it by 2.** So to make the biggest difference — in either direction — you work on the leading digit.

A cyclist covers 15 km in one hour. How far will she go in 4 hours at the same speed?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 15 km in 1 hour
>
> in 4 hours:  15 × 4 = 60 km
> ```
> **Why multiply: the SAME distance is covered in each hour**, so four hours means four lots of 15 km.
>
> **The words "at the same speed" are doing real work here.** Without them, the four hours could each be different and you could not multiply at all.

A school has 461 girls and 439 boys going on a trip. How many cars (4 seats) and how many minibuses (25 seats) are needed?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total people = 461 + 439 = 900
>
> CARS (4 each)      900 ÷ 4  = 225 cars
> MINIBUSES (25 each) 900 ÷ 25 = 36 minibuses
> ```
> **The first step is the one people forget: ADD the girls and boys.** The question is about the whole school, not about either group.
>
> **A useful check on the addition: 461 + 439 — the 61 and 39 make 100, so the total is 400 + 400 + 100 = 900.** Looking for pairs that make a round number is quicker than carrying digits.

---

How many boats (20 people each) and how many aeroplanes (180 people each) would those 900 people need?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> BOATS (20 each)      900 ÷ 20 = 45 boats
> AEROPLANES (180 each) 900 ÷ 180 = 5 aeroplanes
> ```
> **Notice the pattern across all these answers: THE BIGGER THE VEHICLE, THE FEWER YOU NEED.**
> ```
> cars (4)        → 225
> minibuses (25)  → 36
> boats (20)      → 45
> aeroplanes (180)→ 5
> ```
> **This is division showing its meaning:** dividing the same 900 by a bigger number always gives a smaller answer, because each vehicle is carrying more of the load.

---

A book has about 200 pages with about 50 words on each. About how many words are in the book?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 200 × 50 = 10,000 words
> ```
> **And nobody had to count a single word.**
>
> **How the trick works: MULTIPLY two things you CAN estimate**, to get one thing you could never count.
>
> **This is what estimation is for.** The answer is not exact — some pages have more words, some fewer — but **10,000 is close enough to be genuinely useful**, and it took two numbers and one multiplication.

---

Why is the smallest five-digit number bigger than the largest four-digit number?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> largest FOUR-digit number  = 9,999
> smallest FIVE-digit number = 10,000
>
> 10,000 is bigger than 9,999 — by exactly 1
> ```
> **Why this must be so: 9,999 is the very last number the four places can hold.** Add one more and the ones column overflows into the tens, the tens into the hundreds, the hundreds into the thousands, and finally the thousands into a **brand-new ten-thousands column.**
>
> **So a five-digit number can never be smaller than a four-digit one** — the extra digit only exists because the four places ran out.
>
> **This single fact is what makes "count the digits first" a reliable rule for comparing.**
