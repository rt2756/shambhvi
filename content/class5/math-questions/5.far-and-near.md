Which unit — centimetres or metres — would you use for the height of India Gate, the length of a handkerchief, and the depth of a well?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> height of India Gate      — METRES        (about 42 m)
> length of a handkerchief  — CENTIMETRES   (about 40 cm)
> depth of a well           — METRES        (about 50 m)
> ```
> **THE RULE: pick the unit that gives a SENSIBLE-SIZED NUMBER.**
>
> **India Gate is 42 m — which is also 4,200 cm. Both are true**, but 4,200 is an awkward number to carry around and 42 is not.
>
> **A handkerchief is 40 cm — also 0.4 m.** Again both are true, but 40 is far easier to picture.
>
> **So the unit is chosen for CONVENIENCE, not correctness.**

---

Convert: 5 m to cm · 700 cm to m · 3 km to m.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 5 m   → 5 × 100   = 500 cm
> 700 cm → 700 ÷ 100 = 7 m
> 3 km  → 3 × 1,000 = 3,000 m
> ```
> **The rule: BIG unit to SMALL unit, MULTIPLY. SMALL unit to BIG unit, DIVIDE.**
>
> **Why: a centimetre is SMALLER than a metre, so you need MORE of them to make the same length.**
>
> **The check that never fails: THE NUMBER GETS BIGGER WHEN THE UNIT GETS SMALLER.** If your answer went the other way, you divided when you should have multiplied.

---

Write these in centimetres: 5 m 40 cm · 2 m 4 cm · 2 m 40 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 5 m 40 cm = 500 + 40 = 540 cm
> 2 m  4 cm = 200 +  4 = 204 cm
> 2 m 40 cm = 200 + 40 = 240 cm
> ```
> **The method: SPLIT AT THE UNIT.** Convert the metres, then simply add the leftover centimetres.
>
> **Notice that 2 m 4 cm and 2 m 40 cm are NOT the same** — 204 cm and 240 cm.
>
> **The zero matters: 4 cm and 40 cm differ by 36 cm**, which is longer than a school ruler. Reading "2 m 4 cm" carelessly as 240 is a very easy slip.

---

Write 5,792 cm in metres and centimetres.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 5,792 cm = 5,700 cm + 92 cm
>          = 57 m + 92 cm
>          = 57 m 92 cm
> ```
> **The method: ASK HOW MANY HUNDREDS OF CENTIMETRES YOU HAVE.**
>
> **5,792 contains 57 hundreds, with 92 left over** — so 57 metres and 92 centimetres.
>
> **A shortcut you may notice: the last TWO digits become the centimetres**, and everything in front becomes the metres. **That works because 1 m = 100 cm exactly**, so the split falls right at the hundreds column.

---

Compare using `<`, `>` or `=`: 456 cm and 5 m · 6 m 5 cm and 6 m 50 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 456 cm  and  5 m
>    5 m = 500 cm
>    456 < 500      →  456 cm < 5 m
>
> 6 m 5 cm  and  6 m 50 cm
>    605 cm  and  650 cm
>    605 < 650      →  6 m 5 cm < 6 m 50 cm
> ```
> **YOU CANNOT COMPARE UNTIL BOTH ARE IN THE SAME UNIT.**
>
> **Why this matters: "456" LOOKS bigger than "5", but it is not.** The units are doing all the work, and ignoring them gives exactly the wrong answer.
>
> **Convert first, compare second — always in that order.**

---

Compare: 2 m 150 cm and 3 m 50 cm · 238 cm and 138 cm + 1 m.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2 m 150 cm = 200 + 150 = 350 cm
> 3 m  50 cm = 300 +  50 = 350 cm
>    →  they are EQUAL
>
> 238 cm  and  138 cm + 1 m
>    138 cm + 100 cm = 238 cm
>    →  they are EQUAL
> ```
> **Both pairs turn out to be the SAME length, written in different ways.**
>
> **"2 m 150 cm" is an unusual way to write it — the 150 cm is more than a whole metre**, so it could have been written as 3 m 50 cm from the start. **Both are correct; one is just tidier.**
>
> **This is worth seeing once: a mixed measure does not have to be "tidy" to be valid.**

---

Compare 55 cm + 200 cm with 200 cm + 54 cm without adding anything up.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The LEFT side is bigger.**
>
> **Why you need no arithmetic: BOTH SIDES CONTAIN 200 cm.**
> ```
> 55 + 200    versus    200 + 54
>      ↑ same ↑          ↑ same ↑
> ```
> **Ignore what is the same and compare only what differs: 55 against 54.**
>
> **55 is bigger, so the left side is bigger.**
>
> **Looking for what is IDENTICAL on both sides can save all the work** — and it is much safer than adding two three-digit numbers in a hurry.

---

The Statue of Unity is 182 m tall and the Statue of Liberty is 93 m. What is the difference?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 182 − 93 = 89 m
> ```
> **The Statue of Unity is 89 metres taller.**
>
> **A quick check by rounding: 180 − 90 = 90**, and our answer 89 is right beside it ✔
>
> **Estimating before or after a subtraction is a good habit** — it catches a borrowing mistake instantly, and it takes a couple of seconds.

---

Six statues have heights 182 m, 128 m, 108 m, 93 m, 91 m and 38 m. Which two are closest in height, and which two are furthest apart?

> [!ANSWER]
> **First put them in order — that is what makes this easy:**
> ```
> 38 · 91 · 93 · 108 · 128 · 182
> ```
> **Now the gaps between neighbours:**
> ```
> 91 − 38  = 53
> 93 − 91  =  2      ← SMALLEST
> 108 − 93 = 15
> 128 − 108= 20
> 182 − 128= 54
> ```
> **CLOSEST: the 91 m and 93 m statues — only 2 m apart.**
>
> **FURTHEST APART: 182 m and 38 m — a difference of 144 m.**
>
> **Why ordering first is the whole trick: the two closest heights must be NEIGHBOURS in the sorted list**, so you only have to check five gaps instead of all fifteen possible pairs.

---

Which of those statues, if its height were doubled, would equal the height of the 182 m Statue of Unity?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 182 ÷ 2 = 91
> ```
> **The 91 m statue — doubled, it gives exactly 182 m.**
>
> **Why divide rather than trying each one: "doubled equals 182" means "182 is twice the answer"**, and undoing a doubling is a halving.
>
> **This is the addition-and-subtraction idea from Chapter 4, now for multiplication and division:** they undo each other, so a question about one can be answered with the other.

---

How many metres are in a kilometre? What does "kilo" mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1 km = 1,000 m
>
> "KILO" means THOUSAND
> ```
> **So a kilometre is literally a "thousand-metre".**
>
> **Knowing what the prefix means is genuinely useful** — it turns up again in **kilogram** (a thousand grams), which is exactly the same idea applied to weight.
>
> **This unit is used for LONG distances**, where counting in metres would give unwieldy numbers.

---

How many ropes of 500 m, of 250 m and of 10 m are needed to make 1 km?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1 km = 1,000 m
>
> 500 m rope → 1,000 ÷ 500 =   2 ropes
> 250 m rope → 1,000 ÷ 250 =   4 ropes
>  10 m rope → 1,000 ÷ 10  = 100 ropes
> ```
> **The pattern: THE SHORTER THE ROPE, THE MORE YOU NEED.**
>
> **This is division showing its meaning** — dividing the same 1,000 by a smaller number gives a bigger answer.
>
> **And it is the same relationship as choosing units:** a smaller unit always means a bigger number for the same length.

---

A 3-km race has water stations every 500 m. How many are needed, and where do they go?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3 km = 3,000 m
>
> 3,000 ÷ 500 = 6 water stations
>
> placed at:  500 · 1,000 · 1,500 · 2,000 · 2,500 · 3,000 m
> ```
> **Converting the kilometres to metres FIRST is what makes the division possible** — you cannot divide 3 km by 500 m until both are in the same unit.
>
> **A check on the list: the last station is exactly at the finish line, 3,000 m**, which is what you would expect when 500 divides 3,000 exactly.

---

In the same 3-km race, children stand every 300 m to direct the runners. How many children, and where?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3,000 ÷ 300 = 10 children
>
> at:  300 · 600 · 900 · 1,200 · 1,500 · 1,800 · 2,100 · 2,400 · 2,700 · 3,000 m
> ```
> **Ten children, the last one right at the finish.**
>
> **A comparison worth making with the previous question: 6 water stations but 10 children.**
>
> **The children stand CLOSER TOGETHER (every 300 m instead of 500 m), so MORE are needed** — the same "smaller gap, bigger number" pattern again.

---

Red and blue flags are placed alternately every 50 m along the 3-km route. How many of each colour are needed?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3,000 ÷ 50 = 60 flags altogether
>
> placed alternately red, blue, red, blue …
>
> 60 ÷ 2 = 30 RED and 30 BLUE
> ```
> **This question has a HIDDEN SECOND STEP, and it is where marks are lost.**
>
> **The division gives 60 FLAGS — but the question asked for reds and blues SEPARATELY**, so you must halve again.
>
> **Always re-read what was actually asked once the arithmetic is finished.** A correct calculation answering the wrong question is still wrong.

---

Rani has two ribbons, one 3 m 75 cm long and the other 2 m 50 cm. What is their total length, and what is the difference?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> convert both to centimetres first:
>    3 m 75 cm = 375 cm
>    2 m 50 cm = 250 cm
>
> TOTAL      375 + 250 = 625 cm = 6 m 25 cm
> DIFFERENCE 375 − 250 = 125 cm = 1 m 25 cm
> ```
> **Converting to one unit BEFORE adding or subtracting keeps everything simple** — otherwise you would have to carry between the centimetres and the metres.
>
> **And converting the answer BACK at the end is worth doing**, because "6 m 25 cm" is far easier to picture than "625 cm".

---

A shop sells cloth at ₹100 for 5 m. How much is 1 m, and how much would 8 m cost?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1 metre:   ₹100 ÷ 5 = ₹20
>
> 8 metres:  ₹20 × 8 = ₹160
> ```
> **The method — find the cost of ONE first, then multiply.**
>
> **Why this two-step route is worth the trouble: once you know the price per metre, you can answer ANY length**, not just the one asked.
>
> **A check on the size of the answer: 8 m is a bit less than double 5 m, so the cost should be a bit less than ₹200** — and ₹160 fits ✔

Name four instruments used to measure long distances.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> LONG TAPE            — for a playground or a field
> RODOMETER            — a measuring wheel you push along a road
> RANGEFINDER          — measures a distance you cannot walk
> LASER DISTANCE METER — for rooms and buildings
> ```
> **Why so many different tools: they suit different situations.**
>
> **A long tape needs you to walk the whole distance holding one end — impossible across a river.** That is exactly when a **rangefinder** earns its place.
>
> **And a rodometer is for roads, where the wheel can roll along the ground** and count its own turns.

---

How could you measure a long distance with no instrument at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> **MEASURE YOUR OWN STEP FIRST.**
> ```
> 1. Mark out 100 m with a long tape.
> 2. Walk it and COUNT your steps — say it takes 125 steps.
> 3. So one step is about 100 ÷ 125 = 0.8 m, roughly 80 cm.
> 4. Now walk any distance, count the steps, and multiply.
> ```
> **Example: 250 steps × 80 cm = 20,000 cm = 200 m.**
>
> **What you have done is turn your OWN BODY into a measuring instrument** — which is how distances were measured long before tapes existed.
>
> **The important step is the CALIBRATION at the start.** Without knowing the length of your own step, counting steps tells you nothing.
