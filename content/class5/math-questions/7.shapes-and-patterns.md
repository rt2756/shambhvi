Write the pattern for the simplest woven mat, and say why Row 2 must differ from Row 1.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Row 1 — 1 under, 1 over, 1 under, 1 over, … (repeat)
> Row 2 — 1 over, 1 under, 1 over, 1 under, … (repeat)
> ```
> **Row 2 is the EXACT OPPOSITE of Row 1.**
>
> **Why it must be: if two rows went over and under in the SAME places, the strips would not HOLD each other** — nothing would be trapped, and the mat would simply fall apart when lifted.
>
> **By reversing the second row, every strip is caught by the one above and the one below.**
>
> **That alternation is what makes a weave a WEAVE rather than a stack of loose strips.**

---

A mat has Row 1 as "2 over, 1 under" repeated. What must Row 2 be?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Row 1 — 2 over, 1 under, 2 over, 1 under, …
> Row 2 — 2 under, 1 over, 2 under, 1 over, …
> ```
> **Every "over" becomes an "under" and every "under" becomes an "over".**
>
> **The rule is the same as before, just applied to a longer repeating unit:** here the pattern repeats every **three** strips instead of every two.
>
> **The principle does not change with the pattern** — Row 2 always undoes Row 1 so that the strips lock together.

---

How can a whole mat of any size be described in just two lines?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because you write down ONLY THE PART THAT REPEATS.**
> ```
> "2 over, 1 under"  repeated  is the whole of Row 1,
> however wide the mat is
> ```
> **Three strips' worth of instruction describes a mat with a hundred strips.**
>
> **This is what a PATTERN really is: A SHORT RULE THAT PRODUCES SOMETHING LONG.**
>
> **How to read an unfamiliar weave: watch one row until it starts repeating, then write down just that repeating bit.** Everything after it is the same again.

---

What does it mean to say a shape "tessellates"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A shape TESSELLATES if copies of it can cover a region completely — WITH NO GAPS AND NO OVERLAPS.**
>
> **Both conditions matter:**
> - **a GAP means part of the floor is left uncovered**
> - **an OVERLAP means the tiles cannot lie flat**
>
> **This is exactly the requirement for real floor tiles**, which is why the mathematics matters outside a book.
>
> **A REGULAR shape is one with all its sides equal** — an equilateral triangle, a square, a regular pentagon, a regular hexagon.

---

How many equilateral triangles fit around a point? How many squares? How many regular hexagons?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> equilateral triangles  →  6 fit exactly
> squares                →  4 fit exactly
> regular hexagons       →  3 fit exactly
> ```
> **All three tessellate**, because in each case the corners close up around the point with nothing left over.
>
> **The test to remember: fit copies around a SINGLE POINT and see whether you come full circle exactly.**
>
> **If they do, the shape will tile a whole floor** — because every point in the tiling is just another such meeting point.

---

Why do regular pentagons not tessellate?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because THREE fit around a point but leave a GAP, and a FOURTH is too big to fit into it.**
> ```
> 3 pentagons → a gap remains
> 4 pentagons → will not fit; they would overlap
> ```
> **So the corners of a regular pentagon do not divide a full turn evenly.**
>
> **Three is too few and four is too many, and there is nothing in between** — you cannot use three-and-a-bit pentagons.
>
> **Compare with the shapes that succeed: 6 triangles, 4 squares or 3 hexagons close up EXACTLY.** Failing by a little is still failing.

---

Use the idea of turns from Chapter 3 to explain why 4 squares fit around a point.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a square's corner is a QUARTER TURN — a right angle.**
> ```
> 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 1 full turn
> ```
> **Four quarter turns make exactly one whole turn, so four squares close up perfectly with no gap.**
>
> **The same reasoning for the others:**
> ```
> a triangle's corner is a SIXTH of a full turn → 6 of them make a whole
> a hexagon's corner is a THIRD of a full turn  → 3 of them make a whole
> ```
> **And the pentagon fails because its corner is BIGGER than a quarter turn but SMALLER than a third** — so no whole number of them can add to a full turn.
>
> **Tessellation is really a question about fractions of a turn.**

---

Can a tessellation use more than one shape?

> [!ANSWER]
> **YES — and many real floor and wall patterns do exactly that.**
>
> **Common combinations: squares with triangles, hexagons with triangles, octagons with squares.**
>
> **Why mixing helps: two shapes whose corners do not each divide a full turn evenly may still ADD UP to one together.**
>
> **A shape that cannot tile alone can therefore often tile with a partner** — which is why tiled floors are so much more varied than a single repeating square.

---

Name six kinds of quadrilateral and give one distinguishing feature of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> SQUARE        4 equal sides AND 4 right angles
> RECTANGLE     opposite sides equal, 4 right angles
> RHOMBUS       4 equal sides, but the corners are NOT right angles
> PARALLELOGRAM opposite sides equal and parallel
> TRAPEZIUM     just ONE pair of parallel sides
> KITE          two pairs of equal sides, NEXT TO each other
> ```
> **All six have FOUR sides — that is what makes them quadrilaterals.**
>
> **What distinguishes them is which sides are equal, which are parallel, and whether the corners are right angles.**

---

Is a square a rectangle? Is it a rhombus?

> [!ANSWER]
> **YES to both.**
>
> ```
> a RECTANGLE has opposite sides equal and 4 right angles
>    → a square has both, so a square IS a rectangle
>      (a special one, whose sides happen to be all equal)
>
> a RHOMBUS has 4 equal sides
>    → a square has that too, so a square IS a rhombus
>      (a special one, whose corners happen to be right angles)
> ```
> **The categories OVERLAP — a shape can belong to more than one at once.**
>
> **This surprises people who expect the names to be like boxes with nothing shared.** But the definitions are about **properties**, and one shape can have several sets of properties at the same time.

---

A shape has four equal sides. Is that enough to say it is a square?

> [!ANSWER]
> **NO — it could be a RHOMBUS instead.**
>
> **Four equal sides is not enough. You also need FOUR RIGHT ANGLES.**
> ```
> 4 equal sides + right angles → SQUARE
> 4 equal sides, corners tilted → RHOMBUS
> ```
> **Picture pushing a square sideways: the sides stay the same length, but the corners open out and close up.** It is a rhombus now, not a square.
>
> **The lesson for any "which shape am I?" puzzle: CHECK EVERY CLUE, and be sure the ones you have really do pin the shape down.**

---

How would you solve a "which shape am I?" puzzle efficiently?

> [!ANSWER]
> **CHECK THE CLUES ONE AT A TIME AND CROSS OUT WHAT FAILS.**
> ```
> "I have four equal sides"   → leaves SQUARE and RHOMBUS
> "I have four right angles"  → leaves only SQUARE
> ```
> **Two clues were enough; any further clues would be unnecessary.**
>
> **Why working one clue at a time beats trying to picture the whole answer: each clue REMOVES candidates**, and you can often finish long before the list of clues does.
>
> **Noticing which clues turned out to be UNNECESSARY is worth doing too** — it tells you the puzzle gave you more information than it needed to.

---

What makes a circle a circle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **EVERY POINT OF A CIRCLE IS THE SAME DISTANCE FROM THE CENTRE.**
>
> **That single property defines it completely.**
>
> **And it is exactly what a compass does:**
> ```
> the sharp point stays FIXED at the centre
> the pencil is held at a FIXED distance from it
> → so every point the pencil draws is that same distance away
> ```
> **The compass is not a device for drawing round things — it is a device for KEEPING A DISTANCE CONSTANT**, and a circle is what that produces.

---

What is a net? How many squares are in the net of a cube?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A NET is a flat shape that FOLDS UP into a solid.**
>
> **A cube's net has SIX SQUARES, because a cube has SIX FACES.**
>
> **But — and this is the catch — NOT EVERY ARRANGEMENT OF SIX SQUARES FOLDS INTO A CUBE.** Some leave two faces landing in the same place, and a hole somewhere else.
>
> **How to test a net without cutting it out: pick one square as the BASE and imagine folding the others up around it.** If any two try to occupy the same position, the net fails.

---

What do the names "icosahedron" and "dodecahedron" tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ICOSAHEDRON  — "icosa" means TWENTY  → 20 faces
> DODECAHEDRON — "dodeca" means TWELVE → 12 faces
> ```
> **The names are not decoration — they COUNT THE FACES.**
>
> **Once you know that, you never have to memorise which is which.**
>
> **The same trick works right across mathematics:**
> ```
> PENTAGON  — penta = five  → 5 sides
> HEXAGON   — hexa = six    → 6 sides
> KILOMETRE — kilo = thousand → 1,000 metres
> ```
> **Greek and Latin number-words run all through the subject**, and spotting them turns a list of names into something you can work out.

---

Why is a pattern useful in mathematics, judging from this chapter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because A PATTERN IS A SHORT RULE THAT PRODUCES SOMETHING LONG.**
>
> **Two lines of weaving instructions produce a mat of any size.**
>
> **One shape and the rule "fit them around a point" produces a whole tiled floor.**
>
> **The real power: once you know the rule, you know the WHOLE thing without having to look at all of it.**
>
> **And you can predict what comes next** — which is exactly what the tessellation test does. Checking how many shapes fit around **one** point tells you whether they will cover an **entire** region.
