Write the two division facts that come from 9 × 6 = 54, and explain where the second one comes from.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 54 ÷ 6 = 9        54 ÷ 9 = 6
> ```
> **Why there are exactly two: picture 54 coconuts laid out in 9 rows of 6.**
>
> **Read it by rows and you see 9 groups of 6. Turn your head and read it by columns and you see 6 groups of 9.** Nothing moved — only how you looked at it.
>
> **Division asks the array backwards: "I know the total and one side; what is the other?"** There are two sides, so two questions, so two facts.
>
> **This is why division tables don't have to be learnt separately** — they are already inside the multiplication tables.

---

Without dividing, use 25 × 16 = 400 to find 400 ÷ 16.

> [!ANSWER]
> **25.**
>
> **Why: 400 ÷ 16 is asking "16 times WHAT makes 400?"** — and the multiplication fact has already answered it.
>
> **Division is multiplication with one factor hidden.** Once you recognise the fact, there is nothing left to work out.
>
> **The general rule: N = D × Q.** Every division you meet is really a multiplication with a gap in it.

---

Fill in: 1,800 ÷ 18 = ___, and ___ ÷ 100 = 72.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1,800 ÷ 18 = 100          7,200 ÷ 100 = 72
> ```
> **For the first: 18 × 100 = 1,800, so 100 is the missing factor.**
>
> **For the second, work it backwards using N = D × Q: N = 100 × 72 = 7,200.**
>
> **Why running it backwards is safe: N = D × Q is not a rule someone invented** — it just states what division did. So it can be read in either direction whenever one of the three numbers is missing.

---

Here is a pattern. Complete it and say what is happening: 60 ÷ 10 = 6, 600 ÷ 10 = ___, 6,000 ÷ 10 = ___.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 60 ÷ 10 = 6        600 ÷ 10 = 60        6,000 ÷ 10 = 600
> ```
> **Ten times the dividend gives ten times the quotient.**
>
> **Why: if you have ten times as many coconuts but still make the SAME number of groups, each group must be ten times bigger.** There is nowhere else for the extra coconuts to go.
>
> **In the place-value chart the answer simply slides one column to the left each time** — and that sliding IS multiplying by ten.

---

Compare 3,000 ÷ 3 with 3,000 ÷ 30 and with 3,000 ÷ 300. What happens, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3,000 ÷   3 = 1,000
> 3,000 ÷  30 =   100
> 3,000 ÷ 300 =    10
> ```
> **Ten times the DIVISOR gives a TENTH of the quotient.**
>
> **Why: the same 3,000 coconuts shared into ten times as many groups leaves each group a tenth as full.**
>
> **Notice this pulls the opposite way to the last question, exactly as you'd expect** — a bigger dividend makes the piles bigger, a bigger divisor cuts them up more.

---

You know 240 ÷ 16 = 15. Use it to find 480 ÷ 32 and 480 ÷ 16.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 480 ÷ 32 = 15      (BOTH doubled → answer unchanged)
> 480 ÷ 16 = 30      (only the dividend doubled → answer doubles)
> ```
> **Why doubling both changes nothing: twice as many coconuts shared into twice as many groups gives each group exactly what it had before.**
>
> **The two effects — bigger piles, more piles — cancel each other out precisely.**
>
> **This is a genuinely useful trick**, because it lets you swap a horrible division for a friendly one that has the same answer.

---

Turn 1,750 ÷ 250 into an easier division with the same answer, then solve it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **175 ÷ 25 = 7.**
>
> **Why they are equal: dividing BOTH numbers by 10 leaves the quotient untouched** — it is the doubling rule run in reverse.
> ```
> 1,750 ÷ 250   →  175 ÷ 25   →  7
> ```
> **You can shrink further if it helps:** 175 ÷ 25 = 35 ÷ 5 = 7 (both divided by 5 again).
>
> **Check: 250 × 7 = 1,750 ✔** The check costs one multiplication and is always worth it.

---

Do 2,436 ÷ 6 in your head by splitting the number, and say why splitting is allowed.

> [!ANSWER]
> **406.**
> ```
> 2,436 = 2,400 + 36
> 2,400 ÷ 6 = 400        36 ÷ 6 = 6
> 400 + 6 = 406
> ```
> **Why splitting is allowed: sharing 2,436 coconuts among 6 people can be done in two rounds.** Hand out 2,400 first (400 each), then the remaining 36 (6 each).
>
> **Nothing is lost or created by pausing halfway**, so each person ends with 406 either way.
>
> **The skill is spotting the friendly piece**, and it is always a multiple of the divisor — here, 2,400.

---

Do 3,992 ÷ 8 mentally using a subtraction split.

> [!ANSWER]
> **499.**
> ```
> 3,992 = 4,000 − 8
> 4,000 ÷ 8 = 500        8 ÷ 8 = 1
> 500 − 1 = 499
> ```
> **Why the subtraction version works: share out 4,000 coconuts (500 each), then notice you had 8 too many and take back 1 from each person.**
>
> **It is the same "share in rounds" idea, run backwards.**
>
> **Use this split whenever the number sits just BELOW a round one** — 1,992, 796, 4,995 and so on. Hunting for a sum split there would be much harder work.

---

Explain why dividing by 8 is the same as halving three times, then use it on 1,368 ÷ 8.

> [!ANSWER]
> **171.**
> ```
> half of 1,368 = 684      half of 684 = 342      half of 342 = 171
> ```
> **Why it works: halve a pile and you have 2 groups. Halve each of those and you have 4. Halve again and you have 8** — all of the original pile.
> ```
> ÷2 then ÷2 then ÷2  =  ÷8
> ```
> **So the trick works for 4, 8, 16, 32 — every number built out of 2s.**
>
> **It does NOT work for 6 or 12 by halving alone**, because 6 is 2 × 3 — for those you halve once and then divide by 3.

---

Solve 9,648 ÷ 48 mentally.

> [!ANSWER]
> **201.**
> ```
> 9,648 = 9,600 + 48
> 9,600 ÷ 48 = 200      (because 48 × 2 = 96, so 48 × 200 = 9,600)
>    48 ÷ 48 = 1
> 200 + 1 = 201
> ```
> **Why 9,600 was the piece to look for: it is 48 × 200, and multiples of 100 are the ones you can divide at sight.**
>
> **A big divisor is not automatically a hard divisor.** Ask "what round number is a multiple of 48?" and the sum falls apart on its own.

Divide 738 by 6 by taking away chunks, and show a slow way and a fast way.

> [!ANSWER]
> **123.**
> ```
> FAST                          SLOW
>  6) 738 ( 100 + 20 + 3         6) 738 ( 50 + 50 + 10 + 10 + 3
>    −600                          −300
>     ---                           ---
>     138                           438
>    −120                          −300 …  and so on
>     ---
>      18
>     −18
>     ---
>      00        →  123                    →  123
> ```
> **Both give 123. Why the answer can't depend on the chunks you pick: every chunk removed is a whole number of "6 per person" rounds.**
>
> **The quotient is just a running record of how much each person has received.** Six small rounds or three big ones — the total each person ends with is identical.
>
> **So the method cannot go wrong. It can only be slow.**

Before dividing 582 by 6, how can you tell the answer is less than 100?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because 6 × 100 = 600, which is already MORE than 582.**
>
> **So 100 rounds cannot be handed out — the answer must be under 100.** (It is 97.)
>
> **Why estimating first is worth the ten seconds: it tells you which chunk to start with.** Knowing the answer is just under 100 makes 90 the obvious first try, which finishes the division in two steps instead of six.
>
> **It also catches disasters.** If your final answer came out as 970 or 9, you would know instantly that something had gone wrong.

---

Find the quotient and remainder for 1,034 ÷ 4, then check with N = D × Q + R.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Q = 258, R = 2.**
> ```
> check:  4 × 258 + 2  =  1,032 + 2  =  1,034 ✔
> ```
> **Why the check works: D × Q is what actually got shared out, and R is what was left because there wasn't enough for another round.**
>
> **Together they must rebuild N, because nothing else happened to the coconuts.**
>
> **N = D × Q + R is not a formula to memorise — it is a description of what you just did.**

---

A pupil writes "47 ÷ 5 = 8 remainder 7". What is wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The remainder 7 is bigger than the divisor 5, so the division stopped too early.**
>
> **Why that is always a mistake: if 7 are left over, another full group of 5 could still be handed out.**
> ```
> correct:  47 ÷ 5 = 9 remainder 2      (5 × 9 + 2 = 47 ✔)
> the wrong answer even fails the check: 5 × 8 + 7 = 47 — the arithmetic
> is fine, but 8 is not the LARGEST possible quotient
> ```
> **The rule: R must always be SMALLER than D.** A remainder as big as the divisor is a signpost that you have not finished.

Divide 3,015 by 5 using place value, and explain the zero in your answer.

> [!ANSWER]
> **603.**
> ```
>       Th H  T  O
>  5) 3,015 ( 0  6  0  3
>    3 Thousands ÷ 5 → not possible, regroup as 30 Hundreds
>    30 Hundreds ÷ 5 = 6 Hundreds
>     0 Tens ÷ 5     = 0 Tens        ← this zero
>    15 Ones ÷ 5     = 3 Ones
> ```
> **The 0 is recording a real fact: each share gets NO whole tens.**
>
> **Leave it out and you have written 63, which is nearly ten times too small.**
>
> **The rule of thumb: once you have started writing the quotient, EVERY place gets a digit — even if that digit is 0.** Skipping a place silently shifts everything after it.

Predict how many digits 4,182 ÷ 17 will have, before you divide.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Three digits — the answer is in the hundreds.**
> ```
> 17 × 100 = 1,700    fits inside 4,182
> 17 × 1,000 = 17,000 far too big
> so the answer is between 100 and 1,000 → 3 digits
> (it is 246 remainder 0)
> ```
> **Why this is worth doing first: it is the best protection against a dropped zero.**
>
> **If your final answer came out with only two digits, you would know at once that a place had gone missing** — without having to re-do the whole division.

296 children are going on a picnic. Each van seats 32. How many vans are needed?

> [!ANSWER]
> **10 vans.**
> ```
> 296 ÷ 32 = 9 remainder 8      (32 × 9 = 288)
> ```
> **Why the answer rounds UP to 10 and not down to 9: the 8 left-over children are real children who still need to travel.**
>
> **A remainder cannot be thrown away when it is people.**
>
> **The division is correct; the QUESTION is asking something slightly different** — not "how many full vans?" but "how many vans before nobody is left standing?"

---

A farmer packs 617 mangoes into crates of 24. Answer three questions: how many full crates? how many crates does he need? how many mangoes are left unpacked if he only uses full crates?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 617 ÷ 24 = 25 remainder 17      (24 × 25 = 600)
>
> full crates          → 25       (ignore the remainder)
> crates needed        → 26       (round up — the 17 still need a crate)
> left unpacked        → 17       (the remainder IS the answer)
> ```
> **One division, three different answers.**
>
> **Why: the arithmetic settles what happened, but only the wording settles what to REPORT.**
>
> **Always read what is being counted before deciding what to do with the remainder.** That reading is the real work; the dividing is the easy part.

---

₹6,300 is shared equally among 7 workers. What does each get? What if it were shared among 14 instead — and how much money would be needed for all 14 to get the original amount?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> among 7  : 6,300 ÷ 7 = ₹900 each
> among 14 : 6,300 ÷ 14 = ₹450 each   — LESS, because the same money
>            is split into twice as many shares
> to give 14 workers ₹900 each: 14 × 900 = ₹12,600
> ```
> **Why doubling the workers halves the share: the money did not grow, so each extra person's share has to come out of somebody else's.**
>
> **And why ₹12,600 is needed to keep the share at ₹900: twice as many people at the same rate costs twice as much.**
>
> **These are the divisor-and-quotient rules from earlier, met in real life.**

A shopkeeper earned ₹9,913 selling coconut husk at ₹23 per kg. How many kilograms did he sell?

> [!ANSWER]
> **431 kg.**
> ```
> 23) 9,913 ( 400 + 30 + 1
>    −9,200          23 × 400
>      ---
>      713
>     −690           23 × 30
>      ---
>       23
>      −23           23 × 1
>      ---
>       00                   →  431
> check: 23 × 431 = 9,913 ✔
> ```
> **Why 400 was a good first chunk: 23 × 500 = 11,500, which overshoots 9,913, while 23 × 400 = 9,200 fits.**
>
> **Testing the round hundreds first is how you find the leading digit fast** — and it is exactly what long division does silently.

Tender coconuts bought at ₹20 are sold at ₹35. If ₹8,890 was earned from sales, how many were sold and what was the extra earning?

> [!ANSWER]
> **254 coconuts; extra earning ₹3,810.**
> ```
> number sold:  8,890 ÷ 35 = 254        (35 × 254 = 8,890 ✔)
> cost price :  254 × 20 = ₹5,080
> extra      :  8,890 − 5,080 = ₹3,810
> ```
> **Why the second step needed the first: you cannot find the cost until you know how many were bought.**
>
> **Multi-part problems are ladders** — each answer is the number the next rung needs.
>
> **Quick check: the profit is ₹15 per coconut, and 254 × 15 = ₹3,810 ✔** Two independent routes to the same number is the strongest check there is.

Complete Munshi Lal's record book: radish ₹26/kg × 78 kg; potato ₹20/kg for a total of ₹2,240; cabbage ₹32/kg × 56 kg; green peas 125 kg for ₹3,125. What did he earn in all?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> radish     : 26 × 78   = ₹2,028
> potato     : 2,240 ÷ 20 = 112 kg
> cabbage    : 32 × 56   = ₹1,792
> green peas : 3,125 ÷ 125 = ₹25 per kg
> total      : 2,028 + 2,240 + 1,792 + 3,125 = ₹9,185
> ```
> **Why two rows needed division and two needed multiplication: it depends on which box is empty.**
> ```
> rate and quantity known  →  MULTIPLY to get the total
> total and rate known     →  DIVIDE to get the quantity
> total and quantity known →  DIVIDE to get the rate
> ```
> **All three are the same fact family — Total = Rate × Quantity — with a different number hidden each time.**

A carpenter needs 4 large clips per shelf and has 120. He needs 32 screws per shelf and has 2,800. He needs 8 short panels and has 306. How many shelves can he make?

> [!ANSWER]
> **30 shelves.**
> ```
> 120 ÷ 4    = 30          ← smallest
> 2,800 ÷ 32 = 87 remainder 16
> 306 ÷ 8    = 38 remainder 2
> ```
> **Why the SMALLEST answer wins: a shelf needs ALL the parts.**
>
> **Once the large clips run out at 30 shelves, the leftover screws and panels are useless — there is nothing to fasten them with.**
>
> **The scarcest ingredient sets the limit, exactly as the eggs do when you are baking.** Having plenty of flour does not let you make more cakes.

Naina buys 5 kg of ice cream, shares it equally among 23 friends, and 400 g is left. How much did each friend eat?

> [!ANSWER]
> **200 g each.**
> ```
> 5 kg = 5,000 g
> shared out: 5,000 − 400 = 4,600 g
> each friend: 4,600 ÷ 23 = 200 g
> ```
> **Why you must subtract the leftover FIRST: the 400 g never got shared, so it was never part of anybody's portion.**
>
> **Dividing 5,000 by 23 would spread the leftover among the friends too** — and give an answer that doesn't match what actually happened.
>
> **Check: 23 × 200 + 400 = 4,600 + 400 = 5,000 ✔** This is N = D × Q + R doing its job.

Megha packs 15 packets of biscuits for a 4-day trip. Each packet has 8 biscuits and there are 6 people. How many biscuits does each person get per day?

> [!ANSWER]
> **5 biscuits.**
> ```
> total biscuits : 15 × 8 = 120
> per person     : 120 ÷ 6 = 20
> per person per day : 20 ÷ 4 = 5
> ```
> **Why it takes three steps: the question shares the biscuits twice over — first among people, then across days.**
>
> **The order does not matter — 120 ÷ 4 = 30 a day, then 30 ÷ 6 = 5 each — because dividing by 6 and then by 4 is the same as dividing by 24 either way round.**
>
> **Getting the same answer by the other route is a free check.**

I am a 3-digit number. Divided by 5, I give 42. Doubled, I give 420. Who am I?

> [!ANSWER]
> **210.**
> ```
> from the first clue : N ÷ 5 = 42, so N = 5 × 42 = 210
> from the second     : N × 2 = 420, so N = 420 ÷ 2 = 210 ✔
> ```
> **Why either clue alone was enough: each one is a fact family with one number hidden**, and N = D × Q lets you read it backwards.
>
> **So why give two clues?** Because agreeing answers make certain what one answer only makes likely. **210 is also genuinely 3-digit ✔** — the third condition quietly confirms it too.

Decide whether each is always, sometimes or never true, with a reason: (a) adding two odd numbers gives an even number; (b) doubling a number gives an odd number; (c) multiplying by 5 gives a number ending in 0.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (a) ALWAYS    (b) NEVER    (c) SOMETIMES
> ```
> **(a) Why always: every odd number is "an even number plus 1".** Add two of them and the two spare 1s pair up into another 2 — so the total is even. 7 + 9 = 16, 13 + 5 = 18.
>
> **(b) Why never: doubling makes two equal groups**, so the result can always be halved exactly — which is what "even" means. There is no escape.
>
> **(c) Why sometimes: 5 × 4 = 20 ends in 0, but 5 × 3 = 15 does not.** Even × 5 ends in 0; odd × 5 ends in 5.
>
> **Notice the different kinds of evidence needed: "sometimes" wants one hit AND one miss; "always" and "never" want a REASON, because no number of examples could ever cover them all.**

"Doubling one number and halving the other keeps the product the same." Is this always true? Show it, and explain why.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, always (whenever the halving can be done).**
> ```
> 16 × 25  →  8 × 50  = 400
>          →  4 × 100 = 400        much easier to see!
> 14 × 35  →  7 × 70  = 490
> ```
> **Why it always works: you multiplied the total by 2 and divided it by 2. Those two moves cancel exactly.**
>
> **Picture it as an array: cut the rectangle in half across and lay the two halves side by side.** The shape changed; the number of squares did not.
>
> **This is not a curiosity — it is a working tool.** 16 × 25 is unpleasant, 4 × 100 is instant, and they are the same sum.

Rani needs 250 samosas and can buy packs of 6 or packs of 8. Which pack should she choose?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Packs of 6 — she wastes less.**
> ```
> packs of 6 : 250 ÷ 6 = 41 remainder 4  →  42 packs = 252 samosas, 2 spare
> packs of 8 : 250 ÷ 8 = 31 remainder 2  →  32 packs = 256 samosas, 6 spare
> ```
> **Why neither divides exactly: 250 = 2 × 5 × 5 × 5, and neither 6 nor 8 is built only from those factors.** So some waste is unavoidable, and the real question is which wastes less.
>
> **Note that both remainders forced a round-up** — 41 packs would leave 4 guests without a samosa.
>
> **This is a question where the maths gives two valid answers and a REASON picks between them.** If she wanted fewer trips to the counter, 32 packs of 8 might win instead.

Sofia has only ₹50 and ₹20 notes and must pay exactly ₹520. Find all the ways.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Six ways.**
> ```
> ₹50 notes :  0    2    4    6    8   10
> ₹20 notes : 26   21   16   11    6    1
> check     : 0+520, 100+420, 200+320, 300+220, 400+120, 500+20  ✔
> ```
> **Why the number of ₹50 notes is always EVEN: ₹520 ends in 0, and ₹20 notes always give a multiple of 20.**
>
> **An odd number of ₹50 notes gives a total ending in 50** (like ₹150, ₹250), and no number of ₹20 notes can ever fix that — twenties never produce a 50 at the end.
>
> **So checking only 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 covers every possibility.** Spotting that halves the work and, more importantly, proves nothing was missed.
