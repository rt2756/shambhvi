Meena faces the rising Sun. Which direction is she facing, and what is behind her?

> [!ANSWER]
> **She faces EAST; WEST is behind her.**
> ```
> left hand  → north
> right hand → south
> behind     → west
> ```
> **Why the left hand points north: the four directions run N → E → S → W going CLOCKWISE.**
>
> **East is one quarter turn clockwise from north, so facing east puts north one quarter turn ANTICLOCKWISE from your face — which is your left.**
>
> **This is Chapter 3's quarter turns doing real work.** Knowing the clockwise order lets you rebuild the hand positions from anywhere, which a memorised rhyme cannot.

---

In the evening, Ravi faces the setting Sun. Where is north now?

> [!ANSWER]
> **He faces WEST, so north is on his RIGHT hand.**
> ```
> facing west : right hand → north
>               left hand  → south
>               behind     → east
> ```
> **Why everything reverses: he has turned round to face the opposite direction, so left and right swap over.**
>
> **The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west** — that single fact gives you two free compasses a day, one in the morning and one in the evening.
>
> **Check by reasoning, not memory: from west, one quarter turn clockwise gives north, and clockwise from your face is your right ✔**

---

A cyclist is heading north and takes a right turn. Which way is she now going? What if she had turned left?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Right turn → EAST. Left turn → WEST.**
> ```
> a RIGHT turn is a quarter turn CLOCKWISE:
>    north → east → south → west → north
> a LEFT turn is a quarter turn ANTICLOCKWISE:
>    north → west → south → east → north
> ```
> **Why you can work this out instead of imagining yourself on the bicycle: the four directions sit in a fixed clockwise circle**, and a quarter turn simply moves you one place round it.
>
> **This never fails, even for a route with six turns in it.** Imagining yourself in the saddle usually does.

---

A bus heads south, turns right, then right again. Which way is it going?

> [!ANSWER]
> **North.**
> ```
> south ─right→ west ─right→ north
> ```
> **Why two right turns reverse your direction: each right turn is a quarter turn, so two of them make a HALF turn** — and a half turn always points you the opposite way.
>
> **You can check with any starting direction:** east → south → west, and east and west are opposites ✔
>
> **Four right turns would bring it back to south**, because four quarter turns make a full turn — the same counting as the firki in Chapter 10.

---

Why do maps use "north" instead of "left" in their directions?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because "left" describes the PERSON and "north" describes the PLACE.**
>
> **Two people standing back to back both point "left" — in opposite directions.** The word depends entirely on which way you happen to be facing.
>
> **North is the same for everybody, everywhere on the map.** Two people who disagree about left will always agree about north.
>
> **This is exactly why every map carries a NORTH ARROW.** Without it, "the hospital is to the left" is meaningless — left of whom?

---

A map is printed with its north arrow pointing to the right of the page. Something is drawn above another thing. Which direction is it in?

> [!ANSWER]
> **West.**
> ```
> if the arrow points RIGHT, then right = north
> going clockwise from there: down = east, left = south, up = west
> ```
> **Why you cannot just assume up is north: only MOST maps are printed with north up.** The arrow, not the paper, decides.
>
> **How to work it out safely: turn the page (or your head) until the arrow points up.** Now the usual rules apply, and you can read off the answer.
>
> **Checking the arrow first is step one of every map question**, and skipping it causes nearly every mistake.

---

On a map with north up, Golu's house is drawn below and to the right of Tinku's. Describe its position in directions.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Golu's house is to the SOUTH-EAST of Tinku's.**
> ```
> with north up:   up = north     down = south
>                  right = east   left = west
> below → south,   to the right → east
> ```
> **Why one word is not enough: the house is displaced in TWO directions at once**, so a single direction would be only half the story.
>
> **South-east is the direction exactly between south and east** — it means "some of each".
>
> **Note this is a description of Golu relative to Tinku.** Tinku is north-west of Golu — the same fact, told from the other end, exactly like factors and multiples.

---

Why does a map need a key, instead of just drawing everything as it really looks?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because things drawn to true scale would be too small to see.**
>
> **A hospital drawn to true scale on a street map would be a grey speck.** You would not recognise it and could not find it.
>
> **A symbol is deliberately the WRONG size, so that it CAN be seen. The key is the promise that says what the exaggeration means.**
>
> **Every map trades some truth for usefulness**, and the key is where that bargain is written down. A map with no key is a picture, not a map.

---

Two metro routes go from A to B. One has 14 stations and no change of line. The other has 9 stations but two changes. Which is better?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It depends on what "better" means — and the question cannot be answered until you say.**
> ```
> fewest stations  →  the 9-station route
> fewest changes   →  the 14-station route
> least total time →  probably the 14-station route
> ```
> **Why the longer ride often wins on time: every change costs several minutes** — walking to another platform and waiting for the next train. **Two changes can easily cost more than five extra stations.**
>
> **And a no-change route is far easier with a group of children**, since nobody can get lost on a platform.
>
> **Being clear about what you are optimising is half of every real-world problem.** "Best" with no "best for what" is not a question yet.

---

Why should you never use a metro map to judge how far apart two stations are?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because metro maps stretch and bend the real geography on purpose.**
>
> **Their job is to show which stations CONNECT to which**, so the lines are drawn as neat straights and gentle bends that are easy to follow.
>
> **Real distances and directions are sacrificed to make that possible.** Two stations that look next door may be several kilometres apart, and a line drawn going "up" may run east on the ground.
>
> **A map is always a bargain between truth and usefulness**, and this one has traded away distance entirely.

---

On a grid, what is the difference between the point (5,2) and the point (2,5)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are two completely different places.**
> ```
> (5,2) → 5 steps across (east), then 2 steps up (north)
> (2,5) → 2 steps across, then 5 up
> ```
> **Why the order matters so much: a pair of numbers cannot locate anything until everyone agrees which number is which.** Without a rule, "5 and 2" is just two numbers.
>
> **So the agreement is: ACROSS FIRST, THEN UP.**
>
> **A memory hook that follows the reasoning: you walk along the corridor before you climb the stairs.** Across, then up.

---

A lion is 3 steps east and 7 steps north of the corner. Write its coordinates.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(3,7).**
>
> **Why not (7,3): the first number is always the ACROSS number.** 7 steps across and 3 up would put the lion somewhere else entirely.
>
> **How to check yourself: read the pair back as a set of instructions** — "go 3 across, then 7 up" — and see whether it matches the description you were given.
>
> **Reading it back out loud catches the swap almost every time.**

---

Anu counts the corner of the grid as square number 1, so she writes the first crossing as (2,2) instead of (1,1). What has gone wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **She started counting at 1 instead of 0. Every one of her answers will be one step out.**
>
> **Why the corner must be 0: the numbers count STEPS TAKEN, not squares visited.** Standing at the corner, you have taken no steps at all — so it is (0,0).
>
> **After one step east and one step north you have taken one of each, giving (1,1).**
>
> **This is exactly the fence-post trap from earlier chapters.** Counting the starting point as one is the commonest off-by-one mistake there is.

---

An ant crawls from (0,0) to (4,3), moving only east and north. How many steps is that? Does the route matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **7 steps, and no — every staircase route is the same length.**
> ```
> E E E E N N N        N E N E N E E        E N E N E N E
> ```
> **Why the count cannot change: the ant MUST make 4 eastward steps to cross 4 columns and 3 northward steps to climb 3 rows.**
>
> **Each east step brings it one column closer; each north step one row closer.** There is no way to save any of them.
>
> **The ORDER can be shuffled any way you like, but the COUNT cannot.** 4 + 3 = 7, always — so there is no clever shortcut to find.

---

How far apart are (2,1) and (7,5) on a grid, moving only along the lines?

> [!ANSWER]
> **9 steps.**
> ```
> across : 7 − 2 = 5 steps east
> up     : 5 − 1 = 4 steps north
> total  : 5 + 4 = 9
> ```
> **Why you can subtract instead of tracing a path: the number of columns to cross is just the difference of the across-numbers**, and the same for the rows.
>
> **And since every staircase route has the same length, that total IS the distance.**
>
> **You never have to draw a route** — which is exactly the point of using coordinates in the first place.

---

Could the ant ever take a longer route between the same two grid points? How?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — by going west or south at some point and then undoing it.**
> ```
> from (0,0) to (2,0):  E E                  = 2 steps
>                       E E E W              = 4 steps (one wasted pair)
> ```
> **Why every wasted move costs TWO steps, not one: the wrong-way move takes you further off, and a second step is needed to come back.**
>
> **So all the shortest routes use only east and north, and any route that uses west or south is strictly longer.**
>
> **This is why "all staircase routes are equal" is not the same as "all routes are equal".** Only the ones that never turn back tie for first place.

---

The panda is at (1,1) and the tortoise at (4,1). What direction is the tortoise from the panda, and how far?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Due east, 3 steps.**
> ```
> across : 4 − 1 = 3 steps east
> up     : 1 − 1 = 0 steps
> ```
> **Why "due east" with no north or south in it: the second numbers are EQUAL, so no climbing is needed at all.**
>
> **When the up-numbers match, the two points are on the same row — a straight east-west line.**
>
> **Likewise, matching across-numbers put two points on the same column, a straight north-south line.** Spotting this saves you working out a direction that isn't there.

---

Write instructions for an ant to crawl from (1,2) to (5,6), then say why "go north-east" would not be enough.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> crawl 4 cm east, then 4 cm north
> (or 4 north then 4 east, or any staircase mixture — all 8 steps)
> ```
> **Why "go north-east" is not enough: it gives a direction but no DISTANCE.** The ant would set off correctly and never know when to stop.
>
> **Every leg of every route needs both a distance and a direction.**
>
> **This is the same discipline as writing "5 kg" rather than "5".** A number with no unit, or a move with no direction, does not actually say anything.

---

A treasure map says "from the well, go 3 steps north, 4 east, 2 south, 4 west". Where do you end up?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1 step north of the well.**
> ```
> north–south : 3 north − 2 south = 1 north
> east–west   : 4 east  − 4 west  = 0
> ```
> **Why you may add up the north-south moves separately from the east-west ones: they are independent.** Walking east never changes how far north you are.
>
> **Opposite directions cancel** — that is why 4 east and 4 west leave nothing behind.
>
> **The whole walk of 13 steps ends just 1 step from where it started.** A long route is not the same thing as a distant destination.

---

A tent is west of the road, and another is north of the green tent, which is closest to the lake. On a north-up map, where do you look for each?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> "west of the road"         →  LEFT of the road on the page
> "closest to the lake"      →  measure, don't guess — compare the gaps
> "north of the green tent"  →  directly ABOVE the green one on the page
> ```
> **Why the clues must be taken in order: the purple tent's clue mentions the green tent**, so the green one has to be found first.
>
> **Clues that refer to each other form a chain**, and starting in the middle of a chain leaves you with nothing to hold on to.
>
> **Look for the clue that depends on nothing else, solve that one, and the rest unlock in turn.** It is the same "ladder" idea as multi-step arithmetic.

---

A bus leaves the parking area heading north, turns right onto Hospital Road, and drives to Stop 1. In which direction is Stop 1 from the parking area?

> [!ANSWER]
> **North and then east of it — so north-east overall.**
> ```
> leg 1 : north out of the parking area
> turn right while heading north → now heading EAST
> leg 2 : east along Hospital Road
> ```
> **Why the turn gives east: a right turn is a quarter turn clockwise, and one step clockwise from north is east.**
>
> **Why the overall direction is "north-east" and not simply "east": the bus finished both north AND east of where it began**, so neither word alone is the whole truth.
>
> **The route describes the journey; north-east describes the result.** They are different questions and often have different answers.

---

Prem's house is at (9,4) and Stop 2 is at (9,9). Which way must Prem walk?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Due north, 5 steps.**
> ```
> across : 9 − 9 = 0     up : 9 − 4 = 5 north
> ```
> **Why no east or west is needed: the across-numbers are identical, so the two places are on the same column.**
>
> **A straight walk north is the shortest possible route** — there is nothing sideways to undo.
>
> **Coordinates answer this in one glance.** Reading it off a street map by eye would take much longer and be far less certain.

---

Which is longer: (0,0) to (6,1), or (0,0) to (4,3)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are the same — 7 steps each.**
> ```
> (6,1) : 6 + 1 = 7 steps
> (4,3) : 4 + 3 = 7 steps
> ```
> **Why two very different-looking journeys tie: grid distance depends only on the TOTAL of the two moves**, not on how they are split.
>
> **One is a long dash with a small climb; the other is a broad staircase. The ant does the same amount of work.**
>
> **Careful, though — this is only true for an ant confined to the lines.** A bird flying straight would find (6,1) noticeably further.

---

Design a set of grid clues that leads a friend from (0,0) to (5,4), passing through (5,0).

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> "Go 5 steps east.  Now go 4 steps north."
> ```
> **Why this route passes through (5,0): after the first instruction the friend has moved 5 across and 0 up** — which is exactly the point (5,0).
>
> **Why it is still a shortest route: it uses only east and north moves**, 5 + 4 = 9 steps, the minimum.
>
> **Every point on any shortest route has both its numbers between the start's and the finish's.** That is a quick way to check whether a required stop is even possible without a detour.
