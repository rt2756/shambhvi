Three runners finish in 1 min 52 sec, 1 min 49 sec and 1 min 55 sec. Who wins, and why would minutes alone be useless here?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The 1 min 49 sec runner wins — the shortest time is the fastest.**
>
> **Why minutes alone fail: measured in whole minutes, all three results read "1 minute".** The race would have no winner.
>
> **The measurement would have thrown away exactly the information the race was about.**
>
> **A smaller unit gives a finer measurement.** Seconds slice each minute into sixty pieces, so differences of a few sixtieths become visible.

---

Why is an hour divided into 60 minutes rather than 100?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because 60 divides beautifully and 100 does not.**
> ```
> 60 has factors 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60
> half an hour   = 30 min ✔      a fifth  = 12 min ✔
> a third        = 20 min ✔      a sixth  = 10 min ✔
> a quarter      = 15 min ✔      a twelfth = 5 min ✔
> ```
> **Every one is a whole number of minutes.**
>
> **With 100, a third would be 33⅓ minutes** — you would have to say "thirty-three and a third minutes past" every time you meant a third of an hour.
>
> **So 60 was not an accident.** It was chosen thousands of years ago precisely because it shares out so many ways without breaking.

---

For each activity, say whether it takes seconds or minutes: blinking, washing hands, snapping fingers, melting an ice cube, counting to 20.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> SECONDS : blinking · snapping fingers · counting to 20
> MINUTES : washing hands · melting an ice cube
> ```
> **The dividing line: could you finish before you had counted to sixty?** If yes, it is seconds.
>
> **Why choosing the right unit matters even here: "the ice cube melted in 300 seconds" is true but hard to picture.** "Five minutes" says the same thing in a form a person can feel.
>
> **A good measurement is one that gives a comfortable, ordinary-sized number** — the same rule that governed grams and kilograms.

---

Convert 3 hours 25 minutes into minutes.

> [!ANSWER]
> **205 minutes.**
> ```
> 3 hours = 3 × 60 = 180 minutes
> 180 + 25 = 205 minutes
> ```
> **Why we multiply going from hours to minutes: a minute is SMALLER than an hour, so it takes MORE of them** to fill the same time.
>
> **This is the same "big unit to small unit, multiply" rule as kilograms to grams** — only the number is 60 instead of 1,000.
>
> **You never need to remember the direction. Just ask whether the number should get bigger or smaller.**

---

Convert 260 minutes into hours and minutes.

> [!ANSWER]
> **4 hours 20 minutes.**
> ```
> 260 ÷ 60 = 4 remainder 20
> check: 60 × 4 + 20 = 240 + 20 = 260 ✔
> ```
> **Why you must actually divide, and cannot use the digit-covering trick: 60 is not a power of ten.**
>
> **With weight, 5,050 g split into 5 kg and 050 g just by looking, because 1,000 has three zeros.** Time has no such neat split.
>
> **Notice that N = D × Q + R is doing the work: the quotient is the hours and the REMAINDER is the leftover minutes.**

---

Convert 470 minutes into hours and minutes without doing long division.

> [!ANSWER]
> **7 hours 50 minutes.**
> ```
> count up in hours: 60, 120, 180, 240, 300, 360, 420  → that's 7 hours
> 470 − 420 = 50 minutes left
> ```
> **Why counting up is often better than dividing: seven steps of easy addition are far less error-prone than one awkward division by 60.**
>
> **It also shows you the answer building up**, so a silly result is obvious immediately.
>
> **Check: 7 × 60 + 50 = 420 + 50 = 470 ✔**

---

Convert 12 minutes 45 seconds into seconds, and 500 seconds into minutes and seconds.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 12 min 45 sec = (12 × 60) + 45 = 720 + 45 = 765 seconds
> 500 seconds   = 500 ÷ 60 = 8 remainder 20  →  8 min 20 sec
> ```
> **Why the same two rules serve both conversions: minutes to seconds is ×60, exactly like hours to minutes.**
>
> **The ladder is hours → minutes → seconds, and every step is 60.**
>
> **Learn the move once and it works twice over** — which is exactly what happened with mg → g → kg in Chapter 8.

---

Write these in 24-hour format: 07:20 a.m., 04:45 p.m., 11:10 p.m.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 07:20 a.m.  →  07:20 hours     (a.m. times are unchanged)
> 04:45 p.m.  →  16:45 hours     (4 + 12 = 16)
> 11:10 p.m.  →  23:10 hours     (11 + 12 = 23)
> ```
> **Why p.m. times get 12 added: p.m. means "after midday", and midday is 12 hours after midnight.**
>
> **So a p.m. time is that many hours past 12** — counting from midnight instead means adding those 12 on.
>
> **Any 24-hour time with 13 or more in front is certainly p.m.**, because the morning ran out at 12.

---

Write 18:25 hours and 06:05 hours in 12-hour format.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 18:25 hours  →  18 − 12 = 6   →  06:25 p.m.
> 06:05 hours  →  under 12, so unchanged  →  06:05 a.m.
> ```
> **Why we subtract 12 going back: adding 12 turned a p.m. time into hours-since-midnight, so taking 12 away undoes it.**
>
> **Every conversion rule can be run backwards** — it is a fact family, exactly like N = D × Q.
>
> **The test for which case you are in: is the hour 13 or more?** If yes, subtract 12 and label it p.m.

---

What is 12:00 midnight in 24-hour format? What about 12:00 noon?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 12:00 midnight  =  00:00 hours
> 12:00 noon      =  12:00 hours
> ```
> **Why midnight is 00 and not 24: at midnight the NEW day has just begun, and none of its hours have gone by yet.**
>
> **Writing 24:00 would mean the end of the OLD day — the same instant, but it makes the date ambiguous.** So 00:00 is used, and the date moves on with it.
>
> **Why noon needs no change: 12 hours really have passed since midnight**, so 12 is already the right count.
>
> **These two are the only odd cases in the whole system, and they trip up nearly everybody.**

---

Why do railway timetables use the 24-hour clock?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because "the train leaves at 8:00" is ambiguous — morning or evening?**
>
> **The 12-hour clock uses each number TWICE a day**, so it always needs an a.m. or p.m. tag to be understood. Lose the tag — smudged ink, a hurried note — and the meaning is gone.
>
> **The 24-hour clock gives every moment of the day its OWN number**, from 00:00 to 23:59. No tag is needed, and no two moments share a name.
>
> **The same reasoning is why hospitals, airlines and the army use it.** When being misread is dangerous, ambiguity is not acceptable.

---

How much time passes from 02:20 p.m. to 06:20 p.m.? And from 09:10 a.m. to 09:55 a.m.?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 02:20 p.m. → 06:20 p.m. : the MINUTES already match, so exactly 4 hours
> 09:10 a.m. → 09:55 a.m. : the HOUR already matches, so 55 − 10 = 45 minutes
> ```
> **Why these need no working: look at the two times before you start.**
>
> **If the minutes agree, only whole hours have passed. If the hours agree, only minutes have.**
>
> **Half of all elapsed-time questions are one of these two easy cases**, and spotting that saves you doing anything at all.

---

Find the time from 08:40 a.m. to 11:15 a.m. by counting on.

> [!ANSWER]
> **2 hours 35 minutes.**
> ```
> 08:40  ──+20 min──▶  09:00      (up to the next whole hour first)
> 09:00  ──+2 hr ──▶  11:00
> 11:00  ──+15 min──▶  11:15
>                      20 min + 2 hr + 15 min  =  2 hr 35 min
> ```
> **Why jumping to the next whole hour first: it settles the awkward part while the numbers are still small.**
>
> **After that, the hours are trivial and the last jump is a simple count of minutes.**
>
> **Nothing needs to be borrowed at any point** — which is the whole reason this method is safer than subtraction.

---

Amit subtracts in columns: 11:30 minus 09:45, and writes 1:85. What went wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **He borrowed 10 instead of 60. The answer is 1 hour 45 minutes.**
> ```
> WRONG                 RIGHT
>   11:30                 11:30 is 10 hr 90 min   (1 hour borrowed = 60 min)
> − 09:45               − 09:45
>   -----                 --------
>    1:85                  1 hr 45 min
> ```
> **Why 1:85 must be wrong even before you check: there is no 85th minute of an hour.** Any answer with 60 or more minutes is a signal that a borrow went wrong.
>
> **Column subtraction assumes each place borrows TEN from the next.** In time, minutes borrow **sixty** from hours — the same trap as kilograms and grams borrowing 1,000.
>
> **Counting on never meets this problem at all**, which is why it is the safer habit.

---

Jyoti goes out to play at 05:45 p.m. and comes back after 1 hour 30 minutes. When does she return?

> [!ANSWER]
> **07:15 p.m.**
> ```
> 05:45 + 1 hour   = 06:45
> 06:45 + 30 min   = 07:15 p.m.
> ```
> **Why add the HOURS first: doing the minutes first (45 + 30 = 75) immediately pushes you past an hour and forces an extra regrouping.**
>
> **Hours-first keeps the awkward step to the very end, where it is easiest to see.**
>
> **Check by counting backwards: 07:15 − 30 min = 06:45, − 1 hr = 05:45 ✔**

---

Ragini finished her homework at 09:40 p.m. It took her 1 hour 10 minutes. When did she start?

> [!ANSWER]
> **08:30 p.m.**
> ```
> 09:40 − 1 hour  = 08:40
> 08:40 − 10 min  = 08:30 p.m.
> ```
> **Why we count BACKWARDS here: we know the end and the duration, and want the start.**
>
> **START + DURATION = END is a fact family** — any two of the three give the third, exactly like N = D × Q.
>
> **Check forwards: 08:30 + 1 hr 10 min = 09:40 ✔** Running it the other way is the cheapest possible check.

---

A picnic party leaves at 08:30 a.m. and returns after 4 hours 10 minutes. When do they return?

> [!ANSWER]
> **12:40 p.m.**
> ```
> 08:30 + 4 hours = 12:30
> 12:30 + 10 min  = 12:40 p.m.
> ```
> **Watch the label: the party crossed NOON, so a.m. became p.m.**
>
> **Why 12:30 is p.m. and not a.m.: midday has just gone by, so the afternoon has started.** The half-hour after noon belongs to p.m., not a.m.
>
> **This is one of the two moments in the day when the label flips**, and it is the one people get wrong most often.

---

It is 08:35 p.m. What time will it be after 8 hours and 25 minutes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **05:00 a.m. the NEXT day.**
> ```
> 08:35 p.m. = 20:35 in 24-hour time
> 20:35 + 8 hr = 28:35  — past 24, so subtract 24 → 04:35 next day
> 04:35 + 25 min = 05:00 next morning
> ```
> **Why subtracting 24 works: a whole day has gone by**, so the clock has returned to the same reading with the date moved on one.
>
> **Why 24-hour time makes this much easier: there is no a.m./p.m. to forget flipping.** In 12-hour time you would have to notice midnight passing and switch the label.
>
> **This is precisely why timetables use the 24-hour clock.**

---

Raghav studies four subjects for 50 minutes each. How long is that in hours and minutes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **3 hours 20 minutes.**
> ```
> 4 × 50 = 200 minutes
> 200 ÷ 60 = 3 remainder 20  →  3 hours 20 minutes
> ```
> **Why do the multiplying in ONE unit and convert only at the end: trying to keep hours and minutes separate while multiplying invites mistakes.**
>
> **This is the same advice as Chapter 8's "when the numbers are ugly, convert first".**
>
> **Sense-check: 4 lots of 50 minutes is a bit less than 4 hours, since 50 is a bit less than 60.** 3 hr 20 min fits ✔

---

A film starts at 06:50 p.m. and runs for 2 hours 40 minutes. When does it end?

> [!ANSWER]
> **09:30 p.m.**
> ```
> 06:50 + 2 hours = 08:50
> 08:50 + 10 min  = 09:00      (jump to the whole hour first)
> 09:00 + 30 min  = 09:30 p.m.
> ```
> **Why splitting the 40 minutes into 10 + 30: it lets you land exactly on 09:00 on the way.**
>
> **Round numbers are easy to add to; awkward ones are not.** Choosing where to break the jump is the whole skill.
>
> **50 + 40 = 90 minutes would also work — 1 hour 30 — but you have to remember to carry the extra hour.** The jump method never asks you to remember anything.

---

School lunch starts at 12:25 p.m. and lasts 40 minutes. When does it end?

> [!ANSWER]
> **01:05 p.m.**
> ```
> 12:25 + 35 min = 01:00 p.m.     (up to the next whole hour)
> 01:00 + 5 min  = 01:05 p.m.
> ```
> **Why the hour goes from 12 to 1 and not to 13: on a 12-hour clock the numbers run 12, 1, 2, 3 …** The hour after 12 is 1, not 13.
>
> **It stays p.m. throughout** — the flip happened at noon, before lunch started.
>
> **In 24-hour time this is 12:25 + 40 min = 13:05**, which is tidier because the numbers just keep counting up.

---

A cricket match starts at 09:30 a.m. and ends at 04:15 p.m. How long did it last?

> [!ANSWER]
> **6 hours 45 minutes.**
> ```
> 09:30 a.m. = 09:30      04:15 p.m. = 16:15
> 09:30 ──+30 min──▶ 10:00
> 10:00 ──+6 hr ──▶ 16:00
> 16:00 ──+15 min──▶ 16:15
>                    30 min + 6 hr + 15 min = 6 hr 45 min
> ```
> **Why converting to 24-hour time first was worth it: the match crosses noon, so counting in 12-hour time means switching from a.m. to p.m. halfway through.**
>
> **In 24-hour time the numbers simply increase, and nothing special happens at midday.**
>
> **The awkward moment has been removed rather than navigated** — always a better plan than being careful.

---

A bus leaves every 25 minutes starting at 07:00 a.m. When is the fourth bus?

> [!ANSWER]
> **08:15 a.m.**
> ```
> 1st  07:00
> 2nd  07:25      (one gap of 25 min after the first)
> 3rd  07:50      (two gaps)
> 4th  08:15      (THREE gaps: 3 × 25 = 75 min = 1 hr 15 min)
> 07:00 + 1 hr 15 min = 08:15 ✔
> ```
> **Why THREE gaps and not four: the first bus leaves at the start, before any waiting has happened.**
>
> **Counting gaps rather than buses is the whole trick.** Four buses have three gaps between them, just as four fence posts have three panels.
>
> **Multiplying by 4 would give 08:40 — a bus that does not exist.**

---

Sunil says "10:15 p.m. in 24-hour time is 10:15 hours." Correct him.

> [!ANSWER]
> **It is 22:15 hours.**
> ```
> 10 + 12 = 22   →   22:15 hours
> ```
> **Why he is wrong: 10:15 hours is a quarter past ten in the MORNING** — a completely different time, twelve hours out.
>
> **The rule he forgot: a.m. times stay as they are; p.m. times get 12 added.**
>
> **A quick check that would have caught it: 24-hour times before 12 are always morning.** If you have written a p.m. time with an hour under 12, something has gone wrong.

---

Two clocks show 03:40 p.m. and 15:40 hours. Are they showing different times?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — they are showing the SAME moment.**
> ```
> 03:40 p.m.  →  3 + 12 = 15  →  15:40 hours ✔
> ```
> **Why they look so different: they are two ways of NAMING one instant, not two instants.**
>
> **This is exactly like 5 kg 50 g and 5,050 g from Chapter 8** — different notation, identical quantity.
>
> **The habit worth building: convert both to the same format before comparing.** Judging by appearance fails here just as it did with weights.

---

An exam runs from 10:00 a.m. to 01:00 p.m., with a 15-minute break in the middle. How long is the actual writing time?

> [!ANSWER]
> **2 hours 45 minutes.**
> ```
> total elapsed: 10:00 a.m. to 01:00 p.m. = 3 hours
> minus the break:  3 hr − 15 min = 2 hr 45 min
> ```
> **Why you must subtract the break: elapsed time counts every minute between start and finish, including the ones nobody was writing in.**
>
> **The clock does not know what you are doing** — it only measures how much time went by.
>
> **This is a common trap in timetable questions.** Always ask whether the question wants the total elapsed time or only the working part of it.
