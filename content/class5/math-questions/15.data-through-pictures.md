A survey of 40 children records: ½ hr — 6, 1 hr — 12, 1½ hr — 10, 2 hr — 8, more than 2 hr — 4. How many watch less than 2 hours? How many watch at most 2 hours?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> less than 2 hours : 6 + 12 + 10       = 28 children
> at most 2 hours   : 6 + 12 + 10 + 8   = 36 children
> ```
> **Why the two answers differ by 8: "less than 2" EXCLUDES the children who said exactly 2 hours; "at most 2" INCLUDES them.**
>
> **Read the boundary words with care.** "less than", "more than", "at least" and "at most" each draw the line in a different place.
>
> **Most wrong answers in data questions come from misreading the boundary, not from adding badly.**
>
> **Check the table first: 6 + 12 + 10 + 8 + 4 = 40 ✔**

---

Why is a table already doing mathematics, before any sum is worked out?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because it has GROUPED the answers and put them IN ORDER.**
>
> **Forty loose replies become five numbers.** Every child who said "1 hour" is now a single entry, 12. Nothing has been lost, but the mess is gone.
>
> **And the rows run from least to most.** That order is what makes "how many watch less than 2 hours?" answerable at a glance instead of by re-reading forty replies.
>
> **Data is not information until it is organised.** The table is the first step of every question in this chapter.

A pictograph shows 🧸🧸🧸🧸🧸🧸 for toys, with the key "🧸 = 5 toys". How many toys are there? What if the key said 10?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> key = 5  →  6 × 5  = 30 toys
> key = 10 →  6 × 10 = 60 toys
> ```
> **The same six pictures mean two completely different numbers.**
>
> **Why: the pictures themselves carry no quantity at all.** The KEY is the sentence that turns pictures into numbers.
>
> **Cover the key and the graph says nothing.** So the key is not a label on the side — it is the most important line on the page, and reading it first is the habit to build.

A shop has 45 board games. Using a scale of 1 icon = 5, how many icons must be drawn?

> [!ANSWER]
> **9 icons.**
> ```
> 45 ÷ 5 = 9
> ```
> **Why DRAWING is a division while READING is a multiplication:**
> ```
> reading : 9 icons × 5 each = 45 games
> drawing : 45 games ÷ 5 each = 9 icons
> ```
> **They are the same fact family from Chapter 9**, so a mistake in one direction is caught by checking the other.
>
> **Always run the check.** It costs one multiplication and catches almost every slip.

---

A pictograph uses ▮ = 10 books and shows ▮ ▮ ▮ ▯ (three whole and one half). How many books?

> [!ANSWER]
> **35 books.**
> ```
> 3 whole icons × 10 = 30
> 1 half icon        =  5
> total              = 35
> ```
> **Why half icons exist: without them the graph could only ever show 10, 20, 30, 40 …** and any real number in between would be impossible to draw.
>
> **A half icon means half the key value** — here half of 10, which is 5.
>
> **This works cleanly because 10 is even.** With an odd key, half an icon means an awkward fraction — which is the next question.

---

Rani wants to draw a pictograph of 20, 14 and 22 vehicles using 1 icon = 3. Why won't it work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because 3 does not divide any of them exactly.**
> ```
> 20 ÷ 3 = 6 r 2      14 ÷ 3 = 4 r 2      22 ÷ 3 = 7 r 1
> ```
> **And half an icon would mean 1½ vehicles — nonsense for something you count.**
>
> **Why the scale must divide the data: every value has to be drawable as a whole (or half) number of icons.**
>
> **Check that your scale divides every value BEFORE you commit to it.** A scale of 2 would work here for 20, 14 and 22 — all even.

---

Data values are 40, 25, 30 and 15. Which scale would you choose for a pictograph, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1 icon = 5.**
> ```
> 40 ÷ 5 = 8      25 ÷ 5 = 5      30 ÷ 5 = 6      15 ÷ 5 = 3
> ```
> **Why 5: it is a COMMON FACTOR of all four values, so every one draws exactly, with no halves and no remainders.**
>
> **Why not 1: you would draw 110 icons and nobody could count them.**
>
> **Why not 10: 25 and 15 would need half icons, and while that works, 5 is tidier.**
>
> **The scale is chosen AFTER looking at the data, never before** — and Chapter 13's common factors are exactly the tool for choosing it.

Deepti records two-wheelers with 1 icon = 3: Monday 7 icons, Wednesday 4, Friday 6. Find the daily counts, the total, and how many fewer on Wednesday than Monday.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> Monday    7 × 3 = 21
> Wednesday 4 × 3 = 12
> Friday    6 × 3 = 18
> total             51 two-wheelers
> Wednesday is 21 − 12 = 9 fewer than Monday
> ```
> **Why the scale of 3 works here: 21, 12 and 18 are all multiples of 3**, so each draws as a whole number of icons.
>
> **Sense-check on the total: 51 ÷ 3 = 17, and 7 + 4 + 6 = 17 icons ✔** The icons and the vehicles agree.
>
> **Comparing days is done on the VALUES, not the icons** — though here, since the scale is the same throughout, the icon difference (3) times the scale gives the same 9.

---

Why must every bar in a bar graph start at 0?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the eye compares HEIGHTS, and a bar that starts higher up looks shorter than it is.**
> ```
> honest : a bar for 8 is twice as tall as a bar for 4
> cut off at 3 : the 8-bar shows 5 and the 4-bar shows 1 —
>                it now looks FIVE times as tall
> ```
> **The numbers would still be printed correctly, but the picture would tell a different story from the numbers.**
>
> **A bar graph is meant to be read at a glance.** A bar that doesn't start at 0 is a lie told with a picture, and it is one of the commonest tricks in advertisements.

---

Why must all the bars be the same width?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because width carries no information — only height does.**
>
> **A wider bar catches the eye and suggests importance that is not in the data.** The reader would come away thinking that category mattered more, for no reason at all.
>
> **Equal widths and equal gaps make the graph fair: the only thing that varies is the one thing that is supposed to vary.**
>
> **The third rule matches it: the scale up the side must be evenly spaced.** If 1 to 2 took a centimetre and 2 to 3 took three, comparing heights would be meaningless.

---

Sheela's day: sleeping 9, school 6, studying 2, eating and playing 4, other 3 hours. Check her data and find how many more hours she sleeps than studies.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total : 9 + 6 + 2 + 4 + 3 = 24 hours ✔
> sleep − study : 9 − 2 = 7 hours more
> ```
> **Why the total check matters: a day has exactly 24 hours.** If her numbers came to 22, an activity has been left out; if 26, something has been double-counted.
>
> **Every dataset has some built-in check like this** — percentages summing to 100, a class register summing to the class size.
>
> **Finding and using that check is the cheapest way to catch an error before it spreads into every answer.**

Raman's graph counts in ones and Sheela's counts in fives. Raman's "study" bar looks taller than Sheela's. Who studies more?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You cannot tell from the pictures — you must read the numbers.**
>
> **Why: a 4-hour bar on a graph counting in ones is taller than a 6-hour bar on a graph counting in fives.** The taller bar is the smaller number.
>
> **Bar length is only meaningful within ONE graph, where the scale is fixed.**
>
> **Never compare two graphs by the length of their bars until you have checked that both use the same scale.** Compare the numbers, never the ink.

Canteen food waste in kg: Mon 4, Tue 6, Wed 8, Thu 3, Fri 5. Find the total, the worst day, and how many children could have been fed if 1 kg feeds 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> total : 4 + 6 + 8 + 3 + 5 = 26 kg
> worst : Wednesday, 8 kg
> fed   : 26 × 3 = 78 children
> ```
> **Why the last figure is the point of the whole exercise: "26 kg" is a number, but "78 children could have eaten" is a REASON TO ACT.**
>
> **The arithmetic is trivial; the translation is what makes data useful.**
>
> **Data is collected in order to decide something.** A graph nobody draws a conclusion from was a waste of the time it took to make.

---

From that same data, predict which item will be wasted most next week. How confident should you be?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Khichdi is the likeliest — but this is a guess, not a fact.**
>
> **Why it is the reasonable guess: it was much the worst this week (8 kg against 3–6 kg for everything else).** A single big gap is weak but real evidence.
>
> **Why you should not be confident: one week is a very small amount of evidence.** The cook might change the recipe, or the weather might change what children feel like eating.
>
> **Say "likely" rather than "will".** Knowing how much your data can carry is as important as reading it correctly.

---

In a picture of 15 children, 7 are wearing glasses. Is the statement "more than half the children wear glasses" true?

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
> ```
> half of 15 = 7½
> 7 is LESS than 7½
> ```
> **Why the picture can deceive: glasses are eye-catching, so a group with 7 of them can look full of glasses.**
>
> **"More than half" always needs COUNTING, never impression.**
>
> **Note how narrow the margin is — one more child with glasses would make it true.** Claims near the boundary are exactly the ones worth checking rather than eyeballing.

---

"All the girls in the picture are wearing caps." What would you need to show this is false?

> [!ANSWER]
> **One girl without a cap. That's all.**
>
> **Why one is enough: "all" is a claim about every single one, so a single exception destroys it.** You do not have to check the rest.
>
> **But to show it is TRUE you must check every girl** — no shortcut exists.
>
> **This is the same asymmetry as Chapter 9's always/sometimes/never, and Chapter 11's "more area means more perimeter".** Noticing which kind of claim you are testing is one of the most useful habits of thought there is.

---

"Some children are wearing both glasses and a cap." How would you disprove this?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By checking EVERY child and finding none with both.**
>
> **Why one non-example proves nothing: "some" means "at least one".** Finding a child with glasses but no cap says nothing about the others.
>
> **So "some" and "all" are disproved in opposite ways:**
> ```
> "all"  → find ONE exception, and you are done
> "some" → check EVERY case before you can say no
> ```
> **This is why "some" claims are so hard to argue against and so easy to prove** — a single example settles them.

---

Which is better for a school noticeboard about how children travel to school: a pictograph or a bar graph?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A pictograph — but only because of what the noticeboard is for.**
>
> **Why: a pictograph is friendly and grasped at a glance**, with little bus and bicycle icons that tell the story without any reading.
>
> **A bar graph would be the better choice for a report to the head teacher**, because exact values can be read straight off the axis and close values compared precisely.
>
> **Neither is "better" in general.** The question is always what the reader needs — a quick impression or an exact number.

---

A pictograph of pets uses 🐾 = 4 pets. A row shows 5 whole icons and one half. How many pets?

> [!ANSWER]
> **22 pets.**
> ```
> 5 × 4 = 20
> half icon = 2
> total = 22
> ```
> **Why half of 4 is a sensible 2: the key value is even, so half an icon lands on a whole number of pets.**
>
> **Had the key been 5, half an icon would mean 2½ pets** — impossible, and a sign the scale was badly chosen.
>
> **Rule of thumb: for things you count, choose an EVEN key value if you expect to need half icons.**

---

Two classes each show their favourite fruits as a pictograph. Class A uses 1 icon = 2 children, Class B uses 1 icon = 5. Class A's mango row is longer. Do more Class A children like mangoes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You cannot tell without reading both keys and multiplying.**
> ```
> Class A: 6 icons × 2 = 12 children
> Class B: 4 icons × 5 = 20 children
> ```
> **Class B has MORE mango-lovers despite the shorter row.**
>
> **Why: row length measures ICONS, not children, and the two graphs count differently.**
>
> **This is exactly the same trap as comparing bar graphs with different scales.** In both cases the fix is identical: convert to actual numbers before comparing anything.

---

A bar graph shows rainfall for four months: 12, 30, 45, 21 mm. What total scale should the vertical axis use, and in what steps?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Up to 50, in steps of 5 (or 10).**
>
> **Why the top must be at least 45: the tallest bar has to fit on the page.** A 50 top leaves a little headroom and is a round number.
>
> **Why steps of 5: with steps of 1 you would need 50 lines — too crowded to read.** With steps of 20 you could not tell 21 from 30 at a glance.
>
> **The rule is the same as choosing a pictograph scale:** enough divisions to show the differences that matter, few enough to be readable. **Look at the data first, then choose.**

---

A graph of test scores has its vertical axis starting at 60 instead of 0. Two scores, 65 and 70, appear as bars of very different heights. What is wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The graph exaggerates a small difference into a large-looking one.**
> ```
> true difference : 70 − 65 = 5 marks out of 100 — small
> as drawn        : bars of 5 and 10 above the 60 line — one looks
>                   TWICE the other
> ```
> **Why this matters: the reader's eye takes the ratio of the heights as the ratio of the scores**, and here that ratio has been invented by the axis.
>
> **The numbers on the page are honest; the picture is not.**
>
> **Always check where the vertical axis begins before believing any bar graph** — in newspapers and advertisements it very often does not begin at 0.

---

A class of 30 is surveyed on favourite sport. The four bars read 8, 7, 6 and 5. Is anything wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — the bars total 26, not 30. Four children are unaccounted for.**
> ```
> 8 + 7 + 6 + 5 = 26,  but the class has 30
> ```
> **Why this is worth catching: either four children did not answer, or a category (perhaps "other") has been left off the graph.**
>
> **Either way the graph is incomplete, and any percentage or "most popular" claim from it is suspect.**
>
> **Every survey has a built-in check: the parts should account for the whole.** Doing that check first, before answering anything, is the fastest way to spot a broken dataset.
