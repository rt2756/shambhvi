Tamanna has two chocolates of different sizes. She says 1/3 of one is bigger than 1/2 of the other. Can she be right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **YES — she can be right, if the chocolate she took a third of is much bigger.**
>
> **Why: a fraction is not a fixed amount. It is a fixed SHARE of something.**
>
> **"One half" means nothing on its own — half of WHAT is the question that must be answered first.**
>
> **THE RULE THAT FOLLOWS: to compare two fractions, THE WHOLES THEY COME FROM MUST BE THE SAME.**
>
> **So when we say 1/2 is greater than 1/3, we always mean of the SAME whole** — and that assumption is usually left unsaid.

---

What are equivalent fractions? Give four fractions equivalent to 1/3.

> [!ANSWER]
> **EQUIVALENT FRACTIONS are different NAMES for the SAME part of a whole.**
> ```
>   1     2     3     4     5
>   —  =  —  =  —  =  —  =  —
>   3     6     9    12    15
> ```
> **You can see it by shading: shade one-third of a shape, then draw lines cutting it into more equal parts.** The shaded region never changes — only the number of pieces does.
>
> **The name changes; the amount does not.**

---

Explain why cutting each piece into smaller pieces does not change the value of a fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because you have cut THE SAME AMOUNT into MORE, SMALLER pieces.**
> ```
> break each 1/2 piece into 2 equal parts → each becomes a 1/4 piece
> so 2 pieces of 1/4 make 1/2:      1/2 = 2/4
> ```
> **The pieces are HALF the size, but there are TWICE as many.**
>
> **The two changes cancel each other exactly**, so the total amount you are holding is untouched.
>
> **This is the whole idea behind equivalent fractions** — and it is why you can always rename a fraction without changing what it is worth.

---

State the rule for making equivalent fractions, and explain why the same number must be used on top and bottom.

> [!ANSWER]
> **MULTIPLY THE TOP AND THE BOTTOM BY THE SAME NUMBER.**
> ```
>   1    ×2     2    ×3     3     ×4     4
>   —   ---→    —   ---→    —    ---→    —
>   3    ×2     6    ×3     9     ×4    12
> ```
> **Why the SAME number both times:**
> - **the BOTTOM counts how many pieces the WHOLE was cut into**
> - **the TOP counts how many of those pieces you HAVE**
>
> **Cutting everything into twice as many pieces DOUBLES BOTH counts** — the whole and your share alike.
>
> **So the share stays exactly the same.** Change only one of them and you would be taking a different amount.

---

Why is 1/3 = 2/6 correct but 1/3 = 2/4 wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1/3 = 2/6   ✔   both MULTIPLIED by 2
> 1/3 = 2/4   ✘   1 was ADDED to both
> ```
> **Multiplying keeps the RATIO of your-pieces to all-pieces the same. Adding does not.**
>
> **Think of it as sharing:** with 1/3 you have one piece out of three. **Double everything and you have two pieces out of six — still the same share.**
>
> **But "add one to each" gives two pieces out of four, which is HALF** — a genuinely bigger portion, not a renaming.
>
> **The test: 1/3 is less than a half, and 2/4 IS a half. They cannot be the same.**

---

Fill in the boxes so the fractions are equivalent: 2/5 = ?/10 · 3/4 = ?/16 · 5/9 = 25/?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2/5 = 4/10      because 5 × 2 = 10, so 2 × 2 = 4
> 3/4 = 12/16     because 4 × 4 = 16, so 3 × 4 = 12
> 5/9 = 25/45     because 5 × 5 = 25, so 9 × 5 = 45
> ```
> **The method every time: FIND THE MULTIPLIER FIRST from the pair you are given, then apply it to the other number.**
>
> **In the last one the multiplier came from the TOP, not the bottom** — which is why you must look at whichever pair is complete rather than always at the denominators.

---

Which of these pairs are equivalent: 2/3 and 3/4 · 3/5 and 6/10 · 4/12 and 2/6 · 6/9 and 1/3?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2/3 and 3/4    ✘  no whole number multiplies 3 into 4
>
> 3/5 and 6/10   ✔  3 × 2 = 6  and  5 × 2 = 10
>
> 4/12 and 2/6   ✔  2 × 2 = 4  and  6 × 2 = 12
>
> 6/9 and 1/3    ✘  dividing 6/9 by 3 gives 2/3, not 1/3
> ```
> **The check: can you get from one to the other by MULTIPLYING (or dividing) top and bottom by the SAME number?**
>
> **6/9 is a good trap: 6 and 9 do share a factor of 3, so it does simplify — but it simplifies to 2/3, NOT 1/3.** Simplifying is not the same as guessing the answer.

---

Compare using `<` or `>`: 1/4 and 3/4 · 3/5 and 4/5 · 5/7 and 2/7.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1/4 < 3/4
> 3/5 < 4/5
> 5/7 > 2/7
> ```
> **The rule: SAME BOTTOM NUMBER → the one with the BIGGER TOP NUMBER is bigger.**
>
> **Why it is that simple: the bottom number tells you the SIZE of each piece.**
>
> **If the bottom numbers match, every piece is the same size — so you are just COUNTING PIECES.** Three quarters beats one quarter for the same reason three biscuits beat one biscuit.

---

Sevi ate 4/6 of a paratha and Shami ate 4/5 of one. Who ate more, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **SHAMI ate more — 4/5 is greater than 4/6.**
>
> **This feels backwards at first, so here is why.**
>
> **The bottom number says HOW MANY PIECES THE WHOLE WAS CUT INTO.**
> ```
> cut a paratha into 6 pieces → each piece is SMALL
> cut a paratha into 5 pieces → each piece is BIGGER
>
> so a 1/6 piece is SMALLER than a 1/5 piece
> ```
> **Both of them took FOUR pieces. But Shami's pieces were bigger.**
>
> **Four bigger pieces beat four smaller ones**, so 4/5 > 4/6.

---

State the rule for comparing fractions with the same top number, and give an everyday way to remember it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **SAME TOP NUMBER → the one with the SMALLER BOTTOM NUMBER is bigger.**
> ```
> 3/7 > 3/8        6/9 > 6/10        2/5 > 2/7
> ```
> **The everyday version: would you rather share a cake with 5 people or with 10?**
>
> **With 5 people you get a much bigger slice.**
>
> **MORE PEOPLE SHARING MEANS A SMALLER SLICE EACH** — and a bigger bottom number means exactly that: more people sharing the same cake.
>
> **So a big bottom number makes a fraction SMALL, not large.**

---

Compare using `<` or `>`: 3/8 and 3/7 · 4/9 and 4/10 · 2/7 and 2/5.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3/8 < 3/7        because 1/8 pieces are smaller than 1/7 pieces
> 4/9 > 4/10       because 1/9 pieces are bigger than 1/10 pieces
> 2/7 < 2/5        because 1/7 pieces are smaller than 1/5 pieces
> ```
> **Every one of these has the same top number, so the comparison is decided entirely by the PIECE SIZE.**
>
> **A one-line way to say it: SAME NUMBER OF PIECES, so whoever has the BIGGER PIECES has more** — and bigger pieces mean a smaller bottom number.

---

Which is bigger: 3/4 or 3/5? And which is bigger: 3/4 or 2/4? Explain why the two questions need different rules.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3/4 > 3/5    — SAME TOP, so the smaller bottom wins
> 3/4 > 2/4    — SAME BOTTOM, so the bigger top wins
> ```
> **Why two different rules: they are asking about two different things.**
>
> - **When the BOTTOMS match, all the pieces are the same size — so you compare HOW MANY you have.**
> - **When the TOPS match, you have the same NUMBER of pieces — so you compare HOW BIG they are.**
>
> **One rule looks at the count, the other at the size.**
>
> **Before comparing, always ask: which one is the same, the top or the bottom?** That tells you which rule applies.

---

Maa took 5 pieces of 1/2 paratha. How many parathas is that?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2  =  5/2
>
> 2 halves make 1 whole, so:
>    5 = 2 + 2 + 1
>    → 2 wholes and 1 half left over
>
> 5/2 = 2½ parathas
> ```
> **On a number line:**
> ```
>   0     1/2    2/2    3/2    4/2    5/2
>   |------|------|------|------|------|
>                =1            =2     =2½
> ```
> **The key step: ASK HOW MANY PIECES MAKE ONE WHOLE.** Once you know that 2 halves make a whole, counting off whole sets is easy.

---

Radhika took 6 pieces of 1/2 paratha and Dadiji took 7. How many parathas did each eat?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> RADHIKA — 6/2
>    6 = 2 + 2 + 2  → exactly 3 wholes
>    6/2 = 3 parathas
>
> DADIJI — 7/2
>    7 = 2 + 2 + 2 + 1  → 3 wholes and 1 half
>    7/2 = 3½ parathas
> ```
> **Why Radhika's answer is a whole number and Dadiji's is not: 6 IS a multiple of 2, and 7 is not.**
>
> **That is a useful quick check on any such question:** if the top is a multiple of the bottom, the answer comes out whole; **if not, there must be a leftover piece.**

---

Dadaji took 9 pieces of 1/4 paratha. How many parathas did he eat?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 4 quarters make 1 whole
>
> 9 = 4 + 4 + 1
>   → 2 wholes and 1 quarter left over
>
> 9/4 = 2¼ parathas
> ```
> **The method is the same as with halves, only the size of the piece has changed.**
>
> **Ask how many pieces make ONE whole (here, 4), then see how many complete sets of that many you have (two sets of 4), and what is left over (1).**
>
> **Check: 9 is not a multiple of 4, so a leftover was expected** ✔

---

How can you tell at a glance whether a fraction is more than one whole?

> [!ANSWER]
> **COMPARE THE TOP WITH THE BOTTOM.**
> ```
> top SMALLER than bottom  → LESS than 1        3/4, 2/5, 7/9
> top EQUAL to bottom      → EXACTLY 1          4/4, 9/9
> top BIGGER than bottom   → MORE than 1        5/2, 9/4, 7/3
> ```
> **Why: the bottom number is how many pieces make ONE WHOLE.**
>
> **So if you have MORE pieces than that, you must have more than one whole** — you have filled one up and started on another.
>
> **This makes 4/4 = 1 obvious rather than surprising:** you have exactly enough pieces to make one whole and none left over.

---

Write 11/3 as wholes and a fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 3 thirds make 1 whole
>
> 11 = 3 + 3 + 3 + 2
>    → 3 complete sets of 3, with 2 left over
>
> 11/3 = 3⅔
> ```
> **The leftover 2 stays as 2 THIRDS, not as 2 of anything else** — the pieces have not changed size just because you finished counting the whole ones.
>
> **A check: 3 wholes would be 9/3, and 4 wholes would be 12/3. Our answer must sit between 3 and 4** ✔ — and 3⅔ does.

---

Make one whole using only 1/6 and 1/12 pieces, in two different ways.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> WAY 1 — six 1/6 pieces
>    1/6 × 6 = 6/6 = 1
>
> WAY 2 — twelve 1/12 pieces
>    1/12 × 12 = 12/12 = 1
>
> WAY 3 — a mixture: five 1/6 pieces and two 1/12 pieces
>    5/6 + 2/12 = 5/6 + 1/6 = 6/6 = 1
> ```
> **Why the mixture works: TWO 1/12 pieces make ONE 1/6 piece**, since 2/12 = 1/6.
>
> **So you can swap any 1/6 piece for two 1/12 pieces and the total stays exactly 1.** That is equivalent fractions being useful rather than just being true.

---

Arrange in increasing order: 3/8, 5/8, 1/8, 7/8.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1/8 · 3/8 · 5/8 · 7/8
> ```
> **This one is easy because ALL THE BOTTOM NUMBERS ARE THE SAME.**
>
> **Every piece is one-eighth, so the pieces are all the same size** — and ordering the fractions is just **ordering the counts 1, 3, 5, 7.**
>
> **Whenever the denominators match, a comparison question turns into ordinary counting.**

---

Arrange in increasing order: 2/9, 2/3, 2/5, 2/7.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2/9 · 2/7 · 2/5 · 2/3
> ```
> **Notice that this is the REVERSE of the order of the bottom numbers** (9, 7, 5, 3).
>
> **Why: all the tops are 2, so each fraction is two pieces. What differs is the SIZE of the piece.**
>
> **The BIGGEST bottom number gives the SMALLEST pieces, and so the smallest fraction.**
>
> **The list of denominators runs downwards while the list of fractions runs upwards** — which is exactly the "same top" rule in action.

---

Is 1/2 of a small pizza the same as 1/2 of a large pizza? What does this tell you about fractions?

> [!ANSWER]
> **NO — they are not the same amount of pizza.**
>
> **But they ARE the same FRACTION.** Each person got half of what was in front of them.
>
> **What this tells you: a fraction describes a SHARE, not an AMOUNT.**
>
> **"Half" answers the question "what portion?" — it does not answer "how much?" until you also know what the whole was.**
>
> **This is why every comparison of fractions silently assumes the same whole**, and why Tamanna's chocolate puzzle at the start of the chapter is not a trick but a real warning.
