How do you tell how much someone has turned?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By comparing WHERE THEY ENDED UP with WHERE THEY STARTED.**
>
> **A giant wheel has made a FULL TURN when it comes back to its starting point.** The minute hand of a clock does the same.
>
> **Why the starting position is so important: turning is not about which way you are FACING, it is about HOW FAR YOU HAVE MOVED ROUND.**
>
> **Two children could be facing exactly the same way, yet one may have turned a quarter and the other three-quarters.** Without knowing where each began, you cannot tell.

---

What do two half turns in the same direction make? And two quarter turns? And four quarter turns?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 2 half turns    = a FULL turn        1/2 + 1/2 = 1
> 2 quarter turns = a HALF turn        1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2
> 4 quarter turns = a FULL turn        1/4 × 4   = 1
> ```
> **Why the fraction arithmetic works here: A FULL TURN IS THE "WHOLE".**
>
> **A quarter turn is a quarter OF A FULL TURN**, in just the way a quarter chapati is a quarter of a whole chapati.
>
> **So everything you learned about fractions in Chapter 2 applies to turns without any change at all.**

---

Name the four kinds of angle and the turn that produces each.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Turn | Angle |
> |---|---|
> | **less than 1/4 turn** | **ACUTE** |
> | **exactly 1/4 turn** | **RIGHT** |
> | **between 1/4 and 1/2 turn** | **OBTUSE** |
> | **exactly 1/2 turn** | **STRAIGHT** |
>
> **THE RIGHT ANGLE IS THE LANDMARK — every other name is a comparison with it.**
>
> **Acute means SMALLER than a right angle; obtuse means BIGGER than one.**
>
> **So if you can recognise a right angle, you can name any of the others** without measuring anything.

---

Why does a straight angle look like a straight line?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because after HALF a turn, the moving arm points exactly OPPOSITE to where it began.**
>
> **The two arms — the one that stayed still and the one that turned — now lie in one straight line.**
>
> **A picture in words:**
> ```
> start:   both arms together, pointing the same way
> half turn later: one arm has gone right round to the other side
>                  ←—————————•—————————→
> ```
> **So a "straight angle" is not a strange exception.** It is simply what a half turn looks like once you draw it.

---

How can you test whether an angle is acute, right or obtuse without a protractor?

> [!ANSWER]
> **USE THE CORNER OF ANY PIECE OF PAPER — a page corner is a RIGHT ANGLE.**
> ```
> hold the corner against the angle:
>
>    angle NARROWER than the corner  → ACUTE
>    angle FITS the corner exactly   → RIGHT
>    angle WIDER than the corner     → OBTUSE
> ```
> **Why a page corner is reliable: it is made by two straight cuts meeting SQUARELY**, which is exactly the definition of a right angle.
>
> **You already own a right-angle tester** — several, in fact: any book, any table corner, any tile.

---

Can two acute turns together make another acute turn?

> [!ANSWER]
> **SOMETIMES YES, SOMETIMES NO — and that is what makes the question interesting.**
> ```
> a SMALL acute + a SMALL acute
>    → may still be less than a quarter turn  → still ACUTE
>
> a LARGE acute + a LARGE acute
>    → goes PAST a quarter turn               → OBTUSE
> ```
> **So there is no single answer to "acute plus acute".**
>
> **Why: "acute" describes a whole RANGE of turns, not one fixed size.** Anything less than a quarter turn counts, from the tiniest sliver up to almost a quarter.
>
> **This is worth noticing generally: angle names are LABELS FOR RANGES**, so you cannot always predict what two of them add up to.

---

Classify these turns: 1/8 · 2/8 · 3/8 · 4/8 of a full turn.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1/8 turn — half of a quarter turn, so a small ACUTE angle
> 2/8 turn = 1/4 turn — a RIGHT angle
> 3/8 turn — more than a quarter, less than a half → OBTUSE
> 4/8 turn = 1/2 turn — a STRAIGHT angle
> ```
> **The equivalent fractions of Chapter 2 are doing all the work here.**
>
> **2/8 = 1/4 is what makes 2/8 a right angle, and 4/8 = 1/2 is what makes 4/8 a straight angle.**
>
> **Without recognising those, the eighths would just be a list of numbers.**

---

How do you make a tool to measure turns, and why is a circle folded into 8 parts a good choice?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1. Cut out a CIRCLE from tracing paper.
> 2. FOLD it to make 8 EQUAL parts.
> 3. Attach a straw at the centre and mark a starting point.
> ```
> **Why EIGHT parts rather than some other number:**
>
> **Eighths let you show quarters AND halves exactly** — 2/8 is a quarter and 4/8 is a half — **while also giving you smaller steps in between.**
>
> **Folding is what guarantees the parts are EQUAL.** Fold in half, then in half again, then again: 2 → 4 → 8. **No measuring is needed, and no ruler could do it more accurately.**

---

A giant wheel starts at the top. After a half turn, where is your seat? After another half turn?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> after a HALF turn   → your seat is at the BOTTOM, opposite where it began
> after ANOTHER half turn → back at the TOP, where it started
> ```
> **Because 1/2 + 1/2 = 1, a full turn.**
>
> **What this shows nicely: a full turn brings you back to exactly where you began.**
>
> **So a full turn "looks like" no turn at all in the final position** — which is precisely why the turn must be measured by watching the journey, not just the endpoint.

---

Name three everyday objects that turn, and say roughly how far each can turn.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> DOOR with a hinge — swings well PAST a quarter turn, often near a half turn
> SCISSORS         — open to LESS than a quarter turn
> CLOTHES CLIP     — opens only a little; much less than a quarter turn
> TAP              — usually a half turn or more
> FILE COVER       — opens right back, past a half turn
> ```
> **Why different objects have different limits: the turn is stopped by what the object is FOR, or by something in the way.**
>
> **A door stops when it hits the wall; scissors stop when the handles meet.**
>
> **The limit is PHYSICAL, not mathematical** — nothing in maths prevents a bigger turn, only the object itself does.

---

The minute hand of a clock moves from 12 to 3. What fraction of a turn is that, and what angle has it made?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a full turn of the minute hand takes it all the way round, 12 → 12
>
> 12 to 3 is a QUARTER of the way round
>
> so it has made a 1/4 turn — a RIGHT ANGLE
> ```
> **How to see the quarter: the clock face has four quarter-marks at 12, 3, 6 and 9**, dividing the circle into four equal parts.
>
> **A useful extra: 12 to 6 would be a HALF turn (a straight angle), and 12 to 9 would be THREE-QUARTERS.**

---

The minute hand moves from 12 to 2. Is the angle acute, right or obtuse?

> [!ANSWER]
> **ACUTE.**
>
> **Why: 12 to 3 is a quarter turn — a right angle. The hand has stopped at 2, BEFORE reaching 3.**
> ```
> 12 → 2  is LESS than  12 → 3
>       so LESS than a quarter turn
>       so ACUTE
> ```
> **The reasoning method is worth keeping: FIND THE NEAREST LANDMARK FIRST (here, the quarter turn at 3), then ask whether you fell short of it or went past it.**
>
> **You never have to measure — only to compare.**

---

Sort these into acute, right and obtuse: 1/8 turn · 1/4 turn · 3/8 turn · 1/16 turn · 5/16 turn.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ACUTE   — 1/8 turn, 1/16 turn      (both less than 1/4)
> RIGHT   — 1/4 turn
> OBTUSE  — 3/8 turn, 5/16 turn      (both more than 1/4, less than 1/2)
> ```
> **How to compare an unfamiliar fraction with a quarter: WRITE THE QUARTER WITH THE SAME BOTTOM NUMBER.**
> ```
> is 5/16 more than a quarter?   1/4 = 4/16,  and 5/16 > 4/16   → yes
> is 1/16 more than a quarter?   1/4 = 4/16,  and 1/16 < 4/16   → no
> ```
> **Once the bottoms match, you just compare the tops** — the same-denominator rule from Chapter 2.

---

Draw (in words) a five-sided shape with two right angles, two obtuse angles and one acute angle. How many angles must such a shape have in total?

> [!ANSWER]
> **FIVE angles in total — a five-sided shape has one angle at each corner, and it has five corners.**
> ```
> 2 right + 2 obtuse + 1 acute = 5 angles      ✔ it fits
> ```
> **A way to build one: start with a rectangle-like corner (giving two right angles side by side), then slope the remaining sides so that two corners open out (obtuse) and one closes in sharply (acute).**
>
> **The general fact worth carrying forward: the NUMBER OF ANGLES ALWAYS EQUALS THE NUMBER OF SIDES.** A triangle has 3 of each, a quadrilateral 4, a pentagon 5.

---

Reema takes a quarter turn, then another quarter turn, then a half turn, all in the same direction. Where does she end up?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/2
>
> the two quarters make a half:   1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2
> then                            1/2 + 1/2 = 1
>
> so she has made a FULL TURN
> ```
> **She ends up FACING EXACTLY WHERE SHE STARTED.**
>
> **Why the answer is not obvious from the final position: she has genuinely turned all the way round**, even though she looks as if she never moved.
>
> **This is exactly why a turn must be measured by adding up the journey**, not by looking at where the person finishes.

---

Why is a full turn taken as "the whole" when measuring angles?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a full turn is the LARGEST distinct amount of turning there is — after it, you are back where you started and everything repeats.**
>
> **So it makes a natural "one", exactly as a whole chapati makes a natural "one" for fractions.**
>
> **Everything else is then a fraction of it:**
> ```
> a right angle    = 1/4 of a full turn
> a straight angle = 1/2 of a full turn
> a 3/8 turn       = 3/8 of a full turn
> ```
> **And that choice is what lets all the fraction work of Chapter 2 be reused here without learning anything new** — comparing turns is comparing fractions.
