If all the water on Earth were a 200 ml glass, how much would the usable freshwater be? What does that tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **About one 5 ml teaspoonful.**
>
> **Everything humans, animals, birds, crops and forests drink and grow with comes out of that teaspoon.** The rest of the glass is ocean.
>
> **Why the comparison is worth remembering: "the Earth has plenty of water" is true and misleading at the same time.**
>
> **It has plenty of water; it has very little water we can USE.** Saying it as a glass and a spoon makes the difference impossible to forget.

---

A sailor adrift at sea drinks seawater. Why does this make things worse rather than better?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because his body must get rid of the extra salt, and it can only do that by passing it out DISSOLVED IN WATER.**
>
> **So drinking seawater costs the body more water than it delivers.** He dries out faster than if he had drunk nothing at all.
>
> **This is why salty water makes you thirstier, not less thirsty.**
>
> **It is also why "there is water everywhere" does not help a sailor** — being surrounded by water is not the same as having water you can use.

---

Ocean water cannot be drunk. Name three things it is genuinely useful for.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> SALT        — the salt pans of Gujarat dry seawater in the sun to
>               collect salt; one of India's largest salt-producing areas
> FISHING     — a huge part of India's food supply
> TRANSPORT   — ships carry most of the world's goods
> ```
> **And the biggest one of all: EVAPORATION.** Nearly all the rain that falls on land began as water evaporating from the sea.
>
> **Why that matters: the ocean is not a separate store of useless water.** It is the engine that keeps refilling the teaspoon.

---

Ice is put in a dry steel glass and droplets appear on the outside. Where does that water come from?

> [!ANSWER]
> **From the AIR — not from inside the glass.**
>
> **Why it cannot have leaked through: steel has no holes, and the outside gets wet even if the glass is sealed.**
>
> **What really happens: there is always invisible water vapour in the air around us. The cold glass cools the air touching it, and cool air cannot hold as much vapour** — so the extra turns back into liquid on the cold surface.
>
> **This is CONDENSATION, the single most important idea in the water cycle.** It is also what makes dew form on grass at dawn and mist appear on a window in winter.

---

A saucer of water left in sunlight for three days is empty. Has the water been destroyed?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No. It has EVAPORATED into the air.**
>
> **The Sun's heat gave the water enough energy to leave the saucer as invisible water vapour**, one tiny particle at a time.
>
> **Why you cannot see it: water vapour is a gas, and this one happens to be colourless.** Invisible is not the same as absent.
>
> **The rule worth carrying: water that vanishes has always gone somewhere.** Following where is how the whole water cycle was worked out.

---

Name the four changes of form of water and say what causes each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> ice → water    MELTING        caused by HEAT
> water → vapour EVAPORATION    caused by HEAT  (boiling is fast evaporation)
> vapour → water CONDENSATION   caused by COOLING
> water → ice    FREEZING       caused by COOLING
> ```
> **Why they pair up like that: heating always moves water "upward" through solid → liquid → gas, and cooling always moves it back down.**
>
> **Nothing is created or destroyed at any step** — the same water is simply arranged differently.
>
> **Knowing the pattern means you never have to memorise the four names separately.** Ask "am I heating or cooling?" and half the answer is already there.

---

Put the water cycle in order and say what drives it.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> the SUN heats water in oceans, rivers, lakes and soil
>    → EVAPORATION    water rises as invisible vapour
>    → CONDENSATION   high up it is cold, so vapour becomes tiny
>                     droplets → clouds
>    → PRECIPITATION  droplets join, grow heavy and fall as rain,
>                     snow or hail
>    → COLLECTION     water returns to rivers, lakes, oceans and soil
>    → and it begins again
> ```
> **The Sun drives the whole thing.** Without its heat there would be no evaporation, so no clouds, so no rain.
>
> **Why cold air high up matters just as much: it forces the vapour back into droplets.** Heat alone would just carry the water up and leave it there.

---

Water is never used up. Why do droughts happen then?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the cycle takes TIME and does not deliver water where and when we want it.**
>
> **Rain may fall on the sea instead of the field. A river may be far from the village. A monsoon may arrive two months late.**
>
> **So the TOTAL amount of water on Earth never falls — but the amount available HERE, NOW, and CLEAN certainly can.**
>
> **That gap between "exists somewhere" and "available here" is exactly what a drought is**, and it is why water conservation is not pointless even though water is never destroyed.

---

Coloured water is sealed in a transparent bag and left in the sun. Droplets form on the inside top and run back down. What does this prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That water is RECYCLED, not created or destroyed.**
>
> **Nothing can get in or out of the sealed bag — and yet water keeps moving:** heating and evaporating at the bottom, condensing on the cooler upper surface, running back down.
>
> **Why the sealing is the important part of the experiment.** If the bag were open you could always argue that new water crept in. Sealed, there is no such escape — the same water must be going round.
>
> **It is the whole water cycle, shrunk to an afternoon.**

---

What is groundwater, and how do we reach it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Groundwater is rainwater that has soaked into the soil and sunk down through layers of soil and rock, to be stored deep underground.**
> ```
> we reach it with: wells · borewells · tube wells · handpumps
> ```
> **Why it collects rather than draining away: below the soil are layers of rock that water cannot pass through easily**, so the water above them fills the gaps and stays.
>
> **It is not an underground lake but water held in the spaces between grains of soil and cracks in rock** — more like a wet sponge than a swimming pool.

---

Explain why a heavily paved city slowly runs out of groundwater, even though the same amount of rain falls on it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because rain can only sink in where there are GAPS to sink through — and concrete has none.**
>
> **Soil has spaces between its particles; a paved road does not.** On concrete the rain simply runs off into a drain and away, never reaching the ground below.
>
> **So the more a city is paved, the less its groundwater is RECHARGED.** The water arrives and is turned away at the door.
>
> **Meanwhile the city keeps pumping groundwater OUT for its taps.** Taking out more than goes in has only one ending.

---

Which of these help rainwater get back underground: a concrete road, a green lawn, a paved courtyard, a soak pit, a row of trees?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> HELP    : green lawn · soak pit · row of trees
> DO NOT  : concrete road · paved courtyard
> ```
> **Why the lawn and the trees work: both leave open soil with spaces for water to seep into**, and roots loosen the soil further, opening more channels.
>
> **Why a soak pit works: it is a deliberate hole filled with gravel, made so water has somewhere to go instead of running off.**
>
> **The single test in every case: can the water find a gap to get down?** If not, it leaves.

---

What are johads, and what problem do they solve?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Johads are traditional small earthen dams in Rajasthan that catch rainwater and let it seep down to recharge groundwater.**
>
> **The problem they solve: in a dry region, the rain arrives all at once and runs away.** A johad slows it down and holds it in one place long enough to sink in.
>
> **Why "slowing water down" is the whole idea: water that is standing still has time to soak in; water that is racing away does not.**
>
> **This is exactly what a modern soak pit does** — an old technology and a new one, both built on the same piece of understanding.

---

Mustard seeds are poured onto crumpled paper covered with a smooth sheet. They curve, gather in hollows, and never travel straight. What does this show about rivers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That water always goes downhill, and the SHAPE OF THE LAND decides which downhill.**
>
> **The seeds follow the folds, curve round the high bits and collect in the low bits — exactly as water flows, stops or curves according to the land in its path.**
>
> **Why the seeds gather in hollows: that is where lakes and ponds come from.** A hollow is simply a place with no downhill left.
>
> **And over long times the traffic goes both ways** — flowing water also wears away and reshapes the land, carving the valleys it runs through.

---

Why do the Ganga and the Godavari flow to the Bay of Bengal while the Narmada flows to the Arabian Sea?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of which way the land slopes where they start.**
>
> **A mountain range acts like the ridge on the crumpled paper: rain falling on one side runs one way, rain on the other side runs the other.**
> ```
> to the BAY OF BENGAL  — Ganga, Brahmaputra, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi
> to the ARABIAN SEA    — Narmada, Tapi, Mahi, Sabarmati
> ```
> **Most of peninsular India tilts gently eastward, which is why the eastern list is so much longer.**
>
> **Two raindrops landing a metre apart on a ridge can end up in seas a thousand kilometres apart.** Nothing about the drops differs — only where they landed.

---

The Luni river reaches no sea at all. How is that possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because there is no downhill path onwards.**
>
> **Rising in the Aravalli range in Rajasthan, the Luni ends in the marshy Rann of Kutch in Gujarat** — the only major Indian river that never reaches a sea.
>
> **Why a river needs a downhill path all the way: water cannot climb.** If the land ahead rises, or simply flattens into marsh in a hot dry region, the river spreads out, soaks in and evaporates.
>
> **The Luni does not fail to find the sea. There is no route by which it could.**

---

Why can a fish not survive in air, and a dog not survive underwater? Give one reason that covers both.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because each animal's breathing organ works in only ONE medium.**
>
> **Gills need to be surrounded by water to take oxygen out of it; in air they collapse and dry out.** **Lungs need air; filled with water they cannot work.**
>
> **Note what is NOT the reason: it is not that water has no oxygen or air has no water.** There IS oxygen dissolved in water, and there IS water vapour in air.
>
> **The animal simply cannot reach the one its body is not built for.** That is what "adapted to its habitat" really means — not that the animal chose well, but that its body only functions where it belongs.

---

Compare an animal that lives on land with one that lives in water, on three points.

> [!ANSWER]
> | On land | In water |
> |---|---|
> | breathes air with **lungs** | many breathe dissolved oxygen through **gills** |
> | **legs** for walking | **fins and a streamlined body** for swimming |
> | legs must support the body's weight | **the water holds the body up** |
>
> **Why the third point is easy to miss and very important: a whale would be crushed by its own weight on land**, because its skeleton never had to carry it.
>
> **Water supports living things in a way air cannot**, which is why the largest animals that ever lived are sea animals.

---

A pond heron has long thin legs and a long pointed beak. Explain how each helps it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Long thin legs let it stand in shallow water without swimming and without getting its body wet.** Thin legs also disturb the water very little, so fish do not flee.
>
> **A long pointed beak works like a spear** — it can be driven quickly into the water to catch a fish, and its length means the bird's head need not go in after it.
>
> **Why "stand still" is the heron's whole strategy: a fish notices movement, not shapes.** The legs make waiting possible and the beak makes the single fast strike effective.
>
> **The body plan and the hunting method fit each other.** Neither would work alone.

---

A water scorpion lives underwater but has a tube at its tail reaching the surface. What is the tube for, and what does it tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It is a breathing tube — it takes AIR from above the surface.**
>
> **What it tells you: the water scorpion has not solved the underwater breathing problem the way a fish has.** It has no gills, so it must keep a connection to the air.
>
> **Why this is worth noticing: living in water and breathing water are two different things.** Whales, turtles and water scorpions all live in water and all breathe air.
>
> **The tube is a compromise** — it lets the insect hunt underwater while keeping the one thing it cannot do without.

---

Put a drop of water on plain paper, then on paper coloured thickly with a wax crayon. What happens, and what does it explain about lotus leaves?

> [!ANSWER]
> **On plain paper the drop soaks in. On waxed paper it sits on top as a bead.**
>
> **Floating leaves like the lotus have a WAXY COATING that does exactly the same job.** Water rolls off instead of soaking in, so the leaf stays dry on top.
>
> **Why the plant needs this: a leaf that soaked up water would become heavy, sink, and rot.**
>
> **The wax is not decoration — it is the plant's raincoat.** The crayon experiment is a fair model of it because wax is doing the same thing in both cases.

---

What is the difference between a lotus and water hyacinth in how they grow?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> LOTUS / WATER LILY  — ROOTED FLOATING: roots anchored in the pond bed,
>                       leaves and flowers on the surface
> WATER HYACINTH      — FREE-FLOATING: the whole plant drifts, roots
>                       hanging in the water
> ```
> **Why the difference matters: a rooted plant stays where it is, so a lotus pond looks much the same year after year.**
>
> **A free-floating plant travels, and water hyacinth spreads very fast.** It can cover a whole pond surface, block out the sunlight and starve the plants below.
>
> **A plant is not "good" or "bad" in itself.** How it grows decides whether it fits into a pond or takes it over.

---

Draw a river food chain and explain what happens if all the small fish disappear.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> tiny water plants → small fish → big fish → heron / otter / crocodile → humans
> ```
> **Every arrow means "is eaten by".**
>
> **If the small fish vanish, the effect travels BOTH ways along the chain.** Upward: the big fish starve, and so eventually do the herons and otters. Downward: nothing is eating the tiny water plants, so they multiply and can choke the pond.
>
> **Why both directions matter: every living thing in a chain is both a meal and an eater.** Removing it leaves a gap on two sides at once.
>
> **This is why "just one species" is never just one species.**

---

Match each to its form of water: snow, steam, ocean water, rainwater.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> snow          → SOLID form of water
> steam         → VAPOUR (gas) form of water
> ocean water   → liquid, but SALTY — not fit for drinking
> rainwater     → liquid FRESHWATER
> ```
> **Why rain is fresh even though it comes from the salty sea: evaporation leaves the salt behind.**
>
> **Only the water turns into vapour; the salt is too heavy and stays.** That is exactly how the salt pans of Gujarat work, seen from the other end — they dry the water off and keep the salt.
>
> **So the water cycle is not just a transport system. It is a purification system too.**

---

Why do so many living things gather near water bodies?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because every living thing needs water, and none can travel very far without it.**
>
> **Plants grow well near water, so plant-eating animals come to feed, and the animals that eat them follow.** Birds nest nearby because both food and drink are close.
>
> **Why nearness matters so much: an animal that must walk a day to drink spends that day not eating, not resting and exposed to danger.**
>
> **The result is that a pond is far richer in life than the dry land a kilometre away** — and it is also why human villages and cities grew up on riverbanks.

---

What would happen in your region if it did not rain for two years?

> [!ANSWER]
> **First the surface water goes: ponds dry, rivers shrink to trickles, lakes fall.**
>
> **Then groundwater is pumped harder to make up for it — and with no rain, nothing is recharging it, so wells and handpumps run dry too.**
>
> **Then crops fail, because fields depend on rain or on the water that rain replaced.** Animals that lived in the ponds die or leave; livestock have nothing to drink.
>
> **Why the order matters: the surface stores empty first because they are small and exposed.** Groundwater lasts longer, which is exactly why it becomes the emergency store — and why using it up in good years is so serious.

---

Rainwater falls on a forest and on a city. Trace what happens to it in each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> FOREST  leaves slow the falling rain → it lands gently on soft soil
>         → roots and loose soil let it SOAK IN → recharges groundwater
>         → the rest feeds streams slowly, over weeks
>
> CITY    lands on concrete, tiles and tar → cannot soak in anywhere
>         → runs off fast into drains → arrives in the river all at once
>         → groundwater gets nothing
> ```
> **Why the city version causes floods as well as shortages — the same fact does both.**
>
> **Water that cannot soak in must go somewhere, and it goes there quickly and all together.** So the city gets a flash flood in the afternoon and a dry well in the summer.
>
> **A forest turns a downpour into a slow release; a paved city turns it into a surge and a loss.**

---

Design one feature for a school building that would help conserve water, and explain why it works.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A rooftop rainwater collection system feeding a soak pit or storage tank.**
>
> **Why the roof: it is a large, clean, already-paved surface that catches a lot of rain and currently throws it all away into a drain.**
>
> **Why it works: the water is collected before it reaches the ground and mixes with dirt**, so it is clean enough for washing, gardens and flushing — and any surplus can be sent down a soak pit to recharge groundwater rather than run off.
>
> **Other good answers with the same reasoning: keeping the playground as open soil rather than paving it, planting trees, and a pond to hold monsoon water.** **In every case the principle is one of two things: slow the water down, or catch it before it escapes.**
