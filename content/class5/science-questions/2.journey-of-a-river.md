Trace the Godavari from where it begins to where it ends.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> SOURCE  Brahmagiri Hills of the Western Ghats, at Trimbakeshwar,
>         Maharashtra — it starts as a small STREAM
>    ↓    tributaries join: Manjira, Indravati, Sabari
>    ↓    flows through Maharashtra, Telangana, Chhattisgarh,
>         Odisha and Andhra Pradesh — 1,465 km
> MOUTH   spreads into a DELTA at the Coringa Mangrove Forests
>         and meets the Bay of Bengal
> ```
> **It is India's second longest river after the Ganga**, and is called *Dakshina Ganga* because it flows through southern India.

---

Why does a river get wider as it travels, even though nothing is pushing water into it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because every stream that joins ADDS water, and none takes any away.**
>
> **Rain falling anywhere in the whole valley eventually drains into the same channel** — the land tilts that way.
>
> **So the river can only collect, all along its length.** That is why the Godavari starts as a stream you could step across and ends wide enough to need a delta.
>
> **The smaller rivers joining are its TRIBUTARIES** — and they join it, not the other way round, because of which way the land slopes.

---

What is a delta, and why does a river form one at its mouth?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A delta is the fan of many small channels a river splits into where it meets the sea.**
>
> **Why it forms: all the way down, the river has been carrying tiny bits of soil and sand, because moving water can carry things.**
>
> **Where it meets the sea the water slows almost to a stop — and slow water cannot carry a load.** So the soil is dropped.
>
> **The dropped soil builds into banks and islands, and the river has to find its way round them**, splitting again and again.
>
> **A delta is a river's cargo, put down where it finally ran out of push.**

---

Why are deltas so often ringed with mangrove forests?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because mangroves are one of the very few kinds of plant that survive in salty, muddy, waterlogged ground — and that is exactly what a delta is.**
>
> **Where the fresh river water meets the salty sea, ordinary plants die.** Salt draws water out of roots instead of letting it in.
>
> **So the mangroves have almost no competition there**, and can take over an entire coastline.
>
> **A habitat that is hard to live in is not empty** — it is occupied by whatever solved that particular problem.

---

Explain the difference between a perennial and a seasonal river, and what really decides which one a river is.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PERENNIAL — flows all year round    (Godavari, Ganga)
> SEASONAL  — flows only in the rains
> ```
> **What decides it: whether the river has a source OTHER than this season's rain.**
>
> **A river fed only by rain must stop when the rain stops.** A river fed by **melting mountain snow, or by groundwater seeping in along its bed**, keeps receiving water even in the driest months.
>
> **Why this matters to the people beside it: a perennial river can supply a city all year.** A seasonal one leaves them to find water for six months — a completely different life.

---

Why was almost every ancient city in the world built beside a river?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because water is heavy and cannot be carried far.**
>
> **A family needs many litres every single day, and before pipes and pumps that meant living within walking distance of the water.**
>
> **A river also delivers free of charge: fertile soil on its banks, fish, a route for boats, and water for crops.**
>
> **So a riverbank was not a pleasant choice — it was very nearly the only possible one.** Those cities are still there today because cities, once built, do not move.

---

List four ways people depend on a river like the Godavari every day.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> DRINKING, cooking and washing at home
> IRRIGATION — water carried to fields in canals
> INDUSTRY   — factories need large quantities of water
> FISHING    — a livelihood for whole communities
> ```
> **Also transport by boat, electricity from dams, pilgrimage at places like Nashik, and ecotourism.**
>
> **Why the list is worth writing out: when you open a tap it is hard to picture any of this.** The water seems to come from the tap.
>
> **Knowing where it really comes from is what makes "do not waste it" mean something.**

---

A dam is built on a river. Make two lists: what it gives, and what it costs.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Gives | Costs |
> |---|---|
> | water stored for the **dry season** | the reservoir **floods land** upstream |
> | **irrigation** all year | **forests and wildlife habitats** are drowned |
> | **electricity** from falling water | **people lose their homes** and must move |
> | some **flood control** downstream | less water and less silt reach the land below |
>
> **Why it is a genuine trade and not a simple good or bad: every entry in the first column is real, and so is every entry in the second.**
>
> **Anyone who tells you a dam is purely good, or purely bad, has looked at only one column.**

---

Why is deciding whether to build a dam so difficult, beyond the engineering?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the benefits and the costs fall on DIFFERENT PEOPLE.**
>
> **The water, the crops and the electricity go to towns and farms DOWNSTREAM, often far away.**
>
> **The flooded land, the lost forest and the moved villages are all UPSTREAM, close by.**
>
> **So the question is not "do the benefits outweigh the costs?" but "who is being asked to give up what, and what do they get in return?"**
>
> **That is a question about fairness, and no amount of engineering can answer it.**

---

Name two ways the harm caused by a dam can be reduced.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> resettle displaced families properly, with land and work
> leave a MINIMUM FLOW of water in the river below the dam
> plant new forest elsewhere to replace what was drowned
> build FISH LADDERS so migrating fish can still get upstream
> ```
> **Why the minimum flow matters: a dam can stop a river completely**, and everything living downstream — fish, farms, mangroves — was relying on it arriving.
>
> **Why fish ladders: fish like the hilsa travel from the sea up into rivers to lay eggs.** A wall across the river ends that journey and the next generation with it.
>
> **A cost that is planned for is a much smaller cost than one that is ignored.**

---

Stir sugar into one glass of water and oil into another. What do you see, and which glass is the more worrying?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> SUGAR — DISSOLVES: it vanishes and the water looks perfectly clear
> OIL   — DOES NOT dissolve: it floats in visible blobs
> ```
> **The SUGAR glass is the worrying one.**
>
> **Why: the sugar is entirely there — you could taste it — and yet nothing can be seen.**
>
> **Harmful chemicals, fertilisers and soapy waste behave like the sugar, not the oil.** They dissolve, and the water still looks clean.
>
> **So "it looks clean" is not evidence that water is safe to drink.** You cannot judge water by eye — which is exactly why water has to be tested.

---

Sort these: salt, sand, detergent, plastic, chalk powder, fertiliser. Which dissolve in water?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> DISSOLVE      salt · detergent · fertiliser
> DO NOT        sand · plastic · chalk powder
> ```
> **Why the split matters for a river: the things that do not dissolve can at least be SEEN and filtered out.**
>
> **The things that dissolve pass straight through a cloth filter and leave no trace.**
>
> **This is also why plastic, though visible, is such a problem in a different way** — it does not dissolve and it does not rot, so it simply stays, for decades.

---

Fertiliser washes off a field into a river and the surface turns green. Explain how this kills the fish, step by step.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> fertiliser reaches the water
>    → tiny water plants and algae MULTIPLY very fast (that is what
>      fertiliser is for)
>    → they cover the surface like a GREEN BLANKET
>    → the blanket BLOCKS SUNLIGHT, so the plants below it die
>    → all those dead plants ROT, and rotting USES UP the oxygen
>      dissolved in the water
>    → the fish SUFFOCATE
> ```
> **Why this is so surprising: nothing here was poisonous.**
>
> **The fertiliser did exactly what it was designed to do — grow plants — in a place where nobody wanted plants grown.**
>
> **A river covered in green looks full of life and is being killed by it.** The harm came entirely from too much of a good thing arriving in the wrong place.

---

Where do the plastic wrappers we drop end up, and why is that a problem for a river?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Rain and wind wash them into drains, drains lead to rivers, and rivers lead to the sea.**
>
> **Why plastic is worse than most litter: it does not dissolve and it does not rot.** It stays for decades, breaking into smaller and smaller pieces that never go away.
>
> **Fish and turtles swallow it, mistaking it for food, and starve with full stomachs.**
>
> **The best fix is not cleaning up but not making the waste: using fewer single-use items.** Once plastic reaches a river, getting it back is very nearly impossible.

---

Why does cutting down trees on the hills make floods worse, when trees are nowhere near the river?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> WITH TREES     leaves break the rain's fall → it lands gently on soft
>                soil → roots keep the soil loose → water SOAKS IN
>                → reaches the river slowly, over days
>
> WITHOUT TREES  rain hits bare hard soil → cannot soak in → runs off
>                fast → ALL of it reaches the river at once
> ```
> **The same amount of rain falls in both cases. What changes is the TIMING.**
>
> **A river can carry a week's rain over a week. It cannot carry a week's rain in an afternoon.**
>
> **So deforestation does not create water — it delivers the same water all at the same moment**, which is precisely what a flood is.

---

Match each flood problem to what it affects: dirty water, broken roads, lost crops, schools shut.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> dirty water    →  safe drinking, and people's health
> broken roads   →  travel and transport
> lost crops     →  food supply
> schools shut   →  learning
> ```
> **Why one problem can affect more than one thing: broken roads also stop food and medicine arriving**, so they damage health and food supply too.
>
> **This is the point of the exercise.** A flood is not a list of separate problems — the damage spreads sideways, because everything a town does depends on everything else.

---

Why is "never walk or drive through flood water" such an important rule?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because moving water only knee-deep can knock an adult off their feet.**
>
> **And because you cannot see what is under it.** Muddy flood water hides open drains, missing manhole covers, broken glass and live electrical wires.
>
> **A road you have walked a thousand times is not the same road once it is under water** — the surface may have been washed away entirely.
>
> **The rule is absolute rather than "be careful" for a good reason: careful judgement needs information, and flood water removes exactly the information you would need.**

---

Give one flood safety action for BEFORE, one for DURING, and one for AFTER, with the reason for each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> BEFORE  prepare an emergency bag with food, medicine and a torch —
>         because once water is rising there is no time to gather things
> DURING  disconnect electricity and gas — because water and electricity
>         together are lethal, and gas leaks cause fires
> AFTER   do not return home until it is declared safe — because damaged
>         buildings collapse, and standing water carries disease
> ```
> **Why the three stages have different logic: BEFORE is about preparation, DURING is about survival, AFTER is about not being harmed by a danger that has not left with the water.**
>
> **Most flood injuries happen in the "after" stage**, when people believe the danger has passed.

---

Which is worse for a village — a flood or a drought? Argue your answer.

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are different in kind, and a drought more often ends a village.**
>
> **A flood is a disaster that ENDS.** The water goes down, and people rebuild in the same place.
>
> **A drought has no known end date.** If the wells and the river stay dry for months, there is nothing to wait for — so families leave for places where water is still available, and often do not return.
>
> **But a flood kills faster and destroys more in a single day.**
>
> **The honest answer: a flood is a sharper disaster, a drought a deeper one.** Both need preparing for, and the same region often faces both.

---

Chennai ran water trains in 2019 and Bengaluru flooded in 2022. What does that pair of facts teach?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That managing water means preparing for too LITTLE and too MUCH — often in the same country and within a few years.**
>
> **Why both happen: the same underlying cause can produce either.** Paved cities cannot absorb rain, so the water rushes away as a flood in the monsoon and there is nothing left underground for summer.
>
> **A city that solves only one of the two has solved neither.**
>
> **The fixes overlap, which is the encouraging part: ponds, lakes, open soil and trees all reduce floods AND store water for the dry season.**

---

What is a water ATM and what problem does it solve?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A machine that gives clean water in exchange for a card or coin.**
>
> **The problem it solves: many places have no reliable piped supply of safe drinking water**, so people must either buy expensive bottled water or drink water that may make them ill.
>
> **Why it helps: it puts treated water within walking distance at a small price**, and dispenses only what is asked for, so little is wasted.
>
> **Why it is not a complete answer: it treats the symptom.** A city still needs a clean river and a working supply system — the ATM is a bridge, not a destination.

---

A tap in your home drips slowly all month. Is this really worth fixing?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — a slow drip wastes far more than almost any single act of care can save.**
>
> **Why: a drip loses water every second of every day, whether anyone is home or not.** A dozen drops a minute becomes many litres a day and hundreds over a month.
>
> **Compare that with turning the tap off while brushing — a good habit, but it acts for two minutes a day.**
>
> **The pattern behind all water saving: stop the losses that happen without anybody noticing.** Those are always bigger than the ones you can see.

---

Why is water that has been used to wash vegetables worth keeping?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because it was never dirty enough to throw away — it can water plants perfectly well.**
>
> **Why this matters more than it sounds: the water in your tap has been collected, carried, cleaned and pumped**, and all of that costs energy and money.
>
> **Treated drinking water is the most expensive water there is, and pouring it on a plant is a waste of the treatment, not just the water.**
>
> **The general rule: match the quality of the water to the job.** Plants do not need drinking-quality water; people do.

---

You are planning a new city beside a river. Give one step each for keeping the river clean, preventing floods, and having enough water in summer.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> CLEAN   treat all sewage and factory waste BEFORE it reaches the
>         river — because once dissolved, it cannot be got back out
> FLOODS  keep open soil, parks and ponds instead of paving everything —
>         so rain soaks in slowly instead of arriving all at once
> SUMMER  harvest rainwater from every rooftop into tanks and soak pits —
>         so the monsoon's water is still there in April
> ```
> **Notice that the second and third steps are the SAME step.** Land that absorbs rain both prevents the flood and stores the water.
>
> **That is the most useful thing to know about water planning: the best solutions solve both extremes at once**, which is why they are worth the cost.

---

Could you design a simple water filter from everyday materials? What would it remove and what would it not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Layers in a bottle with the bottom cut off, inverted: cloth, then fine sand, then coarse sand, then small stones, with cloth at the neck.**
>
> **Why the layers go coarse to fine as the water travels down: the big stones catch leaves and large bits first**, so the fine sand does not clog immediately.
>
> **What it removes: mud, sand, leaves, insects — everything that does NOT dissolve.**
>
> **What it does NOT remove: anything dissolved — salt, chemicals, fertiliser — or germs.**
>
> **This is the sugar-and-oil lesson again.** The filter deals with the oil, not the sugar — so filtered water still needs boiling before it is safe to drink.

---

Why would a blind river dolphin be especially harmed by a busy, polluted river?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the Ganges river dolphin finds its way and its food entirely by SOUND, not sight.**
>
> **So noise from boat engines is not a nuisance to it — it is the equivalent of blinding a sighted animal.** It cannot navigate or hunt through the din.
>
> **And because it must surface to breathe, it cannot escape by going deep.**
>
> **Why this is a general lesson: harm depends on the animal, not just on the pollution.** A change that barely troubles a fish can be fatal to a creature whose whole life depends on the sense that change affects.
