What four things does a "green school" do?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> manages its WASTE          separates it, sends what can be reused
> SAVES WATER                fixes leaks, catches rainwater
> uses ELECTRICITY carefully lights and fans off when nobody is there
> grows GREEN                plants trees and looks after them
> ```
> **What all four have in common: each is "save something that is easy to waste".**
>
> **Water, electricity and materials share a strange property — nobody notices them leaving.** A dripping tap makes no mess; a fan in an empty room looks exactly like a fan doing its job.
>
> **So they are wasted by DEFAULT, not by decision.** Nobody chooses to waste them, which is why noticing has to come first.

---

Why do the school explorer teams get specific questions instead of being told to "look around"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because a person told to look around sees only what they already expected.**
>
> **A person given a specific question — "are the water tanks covered?" — goes and finds out.**
>
> **Why this matters beyond school: this is exactly how scientists work.** You cannot observe everything, so you decide in advance what you are observing FOR.
>
> **The question comes first, the looking second.** Reversing that order is the difference between an investigation and a wander.

---

After exploring, four questions are asked. Which is the most valuable, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> What did you find?
> Was anything concerning?
> Did anything SURPRISE you?      ← the most valuable
> What made you proud?
> ```
> **Why "surprise" is the best question: a surprise is the sign that you held a belief which was wrong.**
>
> **Everything else confirms what you suspected.** Only the surprise teaches you something new.
>
> **This is why scientists chase surprises rather than avoid them.** An experiment that goes as expected changes nothing.

---

What goes in a green bin and what goes in a blue one?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> GREEN — WET waste : food peels, leftovers, garden waste — things that ROT
> BLUE  — DRY waste : paper, plastic, glass, metal, cloth — things that
>                     do NOT rot
> ```
> **Why the split is by "rots / does not rot" rather than by material: because that decides what can be DONE with it.**
>
> **Wet waste can be composted into useful soil. Dry waste can be recycled into new things.**
>
> **The bins are not sorted by what the waste IS but by where it is GOING.**

---

Why does mixing wet and dry waste ruin both?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because each half's use is destroyed by the other.**
>
> **Wet waste can be composted — but not with plastic and glass mixed in.** **Dry waste can be recycled — but wet food waste soaks the paper and dirties everything, and dirty material cannot be recycled.**
>
> **So separated, both halves are worth something. Mixed, both halves are rubbish.**
>
> **And the separating has to happen at the MOMENT of throwing.** Nobody can un-mix it afterwards — which is exactly why it is your job and not the sanitation worker's.

---

Why are old newspapers never put in any bin?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because they are worth money — recyclers buy them.**
>
> **Different recyclers collect different things: paper, metal, bottles, plastic.**
>
> **Why this system works so well: it is a TRADE, not a favour.** Both sides gain, so nobody has to be persuaded to keep it going.
>
> **It is one of the oldest recycling systems in the world**, and it has survived because it pays for itself.

---

How does separating your waste properly protect sanitation workers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because they are the ones who open the bag.**
>
> **Broken glass, sharp metal, needles and medical waste hidden in a mixed bin cut the hands of whoever handles it.** Wet waste mixed with dry also means sorting by hand through rotting material.
>
> **So separating is not only about compost and recycling — it is about the safety of a specific person.**
>
> **This is worth saying plainly: the two seconds you save by not separating are paid for by somebody else's injury risk.**

---

Two tiles are painted black and white and left in the sun. Which gets hotter, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The BLACK one gets much hotter.**
>
> **Why: a black surface ABSORBS most of the sunlight falling on it and turns it into heat.** A white surface **REFLECTS** most of it straight back, so that energy never becomes heat in the tile at all.
>
> **Note what is NOT happening: the white paint is not "keeping heat out".** The light simply bounces away instead of being taken in.
>
> **This is why light-coloured clothes are more comfortable in summer than dark ones** — the same physics, worn.

---

What would happen if you painted your school roof white?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The rooms below would be noticeably cooler.**
>
> **Why: the roof takes the full force of the midday sun, and a dark roof absorbs that energy and passes the heat down into the rooms.** A white roof reflects most of it back into the sky.
>
> **How much difference it makes: enough that whole cities now do it** as a cheap way to reduce summer heat.
>
> **Why it is such a good solution: it costs one afternoon of painting and then works every summer for years, with nothing to switch on and no electricity bill.**

---

Name three ways to keep a room cool that do not need electricity, and explain each.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> SHADE TREES  stop the sunlight before it ever reaches the wall
> WINDOWS placed so a breeze passes through — moving air carries heat away
> THICK WALLS  take hours to warm through, so the inside stays cool
>              through the hottest part of the day
> ```
> **Also: a white roof, and a clay pot for cool drinking water.**
>
> **What they all share: each is a DESIGN choice, made once, that then keeps working for free.**
>
> **A fan cools a room only while electricity flows into it. A tree, a white roof and a well-placed window cool a room for thirty years and are never switched on.**

---

A glass fills from a dripping tap in 10 minutes. How much water is lost in a day and a month?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> a glass is about 250 ml
> in one hour  →  6 glasses  = 1.5 litres
> in one day   →  144 glasses = about 36 litres
> in one month →  more than 1,000 litres
> ```
> **Why this is so surprising: a drop is obviously nothing.**
>
> **The mistake is judging the DROP instead of the DRIP.** The drop is tiny, but it repeats every second of every day, whether anyone is at school or not.
>
> **A leak works while you sleep. That is what makes it big.**

---

Which saves more water: everyone in a class turning off the tap while brushing, or fixing one leaking tap?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fixing the leak, very often.**
>
> **Why: turning off the tap while brushing is a good habit, but it acts for about two minutes a day per person.** A leak acts for 1,440 minutes a day, every day, with nobody present.
>
> **This is not an argument against good habits** — it is an argument for looking in the right place first.
>
> **The general rule: look for the losses that happen when nobody is there.** Those are always the large ones, because nothing interrupts them.

---

Two glasses of water are put out, one under a tree and one in the open sun. What happens, and why is the tree better than a tin sheet of the same size?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The glass in the sun warms much more.**
>
> **The tree's canopy stopped the sunlight before it arrived — that much a tin sheet would also do.**
>
> **But a tree does more: water evaporating from its leaves takes heat away with it**, so the air under a tree is cooler than the air under a sheet of tin.
>
> **A tree is a shade AND a cooler.** That is why a row of trees lowers the temperature of a whole street, not just the strip beneath them.

---

Name four things a tree gives besides shade.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> keeps the AIR clean
> provides FOOD and shelter to birds and insects
> its ROOTS hold the soil in place and help rain soak in
> it COOLS the air by evaporating water from its leaves
> ```
> **Why the root job matters more than it sounds: soil held by roots does not wash away in heavy rain**, and soil that lets water soak in recharges groundwater instead of flooding the street.
>
> **This is Chapter 2's flood lesson, met from the other side.** A tree does its most important work where you cannot see it.

---

Why does planting particular flowers bring butterflies to a school garden?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because butterflies are choosy — particular butterflies feed on and lay eggs on particular plants.**
>
> **Plant the right flowers and those butterflies come; plant the wrong ones and no amount of hoping will help.**
>
> **And the birds that eat insects follow the insects.**
>
> **Why this is the useful way to think about it: you are not asking wildlife to arrive — you are giving it a REASON to.** A habitat is not a place with a welcome sign; it is a place that supplies what something needs.

---

Why is the rule "walk facing the traffic" when there is no footpath?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because it puts you in a position where YOU can act.**
>
> **Walking with your back to the traffic, you cannot see a vehicle coming towards you** and must rely entirely on the driver seeing you.
>
> **Facing it, you can see the danger and step aside.**
>
> **This is the shape of every good safety rule: it moves you from hoping somebody else does the right thing to being able to react yourself.** Look for that pattern and safety rules stop being a list to memorise.

---

Design a signboard for a school gate. What makes a good one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **One clear message, in very few words or a single symbol.**
>
> **Why so short: a driver has about one second to read it while moving.** A sign with three sentences is a sign nobody reads.
>
> **Good examples: "SCHOOL — GO SLOW", a walking-children symbol, "20 km/h".**
>
> **What else helps beyond a sign: a speed breaker (which does not need to be read at all), a marked pedestrian crossing, staggered exit times so the crowd is smaller, and adults on duty at the gate.**

---

In a fire, why should you crawl low under smoke?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because SMOKE RISES, so the cleanest air is near the floor.**
>
> **Why smoke rises: hot air is lighter than cool air, and smoke travels with the hot air from the fire.** It fills a room from the ceiling downwards.
>
> **Why this is more urgent than it sounds: smoke harms people long before flames reach them.** Most fire deaths are caused by breathing smoke, not by burning.
>
> **Covering your mouth and staying low buys you the one thing you need — breathable air while you get out.**

---

Why must you never go back into a building once you are outside?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because conditions change in seconds, and more people are hurt returning than in the original escape.**
>
> **A route that was clear a minute ago may now be full of smoke; a room that was safe may be alight.** And once you are back inside, nobody knows you are there.
>
> **Why it is stated as an absolute rule rather than "be careful": careful judgement needs information**, and a burning building gives you none.
>
> **The right action is to tell staff at the assembly point that someone is missing, immediately.** Rescuers who know WHO and WHERE can act far faster and far more safely than you can.

---

Why is a fire drill practised when there is no fire?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because in a real emergency there is no time to read instructions or make a plan. People do what they have already done before.**
>
> **A drill installs the route and the actions into your body**, so that fear does not have to be overcome first.
>
> **That is the entire purpose: not to LEARN the rules, but to make them AUTOMATIC.**
>
> **This is also why a drill is done more than once, and without warning.** A rehearsal you were expecting is not a rehearsal of the real thing.

---

One student scribbles on a bench. Another watches and says nothing. Has the second student done anything?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — they have taught the first that it is acceptable.**
>
> **Why silence is not neutral: the person doing it is watching for a reaction.** No reaction reads as approval.
>
> **This is why "I did not do anything" is not quite the same as "I did nothing wrong".**
>
> **But speaking up is hard, and there are gentler versions of it:** telling a teacher, helping clean it afterwards, or simply not laughing along. **Any of those changes the signal.**

---

A younger child copies an older one throwing a wrapper. What does this show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That behaviour spreads by EXAMPLE far more than by instruction.**
>
> **The younger child has been told not to litter, probably many times.** They copied what they SAW, not what they were told.
>
> **Why this makes older students unusually powerful: they are being watched whether they know it or not**, and are teaching all day without meaning to.
>
> **It also explains why adults' behaviour matters so much.** A rule that adults break is a rule that has already been repealed.

---

Two students push in at the water tap while others wait. Why does this matter, if it only saves them a minute?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Apply the test: would this be all right if EVERYONE did it?**
>
> **One person cutting the queue barely matters. Everyone cutting the queue means there IS no queue** — just a crush where the strongest get water and the smallest wait longest.
>
> **So the minute saved was taken from someone, and the person it was taken from is usually the one least able to object.**
>
> **Most rules about behaviour are this one question, already answered.** Asking it yourself is quicker than learning the rules one at a time.

---

A group of children is noisy in a public park where others are doing yoga. What is the issue?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A shared space belongs to everyone in it, and being noisy takes something away from the others.**
>
> **Why it is not simply "children should be quiet": playing loudly in a park is entirely reasonable.** The issue is doing it beside people who need quiet, when moving twenty metres away would cost nothing.
>
> **The question is not "am I allowed to?" but "is this the place?"**
>
> **Most conflicts in shared spaces are like this — not one side being wrong, but two reasonable uses colliding in one spot.**

Imagine a school day from the point of view of a student who uses a wheelchair. What might be hard?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Steps with no ramp; a toilet too narrow to enter; a lab bench too high; a playground you can watch but not join; a classroom on the first floor with no lift.**
>
> **And the things that are hard but not physical: being helped when you did not ask, or being left out because it is easier.**
>
> **Why this exercise is worth doing: a building that "works fine" works fine FOR THE PEOPLE WHO CAN USE IT.** Everyone else's difficulty is invisible to everyone else.
>
> **Most of these problems have cheap fixes — a ramp, a wider door, a table at the right height.** They stay unfixed mainly because nobody whose day is easy has noticed.

You are principal for one day. Name three changes for happiness, safety and greenery, with reasons.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> HAPPINESS  a shaded seating area in the playground — so children who
>            do not want to run about have somewhere to be, instead of
>            standing at the edge
> SAFETY     a marked pedestrian crossing and a speed breaker at the gate
>            — a speed breaker works on drivers who are not reading signs
> GREENERY   a compost pit plus trees along the sunniest wall — the pit
>            turns wet waste into soil for the trees, and the trees cool
>            the classrooms behind that wall
> ```
> **Notice the third one solves two problems with one action.** The waste has somewhere useful to go AND the building gets cooler.
>
> **The best changes are usually the ones that connect two problems**, which is why it is worth listing the problems before choosing the fixes.
