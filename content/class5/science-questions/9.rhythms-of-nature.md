Give one change that happens in minutes, one over a year, and one over ages.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> MINUTES   the sky changing colour · a cloud moving · a puddle shrinking
> A YEAR    leaves falling and growing back · a bird migrating and returning
> AGES      a river carving a valley · mountains rising
> ```
> **Why we notice some and miss others: we notice a change when it happens fast enough to compare with what we remember.**
>
> **A change much slower than that becomes invisible** — nobody has ever seen a valley deepen, yet it is happening.
>
> **So "nothing is changing" almost always means "nothing is changing at MY speed."** That is why scientists take measurements over time instead of trusting the impression.

---

The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west. Is the Sun moving?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — the EARTH is turning.**
>
> **The Sun stays where it is. The Earth ROTATES on its own axis, once in about 24 hours.**
>
> **Why it looks the other way round: you are standing ON the thing that is turning.**
>
> **When a bus moves off, the trees outside seem to slide backwards.** You know they do not really — but that is exactly what you see. The Earth carries you east, so everything in the sky appears to swing west.

---

If the Earth is spinning, why can't you feel it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the turn never CHANGES, and everything around you is turning with you.**
>
> **You only feel movement when something alters** — a bus starting, stopping or going round a corner. Sitting in a bus at a steady speed on a smooth road, you feel nothing.
>
> **The Earth's spin is perfectly smooth and has been going on all your life.**
>
> **And the air, the ground, your school and everything in it are moving at exactly the same speed as you.** Nothing is being left behind, so there is nothing to notice.

---

A torch is shone at a globe. What does the lit side show, and what happens when you turn the globe?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The lit side has DAY and the dark side has NIGHT. Turning the globe carries each country from the lit side into the dark side and back again.**
>
> **Why exactly HALF is lit: the Earth is a ball, and light travels in straight lines.** Light reaching a ball can only fall on the half facing the source — the far half is in the ball's own shadow.
>
> **So it is always daytime somewhere and night somewhere else, at the same moment.**
>
> **Note the torch is held STILL.** The whole demonstration works with only one thing moving — the Earth.

---

When it is daytime in India, where is it night?

> [!ANSWER]
> **On the opposite side of the globe — over the Pacific Ocean and the Americas.**
>
> **Why: the two sides are exactly half a turn apart**, so when one faces the Sun the other faces away.
>
> **How to check on a globe: find India, then look at the point directly through the middle and out the other side.** That is roughly where midnight is when India has midday.
>
> **This is also why a phone call to another country can wake somebody up.** There is no single time "now" for the whole Earth.

---

Why does Dong in Arunachal Pradesh see sunrise before Gujarat does?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the Earth turns from west to east, so eastern places move into the sunlight first.**
>
> **Dong is India's easternmost inhabited village — the very first Indian land the Sun's rays touch each morning.**
>
> **How much difference it makes: India is wide enough that the far east sees sunrise well over an hour before the far west.**
>
> **Why India still uses ONE clock time throughout: it is far simpler for trains, offices and broadcasts.** The cost is that in the far east the sun rises very early and in the far west it sets very late.

---

What is a globe, and what do its colours mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A globe is a model of the Earth, shaped like a ball.**
> ```
> BLUE parts  seas and oceans — about THREE-QUARTERS of the surface
> the rest    LAND, where all the countries are
> ```
> **An OCEAN is vast and open; a SEA is smaller and partly enclosed by land.**
>
> **Why a globe rather than a flat map: the Earth is a ball, and no flat picture of a ball can be right about everything at once.** A flat map must stretch something — which is why Greenland looks enormous on many of them.

---

Why do the seasons come back in the same order every year?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because the Earth travels all the way round the Sun once a YEAR — and is TILTED as it goes.**
>
> **For part of the year your half of the world leans a little towards the Sun: longer days, more direct sunlight, warmer.** For the other part it leans away: shorter days, weaker sunlight, colder.
>
> **The journey takes exactly a year and then starts again.** That is why the pattern REPEATS rather than merely happening.
>
> **A season is not a stage of the weather. It is a POSITION IN A JOURNEY** — which is why it is so reliable.

---

Explain what a monsoon is and why India has one.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A monsoon is a wind that CHANGES DIRECTION with the season.**
>
> **Why it happens: land heats up faster than the sea, and cools faster too.**
> ```
> SUMMER  the land becomes far hotter than the Arabian Sea and the Bay
>         of Bengal → moist air is drawn IN from the sea → heavy rain
> WINTER  the land is cooler than the sea → the winds REVERSE, blowing
>         OUT from the land → dry
> ```
> **Why this matters so much: it is why India has a distinct rainy season** rather than rain spread evenly through the year. **Nearly the whole farming calendar is built around it.**

---

Name India's six seasons in order.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> VASANTA  (spring)     Feb–Apr   flowers bloom, new leaves, pleasant
> GRISHMA  (summer)     Apr–Jun   hot and dry, ponds shrink, long days
> VARSHA   (monsoon)    Jun–Aug   heavy rain, rivers full, all green
> SHARAD   (autumn)     Aug–Oct   rain eases, clear skies, crops ripen
> HEMANT   (pre-winter) Oct–Dec   cooling, dew in the mornings
> SHISHIR  (winter)     Dec–Feb   cold, short days, mist and fog
> ```
> **Why six and not four: the four-season scheme comes from cooler countries where the rains are not a separate event.**
>
> **In India the monsoon is far too important to be folded into "summer".** A calendar should name the changes that actually matter where you live.

---

Winter in Kerala is nothing like winter in Kashmir. Give three reasons regions differ.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> HOW FAR NORTH   the far north gets much colder winters
> HOW CLOSE TO THE SEA  the sea warms and cools SLOWLY, so coastal
>                 places are mild and even; inland places swing between
>                 fierce heat and real cold
> MOUNTAINS       they force moist air upward, so one side of a range
>                 is soaked and the other is dry
> ```
> **Why the sea has such a levelling effect: water takes far more heating to warm up and gives its heat back slowly.**
>
> **A coastal town is sitting beside an enormous store of steady temperature.** An inland town has nothing to steady it.

---

India contains both Mawsynram and the Thar Desert. What does that show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That "India's climate" is not one thing.**
>
> **Mawsynram in Meghalaya is the wettest inhabited place on Earth; the Thar Desert is among the driest** — in the same country, in the same year.
>
> **Why the difference is so extreme: Mawsynram sits where moist monsoon air is forced up against hills and must drop its water.** The Thar lies where that air has already passed by or arrives dry.
>
> **The practical consequence: a single national rule about water, crops or building would fit neither place.** Anything sensible has to be local.

---

Why do some crops grow best in winter and others in summer?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because each plant needs a particular amount of warmth, light and water — and different seasons supply different amounts.**
>
> **Wheat and mustard do well in the cool, dry winter; rice needs the heavy water of the monsoon; some vegetables need the long hot days of summer.**
>
> **Why farmers cannot simply plant whatever they like whenever they like: a crop sown in the wrong season either dries out, drowns, or grows without ever fruiting.**
>
> **Choosing WHEN to sow is as important a decision as choosing WHAT** — and the season decides it, not the farmer.

Match these to their season: Holi, Diwali, Pongal, Baisakhi.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> PONGAL, MAKAR SANKRANTI  →  WINTER — the harvest is in, a thanksgiving
> HOLI                     →  SPRING — flowers bloom, the cold ends
> DIWALI                   →  AUTUMN — after the harvest, when there
>                             is plenty
> BAISAKHI, GUDI PADWA, VISHU, RONGALI BIHU
>                          →  NEW YEAR and harvest — a new farming
>                             year begins
> ```
> **Notice that three of the four are tied to a harvest.** That is not a coincidence — see the next question.

---

Why do so many festivals fall just after a harvest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because three things must line up before a big celebration is even possible: FOOD to spare, WORK finished, and EVERYBODY free at the same time.**
>
> **A harvest is the one moment in the year when all three are true.**
>
> **So festivals are not scattered randomly through the year** — they cluster where the farming calendar makes room for them, which is why so many fall in January and around October–November.
>
> **India celebrates over a thousand festivals a year**, and this is a large part of the reason.

---

In Odisha, farmers begin sowing when the koel starts singing. How can a bird know the rains are coming?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It does not know — it REACTS.**
>
> **The koel is responding to the same changes in warmth, moisture and daylight that also bring the monsoon.** It is not predicting anything; it is simply noticing sooner than a person would.
>
> **Why that still makes it a useful sign: the bird's senses are tuned to changes too small for us to feel.** So its behaviour is an early reading of conditions we will notice later.
>
> **The same is true of ants carrying eggs to higher ground before rain.** Free information, if you know how to read it.

---

Why is Nag Panchami, honouring snakes, celebrated during the monsoon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because that is when snakes come out of their flooded burrows and are most often seen near people.**
>
> **Why they come out: heavy rain fills the underground holes they live in**, so they must move to drier, higher ground — which is often where houses and fields are.
>
> **So the festival falls at exactly the time when respect and caution around snakes matter most.**
>
> **This is a pattern worth noticing across many festivals: a custom that looks purely religious often sits precisely where it is practically useful.**

---

Name four cycles from your science this year and say what they share.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> DAY AND NIGHT   one turn of the Earth        — about 24 hours
> THE SEASONS     one journey round the Sun    — one year
> THE WATER CYCLE evaporation → clouds → rain → collection → repeat
> A LIFE CYCLE    egg → caterpillar → cocoon → moth → egg
> ```
> **What they share: the LAST step produces the FIRST, so there is no end.**
>
> **Why that matters so much: a cycle makes the future PREDICTABLE.** Farmers can plan a sowing months ahead; birds migrate back to a place they left a year ago; you know it will be cold in December.
>
> **Change is not the opposite of order.** In nature, the changing IS the order.

---

Would you like one season to last all year? Argue both ways.

> [!ANSWER]
> **For: no more discomfort — no heat that exhausts you, no cold that hurts, no floods.**
>
> **Against — and this is much the stronger case: almost everything would stop.**
>
> **Crops need different seasons at different stages: rain to grow, dry weather to ripen and be harvested.** A permanent monsoon would rot the grain; a permanent summer would leave nothing to harvest at all.
>
> **Trees that lose leaves, animals that hibernate or migrate, and insects with a yearly life cycle all depend on the change.**
>
> **The season you dislike is doing a job for something.** That is the honest answer.

---

Compare a globe with a flat map. Which is more accurate?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The globe — because the Earth really is a ball.**
>
> **Why a flat map must always be wrong somewhere: you cannot flatten the surface of a ball without stretching or tearing it.** Try flattening an orange peel.
>
> **So every flat map stretches something.** On many common maps, countries far from the equator look far larger than they are.
>
> **Why we use flat maps anyway: you can fold one, print it, put it on a wall or in a book.** **A globe is truer; a map is usable. Both are worth having, and knowing the cost of each is the real skill.**

The class chart tracks plant life, animals, air, water and human activities across four periods of the year. Why track all five together?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because they change TOGETHER, and the connections are the interesting part.**
>
> **Tracked separately, you get five lists.** Tracked together, you see that when the rains come, the ponds fill AND the frogs appear AND the farmers sow AND the trees green — all in the same weeks.
>
> **Why that matters: it shows the season is one event, not five coincidences.**
>
> **This is the same idea as the forest in Chapter 5** — the parts are doing each other's work, so studying one alone tells you less than half the story.

---

You grew taller this year, and so did a tree outside. Are these the same kind of change?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both are growth, but they differ in one important way: yours will STOP and the tree's mostly will not.**
>
> **A person grows for about eighteen years and then stays the same height for the rest of their life.** Most trees keep adding growth every year they live.
>
> **Why the difference matters: you can date a tree from its growth** — its rings record every year — while you cannot tell a person's age from their height at all.
>
> **Both are examples of slow change you only notice by comparison** — a pencil mark on a doorframe, or a photograph from last year.

---

Someone says "the weather is just random". Using this chapter, argue against them.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Weather on a given day is unpredictable; the SEASON is not.**
>
> **Nobody can say whether it will rain on 14 July. But everybody in India knows that July is far more likely to be wet than January** — and farmers, festivals and school calendars are all built on that certainty.
>
> **Why the season is reliable when the day is not: the season comes from the Earth's position in its yearly journey**, which never varies. The day's weather comes from clouds and winds, which do.
>
> **So there are two different questions here**, and confusing them is what makes the weather seem more random than it is.
