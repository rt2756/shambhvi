Why do woven strips hold together while stacked strips slide apart?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because weaving TRAPS every strip.**
>
> **Stacked strips are simply lying on each other — nothing holds anything**, so they slide apart the moment you lift them.
>
> **Woven, each strip is held down by the strips it goes under and holds down the ones it goes over.**
>
> **Each thread is doing two jobs at once: being gripped and gripping.** That mutual grip is the whole of weaving.

---

Why must each row of a weave be the opposite of the row above it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because if two rows went over and under in the SAME places, nothing would be trapped.**
>
> **The strips would all pass on the same side of each other, so there would be no interlocking at all — just a stack again.**
>
> **Reversing each row means every strip is caught by the one above and the one below.**
>
> **This is the same rule as the woven mats in Class 5 maths Chapter 7.** The mathematics of the pattern and the reason it works are one and the same thing.

---

What is a baya weaver bird's nest made of, and what does the difference between an expert's nest and a young bird's tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Grass, threaded over and under to make a strong hanging pouch.**
>
> **An expert bird weaves a very fine nest; a young bird's first attempts are rough.**
>
> **What that tells you: even weaving has to be LEARNT.** The bird is not simply born knowing how — it improves with practice, like a human weaver.
>
> **Why it is worth noticing: it is easy to assume animals do everything by instinct and people do everything by learning.** The truth is untidier, and more interesting.

---

Look at your shirt through a magnifying glass. What do you see, and how does it relate to a paper mat?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A criss-cross pattern of threads going over and under each other — exactly the paper mat, only far finer.**
>
> **Why the pattern is the same at both scales: the reason weaving works does not depend on size.** Trapping and being trapped works with paper strips a centimetre wide and with threads too thin to see.
>
> **The only difference is the material and how small you can make it.**
>
> **This is a good habit generally: when something looks complicated, look for the simple thing repeated.**

Name three Indian handloom traditions and where they come from.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> KANJEEVARAM — Tamil Nadu
> PASHMINA    — Kashmir (wool from the Changthangi goat of Ladakh)
> IKAT        — Odisha and Gujarat
> PATOLA      — Patan, Gujarat: one saree takes 6 months to a year
> ```
> **Why the Patola figure is worth pausing on: six months to a year of one person's work for a single saree.**
>
> **That is why such a saree costs what it does** — you are not buying cloth, you are buying most of a year of somebody's skilled life.

---

Why does handloom weaving matter to India beyond the cloth it produces?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It employs over 45 lakh people, especially women and rural artisans — one of the largest cottage industries in the world.**
>
> **It uses NO ELECTRICITY**, so it is genuinely eco-friendly and can be done at home, in a village, anywhere.
>
> **And each tradition carries designs and techniques that exist nowhere else.**
>
> **Why that last point is urgent: if the last weaver of a pattern stops, the pattern is gone.** There is no book that contains it — it lives only in the hands that know it.

---

Pull a strand from a ball of cotton and it snaps. Twist it as you pull and it holds. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because twisting presses the fibres against each other, and pressed fibres cannot slide past one another.**
>
> **An untwisted strand fails because the fibres are SHORT and simply slip apart.**
>
> **The beautiful part: pulling on a twisted thread TIGHTENS the twist, which grips harder still.** The thread defends itself against exactly the force trying to break it.
>
> **So a thread is not a long fibre.** It is thousands of short fibres held together by nothing but friction — and that is why a rope of any length can be made from fibres a few centimetres long.

---

What is a charkha, and what does it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A spinning wheel — it twists cotton fibres into thread, exactly as your fingers and a pencil do, only faster and far more evenly.**
>
> **Why evenness matters: a thread with thick and thin patches breaks at the thin patches**, and weaves into a lumpy cloth.
>
> **A wheel keeps the twist and the pull steady**, so every centimetre of thread is the same as the last.
>
> **The machine is not doing anything your fingers cannot do.** It is doing it identically, thousands of times over.

---

Why did Gandhiji make spinning a symbol of the freedom struggle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because knowing how to make your own cloth means not depending on anyone else for it.**
>
> **Spinning the thread and weaving the fabric turns a village into something that can clothe itself.** The cloth made this way is **KHADI**.
>
> **Why a symbol was needed rather than an argument: a charkha is a thing anybody could hold and use.** It made *atmanirbhar* — self-reliance — something you could do that afternoon, not something to agree with.
>
> **A symbol that people can actually take part in is far stronger than one they can only look at.**

Sort these into natural and synthetic: cotton, nylon, wool, polyester, jute, silk, rayon.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> NATURAL    cotton (plant) · jute (plant) · wool (sheep, goat) ·
>            silk (silk moth)
> SYNTHETIC  nylon · polyester · rayon
> ```
> **The dividing line: natural fibres come from something that GREW; synthetic ones are manufactured.**
>
> **Note that natural fibres split further into PLANT and ANIMAL sources**, which matters because they behave differently — plant fibres like cotton breathe well, animal fibres like wool trap warmth.

---

Is a natural fibre always better than a synthetic one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — they suit different jobs.**
>
> **Natural fibres took land, water and months of growing, and they eventually rot away when discarded.**
>
> **Synthetic fibres can be made in any quantity with chosen properties — strong, stretchy, waterproof, cheap — but most do NOT rot**, so they stay as waste for decades.
>
> **Cotton breathes and is comfortable in heat. Nylon makes a rope that will not rot in the rain.**
>
> **The sensible question is not "which is good?" but "which suits this job?" — and increasingly, "what happens to it afterwards?"**

---

How could you guess what a piece of cloth is made of, just by handling it?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> cotton    soft, not shiny, not stretchy, comfortable in heat
> silk      very smooth and SHINY, light
> wool      thick, slightly rough, very warm
> jute      coarse and rough — sacks and ropes
> polyester smooth, slightly shiny, does not crease, often stretchy
> ```
> **Why shininess is such a good clue: a shiny fibre has a very smooth surface that reflects light evenly.**
>
> **Silk and many synthetics do; cotton and wool have rougher surfaces that scatter the light**, so they look dull.
>
> **You are reading the surface of something far too small to see, from how it looks.**

Put the silk moth's life cycle in order: cocoon, eggs, moth, caterpillar.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> 1. the silk moth LAYS EGGS
> 2. the eggs hatch into tiny CATERPILLARS (silkworms)
> 3. the caterpillars eat mulberry leaves and grow big
> 4. each spins a COCOON of silk thread around itself
> 5. an adult MOTH emerges from the cocoon
> 6. and the cycle begins again
> ```
> **Why it is called a CYCLE: the last stage produces the first, so there is no end.**
>
> **A life cycle is the pattern of growth and change every living thing goes through.** A butterfly, a mango tree and a person all have one — they differ in the stages, not in the idea.

---

Why is silk so fine and so shiny, when cotton is not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because silk arrives as ONE enormously long strand and never needed to be spun.**
>
> **The caterpillar builds its cocoon by winding a single continuous thread round and round itself, sometimes hundreds of metres long.**
>
> **So there are no short fibre-ends sticking out** — nothing to scatter the light, nothing to weaken the thread.
>
> **Cotton must be twisted precisely to make up for being short.** Silk never had that problem, and that single fact explains its smoothness, its shine and its strength together.

---

How is silk thread got out of a cocoon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The cocoons are put in hot water and the thread is gently pulled out and unwound, then made into yarn.**
>
> **Why hot water: it softens the natural gum holding the strand's coils together**, so the thread can be unwound without tearing.
>
> **Why unwinding rather than cutting: cutting would turn one continuous strand into thousands of short pieces** — destroying the exact property that makes silk worth having.
>
> **The whole method is designed around keeping the thread in one piece.**

---

How does a tailorbird build its nest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It sews. With its beak it pokes holes along the edge of a big leaf and pulls plant fibre or spider silk through, exactly as a tailor uses needle and thread.**
>
> **It makes a green sleeve this way and pads it to hold its eggs.**
>
> **Why "sewing" is genuinely the right word: it is not gluing or knotting — it is passing a thread repeatedly through two edges to join them.** That is the definition.
>
> **The bird arrived at the same solution people did, for the same problem, with a beak instead of a needle.**

---

Describe a running stitch.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> knot the thread
> come UP at A · go DOWN at B · come UP at C · go DOWN at D
> keep going: up, down, up, down, in a straight line
> ```
> **Why it is the first stitch anyone learns: it is one simple motion repeated**, and it joins two pieces of cloth well enough for most purposes.
>
> **Why the knot at the start matters: without it, the very first pull drags the thread straight out of the cloth.**
>
> **And a knot is needed at the END too, for the reason in the next question.**

---

If one thread breaks in a running stitch, what happens? What if one thread breaks in a woven cloth?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The stitched seam can open. The woven cloth holds.**
>
> **Why: a running stitch is ONE thread doing the whole job.** Cut it anywhere and it can pull out in both directions from that point.
>
> **A woven cloth is thousands of threads, each held by its neighbours.** One broken thread leaves a small flaw and the rest carry on.
>
> **This is the difference between a CHAIN and a NET.** A chain fails at its weakest link; a net loses one knot and keeps working.
>
> **It is also why seams are knotted at both ends**, and why strong seams use stitches that lock rather than simply run.

Kantha began as a way of joining worn-out cloths. Why is that worth knowing?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because its beauty grew directly out of THRIFT.**
>
> **Old cloths that were too worn to wear were layered and stitched together into a quilt** — and the running stitches used to hold the layers became a pattern, then an art.
>
> **Why this matters: it shows that a craft can begin as a practical solution and become beautiful along the way.**
>
> **Nobody set out to invent a decorative style.** They set out not to waste cloth, and did it so many thousands of times that the stitching became something worth looking at.

---

How does bandhani make its pattern of dots?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Small parts of the cloth are tied tightly with thread BEFORE dyeing, so the dye cannot reach them.**
>
> **When the ties are removed, each tied spot is still the original colour — and that becomes a dot in the pattern.**
>
> **Why the pattern is made by what is NOT dyed: this is the clever part.** The design exists as an absence rather than an addition.
>
> **It is all done by hand, with just fingers and thread** — which means every dot is placed by a person, and no two cloths are quite identical.

---

Why is an old shirt too valuable to throw away, even if it no longer fits?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because of everything that went into it, none of which has been used up.**
>
> **A cotton shirt began as a plant that took land, water, months of growing and a harvest.** Then the cotton was cleaned, spun into thread, woven into cloth, dyed, cut and stitched.
>
> **All that work is still sitting in the shirt. The only thing that changed is its size relative to you.**
>
> **So passing it on is not a small kindness — it is refusing to throw away everything that was spent making it.**
>
> **And if it is synthetic, it will not rot for decades**, which makes the argument stronger, not weaker.

---

List four things families do with clothes instead of throwing them away.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> pass them to a younger sibling or anyone who can use them
> cut them down or restitch them into something smaller
> join old cloths into a QUILT — the kantha tradition
> use worn-out cloth for cleaning
> sell scraps to a recycler
> ```
> **Why this list is longer in India than in many countries: cloth has been expensive relative to income for most of history**, so a whole set of habits grew around not wasting it.
>
> **Those habits are now valuable for a different reason** — textile waste has become one of the world's largest waste problems, and India already knows what to do with an old shirt.

---

Why can a rope be strong even though it is made from short fibres?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Because twisting turns short fibres into something continuous.**
>
> **Each fibre is pressed against its neighbours so tightly that it cannot slide out** — and pulling on the rope tightens the twist, gripping harder still.
>
> **So the rope's strength is not the strength of any one fibre.** It is the friction between millions of them, renewed by the very force trying to pull them apart.
>
> **This is why a rope has to be TWISTED and not merely bundled.** A bundle of the same fibres falls apart in your hands.

You have a paper mat, some stitched cloth and a leaf plate to display. What would you write on the labels?

> [!ANSWER]
> **How it was made, what it is made of, and the one idea it shows.**
> ```
> "Paper mat — woven over and under. Each strip is held down by the
>  ones it passes under, so nothing can slide out."
> "Stitched cloth — joined with a running stitch. One thread does the
>  whole job, so it is knotted at both ends."
> "Leaf plate — pinned with twigs. No glue, no thread, nothing to
>  throw away afterwards."
> ```
> **Why writing the REASON matters more than the method: anyone can copy a method.**
>
> **The reason is what lets you make something you have never been shown** — and it is the only part of the label a visitor will still remember next week.
