## 8.1 Choosing a sensible unit

**Before measuring anything, ask: is this thing HEAVY or LIGHT?** The unit has to match, or the number comes out absurd.

| Claim | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Iron almirah — 40 g | **Wrong** | 40 g is about one small biscuit |
| Rice bag — 5 kg | **Sensible** | you can lift it with two hands |
| Sofa — 30 g | **Wrong** | far too light; a sofa is tens of kg |
| Water bottle — 650 g | **Sensible** | a little more than half a litre of water |
| Refrigerator — 50 g | **Wrong** | should be around 50 **kg** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE UNIT MUST FIT THE OBJECT.**
>
> **A measurement is a COUNT of some chosen unit** — "40 g" means *forty one-gram pieces*.
>
> **If the unit is far too small, the count becomes enormous and useless** — a fridge really is about 50,000 g, and nobody wants to say that.
>
> **If the unit is far too big, the count shrinks below 1** and you lose all the detail — a biscuit is 0 kg to the nearest kilogram.
>
> **So the right unit is the one that gives a comfortable, ordinary-sized number.** That is the whole reason more than one unit exists.

> [!TIP]
> **A quick sense-check list to carry in your head:**
> ```
> a paperclip, a tablet of medicine   → milligrams / a few grams
> a bar of soap, a bottle of water    → hundreds of grams
> a school bag, a bag of rice         → a few kilograms
> a person, a fridge, a bed           → tens of kilograms
> a truckload of grain                → quintals and tonnes
> ```
> **If your answer lands in the wrong row of this list, you made a mistake somewhere** — check it before writing it down.

**Reading a dial scale:** the dial is a circle marked 0 at the top and running round through 1 kg, 2 kg, 3 kg … back to 0. **Where the needle stops is the weight.** Some dials are marked in kg only, some in 250 g steps (250 g, 500 g, 750 g, 1 kg) — **always read the labels first, because the same-looking dial can count in very different steps.**

## 8.2 The conversion ladder

```
1,000 mg  =  1 g
 1,000 g  =  1 kg
   100 kg =  1 quintal
10 quintals = 1 tonne     and so   1,000 kg = 1 tonne
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE ONE FACT THAT DRIVES EVERY CONVERSION: to go from a BIG unit down to a SMALL unit you MULTIPLY; to go from small up to big you DIVIDE.**
>
> **Why, in one sentence: a small unit is small, so it takes MORE of them to make the same amount.**
>
> ```
> 3 kg  →  grams:  3 × 1,000 = 3,000 g     (more of a smaller unit)
> 4,000 g → kg  :  4,000 ÷ 1,000 = 4 kg    (fewer of a bigger unit)
> ```
> **You never need to remember which way round it goes** — just ask "should the number get bigger or smaller?" and the answer is forced.

> [!TIP]
> **kg → g and g → mg work in EXACTLY the same way, because both steps are ×1,000.**
> ```
> 5 g  = 5,000 mg          5 kg = 5,000 g
> 4 g 100 mg = 4,100 mg    4 kg 100 g = 4,100 g
> ```
> **Learn the move once and it serves you three times over** — mg→g, g→kg, and (in the next section) ml→l.

**Quintals and tonnes** are for really heavy loads — sacks of grain, lorries, harvests.
```
5,000 kg = 50 quintals = 5 tonnes
9,000 kg = 90 quintals
8 tonnes = 8,000 kg
```
> [!TIP]
> **Check a quintal/tonne answer by walking the ladder both ways.** 5,000 kg ÷ 100 = 50 quintals, and 50 quintals ÷ 10 = 5 tonnes. **Then walk back: 5 × 1,000 = 5,000 kg ✔** If the round trip does not return your starting number, one of the two steps was wrong.

## 8.3 The mixed-unit trap — the most important idea in the chapter

**Sugar weighs 5 kg 50 g. In grams, is that 5,050 g or 5,500 g?**

> [!WARNING]
> **THE ANSWER IS 5,050 g. Writing 5,500 g is the single commonest mistake in this chapter.**
>
> **Why 5,500 is wrong:** 5,500 g means 5 kg **500** g — five hundred grams, ten times too much.
>
> **Why 5,050 is right:** the gram part has to occupy the **last THREE digits**, because it takes 1,000 g to make the next kilogram.
> ```
> 5 kg  50 g
>   ↓    ↓
>   5   050        →  5,050 g
> ```
> **50 g must be padded to 050.** The zero is not decoration — it is holding the hundreds place empty.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE THREE-PLACE RULE, and why it is true.**
>
> **1 kg = 1,000 g, and 1,000 has exactly three zeros.** So every whole kilogram lands three places to the left, and the grams get the three places to the right of it — hundreds, tens and ones of grams.
>
> ```
> kg | H  T  O   ← of grams
>  7 | 0  6  7      7 kg 67 g  = 7,067 g
>  3 | 3  0  0      3 kg 300 g = 3,300 g
>  8 | 0  6  9      8 kg 69 g  = 8,069 g
> ```
> **This is exactly the place-value idea from Chapter 1, wearing different clothes.** Ones, tens, hundreds, thousands — the kilogram is just the thousands column with a name.

**Going the other way, split off the last three digits:**
```
10,760 g  →  10 | 760  →  10 kg 760 g
 4,080 g  →   4 | 080  →   4 kg 80 g
12,042 g  →  12 | 042  →  12 kg 42 g
```
> [!TIP]
> **Cover the last three digits with your finger. What is left is the kilograms; what is hidden is the grams.** No arithmetic needed at all.

## 8.4 Comparing quantities

**You cannot compare until both quantities wear the SAME unit.** Convert first, then compare as ordinary numbers.

| Compare | Convert both | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 kg 600 g and 1,700 g | 1,600 g and 1,700 g | 1 kg 600 g is `<` 1,700 g |
| 1 kg 600 g and 1 kg 60 g | 1,600 g and 1,060 g | 1 kg 600 g is `>` 1 kg 60 g |
| 10 kg 35 g and 10,035 g | 10,035 g and 10,035 g | **equal** |
| 5 kg 50 g and 4 kg 500 g | 5,050 g and 4,500 g | 5 kg 50 g is `>` 4 kg 500 g |
| 900 g + 7,000 g and 7 kg + 900 g | 7,900 g and 7,900 g | **equal** |
| 16 g 50 mg and 50 g 16 mg | 16,050 mg and 50,016 mg | 16 g 50 mg is `<` 50 g 16 mg |
| 2,010 mg and 2 g 100 mg | 2,010 mg and 2,100 mg | 2,010 mg is `<` 2 g 100 mg |
| 9,000 mg and 90 g | 9,000 mg and 90,000 mg | 9,000 mg is `<` 90 g |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY CONVERTING FIRST IS NOT OPTIONAL.**
>
> **A number on its own says nothing — 50 of something can beat 16 of something else.** 50 mg is *lighter* than 16 g, even though 50 is bigger than 16.
>
> **Comparing numbers only works when the two numbers are counting THE SAME THING.** Once both are in mg, they both count milligrams, and then plain number-order is trustworthy.
>
> **This is the same reason you cannot add 3 apples to 4 oranges and call it 7 apples.**

> [!WARNING]
> **"10 kg 35 g" and "10035 g" look different but are the SAME weight.** Never judge by appearance — always convert. Two of the comparisons above turn out to be `=`, and both would be misread on sight.

## 8.5 Adding and subtracting quantities

**Rathna buys 2 kg 500 g of rice and 1 kg 750 g more. How much in all?**

**Three ways, all correct — choose by the numbers you are given.**

```
WAY 1 — mental, splitting into friendly pieces
   2 kg 500 g + 1 kg 750 g
   = (2 kg + 1 kg) + (500 g + 750 g)
   = 3 kg + 1,250 g
   = 3 kg + 1 kg + 250 g          [because 1,000 g regroups to 1 kg]
   = 4 kg 250 g

WAY 2 — columns, kg and g kept in separate columns
       kg |  g
        1 |            ← the carry
        2 | 5 0 0
     +  1 | 7 5 0
     ------------
        4 | 2 5 0      500 + 750 = 1,250 → write 250, carry 1 kg

WAY 3 — convert everything to grams first
     2,500 g + 1,750 g = 4,250 g = 4 kg 250 g
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE CARRY IS "1" AND NOT "10" HERE.**
>
> **In ordinary column addition you carry when a column reaches 10.** In the kg/g layout **the gram column holds up to 999**, and it is 1,000 grams that regroup into 1 kilogram.
>
> **So the carry into the kg column happens at 1,000, not at 10** — because that is what "1 kg = 1,000 g" means.
>
> **Way 3 avoids the issue entirely** by turning everything into one unit, so the ordinary carry-at-ten rule applies again. **That is exactly why Way 3 is the safest when the numbers are awkward.**

**Subtracting — 2 kg 500 g − 1 kg 750 g:**
```
   take 500 g from 2 kg 500 g   →  2 kg
   take 1 kg from 2 kg          →  1 kg          count what you removed:
   take 250 g more              →  750 g         500 + 1,000 + 250 = 1,750 ✔

   or in grams:   2,500 − 1,750 = 750 g
```
> [!TIP]
> **When you cannot take the grams away, borrow ONE KILOGRAM and turn it into 1,000 grams.**
> ```
>    9 l  0 ml        becomes   8 l  1,000 ml
>  − 5 l 300 ml                −5 l    300 ml
>                               3 l    700 ml
> ```
> **This is ordinary borrowing** — the only difference is that you borrow 1,000 instead of 10, for the reason above.

> [!TIP]
> **Choose your method by looking at the numbers first.**
> ```
> ends in 250 / 500 / 750  →  do it in your head (Way 1)
> tidy but not friendly    →  columns (Way 2)
> ugly numbers like 42,275 →  convert to one unit (Way 3)
> ```
> **A good mathematician picks the easy road, not always the same road.**

## 8.6 Multiplying and dividing quantities

**4 sacks of potatoes, each 10 kg 500 g:**
```
   4 × 10 kg 500 g
   = (4 × 10 kg) + (4 × 500 g)
   = 40 kg + 2,000 g
   = 40 kg + 2 kg = 42 kg
```
**4 kg 800 g of nuts shared equally into 4 boxes:**
```
   4 kg ÷ 4 = 1 kg          800 g ÷ 4 = 200 g
   so each box = 1 kg 200 g       (check: 4,800 ÷ 4 = 1,200 g ✔)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY YOU MAY SPLIT THE kg AND THE g APART LIKE THIS.**
>
> **10 kg 500 g really means 10 kg PLUS 500 g** — the two parts are added, not glued.
>
> **And multiplying a sum means multiplying each part:** 4 × (10 kg + 500 g) = 4 × 10 kg + 4 × 500 g. **It is the distributive idea you already use for numbers**, applied to a quantity.
>
> **The same reason lets you divide each part separately** — provided each part divides cleanly. **When it doesn't, convert to a single unit and divide once**, e.g. 8 l 400 ml ÷ 7 = 8,400 ÷ 7 = 1,200 ml = 1 l 200 ml.

> [!WARNING]
> **Splitting is only safe if BOTH parts divide exactly.** 5 kg 300 g ÷ 4 cannot be done part by part — 5 kg ÷ 4 is not a whole number of kg. **Convert: 5,300 ÷ 4 = 1,325 g = 1 kg 325 g.** When in doubt, convert.

**The double number line** — the chapter's tool for "if this, then that":
```
        ×2
  5 kg ────→ 10 kg
   |          |
  ₹73 ────→  ₹146
        ×2
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY BOTH LINES MUST BE MULTIPLIED BY THE SAME NUMBER.**
>
> **If twice as much is bought, twice as much is paid** — the two quantities move together, in step.
>
> **So whatever you do to the top line, do to the bottom.** The two rows stay locked, which is why the picture answers the question without any formula.
>
> ```
> 5 kg plastic costs ₹30  →  10 kg costs ₹60      (×2 on both lines)
> 10 kg iron costs ₹200   →  20 kg costs ₹400     (×2 on both lines)
> 1 kg newspaper is ₹8    →  16 kg is ₹128        (×16 on both lines)
> ```

## 8.7 Capacity — litres and millilitres

**Capacity is how much a container HOLDS.**
```
1,000 ml = 1 l
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **CAPACITY OBEYS EXACTLY THE SAME RULES AS WEIGHT** — because 1 l = 1,000 ml has the same three zeros as 1 kg = 1,000 g.
>
> ```
> 2 l          = 2,000 ml
> 1 l 400 ml   = 1,400 ml       ← NOT 1,040 ml
> 3 l 8 ml     = 3,008 ml       ← the ml part fills three places
> 9 l 90 ml    = 9,090 ml
> 14,075 ml    = 14 l 75 ml     ← cover the last three digits
> 12,200 ml    = 12 l 200 ml
> ```
> **So there is nothing new to learn here.** Every rule of §8.3–§8.6 transfers unchanged, which is the real reward for having understood *why* they worked.

> [!WARNING]
> **1 l 400 ml is 1,400 ml, not 1,040 ml** — the same trap as 5 kg 50 g. **The millilitre part always takes the last three places**, padded with zeros if it is short.

**Sensible units again:** a bucket holds about **20 l**, not 20 ml — 20 ml is about four teaspoons. Two cups of tea need roughly **300–400 ml**, so 1 l is far too much and 500 ml is plenty.

**Worked example — Riya vs Aarav:**
```
Riya  : one 2 l bottle + four 500 ml bottles
        = 2,000 ml + 4 × 500 ml = 2,000 + 2,000 = 4,000 ml = 4 l
Aarav : three 750 ml bottles = 3 × 750 = 2,250 ml = 2 l 250 ml
Riya filled more, by 4,000 − 2,250 = 1,750 ml = 1 l 750 ml
```

**Worked example — the juice vendor:**
```
5 l container, glasses of 250 ml
(a) 5,000 ÷ 250 = 20 full glasses
(b) after 10 glasses: 10 × 250 = 2,500 ml gone, so 2,500 ml = 2 l 500 ml left
(c) 20 glasses × ₹25 = ₹500 from the whole container
```
> [!TIP]
> **Notice how (a) had to be answered before (c) could be.** In a multi-part problem the parts are usually **rungs of a ladder** — solve them in order and each one hands you the next.

## Summary

```
CHOOSE THE UNIT THAT GIVES A COMFORTABLE NUMBER
   too small a unit → an enormous count;  too big → detail lost

THE LADDER
   1,000 mg = 1 g       1,000 g = 1 kg
     100 kg = 1 quintal      10 quintals = 1 tonne = 1,000 kg
   1,000 ml = 1 l

BIG unit → small unit : MULTIPLY   (more of a smaller unit)
SMALL unit → big unit : DIVIDE     (fewer of a bigger unit)

THE MIXED-UNIT TRAP — the big one
   5 kg 50 g = 5,050 g          NOT 5,500 g
   1 l 400 ml = 1,400 ml        NOT 1,040 ml
   the small-unit part always fills the LAST THREE places, padded with 0s,
   because 1,000 has three zeros — it is Chapter 1's place value again
   backwards: cover the last three digits — what's left is kg (or l)

COMPARING — convert to ONE unit FIRST, then compare as plain numbers
   50 mg loses to 16 g even though 50 > 16
   10 kg 35 g = 10,035 g exactly — looks different, is equal

ADDING / SUBTRACTING — three ways, pick by the numbers
   1. split into friendly pieces (mental)
   2. kg | g columns — carry at 1,000, not at 10
   3. convert everything to one unit, then ordinary arithmetic
   borrowing takes 1 kg and turns it into 1,000 g

MULTIPLYING / DIVIDING
   4 × (10 kg + 500 g) = 4 × 10 kg + 4 × 500 g — distributing over a sum
   split only if BOTH parts divide exactly, else convert and divide once

DOUBLE NUMBER LINE — whatever you multiply the top line by,
   multiply the bottom line by the same, because the two move in step
```
