## 14.1 The four directions, and how to find them without a compass

**Stand facing the rising Sun in the morning. You are facing EAST.**
```
                  NORTH
                    ↑
        WEST  ←─────┼─────→  EAST      ← the rising Sun
                    ↓
                  SOUTH

   facing east :  left hand → NORTH
                  right hand → SOUTH
                  behind you → WEST
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY YOUR LEFT HAND POINTS NORTH WHEN YOU FACE EAST.**
>
> **The four directions run N → E → S → W going CLOCKWISE round the circle.** So east is one quarter turn clockwise from north.
>
> **Turning your body to face east means north has moved one quarter turn ANTICLOCKWISE from in front of you — which is exactly your left.**
>
> **This is Chapter 3's quarter turns doing real work.** Once you know the clockwise order, you never have to memorise the four hand positions — you can rebuild them from anywhere.

> [!TIP]
> **A memory hook for the clockwise order: "Never Eat Soggy Wheat" — N, E, S, W, going clockwise.** **But knowing WHY the hands land where they do is better than the rhyme**, because the rhyme fails the moment you face any other way and the reasoning does not.

**The Sun sets in the WEST, so the same trick works in the evening — with everything reversed.**

## 14.2 Why we use directions instead of "left" and "right"

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **"TURN LEFT" DEPENDS ON WHICH WAY YOU ARE FACING. "GO NORTH" DOES NOT.**
>
> **Two people standing back to back both point "left" — in opposite directions.** The word describes the person, not the place.
>
> **North is fixed for everybody, everywhere on the map.** Two people who disagree about left will always agree about north.
>
> **That is why every map carries a NORTH ARROW.** Without it, "the hospital is to the left" is meaningless — left of whom?

> [!TIP]
> **On a map with the north arrow pointing up (the usual way):**
> ```
> up the page    = north          down the page = south
> right          = east           left          = west
> ```
> **But check the arrow first every single time.** Some maps are printed with north sideways, and then "up the page" is not north at all.

**Describing a route needs BOTH ideas together:**
```
"The bus leaves the parking area going NORTH, takes a RIGHT turn onto
 Hospital Road, and then drives EAST to reach Stop 1."
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY A RIGHT TURN WHILE HEADING NORTH TAKES YOU EAST.**
>
> **A right turn is a quarter turn clockwise.** From north, one quarter turn clockwise lands on east.
> ```
> heading NORTH, turn right → EAST      heading EAST,  turn right → SOUTH
> heading SOUTH, turn right → WEST      heading WEST,  turn right → NORTH
> ```
> **So the new direction can always be worked out — you never have to picture yourself in the driving seat.**
>
> **A LEFT turn is a quarter turn anticlockwise**, so it moves you the other way round the same circle: north → west → south → east → north.

## 14.3 Reading a map: the key and the scale

**A map is a small flat picture of a large real place. Three things make it readable:**
```
NORTH ARROW  — tells you which way the map is facing
KEY (legend) — tells you what each symbol and colour means
SCALE        — tells you how much real distance one map-length stands for
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY MAPS USE SYMBOLS AND A KEY INSTEAD OF DRAWING EVERYTHING.**
>
> **A hospital drawn to true scale on a street map would be a tiny grey speck** — you would not recognise it and could not find it.
>
> **A symbol is deliberately the WRONG size, so it can be seen.** The key is the promise that says what the exaggeration means.
>
> **Every map trades some truth for usefulness**, and the key is where that bargain is written down.

> [!TIP]
> **Answering "who lives east of Jaideep?" on a street map:**
> ```
> 1. find the north arrow
> 2. find Jaideep's house
> 3. look along the map in the EAST direction from it — not "to the right"
>    unless the arrow says right is east
> ```
> **Nearly every mistake in map questions comes from skipping step 1.**

## 14.4 Metro maps — choosing between routes

**A metro map is a special kind of map: it shows which stations connect to which, but NOT true distances or directions.**

```
Qutb Minar (Yellow Line) → … → Hauz Khas → change to Magenta Line → …
                          or  → … → Central Secretariat → change to Violet …
```
**There is usually more than one way to get there, so you have to choose.**

| Compare routes by | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Number of stations** | which is shortest to sit through? |
| **Number of changes** | how many times must we get off and find another platform? |
| **Total time** | changes take several minutes each |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY "FEWEST STATIONS" IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY THE BEST ROUTE.**
>
> **Every change of line costs time — walking to another platform and waiting for the next train.** One change can easily cost more than four extra stations.
>
> **So a longer ride with no change often beats a shorter ride with two.**
>
> **This is why the question "which is best?" has no single arithmetic answer.** You must say *best for what* — fewest stations, fewest changes, or least time. **Being clear about what you are optimising is half of every real-world problem.**

> [!TIP]
> **Metro maps stretch and bend the real geography on purpose**, so that all the lines are readable straight lines. **Never use one to judge distance or direction** — two stations that look next door on the map may be far apart on the ground.

## 14.5 Grids and coordinates

**On a grid map of the zoo, every animal is at a crossing of one column and one row.**

```
  4 │  ·   ·   ·   ·
  3 │  ·   ·   ·   ·
  2 │  ·   ·   ·   ·
  1 │  ✱   ·   ·   ✚          ✱ panda at (1,1)
    └────────────────         ✚ tortoise at (4,1)
  0    1   2   3   4
```
```
TO REACH A POINT:  start at 0, go ACROSS first, then UP
WRITTEN AS:        (across, up)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE ORDER MATTERS — (4,1) IS NOT THE SAME PLACE AS (1,4).**
>
> **(4,1) means four steps east and one step north. (1,4) means one east and four north.** They are two completely different points on the map.
>
> **A pair of numbers cannot locate anything until everyone agrees which number is which.** Without a rule, "4 and 1" is just two numbers.
>
> **So mathematicians agreed once and for all: ACROSS FIRST, THEN UP.** The convention is not deep — but following it exactly is what makes the system work.

> [!TIP]
> **A memory hook that follows the reasoning: you walk along the corridor before you climb the stairs.** Across, then up. **Say it out loud the first few times and the habit sticks.**

> [!WARNING]
> **(0,0) IS THE CORNER YOU START FROM, NOT THE FIRST SQUARE.** Counting the corner as 1 puts every single answer one step out. **Always start counting your steps from 0.**

## 14.6 Grid paths — and a surprise about the shortest one

**An ant may crawl only along the grid lines, going east and north.**
```
from (0,0) to the food at (3,2):

   route A : E E E N N          route B : N E N E E
   route C : E N E N E          route D : N N E E E
```
**Every one of these is 5 steps long.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY ALL THESE STAIRCASE ROUTES ARE EXACTLY THE SAME LENGTH.**
>
> **To get from (0,0) to (3,2) the ant must move 3 steps east and 2 steps north — no more, no fewer.**
>
> **Each eastward step brings it one column closer, and there are 3 columns to cross. Each northward step brings it one row closer, and there are 2 rows to climb.**
>
> **The ORDER of the steps can be shuffled any way you like, but the COUNT cannot change.** 3 + 2 = 5, always.
>
> **So there is no "clever shortcut" among the staircase routes — they are all equally short.** The only way to make a route longer is to go west or south at some point and then undo it.

> [!TIP]
> **This gives you a one-second answer to "how far is it on the grid?"**
> ```
> distance = (steps across) + (steps up)
> from (2,1) to (7,5) → 5 across + 4 up → 9 steps
> ```
> **You never have to trace a path.** Just subtract the two across-numbers, subtract the two up-numbers, and add.

**Describing a route in words:** "crawl 2 cm east, then 3 cm north" is a complete instruction — **a distance AND a direction for each leg.**

> [!TIP]
> **A direction with no distance ("go north") or a distance with no direction ("go 3 cm") is only half an instruction.** **Every leg of every route needs both.** This is the same discipline as writing "5 kg" rather than "5" — a number without its unit, or a move without its direction, does not say anything.
