## 9.1 Multiplication and division are the same fact

**35 coconuts in an array of 5 rows and 7 columns gives you THREE facts at once:**
```
      5 × 7 = 35        the multiplication
     35 ÷ 7 = 5         35 split into 7 groups → 5 in each
     35 ÷ 5 = 7         35 split into 5 groups → 7 in each
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY EVERY MULTIPLICATION FACT CARRIES TWO DIVISION FACTS WITH IT.**
>
> **Look at the array of coconuts one way and it is 5 rows of 7. Turn your head and it is 7 columns of 5.** Nothing has moved — only the way you are reading it.
>
> **Division asks the array a question backwards:** "I know the total and one side; what is the other side?" **There are two sides, so there are two questions** — and hence two division facts.
>
> **This is why you never have to learn division tables separately.** Knowing 5 × 7 = 35 already tells you 35 ÷ 5 and 35 ÷ 7.

```
DIVIDEND (N)  ÷  DIVISOR (D)  =  QUOTIENT (Q)

           N  =  D × Q
```
> [!TIP]
> **Every division answer can be CHECKED by multiplying back.** 35 ÷ 7 = 5, and 7 × 5 = 35 ✔ **Never leave a division unchecked** — it costs one multiplication and catches almost every mistake.

**Fact families from bigger numbers work identically:**
```
30 × 30 = 900   →   900 ÷ 30 = 30      (both facts are the same here — 30 is used twice)
15 × 60 = 900   →   900 ÷ 15 = 60  and  900 ÷ 60 = 15
400 × 8 = 3,200 → 3,200 ÷ 400 = 8  and 3,200 ÷ 8 = 400
200 × 16 = 3,200 → 3,200 ÷ 200 = 16 and 3,200 ÷ 16 = 200
```

## 9.2 Patterns in division and place value

```
   40 ÷ 10 =    4          100 ÷ 10 =  10        1,000 ÷ 10 = 100
  400 ÷ 10 =   40          200 ÷ 20 =  10        1,000 ÷ 100 = 10
4,000 ÷ 10 =  400          300 ÷ 100 =  3        2,000 ÷ 2  = 1,000
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY MULTIPLYING THE DIVIDEND BY 10 MULTIPLIES THE ANSWER BY 10.**
>
> **If you have ten times as many coconuts and still make the same number of groups, each group must be ten times bigger.** There is nowhere else for the extra coconuts to go.
>
> ```
>   40 ÷ 10 =   4
>  400 ÷ 10 =  40      ten times the dividend → ten times the quotient
> 4,000 ÷ 10 = 400
> ```
> **In the place-value chart the answer simply slides one column to the left each time** — that sliding IS multiplying by ten.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **AND WHY MULTIPLYING THE DIVISOR BY 10 DIVIDES THE ANSWER BY 10.**
>
> **Same coconuts, ten times as many groups → each group gets a tenth as much.**
> ```
> 2,000 ÷  2  = 1,000
> 2,000 ÷ 20  =   100      ten times the divisor → a tenth of the quotient
> ```
> **The two rules pull in opposite directions, which is exactly what you'd expect** — one makes the piles bigger, the other cuts them up more.

> [!TIP]
> **The two rules together give a very useful third one: DOUBLE BOTH and the answer does not change.**
> ```
> 352 ÷ 11 = 32
> 704 ÷ 22 = 32        both doubled → same answer
> 1,408 ÷ 44 = 32      both doubled again → still 32
>
> but  704 ÷ 11 = 64   (dividend doubled only → answer doubles)
>      352 ÷ 22 = 16   (divisor doubled only → answer halves)
> ```
> **Why: twice the coconuts shared into twice as many groups gives each group the same as before.** **This is how you turn an awkward division into an easy one for free.**

**When divisor and dividend share the same digits, the answer is a power of ten:**
```
110 ÷ 11 = 10      860 ÷ 86 = 10      440 ÷ 22 = 20
8,800 ÷ 88 = 100   7,500 ÷ 750 = 10   2,400 ÷ 24 = 100
```

## 9.3 Mental strategies — split the number

**You already know easy divisions. The trick is to break a hard number into easy pieces.**

```
STRATEGY 1 — split into a SUM
   1,248 ÷ 6      1,248 = 1,200 + 48
                  1,200 ÷ 6 = 200   and   48 ÷ 6 = 8
                  so 1,248 ÷ 6 = 200 + 8 = 208

STRATEGY 2 — split into a DIFFERENCE (when the number is just under a round one)
   1,992 ÷ 4      1,992 = 2,000 − 8
                  2,000 ÷ 4 = 500   and   8 ÷ 4 = 2
                  so 1,992 ÷ 4 = 500 − 2 = 498

STRATEGY 3 — repeated HALVING
   128 ÷ 4        half of 128 is 64, half of 64 is 32     →  32
   168 ÷ 8        84 → 42 → 21   (three halvings)         →  21
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY YOU'RE ALLOWED TO SPLIT THE DIVIDEND UP LIKE THIS.**
>
> **Sharing 1,248 coconuts among 6 people can be done in two rounds** — hand out 1,200 first (200 each), then hand out the remaining 48 (8 each). **Each person ends up with 208 either way.**
>
> **Nothing is lost or created by pausing halfway**, which is exactly why the pieces can be divided separately and the answers added.
>
> **The subtraction version works for the same reason, run backwards:** share out 2,000 (500 each), then discover you had 8 too many and take back 2 from each person.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY DIVIDING BY 4 IS THE SAME AS HALVING TWICE.**
>
> **Halve a pile and you get 2 groups. Halve each of those and you now have 4 groups** — of the original pile.
> ```
> ÷2 then ÷2  =  ÷4          ÷2 three times = ÷8
> ```
> **So the halving trick works for 4, 8, 16, 32 — every number built out of 2s.** It does **not** work for 6 or 12 by halving alone; for those, halve and then divide by 3.

> [!TIP]
> **Pick your split by looking for the friendly number hiding inside.**
> ```
> 545 ÷ 5   = (500 + 45) ÷ 5   = 100 + 9  = 109
> 147 ÷ 7   = (140 + 7) ÷ 7    =  20 + 1  = 21
> 648 ÷ 12  = (600 + 48) ÷ 12  =  50 + 4  = 54
> 9,648 ÷ 48 = (9,600 + 48) ÷ 48 = 200 + 1 = 201
> 775 ÷ 25  = (750 + 25) ÷ 25  =  30 + 1  = 31
> 796 ÷ 4   = (800 − 4) ÷ 4    = 200 − 1  = 199    ← a DIFFERENCE split
> ```
> **The friendly piece is always a multiple of the divisor.** Hunt for that first, and the rest follows.

## 9.4 Dividing by taking away chunks (partial quotients)

**582 coconuts sold equally to 6 customers. Two ways of doing 582 ÷ 6:**

```
SUSIE — takes away small chunks           SUNITHA — takes away big chunks
 6) 582 ( 20+20+20+20+10+7                6) 582 ( 90 + 7
   −120                                     −540
   ----                                     ----
    462                                       42
   −120                                      −42
   ----                                      ----
    342 …  and so on, six steps               00      →  97
                                    →  97
```
**Both give 97. Sunitha's is better because it takes fewer steps** — she asked "how many SIXTIES fit?" instead of "how many twenties?"

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY YOU MAY TAKE THE CHUNKS AWAY IN ANY ORDER AND STILL GET THE SAME ANSWER.**
>
> **Every chunk you remove is a whole number of "6 per customer" rounds.** Removing 120 is giving each customer 20; removing 540 is giving each customer 90.
>
> **The quotient is just a record of how much each customer has received so far.** Whether you hand it out in six small rounds or two big ones, the total each person ends with is the same.
>
> **So the method cannot go wrong — it can only be slow.**

> [!TIP]
> **To choose a good chunk, ESTIMATE FIRST.** 6 × 100 = 600, which is already past 582 — **so the answer is under 100, and 90 is the sensible first try.** One moment of estimating replaces four steps of arithmetic.

**Bigger example — 4,376 coconuts, 8 per litre of oil:**
```
 8) 4,376 ( 500 + 40 + 7
   −4,000          8 × 500
    -----
      376
     −320          8 × 40
     ----
       56
      −56          8 × 7
      ----
       00                    →  547 litres
```
> [!TIP]
> **Notice the chunks go 500, then 40, then 7 — hundreds, then tens, then ones.** Working down the place values in order is exactly what long division does; **partial quotients is just long division with the steps written out loud.**

## 9.5 Remainders — N = D × Q + R

**Sometimes the divisor does not fit a whole number of times.**
```
 726 ÷ 4  →  Q = 181,  R = 2      check:  4 × 181 + 2 = 724 + 2 = 726 ✔
 902 ÷ 16 →  Q = 56,   R = 6      check: 16 × 56 + 6 = 896 + 6 = 902 ✔
 887 ÷ 3  →  Q = 295,  R = 2      check:  3 × 295 + 2 = 885 + 2 = 887 ✔
 767 ÷ 26 →  Q = 29,   R = 13     check: 26 × 29 + 13 = 754 + 13 = 767 ✔
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **N = D × Q + R — and every part of it is doing a job.**
>
> **D × Q is what actually got shared out.** **R is what was left over because there wasn't enough for another full round.** **Together they must rebuild N, because nothing else happened to the coconuts.**
>
> **This is not a formula to memorise — it is a description of what you just did.**

> [!WARNING]
> **THE REMAINDER MUST ALWAYS BE SMALLER THAN THE DIVISOR.**
>
> **If R were as big as D, you could hand out one more full round** — so the quotient wasn't finished.
> ```
> 30 ÷ 4 = 7 remainder 2     ✔   (2 is less than 4)
> 30 ÷ 4 = 6 remainder 6     ✘   (6 ≥ 4 — one more round was possible)
> ```
> **A remainder bigger than or equal to the divisor is a signpost that you stopped too early.**

## 9.6 Place-value division (the long-division layout)

**62 candies among 5 children:**
```
      T O
  5) 62 ( 1 2       6 Tens ÷ 5 = 1 Ten, with 1 Ten left over
    −5             regroup that 1 Ten as 10 Ones → 10 + 2 = 12 Ones
    ----           12 Ones ÷ 5 = 2 Ones, remainder 2
      12
    −10
    ----
       2            →  12 each, 2 left over
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE WHOLE OF LONG DIVISION IS ONE IDEA: WHEN A PLACE WON'T SHARE, BREAK IT INTO THE NEXT PLACE DOWN.**
>
> **2 Tens cannot be split among 3 children as whole tens** — so break the 2 Tens into 20 Ones and share those instead.
>
> **This is the same regrouping you use in subtraction**, just travelling in the same direction: a ten becomes ten ones whenever you need smaller change.
>
> ```
> 324 ÷ 3   =  3 Hundreds ÷ 3       → 1 Hundred
>              2 Tens ÷ 3           → 0 Tens, regroup to 20 Ones
>              20 + 4 = 24 Ones ÷ 3 → 8 Ones
>                                     → 108
> ```

> [!WARNING]
> **THAT ZERO IN 108 IS THE MOST-DROPPED DIGIT IN ALL OF DIVISION — and it is not optional.**
>
> **It is recording a real fact: "each child gets NO whole tens".** Leave it out and you have written 18, which is six times too small.
>
> ```
> 3,005 ÷ 5 = 601      the 0 says "no whole tens each"
> 7,032 ÷ 6 = 1,172
> ```
> **Rule of thumb: once you have started writing the quotient, EVERY place gets a digit** — even if that digit is 0. Skipping a place silently shifts everything after it.

> [!TIP]
> **You can predict how many digits the quotient will have before you start.**
> ```
> 2,874 ÷ 14  →  14 × 100 = 1,400 (fits),  14 × 1,000 = 14,000 (too big)
>                so the answer is in the hundreds — 3 digits
>                2,874 ÷ 14 = 205 remainder 4 ✔
> ```
> **Doing this first is the best protection against a lost zero**, because you'll notice at once if your answer comes out with too few digits.

## 9.7 Real problems don't always want the exact quotient

**342 students, each bus holds 41.  342 ÷ 41 = 8 remainder 14.**
**The answer is 9 buses, not 8.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE ANSWER ROUNDS UP HERE, AND WHY THAT IS NOT A DIVISION RULE.**
>
> **The 14 left-over students are real children who still need to travel.** A remainder cannot be thrown away when it is people, or coconuts to be packed, or shows to be staged.
>
> **The division is right; the QUESTION is asking something slightly different** — not "how many full buses?" but "how many buses before nobody is left standing?"
>
> **Always read what is being counted, then decide what to do with the remainder.**

```
535 coconuts, 25 per bag  →  21 remainder 10  →  22 bags  (the 10 still need a bag)
475 tickets, 45 seats per show → 10 remainder 25 → 11 shows
   and at 2 shows a day, 11 ÷ 2 = 5 remainder 1  →  6 days
```
> [!TIP]
> **Sometimes the remainder is the answer, sometimes it is ignored, sometimes it forces a round-up.**
> ```
> "how many full bags can be filled?"     → ignore the remainder  (21)
> "how many bags are needed?"             → round up              (22)
> "how many coconuts are left unpacked?"  → the remainder itself  (10)
> ```
> **Same division, three different answers.** The arithmetic is the easy part; reading the question is the real work.

**The carpenter problem — when the SMALLEST answer wins:**
```
Each shelf needs 4 long panels, 8 short panels, 16 small clips,
                 4 large clips, 32 screws.
Stock: 264 long, 306 short, 2,400 small clips, 120 large clips, 2,800 screws.

264 ÷ 4  = 66            2,400 ÷ 16 = 150
306 ÷ 8  = 38 r 2          120 ÷ 4  = 30      ← smallest
                         2,800 ÷ 32 = 87 r 16

Only 30 bookshelves can be made.
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE SMALLEST OF THE FIVE ANSWERS IS THE ONE THAT COUNTS.**
>
> **A shelf needs ALL five parts.** Once the large clips run out at 30 shelves, the leftover panels and screws are useless — there is nothing to fasten them with.
>
> **The scarcest ingredient sets the limit**, exactly as the eggs do when you are baking. **Having plenty of flour does not let you make more cakes.**

## 9.8 Statements that are always, sometimes or never true

**A statement is TRUE only if both sides of the "=" have the same value.**
```
8 × 9 = 70 + 2     72 = 72   TRUE
20 − 6 = 7 × 3     14 ≠ 21   FALSE
48 ÷ 3 = 4 × 4     16 = 16   TRUE
89 − 9 = 90 + 0    80 ≠ 90   FALSE
```

| Statement | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Two odd numbers add to an even number | **Always** | each odd is "an even plus 1"; the two spare 1s pair up |
| Multiplying by 2 gives an odd number | **Never** | doubling makes two equal groups, so it always halves exactly |
| Halving a number gives an even number | **Sometimes** | half of 8 is 4 ✔, half of 12 is 6 ✔, half of 4 is 2 ✔ — but half of 6 is 3 ✘ |
| Adding 10 gives a multiple of ten | **Sometimes** | true only if you started on a multiple of ten |
| Reversing a subtraction changes nothing | **Sometimes** | only when the two numbers are equal, since 5−5 = 5−5 but 9−4 ≠ 4−9 |
| Doubling one factor and halving the other keeps the product | **Always** | you multiply by 2 and divide by 2 — they cancel |
| Multiplying by an odd number gives an even number | **Sometimes** | 3 × 4 = 12 even, 3 × 5 = 15 odd — it depends on the other factor |
| Multiplying by 5 gives a number ending in 0 | **Sometimes** | even × 5 ends in 0, odd × 5 ends in 5 |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY "SOMETIMES TRUE" NEEDS TWO EXAMPLES AND "ALWAYS TRUE" NEEDS A REASON.**
>
> **To show a statement is SOMETIMES true you must produce one case where it holds AND one where it fails.** One example alone proves nothing about the other cases.
>
> **To show a statement is NEVER true, one counter-example is no use at all** — you need an argument that covers every number.
>
> **And to show ALWAYS true, no number of examples is ever enough.** A hundred examples still leave infinitely many untested — **only a reason will do.**
>
> **That is the difference between checking and proving**, and it is the beginning of all real mathematics.

## Summary

```
FACT FAMILIES — one array, three facts
   5 × 7 = 35  →  35 ÷ 7 = 5  and  35 ÷ 5 = 7
   because the array can be read by rows or by columns
   N = D × Q         (and always check a division by multiplying back)

PLACE-VALUE PATTERNS
   ×10 the dividend → ×10 the answer  (more coconuts, same groups)
   ×10 the divisor  → ÷10 the answer  (same coconuts, more groups)
   DOUBLE BOTH      → answer unchanged — use it to make hard sums easy

MENTAL STRATEGIES — find the friendly multiple hiding inside
   1,248 ÷ 6 = (1,200 + 48) ÷ 6 = 200 + 8 = 208     split as a SUM
   1,992 ÷ 4 = (2,000 − 8) ÷ 4 = 500 − 2 = 498      split as a DIFFERENCE
   ÷4 = halve twice · ÷8 = halve three times
      because ÷2 then ÷2 makes 4 groups of the original

PARTIAL QUOTIENTS — take away chunks, record what each person got
   any order works; big chunks just finish faster
   ESTIMATE FIRST to choose a good chunk

REMAINDERS
   N = D × Q + R      D × Q went out, R stayed behind, together they rebuild N
   R must always be SMALLER than D, or another full round was possible

PLACE-VALUE (LONG) DIVISION
   when a place won't share, break it into the next place down
   NEVER drop the 0 — it records "no whole tens each"  (3,005 ÷ 5 = 601)
   predict the number of digits before you start

REAL PROBLEMS — read what the remainder MEANS
   full bags → ignore it · bags needed → round up · left over → it IS the answer
   when several parts are needed, the SCARCEST one sets the limit

ALWAYS / SOMETIMES / NEVER
   sometimes → give one case that works AND one that fails
   always    → examples are never enough; you need a REASON
```
