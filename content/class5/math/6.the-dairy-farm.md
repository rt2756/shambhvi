## 6.1 Order does not matter

**Butter packets arranged 4 rows of 6, or 6 rows of 4 — the total is the same either way.**
```
4 × 6 = 24        6 × 4 = 24
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **YOU CAN MULTIPLY NUMBERS IN ANY ORDER and the answer does not change.**
>
> **Why: turning the arrangement sideways does not add or remove any packets.** The rows become columns and the columns become rows, but nothing has been touched.
>
> **This is genuinely useful, not just true.** If you know 6 × 4 but stumble over 4 × 6, you can simply swap them — **there is only ever one fact to learn, not two.**

## 6.2 Multiplying by 10, 100 and 1,000

```
4 × 10 = 40            3 × 100 = 300           2 × 1,000 = 2,000
20 × 10 = 200          8 × 100 = 800           5 × 1,000 = 5,000
10 × 40 = 400         10 × 100 = 1,000        10 × 1,000 = 10,000
                                              20 × 1,000 = 20,000
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Multiplying by 10 makes a number TEN TIMES bigger — so every digit moves ONE PLACE to the LEFT.**
> **Multiplying by 100 makes it a HUNDRED times bigger — every digit moves TWO places left.**
> **Multiplying by 1,000 — THREE places left.**
> ```
> 4 → 40      the 4 moved from Ones into Tens
> 4 → 400     the 4 moved from Ones into Hundreds
> ```
> **Why zeros appear: the places the digits left behind are now EMPTY, and a zero holds each one open.**
>
> **The zeros are not "added on" as a trick — they are placeholders**, doing exactly the job zeros always do.

**Bigger cases work the same way, by splitting off the tens:**
```
30 × 20  =  (3 × 10) × (2 × 10)
         =  3 × 2 × 10 × 10
         =  6 × 100
         =  600
```
> [!TIP]
> **This is why you can "multiply the front digits and count the zeros".**
> ```
> 60 × 50  →  6 × 5 = 30, and two zeros  →  3,000
> 400 × 10 →  4 × 1 = 4,  and three zeros →  4,000
> 700 × 8  →  7 × 8 = 56, and two zeros   →  5,600
> ```
> **It works because ORDER DOES NOT MATTER** — you are allowed to gather all the tens together and deal with them at the end.

## 6.3 Many ways to multiply — 18 × 5

**All of these give 90, and every one of them is correct:**
```
HALVING       half of 18 is 9;  9 × 5 = 45;  45 + 45 = 90

DOUBLING      double 18 → 36;  double 36 → 72;  72 + 18 = 90
              (that is 4 lots plus 1 lot = 5 lots)

SPLITTING     18 = 10 + 8;  10 × 5 = 50 and 8 × 5 = 40;  50 + 40 = 90

REARRANGING   18 × 5 = 9 × 10 = 90

NEAREST TEN   20 × 5 = 100;  but that is 2 lots too many;
              100 − 10 = 90
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why so many methods are worth knowing: DIFFERENT NUMBERS SUIT DIFFERENT METHODS.**
>
> **There is no single "proper" way to multiply.** The skill is looking at the numbers first and choosing the route that makes them easy.

## 6.4 Halving and doubling

```
3 × 18                     22 × 5
double ↓  ↓ half           half ↓   ↓ double
6 ×  9  = 54               11 × 10 = 110
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **HALVE ONE NUMBER AND DOUBLE THE OTHER — the answer does not change.**
>
> **Why: you have taken away half of one side and given exactly that much back to the other.**
> ```
> 3 × 18  — think of 3 rows of 18
> cut each row in half and lay the halves out separately
>    → now 6 rows of 9
> the packets have been REARRANGED, not removed
> ```
> **When it helps most: with 5s and 25s.**
> ```
> 22 × 5  →  11 × 10 = 110      because 5 doubles into the easy 10
> 12 × 25 →   6 × 50 = 300      →  3 × 100 = 300
> ```
> **Doubling a 5 gives 10, and doubling a 25 twice gives 100** — the easiest numbers there are.

**More worked examples:**
```
14 × 3  → 7 × 6 = 42
38 × 5  → 19 × 10 = 190
50 × 28 → 100 × 14 = 1,400
15 × 22 → 30 × 11 = 330
```

## 6.5 Using the nearest multiple

```
4 × 19  =  4 × 20 − 4
        =  80 − 4
        =  76

14 × 21 =  14 × 20 + 14
        =  280 + 14
        =  294
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Round to a friendly number, then CORRECT.**
> ```
> 19 is ONE LESS than 20   → so you counted ONE EXTRA lot of 4 → SUBTRACT 4
> 21 is ONE MORE than 20   → so you counted ONE TOO FEW lots of 14 → ADD 14
> ```
> **The correction is always ONE LOT of the other number**, when you are one away from the round number.
>
> **Watch the direction carefully: rounding UP means you must SUBTRACT afterwards, and rounding DOWN means you must ADD.** Getting this backwards is the one real danger of the method.

## 6.6 Splitting into place values — the column method

**A family of 4 makes 35 kg of kitchen waste a month. How much in a year?**
```
12 × 35

split BOTH numbers by place value:
   35 = 30 + 5
   12 = 10 + 2

then multiply every part by every part:

        ×    |   30      5
       ------|-------------
        10   |  300     50
         2   |   60     10
       ------|-------------
             |  360  +  60   =  420 kg
```
**Written as a column sum, the same four products appear:**
```
      35  (30 + 5)
   ×  12  (10 + 2)
   -------------------
      10        (2 × 5)
      60        (2 × 30)
      50       (10 × 5)
     300      (10 × 30)
   -------------------
     420
```
**Or grouped two at a time:**
```
      35
   ×  12
   -------------------
      70        (2 × 35)
     350       (10 × 35)
   -------------------
     420
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **All these methods are THE SAME CALCULATION, written out in more or fewer steps.**
>
> **Why the splitting is allowed: 12 lots of 35 is the same as 10 lots plus 2 lots.** You are simply taking the 12 in two convenient pieces.
>
> **And 35 can be split too — 30 and 5 — which is why four small products appear** instead of two.
>
> **The short column method you may already know does exactly this, but hides the parts.** Seeing them written out is what makes it understandable rather than magic.

**A bigger one — 150 kg of compost at ₹24 per kg:**
```
        ×    |   100     50
       ------|--------------
        20   | 2,000  1,000
         4   |   400    200
       ------|--------------
             | 2,400 +1,200  = ₹3,600
```
**The family saves ₹3,600 a year by making their own compost.**

## Summary

```
ORDER DOES NOT MATTER — 4 × 6 = 6 × 4
   turning the arrangement sideways moves nothing, so one fact serves for two

MULTIPLYING BY 10, 100, 1,000
   × 10 → every digit moves ONE place LEFT
   × 100 → TWO places · × 1,000 → THREE places
   the zeros are PLACEHOLDERS for the places the digits left behind
   30 × 20 = 3 × 2 × 10 × 10 = 6 × 100 = 600
   → multiply the front digits and count the zeros

MANY WAYS TO MULTIPLY 18 × 5 — halving, doubling, splitting, rearranging,
   nearest ten. All correct; choose the one the numbers suit

HALVING AND DOUBLING — halve one, double the other; the answer is unchanged,
   because the packets are REARRANGED, not removed
   best with 5s and 25s:  22 × 5 → 11 × 10 = 110
                          12 × 25 → 6 × 50 → 3 × 100 = 300

NEAREST MULTIPLE — round, then CORRECT by ONE LOT
   4 × 19 = 4 × 20 − 4 = 76        (rounded UP, so SUBTRACT)
   14 × 21 = 14 × 20 + 14 = 294    (rounded DOWN, so ADD)

SPLITTING BY PLACE VALUE — the column method, with the parts shown
   12 × 35 → (10 + 2) × (30 + 5) → 300 + 50 + 60 + 10 = 420
   allowed because 12 lots is 10 lots plus 2 lots
   the short method does the same thing but hides the parts
```
