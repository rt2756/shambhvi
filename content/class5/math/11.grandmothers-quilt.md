## 11.1 Perimeter — the length of the border

**Grandmother wants lace all round the edge of a quilt. The length of that edge is the PERIMETER.**

```
PERIMETER = add up every side, all the way round
```
```
a square of side 4 cm       →  4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16 cm
an equal-sided 5-sided shape, side 5 cm  →  5 × 5 = 25 cm
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY YOU CAN JUST MULTIPLY WHEN ALL THE SIDES ARE EQUAL.**
>
> **Adding the same number over and over IS multiplication.** 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 is four fours.
>
> **So for any shape with all sides equal:** perimeter = (number of sides) × (length of one side). **You are not learning a new formula — you are using the one you already have.**

> [!TIP]
> **If two laces together must cover the border, first find the perimeter, THEN look for two lengths that add up to it.** Guessing which laces "look about right" is guaranteed to fail — the border of a wiggly shape is far longer than it looks.

**Drawing a rectangle with a given perimeter:** perimeter 26 cm means **length + breadth = 13 cm**, because the two lengths and two breadths make 26 between them.
```
perimeter 26 cm →  L + B = 13  →  1×12, 2×11, 3×10, 4×9, 5×8, 6×7  … all work
perimeter 18 cm →  L + B = 9   →  1×8, 2×7, 3×6, 4×5              … all work
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY HALVING THE PERIMETER GIVES L + B.**
>
> **A rectangle's border is L + B + L + B — two lengths and two breadths.** So the perimeter is **two copies of (L + B)**.
>
> **Halve it and you have one copy: L + B.** From there you pick any pair of numbers that add to it.
>
> **This is why there are MANY rectangles with the same perimeter**, not one. The perimeter fixes the total of L and B, not the two numbers themselves.

## 11.2 Measuring area by tiling

**Cover a table top with copies of one shape, with no gaps and no overlaps, and count them.**

| Tiling shape | Covers with no gaps? |
|---|---|
| Triangles | **Yes** |
| Squares | **Yes** |
| Rectangles | **Yes** |
| Circles | **No — gaps are left between them** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY CIRCLES CANNOT BE USED TO MEASURE AREA.**
>
> **Push circles as close together as you like and little curved gaps remain between them.** Those gaps are part of the table but are counted by nothing.
>
> **So "12 circles" does not tell you how much table there is** — it leaves out an unknown amount.
>
> **A measuring unit must account for EVERY bit of the thing being measured**, which is exactly why the tiling shapes must leave no gaps. This is the tessellation idea from Chapter 7, now doing a job.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE SQUARE, AND NOT THE TRIANGLE, IS THE UNIT WE SETTLE ON.**
>
> **Triangles tile perfectly well** — you could measure area in triangles and it would be honest.
>
> **But squares have two advantages nothing else matches:**
> - **all four sides are equal, so it does not matter which way round you lay one down;**
> - **they line up in neat ROWS AND COLUMNS**, and that turns counting into multiplying.
>
> **A square of side 1 unit has an area of 1 SQUARE UNIT.** That is the definition everything else is built on.

> [!TIP]
> **Area is measured in square cm, square m — not cm or m.** **Why: you are counting little squares, and each one is a square centimetre.** **Perimeter is a LENGTH so it uses cm; area is a COUNT OF SQUARES so it uses square cm.** Mixing them up is the commonest slip in this chapter.

**To find the area of anything irregular — your palm, a leaf — trace it on a square grid and count.**
```
count every whole square inside the outline
then count the part-squares and pair them up:
   two halves make one whole; roughly-half ones count as ½
```
> [!TIP]
> **Two people tracing the same leaf will get slightly different answers, and that is fine.** **Any measurement of a curved shape by counting squares is an APPROXIMATION** — a finer grid gives a closer one. Saying "about 34 square cm" is more honest than pretending it is exactly 34.

## 11.3 Area of a rectangle — and why the formula is true

**Grandmother's patchwork: 6 rows of 4 patches.**
```
■ ■ ■ ■        Don't count all 24.
■ ■ ■ ■        There are 6 rows of 4  →  6 × 4 = 24 patches
■ ■ ■ ■
■ ■ ■ ■
■ ■ ■ ■
■ ■ ■ ■
```

```
AREA OF A RECTANGLE  =  LENGTH × BREADTH
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY MULTIPLYING TWO LENGTHS GIVES AN AREA — the key idea of the chapter.**
>
> **Lay unit squares along the length: a rectangle 6 cm long fits 6 squares in one row.** **Its breadth of 4 cm means 4 such rows stack up.**
>
> **So the squares are arranged in an ARRAY — 4 rows of 6 — and counting an array is exactly what multiplication does.**
>
> **The formula isn't a rule about lengths at all. It is a shortcut for counting squares** — and the length and breadth are simply telling you how many fit each way.
>
> **This is the same array you met with the coconuts in Chapter 9.** Rows × columns, again.

> [!TIP]
> **You can always check the formula by counting.** Draw a 3 by 5 rectangle on squared paper, count the squares one by one — you will get 15, and 3 × 5 = 15. **Do this once and you never have to trust the formula on faith again.**

**And the other formulas follow at once:**
```
PERIMETER OF A RECTANGLE = L + B + L + B = (2 × L) + (2 × B)
                            because opposite sides are equal

A SQUARE is a rectangle with L = B, so
   AREA      = side × side
   PERIMETER = 4 × side

   side 5 cm  →  area 25 square cm,  perimeter 20 cm
```

**Working backwards:** if the area is 64 square m and the length is 16 m, then
```
16 × B = 64   →   B = 64 ÷ 16 = 4 m
```
> [!TIP]
> **Any formula can be run backwards, because it is a fact family.** Area = L × B is the same relationship as B = Area ÷ L. **You already know this from N = D × Q — nothing new is needed.**

## 11.4 Same area, different perimeter — the big surprise

**Every one of these rectangles has an area of 12 square cm:**

| Rectangle | Area | Perimeter |
|---|---|---|
| 1 by 12 | 12 sq cm | **26 cm** |
| 2 by 6 | 12 sq cm | **16 cm** |
| 3 by 4 | 12 sq cm | **14 cm** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE AREA CAN STAY FIXED WHILE THE PERIMETER CHANGES SO MUCH.**
>
> **Area counts the INSIDE. Perimeter measures the EDGE. They are answering different questions, so there is no reason for them to move together.**
>
> **Think of the 12 squares as 12 children.** In a long single file (1 by 12) almost every child is on the outside of the line — lots of edge. **Huddled in a 3 by 4 block, the middle children are surrounded and contribute no edge at all.**
>
> **Same twelve children either way. Very different amount of outside.**

> [!WARNING]
> **"IF THE AREA GOES UP, THE PERIMETER GOES UP" IS FALSE — and it is the trap this chapter is built to spring.**
>
> ```
> a 1 by 12 rectangle : area 12 sq cm, perimeter 26 cm
> a 4 by 4 square     : area 16 sq cm, perimeter 16 cm
> ```
> **The second has MORE area and LESS perimeter.**
>
> **One example is enough to destroy an "always" claim** — you never have to check any others. (Chapter 9's always/sometimes/never test at work.)

**And the reverse surprise — every one of these has perimeter 16 cm:**
```
1 by 7  →  area  7 sq cm
2 by 6  →  area 12 sq cm
3 by 5  →  area 15 sq cm
4 by 4  →  area 16 sq cm       ← the biggest
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE PATTERN BEHIND BOTH TABLES: THE SQUARER THE SHAPE, THE BETTER IT DOES.**
>
> **For a fixed area, the most square-like rectangle has the SMALLEST perimeter.**
> **For a fixed perimeter, the most square-like rectangle has the BIGGEST area.**
>
> **Why: a long thin shape is nearly all edge, because every square in it touches the outside.** Squashing it towards a square tucks more squares into the middle, where they add area but no edge.
>
> **This is why farmers fencing a field want it square-ish, and why a long thin room needs far more skirting board than a square room of the same floor space.**

## 11.5 Shapes that aren't rectangles

**A composite shape — break it into rectangles, find each area, and add.**
```
      ┌─────────┐
      │    A    │  4 cm tall, 10 cm wide  →  40 sq cm
      ├────┬────┘
      │ B  │        6 cm tall, 5 cm wide  →  30 sq cm
      └────┘
                total area = 40 + 30 = 70 sq cm
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY YOU MAY ADD THE PIECES FOR AREA — BUT MUST NOT ADD THEM FOR PERIMETER.**
>
> **Area adds up because the squares in piece A and the squares in piece B are all different squares.** None is counted twice, none is missed.
>
> **Perimeter does NOT add up, because the join between A and B is an edge of each piece but is NOT part of the finished shape's border.** Adding the two perimeters counts that inside join twice, when it should be counted zero times.
>
> **So for perimeter, always walk round the OUTSIDE of the finished shape and add only the edges you actually travel along.**

> [!TIP]
> **On a grid you can find the perimeter of any tiled shape by counting the exposed edges of the outer squares** — one unit for each little edge that has nothing next to it.
> ```
> a 2 by 2 block of squares  →  8 exposed edges  →  perimeter 8
> four squares in a line     → 10 exposed edges  →  perimeter 10
> ```
> **Both have area 4. Once again, same area, different perimeter.**

**Two shapes with equal areas can look completely different.** Cut a rectangle into pieces and rearrange them — **the area cannot change, because the same squares are still there, just moved.** The perimeter usually does change.

## Summary

```
PERIMETER = the length all round the border, measured in cm / m
   all sides equal → (number of sides) × (one side)
   rectangle: L + B + L + B = 2×L + 2×B
   square:    4 × side
   perimeter 26 → L + B = 13, so MANY rectangles fit — the perimeter
      fixes the TOTAL of L and B, not the two numbers

AREA = how much surface, measured by TILING, in square cm / square m
   triangles, squares and rectangles tile — CIRCLES DO NOT, they leave
      gaps that get counted by nothing
   the SQUARE is chosen as the unit because all its sides are equal AND
      it lines up in rows and columns
   1 unit square = a square of side 1 unit

AREA OF A RECTANGLE = LENGTH × BREADTH
   WHY: the length says how many squares fit in a row, the breadth says
   how many rows — so it is an ARRAY, and counting an array is multiplying
   it is not a rule about lengths; it is a shortcut for counting squares
   square: area = side × side · run it backwards: B = Area ÷ L

cm for perimeter, SQUARE cm for area — a length versus a count of squares

SAME AREA ≠ SAME PERIMETER
   area 12: 1×12 → perimeter 26 · 2×6 → 16 · 3×4 → 14
   perimeter 16: 1×7 → area 7 · 3×5 → 15 · 4×4 → 16
   "more area means more perimeter" is FALSE — one counter-example kills it

   THE PATTERN: the squarer the shape, the better
      fixed area      → squarest has the SMALLEST perimeter
      fixed perimeter → squarest has the BIGGEST area
   because a long thin shape is nearly all edge

COMPOSITE SHAPES — split into rectangles
   AREAS ADD (different squares, none counted twice)
   PERIMETERS DO NOT (the inside join is not part of the border)
   on a grid, count the exposed edges
```
