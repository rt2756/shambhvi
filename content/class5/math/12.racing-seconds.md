## 12.1 Seconds — a smaller unit for smaller gaps

**Three girls finish a 200 m walking race:**
```
Akira  1 min 55 sec
Sunita 1 min 56 sec
Mary   1 min 57 sec
```
**All three "took a minute". Only seconds can tell them apart — and Akira wins.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY A RACE NEEDS SECONDS AT ALL.**
>
> **Measured in minutes, all three results read "1 minute" — the race has no winner.** The measurement has thrown away exactly the information the race was about.
>
> **A smaller unit gives a FINER measurement.** Seconds slice each minute into sixty pieces, so gaps of one-sixtieth of a minute become visible.
>
> **This is the same argument as Chapter 8's units of weight, run the other way:** there you avoided a unit so small the number got silly; here you need one small enough to show the difference. **The right unit is always decided by what you are trying to see.**

```
1 minute = 60 seconds
1 hour   = 60 minutes
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY 60, WHEN EVERYTHING ELSE IN MATHS USES 10?**
>
> **Because 60 divides beautifully.** Its factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30 and 60.
> ```
> half an hour    = 30 min ✔        a fifth of an hour  = 12 min ✔
> a third         = 20 min ✔        a sixth             = 10 min ✔
> a quarter       = 15 min ✔        a twelfth           =  5 min ✔
> ```
> **Every one of those comes out as a whole number of minutes.**
>
> **Try the same with 100:** a third of 100 minutes is 33⅓ — not a whole number. **You would have to say "33 and a third minutes past" every time you meant a third of an hour.**
>
> **So 60 was not an accident.** It was chosen thousands of years ago precisely because it can be shared out so many ways without breaking.

> [!TIP]
> **Guessing the right unit for an activity:**
> ```
> SECONDS  blinking · snapping fingers · switching on a light ·
>          counting to 20 · sitting down · drinking a glass of water
> MINUTES  washing hands · filling a glass from a slow tap ·
>          melting an ice cube · making a phone call
> ```
> **The dividing line is roughly: could you finish before you'd counted to sixty?** If yes, it's seconds.

## 12.2 Converting between the units

```
hours → minutes : × 60          minutes → hours : ÷ 60
minutes → seconds: × 60         seconds → minutes: ÷ 60
```
> [!TIP]
> **This is the same "big to small, multiply" rule from Chapter 8** — only the number is 60 instead of 1,000. **A minute is smaller than an hour, so it takes MORE of them.** The direction is never something to memorise.

```
1 hr 10 min = 60 + 10  =  70 min          75 min  = 1 hr 15 min
2 hr 45 min = 120 + 45 = 165 min         150 min  = 2 hr 30 min
3 hr 15 min = 180 + 15 = 195 min         220 min  = 3 hr 40 min
4 hr 20 min = 240 + 20 = 260 min         390 min  = 6 hr 30 min

10 min 13 sec = 600 + 13 = 613 sec       320 sec  = 5 min 20 sec
 4 min  8 sec = 240 +  8 = 248 sec       225 sec  = 3 min 45 sec
15 min 40 sec = 900 + 40 = 940 sec     1,000 sec  = 16 min 40 sec
```

> [!WARNING]
> **TIME DOES NOT USE THE "COVER THE LAST THREE DIGITS" TRICK FROM CHAPTER 8.**
>
> **With weight, 5,050 g split into 5 kg and 050 g just by looking**, because 1,000 has three zeros and the digits line up.
>
> **With time you must actually DIVIDE**, because 60 is not a power of ten.
> ```
> 220 min → 220 ÷ 60 = 3 remainder 40  →  3 hr 40 min
> ```
> **The quotient is the hours; the REMAINDER is the leftover minutes.** N = D × Q + R from Chapter 9, doing an everyday job: 60 × 3 + 40 = 220 ✔

> [!TIP]
> **A quick way to divide by 60 without long division: count up in hours.**
> ```
> 390 min:  60, 120, 180, 240, 300, 360 … that's 6 hours, and 390 − 360 = 30
>           →  6 hr 30 min
> ```
> **Six steps of easy addition beat one step of awkward division**, and you are much less likely to slip.

## 12.3 The 12-hour and 24-hour clocks

```
12-HOUR   05:30 a.m.  →  24-HOUR   05:30 hours
          11:55 a.m.  →            11:55 hours
          02:30 p.m.  →            14:30 hours
          05:30 p.m.  →            17:30 hours
          08:45 p.m.  →            20:45 hours
          09:35 p.m.  →            21:35 hours
          11:45 p.m.  →            23:45 hours
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE 24-HOUR CLOCK EXISTS AT ALL.**
>
> **"The train leaves at 8:00" is ambiguous — morning or evening?** The 12-hour clock uses each number **twice a day**, so it always needs an a.m. or p.m. tag to be understood.
>
> **Lose the tag and the meaning is gone**, which on a railway timetable or a hospital chart is dangerous.
>
> **The 24-hour clock gives every moment of the day its OWN number**, from 00:00 to 23:59. No tag is needed, and no two moments share a name.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE CONVERSION RULE, AND WHY IT IS "ADD 12".**
>
> ```
> a.m. times  →  unchanged      (09:35 a.m. = 09:35 hours)
> p.m. times  →  ADD 12 hours   (09:35 p.m. = 21:35 hours)
> ```
> **Why: p.m. means "after midday", and midday is 12 hours after midnight.** So a p.m. time is that many hours past 12 — and counting from midnight instead means adding those 12 on.
>
> **Going back the other way, subtract 12:** 20:45 − 12 = 8:45 p.m.
>
> **Anything with an hour of 13 or more is certainly p.m.**, because the morning ran out at 12.

> [!WARNING]
> **THE TWO TWELVES ARE THE ONLY ODD CASES — and they trip up nearly everyone.**
> ```
> 12:00 midnight  =  00:00 hours     (the day is starting, so zero hours have passed)
> 12:30 a.m.      =  00:30 hours
> 12:00 noon      =  12:00 hours     (12 hours have passed — no change)
> 12:30 p.m.      =  12:30 hours
> ```
> **Why midnight is 00 and not 24: at midnight the new day has just begun**, and no hours of it have gone by yet. **24:00 would mean the end of the old day** — the same instant, but it is written 00:00 so the date is unambiguous too.

## 12.4 Elapsed time — how long did it take?

**The safest method is COUNTING ON, in easy jumps.**

```
09:15 a.m. → 11:30 a.m.

   09:15  ──+45 min──▶  10:00     (up to the next whole hour first)
   10:00  ──+1 hr ──▶  11:00
   11:00  ──+30 min──▶  11:30
                        45 min + 1 hr + 30 min = 2 hr 15 min
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY COUNTING ON IS SAFER THAN SUBTRACTING IN COLUMNS.**
>
> **Column subtraction assumes each place borrows TEN from the next.** In time, minutes borrow **sixty** from hours.
> ```
> WRONG:   11:30            RIGHT:   11:30  is  10 hr 90 min
>        − 09:45                            − 09:45
>          -----                              -------
>          1:85  ← nonsense: 85 minutes         1 hr 45 min
> ```
> **A number like 1:85 can never be a time**, because there is no 85th minute of an hour.
>
> **Counting on never meets this problem**, because you jump to the next whole hour and the awkward part is settled before it can go wrong.

> [!TIP]
> **Choose your jumps to land on round numbers.**
> ```
> 03:18 p.m. → 08:18 p.m.   the MINUTES already match, so it is exactly 5 hours
> 01:15 p.m. → 01:42 p.m.   the HOUR already matches, so just 42 − 15 = 27 minutes
> ```
> **Look at the two times before you start.** If the minutes agree, only hours have passed; if the hours agree, only minutes have. **Half these questions need no working at all.**

## 12.5 Finding the start time, the end time, or the gap

**Every time problem has three quantities, and any two give the third.**
```
START TIME  +  DURATION  =  END TIME
```

| Given | Find | Method |
|---|---|---|
| start and duration | **end** | count FORWARDS |
| end and duration | **start** | count BACKWARDS |
| start and end | **duration** | count ON from start to end |

```
Jyoti leaves at 06:15 p.m. for 1 hr 45 min
   06:15 + 1 hr = 07:15,  then + 45 min = 08:00 p.m.

Ragini finishes at 09:40 p.m. after 1 hr 10 min
   09:40 − 1 hr = 08:40,  then − 10 min = 08:30 p.m.  (backwards)

Picnic leaves 08:30 a.m., returns after 4 hr 10 min
   08:30 + 4 hr = 12:30 p.m.,  then + 10 min = 12:40 p.m.
```
> [!TIP]
> **Always add the HOURS first, then the minutes.** Doing the minutes first often pushes you past an hour boundary and forces an extra regrouping. **Hours-first keeps the awkward step to the very end, where it is easiest to see.**

> [!WARNING]
> **WATCH FOR CROSSING MIDDAY OR MIDNIGHT — the a.m./p.m. label flips.**
> ```
> 08:30 a.m. + 4 hr 10 min = 12:40 p.m.     ← crossed noon: a.m. becomes p.m.
> 08:35 p.m. + 8 hr 25 min = 05:00 a.m. THE NEXT DAY
> ```
> **The second one is much easier in 24-hour time:**
> ```
> 08:35 p.m. = 20:35
> 20:35 + 8 hr = 28:35 — past 24, so subtract 24 → 04:35 the next day
> 04:35 + 25 min = 05:00 next morning
> ```
> **Why subtracting 24 works: a whole day has gone by**, so the clock has returned to the same reading with the date moved on one.
>
> **This is exactly why timetables use the 24-hour clock** — the a.m./p.m. flip cannot be forgotten if there is no a.m./p.m. to forget.

**Adding several equal times:**
```
Raghav studies 4 subjects, 50 minutes each
   4 × 50 = 200 minutes
   200 ÷ 60 = 3 remainder 20   →  3 hours 20 minutes
```
> [!TIP]
> **Do the multiplying in ONE unit (minutes), then convert once at the end.** Trying to keep hours and minutes separate while multiplying invites mistakes — **the same advice as Chapter 8's "convert when the numbers are ugly".**

## Summary

```
1 minute = 60 seconds        1 hour = 60 minutes

WHY SECONDS — to see differences a minute is too coarse to show
   1:55 / 1:56 / 1:57 all read "1 minute" without them, and the race
   has no winner. The right unit is decided by what you must SEE.

WHY 60 AND NOT 100 — 60 divides by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30
   so half, third, quarter, fifth, sixth of an hour are all WHOLE minutes
   a third of 100 minutes would be 33⅓ — the reason 100 was never used

CONVERTING — big unit to small: MULTIPLY by 60 · small to big: DIVIDE
   NO digit-covering trick here — 60 is not a power of ten, so divide
   220 min → 220 ÷ 60 = 3 r 40 → 3 hr 40 min   (N = D × Q + R again)
   or just count up: 60, 120, 180 … and take the leftover

24-HOUR CLOCK — every moment gets its OWN number, 00:00 to 23:59
   no a.m./p.m. tag needed, so nothing can be lost or misread
   a.m. → unchanged · p.m. → ADD 12, because p.m. means "past midday"
   12 midnight = 00:00 (no hours of the new day have passed yet)
   12 noon     = 12:00

ELAPSED TIME — COUNT ON in easy jumps, up to the next whole hour first
   NEVER subtract in columns: minutes borrow 60, not 10,
      and 1:85 is not a time
   if the minutes already match → whole hours passed
   if the hours already match   → only minutes passed

START + DURATION = END — any two of the three give the third
   add HOURS first, then minutes
   crossing noon or midnight flips a.m./p.m. — in 24-hour time, subtract
      24 and move the date on by one day
```
