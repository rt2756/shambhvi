## 13.1 Factors and multiples — two names for one fact

**Arrange 12 counters in a rectangle. Every way you can do it shows a pair of factors.**
```
1 × 12   ■■■■■■■■■■■■
2 ×  6   ■■■■■■
         ■■■■■■
3 ×  4   ■■■■
         ■■■■
         ■■■■
                     factors of 12 :  1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY AN ARRAY SHOWS THE FACTORS.**
>
> **An array with 3 rows of 4 says 3 × 4 = 12** — so both 3 and 4 divide 12 exactly, with nothing left over. **That is precisely what "factor" means.**
>
> **And if a number is NOT a factor, no rectangle can be made from it.** Try 5 rows of 12 counters and the last row comes up short — the leftovers are the remainder.
>
> **So "make a rectangle" and "divides exactly" are the same test**, done with counters instead of arithmetic.

```
FACTOR × FACTOR = MULTIPLE

  3 × 4 = 12       3 and 4 are FACTORS of 12
                   12 is a MULTIPLE of 3 and of 4
```
> [!TIP]
> **Factor and multiple are the same relationship seen from two ends** — like "mother" and "daughter". **3 is a factor of 12 exactly when 12 is a multiple of 3.** There is only one fact here, not two.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY 1 AND THE NUMBER ITSELF ARE ALWAYS FACTORS.**
>
> **Because 1 × N = N is true for every number N.** That single array — one long row of N counters — always exists.
>
> **So every number has at least two factors before you look at all.** The interesting question is whether it has any *others*.

```
factors of 10 : 1, 2, 5, 10          (arrays 1×10, 2×5)
factors of 14 : 1, 2, 7, 14          (arrays 1×14, 2×7)
factors of 20 : 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20   (arrays 1×20, 2×10, 4×5)
factors of 25 : 1, 5, 25             (arrays 1×25, 5×5)
factors of 32 : 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32   (arrays 1×32, 2×16, 4×8)
factors of 46 : 1, 2, 23, 46         (arrays 1×46, 2×23)
factors of 54 : 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18, 27, 54
```
> [!TIP]
> **Factors come in PAIRS, so hunt in pairs.** For 54: 1 pairs with 54, 2 with 27, 3 with 18, 6 with 9. **Stop when the pair meets in the middle** — after 6 × 9 the next try, 7, is already bigger than its partner would be, so there is nothing left to find. **This tells you when you are finished, which guessing never does.**

**A PRIME number has only ONE array — 1 × itself.**
```
13 → only 1 × 13         37 → only 1 × 37
so 13 and 37 have exactly TWO factors: 1 and themselves
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY 13 IS PRIME AND 12 IS NOT — in the language of rectangles.**
>
> **13 counters can only be laid out as one long line.** There is no way to arrange them into equal rows without a gap.
>
> **12 counters can be laid out three different ways**, because 12 has other factors to build with.
>
> **A prime is a number that refuses to make a proper rectangle.** That is not a definition to memorise — it is a thing you can see on the table in front of you.

## 13.2 Jumps on the number line

**A rabbit jumps 4 at a time; a frog jumps 3 at a time. Both start at 0.**
```
rabbit  0 ── 4 ── 8 ── 12 ── 16 ── 20 ── 24 ──
frog    0 ─ 3 ─ 6 ─ 9 ─ 12 ─ 15 ─ 18 ─ 21 ─ 24
                        ↑                    ↑
                     both land            both land
```
**The numbers they BOTH touch — 12, 24, 36, … — are the COMMON MULTIPLES of 3 and 4.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY A JUMPER OF SIZE 4 LANDS EXACTLY ON THE MULTIPLES OF 4.**
>
> **After n jumps the rabbit is at 4 + 4 + … + 4 (n times) = 4 × n.** So the places it can reach are precisely the multiples of 4 — and nowhere else.
>
> **So "can the rabbit reach 36?" and "is 36 a multiple of 4?" are the same question**, and "is 4 a factor of 36?" is that same question a third time.
>
> **The number line turns factors and multiples into something you can walk along.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE COMMON MULTIPLES ARE ALWAYS 12, 24, 36 … — the FIRST one and then its multiples.**
>
> **When the rabbit and frog meet at 12, they are both standing on a landing spot with the whole road ahead of them.** That is exactly the situation they were in at 0.
>
> **So whatever happened between 0 and 12 must happen again between 12 and 24, and again after that.** The meetings repeat forever, spaced 12 apart.
>
> **This is why you only ever need to find the FIRST common multiple.** Every other one is a multiple of it.

**When one jump size divides the other, the answer is simpler still:**
```
spider jumps 3  : 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 …
grasshopper 6   : 6, 12, 18, 24 …
common          : 6, 12, 18, 24 …   ← just the multiples of 6
```
> [!TIP]
> **If the bigger jump is a multiple of the smaller, the grasshopper's every landing is also a spider landing** — because the spider simply takes two hops where the grasshopper takes one. **So the first common multiple is the bigger number itself.**

```
first common multiples
   2 and 3 → 6        3 and 9 → 9        6 and 8 → 24
   2 and 4 → 4        5 and 8 → 40       6 and 9 → 18
   5 and 10 → 10      8 and 12 → 24      9 and 12 → 36
```
> [!WARNING]
> **THE FIRST COMMON MULTIPLE IS NOT ALWAYS THE TWO NUMBERS MULTIPLIED TOGETHER.**
>
> **4 and 6: multiplying gives 24, but they first meet at 12.** **6 and 8: multiplying gives 48, but they first meet at 24.**
>
> **Why: the product always IS a common multiple** (4 × 6 is a multiple of both), **but when the two numbers share a factor, they meet sooner.**
>
> **Multiplying is a safe way to find *a* common multiple. Listing is the way to find the FIRST one.**

## 13.3 Common factors

**Mowgli jumps 3 steps at a time from 0. He can visit friends at 9, 21, 39 and 57 — because 3 is a factor of all of them.**

```
COMMON FACTOR of two numbers = a number that divides BOTH exactly
```
```
12 and 16 → 1, 2, 4          20 and 5  → 1, 5
 8 and 12 → 1, 2, 4           9 and 21 → 1, 3
12 and 15 → 1, 3              6 and 27 → 1, 3
 2 and  9 → 1                 3 and  5 → 1
```
> [!TIP]
> **Every pair has at least one common factor — 1** — because 1 divides everything. **A pair whose ONLY common factor is 1 (like 2 and 9, or 3 and 5) has nothing in common at all except that.**

**Common factors of 24 and 36:** jumps of 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12 all reach both, as does 1.
```
24 : 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24
36 : 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36
common : 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE ONLY COMMON FACTOR OF TWO CONSECUTIVE NUMBERS IS 1.**
>
> **Take 14 and 15. Suppose some number bigger than 1 divided both — say a jumper of that size could land on 14 and also on 15.**
>
> **But those two landings are only ONE step apart, and the jumper's stride is bigger than 1.** It cannot land twice in a single step.
>
> **So no jump bigger than 1 can reach both.** The argument works for any two neighbours — 8 and 9, 99 and 100 — because they are always exactly 1 apart.
>
> **This is a genuine proof, not a pattern spotted in examples.**

## 13.4 Divisibility by 2, 5 and 10

```
divisible by 2  ⟺  the ONES digit is 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8
divisible by 5  ⟺  the ONES digit is 0 or 5
divisible by 10 ⟺  the ONES digit is 0
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY ONLY THE LAST DIGIT MATTERS — for all three rules at once.**
>
> **Every number splits into "a whole lot of tens" plus "the ones digit".**
> ```
> 78  =  70 + 8         345 = 340 + 5
> ```
> **And 10 itself is divisible by 2 and by 5.** So *every* ten is already divisible by 2 and by 5 — 70, 340, 4,000, all of them.
>
> **The tens part can never spoil things.** Whether the whole number divides therefore depends entirely on the ones digit that is left over.
>
> **This is why the rules feel like magic tricks and are actually nothing of the kind.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY EVERY MULTIPLE OF 10 IS AUTOMATICALLY A MULTIPLE OF 2 AND OF 5.**
>
> **Because 10 = 2 × 5.** A number made of whole tens is made of whole 2s and whole 5s at the same time.
>
> **So "divisible by 10 only" is an IMPOSSIBLE category — nothing can go in it.**
> ```
> 30, 40, 90  →  divisible by 2 AND 5 AND 10
> 22, 38, 84  →  divisible by 2 only
> 25, 55, 75  →  divisible by 5 only
> divisible by 10 only  →  no such number exists
> ```
> **Finding that a box must stay empty is a real mathematical result**, not a failure to fill it in.

> [!TIP]
> **Sorting practice — 90, 22, 38, 30, 75, 45, 66, 78, 62, 40, 84, 56, 25, 95, 55:**
> ```
> by 2, 5 and 10 : 90  30  40           (they end in 0)
> by 2 only      : 22  38  66  78  62  84  56
> by 5 only      : 75  45  25  95  55
> by 10 only     : —  empty, and it always will be
> ```

## 13.5 Statements to test

| Statement | True? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Factors of even numbers must be even | **False** | 3 is a factor of 6 |
| Multiples of odd numbers cannot be even | **False** | 6 is a multiple of 3 |
| Factors of odd numbers cannot be even | **True** | see below |
| The product of two numbers is a common multiple of them | **True** | 4 × 6 = 24 is a multiple of both, always |
| The only common factor of two consecutive numbers is 1 | **True** | they are 1 apart — no bigger jump reaches both |
| 0 cannot be a factor of a number | **True** | a jump of 0 never moves off the start |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY AN ODD NUMBER CAN NEVER HAVE AN EVEN FACTOR.**
>
> **If an even number were a factor of N, then N would be that even number multiplied by something.**
>
> **But an even number times anything is even** — it is made of 2s, so the answer is too.
>
> **So N would have to be even, contradicting the fact that it is odd.** No even factor can exist.
>
> **Notice the shape of this argument: assume it happened, and show that something impossible follows.** It is one of the most powerful moves in mathematics, and you have just used it.

> [!TIP]
> **Puzzle-solving with factors:** Mowgli wants to reach Baloo at 30 but avoid Sher Khan at 25.
> ```
> jumps that reach 30 : 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30   (the factors of 30)
> jumps that reach 25 : 1, 5, 25                     (the factors of 25)
> so he must avoid    : 1 and 5
> safe jumps          : 2, 3, 6, 10, 15, 30
> ```
> **To reach 21 AND 35 he needs a COMMON factor: 1 or 7 — so a stride of 7.**
>
> **"Which jump reaches this but not that?" is always a factor question in disguise.**

## Summary

```
ARRAYS SHOW FACTORS — 3 rows of 4 means 3 × 4 = 12, so 3 and 4 divide 12
   a number that is NOT a factor leaves a short last row — the remainder
   FACTOR × FACTOR = MULTIPLE; the two words are one relationship
      seen from opposite ends
   1 and the number itself are ALWAYS factors, since 1 × N = N
   factors come in PAIRS — hunt in pairs and stop when they meet

PRIME — a number with only ONE array, 1 × itself, so exactly two factors
   13 and 37 are prime; they refuse to make a proper rectangle

JUMPS OF SIZE 4 land exactly on the MULTIPLES OF 4, because after
   n jumps you are at 4 × n
   "can it reach 36?" = "is 36 a multiple of 4?" = "is 4 a factor of 36?"

COMMON MULTIPLES — where two jumpers both land
   after the FIRST meeting, the situation repeats exactly as at 0,
      so the meetings recur forever, evenly spaced
   → find the first one; all the others are its multiples
   the product is ALWAYS a common multiple but NOT always the first:
      4 and 6 meet at 12, not 24 — because they share a factor
   if one jump divides the other, the bigger number IS the first meeting

COMMON FACTORS — numbers that divide both
   every pair shares 1 · some share nothing else (2 and 9, 3 and 5)
   TWO CONSECUTIVE NUMBERS share only 1 — a stride bigger than 1
      cannot land twice within a single step

DIVISIBILITY — only the ONES digit matters
   by 2 : ends 0 2 4 6 8 · by 5 : ends 0 or 5 · by 10 : ends 0
   WHY: every number is "whole tens + ones digit", and 10 = 2 × 5,
   so the tens part is already divisible by 2 and by 5 and can never
   spoil things — everything hangs on the leftover ones digit
   "divisible by 10 only" is an EMPTY category, and always will be

AN ODD NUMBER CAN NEVER HAVE AN EVEN FACTOR — an even number times
   anything is even, so the result could not have been odd
```
