## 10.1 Reflection symmetry — the fold-and-cut trick

**To cut out the letter A, you don't draw the whole letter.**
```
1. fold a sheet of paper in half
2. draw HALF the letter A, with its straight edge on the fold
3. cut along the outline — through BOTH layers
4. open it out → a perfect letter A
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THE TWO HALVES COME OUT PERFECTLY MATCHED, EVERY SINGLE TIME.**
>
> **The scissors cut through both layers at once, so the two halves are not "drawn to match" — they ARE the same cut.** No care or skill is involved; the paper does the matching for you.
>
> **And the fold line becomes the LINE OF SYMMETRY.** Fold the finished letter back along it and the two halves land exactly on top of each other.
>
> **That is the definition: a shape has reflection symmetry about a line if folding along that line makes the two halves coincide.**

> [!TIP]
> **The fold has to sit where the line of symmetry is — that is the only decision you make.** The A's line is vertical, so fold top-to-bottom-wise (a vertical crease). **Get the fold wrong and you get half an A joined to a mirror of the wrong half.**

**Fold TWICE and you can make letters with two lines of symmetry:**
```
letter H — fold vertically, then fold again horizontally
           you now have a QUARTER of the paper
           draw a quarter of the H, cut, open out
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY ONE FOLD GIVES 2 MATCHING PIECES AND TWO FOLDS GIVE 4.**
>
> **Each fold doubles the number of layers the scissors pass through.** One fold → 2 layers → 2 identical pieces. Two folds → 4 layers → 4 identical pieces.
>
> **So a two-fold cutout automatically has BOTH a vertical and a horizontal line of symmetry** — you couldn't avoid it if you tried.
>
> **Which is why you can only use the quarter-fold for letters that genuinely have both lines.** Try it on A and you get a strange four-armed thing, not an A.

## 10.2 Lines of symmetry in letters

| Letter | Vertical line | Horizontal line | How to cut it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | ✔ | ✘ | half fold |
| **E** | ✘ | ✔ | half fold |
| **T** | ✔ | ✘ | half fold |
| **V** | ✔ | ✘ | half fold |
| **K** | ✘ | ✔ | half fold |
| **X** | ✔ | ✔ | quarter fold |
| **O** | ✔ | ✔ | quarter fold |
| **H** | ✔ | ✔ | quarter fold |
| **N** | ✘ | ✘ | must be drawn in full |

> [!WARNING]
> **N LOOKS SYMMETRICAL BUT IT IS NOT — and this is the trap of the chapter.**
>
> **Fold an N down the middle and the diagonal stroke goes the wrong way.** The left half's slash runs top-left to bottom-right; the right half needs it running the other way. **They do not land on top of each other.**
>
> **The test is always the fold, never the feeling.** If you cannot fold, trace or imagine one half landing exactly on the other, there is no line of symmetry there.
>
> **N does have another kind of symmetry, though — see §10.3.** That is exactly why it feels balanced.

> [!TIP]
> **A quick way to test a letter without paper: hold a small mirror upright along the proposed line.** If what you see in the mirror completes the letter correctly, the line is a line of symmetry. **This works for shapes, designs and rangoli patterns too.**

## 10.3 Rotational symmetry — turning instead of folding

**Make a firki (paper windmill), put a dot on one blade, and spin it.**
```
initial   ¼ turn   ½ turn   ¾ turn   full turn
   ✔        ✔        ✔        ✔         ✔      ← looks the same every time
```
**The firki has ROTATIONAL SYMMETRY: it looks the same after a turn of less than a full circle.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY THIS IS A COMPLETELY DIFFERENT KIND OF SYMMETRY FROM FOLDING.**
>
> **Reflection symmetry asks: can I FOLD it onto itself?** **Rotational symmetry asks: can I TURN it onto itself?**
>
> **These are separate questions and a shape can answer them independently.**
> ```
> A  — folds ✔   turns ✘        (reflection only)
> N  — folds ✘   turns ✔        (rotational only, at a half turn)
> H  — folds ✔   turns ✔        (both)
> F  — folds ✘   turns ✘        (neither)
> ```
> **All four combinations really happen**, which is proof that neither kind implies the other.

> [!WARNING]
> **A FULL TURN DOES NOT COUNT.**
>
> **Every shape in the world looks the same after a full turn** — you have put it back exactly where it started, so of course it matches.
>
> **A "symmetry" that every single shape possesses tells you nothing.** That is why rotational symmetry means looking the same after turning **less** than a full circle.
>
> **This is the same reasoning as "every number divides by 1".** True, but useless for telling numbers apart.

## 10.4 The order of a rotational symmetry

**Count how many times a shape looks the same during ONE full turn. That count is its ORDER.**

| Shape | Looks the same at | Order |
|---|---|---|
| Letter N, letter S, letter Z | ½ turn | **2** |
| Firki with 4 blades | ¼, ½, ¾ turn | **4** |
| Equilateral triangle | ⅓, ⅔ turn | **3** |
| Square | ¼, ½, ¾ turn | **4** |
| Regular hexagon | every ⅙ turn | **6** |
| Letter A, letter F | nowhere before the full turn | **1** (= no rotational symmetry) |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY LOOKING THE SAME AFTER 1/n OF A TURN MEANS IT LOOKS THE SAME n TIMES.**
>
> **If a quarter turn works once, do it again — you are now at a half turn, and it must work again**, because you have only repeated a move that already succeeded.
>
> **Keep going: ¾, then a full turn. Four quarter turns fill the circle, so there are exactly four matching positions.**
> ```
> smallest turn that works = 1/4  →  order 4
> smallest turn that works = 1/2  →  order 2
> smallest turn that works = 1/3  →  order 3
> ```
> **So you only ever need to find the SMALLEST turn that works.** The order is whatever that fraction's bottom number is — and everything else follows for free.

> [!TIP]
> **This is Chapter 3's turns doing real work.** A quarter turn is a right angle; four of them make a full turn. **The order of a rotational symmetry is just "how many equal turns fit in a circle" — which is why the orders are always whole numbers.**

## 10.5 Symmetry in digits and numbers

**Digits, written in their plain printed form:**
```
REFLECTION symmetry
   0  vertical AND horizontal
   8  vertical AND horizontal
   3  horizontal only
   1  vertical only  (when drawn as a plain stroke)

ROTATIONAL symmetry (half turn)
   0    8    1  (plain stroke)

BOTH
   0    8    1  (plain stroke)

NEITHER
   2   4   5   6   7   9
```
> [!WARNING]
> **THE ANSWER DEPENDS ON HOW THE DIGIT IS WRITTEN, and that is not a flaw in the question.**
>
> **A 1 written as a bare vertical stroke has both symmetries. A 1 with a little flag on top and a base has neither.**
>
> **Symmetry is a property of the SHAPE on the page, not of the number it stands for.** Change the shape and you change the answer — so always say which form you are judging.

**Whole numbers can be symmetrical too:**
```
11        vertical line ✔   horizontal line ✔   half turn ✔   → both
1001      vertical line ✔   horizontal line ✔   half turn ✔   → both
88        both symmetries
609       half turn ✔ (6 becomes 9 and 9 becomes 6) — rotational only
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY 609 TURNS INTO ITSELF BUT DOES NOT FOLD ONTO ITSELF.**
>
> **Turn 609 upside down: the 6 lands where the 9 was and becomes a 9; the 9 lands where the 6 was and becomes a 6; the 0 stays a 0.** You read 609 again.
>
> **But fold it and the digits reverse their ORDER**, which a mirror does and a half turn also does — the difference is that the mirror does not also flip each digit top-to-bottom. **6 in a mirror is not 9; it is a backwards 6.**
>
> **So the two tests really are asking different things**, and 609 is the clearest proof of it.

> [!TIP]
> **To build a symmetric number yourself, work from the outside in.**
> ```
> for a vertical mirror line: use only symmetric digits (0, 1, 8)
>    and read the same both ways → 101, 8118, 10801
> for a half turn: pair 6 with 9 at opposite ends, keep 0, 1, 8 in the middle
>    → 619, 96, 1691, 8698
> ```
> **Building one is a far better test of understanding than spotting one.**

## 10.6 Designs, and how to fix an unsymmetrical one

**If a design ALMOST has rotational symmetry, you can add shapes to complete it.**
```
to get symmetry at a HALF turn  — whatever you add on one side,
                                  add the mirror-through-the-centre on the other
to get symmetry at a QUARTER turn — whatever you add, add THREE more copies,
                                  at ¼, ½ and ¾ turns round the centre
```
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WHY A QUARTER-TURN DESIGN NEEDS FOUR COPIES OF EVERYTHING.**
>
> **Turn the design a quarter and your new shape moves to a new place. For the design to look unchanged, something identical must ALREADY be sitting there.**
>
> **Turn again and the same argument applies, and again.** By the time you are back at the start you have demanded four copies — one at each quarter position.
>
> **So the rule "order 4 means four of everything" isn't a rule at all. It is forced.**

**Colouring a square with two colours so it survives every quarter turn:** colour the four quarter-sections in a repeating pattern — **each quarter must look exactly like the one before it, rotated.** The design then looks the same **4 times** in a full turn.

**Block printing (Rajasthan):** one carved wooden block is stamped four times round a centre.
> [!TIP]
> **This is why block-printed cloth is so full of rotational symmetry — the symmetry comes from the METHOD, not from the artist's care.** Stamping the same block at four quarter positions cannot produce anything else. **The same is true of rangoli, of paper snowflakes, and of the firki.**

> [!TIP]
> **Sorting your own designs into three boxes is the best way to see that the two symmetries are independent:**
> ```
> rotational only   ·   reflection only   ·   both
> ```
> **If a box stays empty, you have not yet understood how they differ** — go back and make one that belongs there.

## Summary

```
REFLECTION SYMMETRY — can you FOLD it onto itself?
   the fold line IS the line of symmetry
   fold-and-cut works because the scissors cut both layers at once,
      so the halves ARE the same cut — not merely drawn to match
   one fold → 2 identical pieces · two folds → 4
      so a quarter-fold cutout always has BOTH lines, unavoidably

   LETTERS   A T V — vertical    E K — horizontal
             X O H — both        N — neither (the diagonal betrays it)
   test with a mirror stood on the line, never by feel

ROTATIONAL SYMMETRY — can you TURN it onto itself?
   a FULL turn does not count — every shape manages that
   ORDER = how many times it matches in one full turn
   smallest turn 1/n  →  order n, because repeating that turn
      keeps working until the circle is filled

THE TWO ARE INDEPENDENT — all four combinations exist
   A folds not turns · N turns not folds · H both · F neither

DIGITS   0 8 (and a plain-stroke 1) have both
         3 folds horizontally · 2 4 5 6 7 9 have neither
   the answer depends on HOW the digit is drawn — symmetry belongs
   to the shape, not the number
   609 survives a half turn (6↔9) but not a mirror — the clearest proof
   that the two tests ask different questions

MAKING DESIGNS
   half-turn symmetry  → add a matching copy through the centre
   quarter-turn symmetry → add THREE more copies, at 1/4, 1/2, 3/4
      forced, because each turn moves your shape somewhere that must
      already be occupied

BLOCK PRINTING, rangoli, firkis, paper snowflakes — the symmetry comes
   from the METHOD, not from careful drawing
```
