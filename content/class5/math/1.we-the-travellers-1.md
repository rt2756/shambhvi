## 1.1 Numbers beyond 1,000

**Keep adding a thousand and see what happens:**
```
1,000 → 2,000 → 3,000 → … → 9,000 → ?
```
**Add one more thousand to 9,000 and you get TEN THOUSAND — written 10,000.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE PATTERN THAT MAKES THE WHOLE NUMBER SYSTEM WORK: every place is TEN TIMES the place on its right.**
> ```
> 10 Ones      = 1 Ten       = 10
> 10 Tens      = 1 Hundred   = 100
> 10 Hundreds  = 1 Thousand  = 1,000
> 10 Thousands = 1 Ten thousand = 10,000
> ```
> **So the place value chart just grows a new column on the left when you run out of room:**
> ```
> TTh    Th    H    T    O
>  1     0     0    0    0     = 10,000
> ```
> **Why we never need new digits: the SAME ten digits 0–9 do all the work.** What changes is **where** a digit sits — and that is the whole idea of place value.

**We use a COMMA to help read large numbers easily.** In the Indian system it goes before the last three digits: `10,000` not `10000`.

## 1.2 Reading and writing five-digit numbers

**They are written exactly the way numbers below 9,999 are — nothing new to learn, just one more column.**

| Number | TTh | Th | H | T | O | Number name |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,001 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | Ten thousand one |
| 10,024 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 2 | 4 | Ten thousand twenty-four |
| 10,458 | 1 | 0 | 4 | 5 | 8 | Ten thousand four hundred fifty-eight |
| 13,520 | 1 | 3 | 5 | 2 | 0 | Thirteen thousand five hundred twenty |
| 20,000 | 2 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | Twenty thousand |
| 45,867 | 4 | 5 | 8 | 6 | 7 | Forty-five thousand eight hundred sixty-seven |

> [!TIP]
> **How to read any five-digit number: SPLIT IT AT THE COMMA.**
> ```
> 45,867  →  "45"  and  "867"
>         →  "forty-five thousand"  then  "eight hundred sixty-seven"
> ```
> **Read the part before the comma, say "thousand", then read the rest.** The comma is telling you exactly where to pause.

**Every number can be broken into its places:**
```
1,380 = 1 Thousand + 3 Hundreds + 8 Tens + 0 Ones
9,123 = 9 Thousands + 1 Hundred + 2 Tens + 3 Ones
```

## 1.3 Comparing large numbers

> [!WARNING]
> **A student said: "9,990 is greater than 49,014 because 9 is greater than 4."**
>
> **THE STUDENT IS WRONG.**
>
> **Why: you must FIRST count the digits, and only then compare digit by digit.**
> ```
> 9,990  has FOUR digits
> 49,014 has FIVE digits
> ```
> **A five-digit number is ALWAYS bigger than a four-digit number**, whatever digits they use. **Even the smallest five-digit number, 10,000, is bigger than the biggest four-digit number, 9,999.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **HOW TO COMPARE TWO NUMBERS, in order:**
> ```
> 1. Count the DIGITS. More digits → bigger number. Stop here if they differ.
> 2. If they have the SAME number of digits, compare from the LEFT,
>    one place at a time, and stop at the first place where they differ.
> ```
> **Why start from the LEFT: the leftmost digit is worth the MOST.** A difference in the ten-thousands column outweighs everything to its right put together.

**Worked:**
```
40,347 and 40,473

TTh   4 = 4     same, look further
Th    0 = 0     same, look further
H     3 < 4     DIFFERENT — stop here

so 40,347 < 40,473
```
**The rest of the digits do not matter once you find the first difference.**

## 1.4 Rounding to the nearest ten, hundred and thousand

**Think of a rabbit at 2,346 whose food is kept at a neighbouring round number. Which one does it reach in the FEWEST jumps?**

```
NEAREST TEN of 2,346
   2,340 ← 2,346 → 2,350
   6 jumps back, 4 jumps forward → 2,350 is nearer

NEAREST HUNDRED of 2,346
   2,300 ← 2,346 → 2,400
   46 jumps back, 54 jumps forward → 2,300 is nearer

NEAREST THOUSAND of 2,346
   2,000 ← 2,346 → 3,000
   346 back, 654 forward → 2,000 is nearer
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ROUNDING means finding the nearest round number — the one you reach in the FEWEST steps.**
>
> **The shortcut, and why it works:**
> ```
> NEAREST TEN      — look at the ONES digit
> NEAREST HUNDRED  — look at the TENS digit
> NEAREST THOUSAND — look at the HUNDREDS digit
>
> digit is 5 or more → round UP
> digit is 4 or less → round DOWN
> ```
> **Why look at exactly that one digit: it tells you which HALF of the gap you are in.** Halfway between two tens is 5; if the ones digit is past 5 you are nearer the top, and if it is below 5 you are nearer the bottom.

**Practice:**
| Number | Nearest ten | Nearest hundred | Nearest thousand |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3,176 | 3,180 | 3,200 | 3,000 |
| 4,017 | 4,020 | 4,000 | 4,000 |
| 5,789 | 5,790 | 5,800 | 6,000 |
| 8,203 | 8,200 | 8,200 | 8,000 |

> [!TIP]
> **Notice 4,017: its nearest hundred and nearest thousand are BOTH 4,000.** That can happen, and it is not a mistake — **4,017 is close to 4,000 in both senses.**

## 1.5 Where large numbers turn up

**Distance a person can travel in ONE HOUR:**
```
on foot          3 – 5 km          1-digit
on horseback    10 – 15 km         2-digit
by cycle        12 – 20 km
by motorbike    40 – 60 km
by train        40 – 160 km        3-digit
by ship         25 – 45 km
by aircraft    750 – 920 km
by spacecraft  at least 28,000 km  5-digit!
```

> [!TIP]
> **A nice way to find a big number without counting anything: MULTIPLY two things you can estimate.**
>
> **A book has about 200 pages, and each page has about 50 words.** So the book has about **200 × 50 = 10,000 words** — and nobody had to count them.
>
> **This is what estimation is for**: getting a sensible number when counting is impossible.

## From the book — activities worked, with reasons

### Continue the pattern

**First find the jump from one number to the next, then keep making that same jump.**
```
5,501 → 6,401 → 7,301 → ____ → ____
jump: 6,401 − 5,501 = 900        so  8,201 → 9,101

73,005 → 72,004 → ____ → ____
jump: 1,001 LESS each time         so  71,003 → 70,002

83,350 → 82,350 → ____
jump: 1,000 less (only the thousands digit drops)   so  81,350
```
> [!TIP]
> **Why check the jump twice:** one gap can fool you. If there are three numbers given, check that both gaps are the same before you trust the rule.

### "9,990 is greater than 49,014 because 9 is greater than 4." Right or wrong?

**Wrong.** The student compared the first digits — but those digits are in **different places**.
```
 9,990  → the 9 is in the THOUSANDS place  → worth 9,000
49,014  → the 4 is in the TEN-THOUSANDS place → worth 40,000
```
**Why the digit count settles it:** any five-digit number is at least 10,000, and any four-digit number is at most 9,999. So **more digits always wins**. Only when the digit counts match do you compare from the left.

### Digit swap

**(a) Swap two digits of 1,478 to make a number bigger than 5,500.**
Swap 1 and 8 → **8,471**. **Why:** the thousands place matters most, so put the biggest digit (8) there.

**(b) Swap two digits of 10,593 to make a number**
- **between 11,000 and 15,000:** swap 0 and 3 → **13,590** (the number starts "13", which sits between 11 and 15 thousand).
- **more than 35,000:** swap 1 and 5 → **50,193** (or 1 and 9 → 90,513). **Why:** the ten-thousands digit has to be 3 or more, so move a big digit to the front.

**(c) Swap two digits of 48,247 to make it**
- **as small as possible:** swap the front 4 with the 2 → **28,447**. **Why:** the smallest digit available (2) goes to the most valuable place.
- **as big as possible:** swap 4 and 8 → **84,247**. **Why:** the biggest digit (8) goes to the front.

### Travel and trips

**A cyclist covers 15 km in one hour. How far in 4 hours?** 15 + 15 + 15 + 15 = 15 × 4 = **60 km**. **Why multiply:** the same distance is added once for every hour.

**461 girls + 439 boys = 900 students. How many vehicles?** Divide 900 by how many fit in one vehicle:
```
bicycle (2)     900 ÷ 2   = 450        tempo traveller (10)  900 ÷ 10  = 90
autorickshaw (3) 900 ÷ 3  = 300        boat (20)             900 ÷ 20  = 45
car (4)         900 ÷ 4   = 225        minibus (25)          900 ÷ 25  = 36
big car (6)     900 ÷ 6   = 150        aeroplane (180)       900 ÷ 180 = 5
```
**Why it comes out exact every time:** 900 happens to be a multiple of all these numbers. If it weren't, you would need **one extra vehicle** for the students left over — nobody gets left behind.

### Pastime mathematics

**River crossing (lion, sheep, grass).** The sheep is the trouble-maker — it can't be left with the lion OR with the grass. So the sheep travels first, and is brought back once:
```
1. take SHEEP across          (lion + grass safe together)
2. come back alone
3. take LION across
4. bring SHEEP back           (else the lion eats it)
5. take GRASS across          (lion + grass safe together)
6. come back alone
7. take SHEEP across          → all safe in 7 crossings
```

**Pile of pebbles (two piles of 7, whoever takes the last pebble wins).** Try 1 and 1, then 2 and 2: the player who moves **second** always wins. **The winning trick: always make the piles equal again.** If your friend takes 3 from one pile, you take 3 from the other. **Why it works:** whenever it is your friend's turn the piles are equal, so they can never take the last pebble without leaving you a matching move — you always get the final one.

**Mira's "you always end with 9" puzzle.**
```
73 − 37 = 36 → 63 − 36 = 27 → 72 − 27 = 45 → 54 − 45 = 9
```
Every difference (36, 27, 45, 9) is in the **9 times table**. **Why:** when you swap the digits, each digit moves between the tens place and the ones place. A digit in the tens place is worth 10 times itself, in the ones place 1 times — a gap of **9 times the digit**. So the difference is always **9 × (difference between the two digits)**:

| Digits | Gap between digits | Difference of numbers |
|---|---|---|
| 3, 7 | 4 | 73 − 37 = 36 = 9 × 4 |
| 1, 9 | 8 | 91 − 19 = 72 = 9 × 8 |
| 2, 8 | 6 | 82 − 28 = 54 = 9 × 6 |
| 4, 5 | 1 | 54 − 45 = 9 = 9 × 1 |
| 1, 3 | 2 | 31 − 13 = 18 = 9 × 2 |
| 1, 4 | 3 | 41 − 14 = 27 = 9 × 3 |
| 1, 6 | 5 | 61 − 16 = 45 = 9 × 5 |
| 1, 8 | 7 | 81 − 18 = 63 = 9 × 7 |

- **A 1-digit answer at the first step:** digits next to each other (4 and 5, 6 and 7…), because the gap is 1 and 9 × 1 = 9.
- **A difference of 27:** digits 3 apart, like 1 and 4 or 2 and 5.
- **Why you always land on 9:** every answer is a two-digit multiple of 9, and the process keeps going until it reaches the only one-digit multiple of 9 — which is 9 itself.

### Breaking a number up in unusual ways

**The book writes 983 = 90 Tens + 83 Ones.** That's allowed — 90 tens is 900, and 900 + 83 = 983. **The trick: work out what the given parts are worth, then the blank is whatever is left over.**
```
68     = 5 Tens + 18 Ones                       (50 + 18)
607    = 4 Hundreds + 207 Ones                  (400 + 207)
5,621  = 4 Thousand + 16 Hundreds + 2 Tens + 1 One     (4,000 + 1,600 + 20 + 1)
7,069  = 5 Thousand + 20 Hundreds + 69 Ones     (5,000 + 2,000 + 69)
37,608 = 2 Ten Thousand + 17 Thousand + 6 Hundreds + 8 Ones   (20,000 + 17,000 + 600 + 8)
43,001 = 3 Ten Thousand + 13 Thousand + 0 Hundreds + 1 One    (30,000 + 13,000 + 1)
```

### How many notes?

```
₹7,934:   ₹10 notes → 793     (7,930 is 793 tens; ₹4 is left over)
          ₹100 notes → 79     (7,900 is 79 hundreds)
          thousands  → 7
          ₹500 notes → 15     (2 in every thousand = 14, and the 934 holds one more 500)
₹65,342:  ₹10 → 6,534   ₹100 → 653   thousands → 65   ₹500 → 130  (65 × 2; the 342 is less than 500)
```
**Why "chop off the end digits" works:** 7,934 has 793 tens because the tens-and-bigger part is 7,930 — cover the ones digit and read what's left.

### The king's horses

The caretaker had 19 horses but each side of the square showed 5, so the king counted 5 × 4 = 20. **The mistake: a horse in a CORNER room is counted on TWO sides.** Moving horses into the corners lets fewer horses still show 5 per side:
```
 2 1 2        each side: 2 + 1 + 2 = 5
 1   1        total horses: 2+1+2 + 1+1 + 2+1+2 = 12
 2 1 2
```
**12 horses still look like "5 a side"** if every room must have a horse — so from 18 the thief could steal **6 more** before the trick fails. (If rooms may be empty, 5 in two opposite corners gives 5 a side with just 10 horses.)

## In a nutshell

```
PLACE VALUE — every place is TEN TIMES the one on its right
   10 Ones = 1 Ten · 10 Tens = 1 Hundred · 10 Hundreds = 1 Thousand
   10 Thousands = 1 TEN THOUSAND = 10,000
   the chart grows a new column TTh on the left; the digits 0–9 never change
   a COMMA before the last three digits makes a number easy to read

READING a five-digit number — split at the comma, read the front part,
   say "thousand", then read the rest:  45,867 → forty-five thousand
   eight hundred sixty-seven

COMPARING
   1. count the DIGITS — more digits means bigger, always
   2. if equal, compare from the LEFT and stop at the first difference
   9,990 is SMALLER than 49,014 — four digits can never beat five

ROUNDING — the nearest round number is the one you reach in fewest steps
   nearest TEN      → look at the ONES digit
   nearest HUNDRED  → look at the TENS digit
   nearest THOUSAND → look at the HUNDREDS digit
   5 or more round UP, 4 or less round DOWN — because that digit says
   which HALF of the gap you are in
```

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** compare digit COUNT first (9,990 < 49,014) · a digit's worth depends on its PLACE · nearest hundred looks at the TENS digit, nearest thousand at the HUNDREDS digit · 5 rounds UP · leftover people still need one more vehicle · corner horses are counted on two sides.
