## 1.1 Numbers beyond 1,000

**Keep adding a thousand and see what happens:**
```
1,000 → 2,000 → 3,000 → … → 9,000 → ?
```
**Add one more thousand to 9,000 and you get TEN THOUSAND — written 10,000.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE PATTERN THAT MAKES THE WHOLE NUMBER SYSTEM WORK: every place is TEN TIMES the place on its right.**
> ```
> 10 Ones      = 1 Ten       = 10
> 10 Tens      = 1 Hundred   = 100
> 10 Hundreds  = 1 Thousand  = 1,000
> 10 Thousands = 1 Ten thousand = 10,000
> ```
> **So the place value chart just grows a new column on the left when you run out of room:**
> ```
> TTh    Th    H    T    O
>  1     0     0    0    0     = 10,000
> ```
> **Why we never need new digits: the SAME ten digits 0–9 do all the work.** What changes is **where** a digit sits — and that is the whole idea of place value.

**We use a COMMA to help read large numbers easily.** In the Indian system it goes before the last three digits: `10,000` not `10000`.

## 1.2 Reading and writing five-digit numbers

**They are written exactly the way numbers below 9,999 are — nothing new to learn, just one more column.**

| Number | TTh | Th | H | T | O | Number name |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,001 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | Ten thousand one |
| 10,024 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 2 | 4 | Ten thousand twenty-four |
| 10,458 | 1 | 0 | 4 | 5 | 8 | Ten thousand four hundred fifty-eight |
| 13,520 | 1 | 3 | 5 | 2 | 0 | Thirteen thousand five hundred twenty |
| 20,000 | 2 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | Twenty thousand |
| 45,867 | 4 | 5 | 8 | 6 | 7 | Forty-five thousand eight hundred sixty-seven |

> [!TIP]
> **How to read any five-digit number: SPLIT IT AT THE COMMA.**
> ```
> 45,867  →  "45"  and  "867"
>         →  "forty-five thousand"  then  "eight hundred sixty-seven"
> ```
> **Read the part before the comma, say "thousand", then read the rest.** The comma is telling you exactly where to pause.

**Every number can be broken into its places:**
```
1,380 = 1 Thousand + 3 Hundreds + 8 Tens + 0 Ones
9,123 = 9 Thousands + 1 Hundred + 2 Tens + 3 Ones
```

## 1.3 Comparing large numbers

> [!WARNING]
> **A student said: "9,990 is greater than 49,014 because 9 is greater than 4."**
>
> **THE STUDENT IS WRONG.**
>
> **Why: you must FIRST count the digits, and only then compare digit by digit.**
> ```
> 9,990  has FOUR digits
> 49,014 has FIVE digits
> ```
> **A five-digit number is ALWAYS bigger than a four-digit number**, whatever digits they use. **Even the smallest five-digit number, 10,000, is bigger than the biggest four-digit number, 9,999.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **HOW TO COMPARE TWO NUMBERS, in order:**
> ```
> 1. Count the DIGITS. More digits → bigger number. Stop here if they differ.
> 2. If they have the SAME number of digits, compare from the LEFT,
>    one place at a time, and stop at the first place where they differ.
> ```
> **Why start from the LEFT: the leftmost digit is worth the MOST.** A difference in the ten-thousands column outweighs everything to its right put together.

**Worked:**
```
40,347 and 40,473

TTh   4 = 4     same, look further
Th    0 = 0     same, look further
H     3 < 4     DIFFERENT — stop here

so 40,347 < 40,473
```
**The rest of the digits do not matter once you find the first difference.**

## 1.4 Rounding to the nearest ten, hundred and thousand

**Think of a rabbit at 2,346 whose food is kept at a neighbouring round number. Which one does it reach in the FEWEST jumps?**

```
NEAREST TEN of 2,346
   2,340 ← 2,346 → 2,350
   6 jumps back, 4 jumps forward → 2,350 is nearer

NEAREST HUNDRED of 2,346
   2,300 ← 2,346 → 2,400
   46 jumps back, 54 jumps forward → 2,300 is nearer

NEAREST THOUSAND of 2,346
   2,000 ← 2,346 → 3,000
   346 back, 654 forward → 2,000 is nearer
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ROUNDING means finding the nearest round number — the one you reach in the FEWEST steps.**
>
> **The shortcut, and why it works:**
> ```
> NEAREST TEN      — look at the ONES digit
> NEAREST HUNDRED  — look at the TENS digit
> NEAREST THOUSAND — look at the HUNDREDS digit
>
> digit is 5 or more → round UP
> digit is 4 or less → round DOWN
> ```
> **Why look at exactly that one digit: it tells you which HALF of the gap you are in.** Halfway between two tens is 5; if the ones digit is past 5 you are nearer the top, and if it is below 5 you are nearer the bottom.

**Practice:**
| Number | Nearest ten | Nearest hundred | Nearest thousand |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3,176 | 3,180 | 3,200 | 3,000 |
| 4,017 | 4,020 | 4,000 | 4,000 |
| 5,789 | 5,790 | 5,800 | 6,000 |
| 8,203 | 8,200 | 8,200 | 8,000 |

> [!TIP]
> **Notice 4,017: its nearest hundred and nearest thousand are BOTH 4,000.** That can happen, and it is not a mistake — **4,017 is close to 4,000 in both senses.**

## 1.5 Where large numbers turn up

**Distance a person can travel in ONE HOUR:**
```
on foot          3 – 5 km          1-digit
on horseback    10 – 15 km         2-digit
by cycle        12 – 20 km
by motorbike    40 – 60 km
by train        40 – 160 km        3-digit
by ship         25 – 45 km
by aircraft    750 – 920 km
by spacecraft  at least 28,000 km  5-digit!
```

> [!TIP]
> **A nice way to find a big number without counting anything: MULTIPLY two things you can estimate.**
>
> **A book has about 200 pages, and each page has about 50 words.** So the book has about **200 × 50 = 10,000 words** — and nobody had to count them.
>
> **This is what estimation is for**: getting a sensible number when counting is impossible.
