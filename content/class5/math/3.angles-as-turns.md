# 3. Angles as turns

## 3.1 What a turn is

**Play "Statue": keep turning on the spot, and stop when someone calls out. How much have you turned from where you started?**

```
a giant wheel makes a FULL TURN when it comes back to its starting point
the minute hand makes a FULL TURN when it returns to where it began
Ashutosh spins right round on one foot — a FULL turn, tracing a circle
Sahana turns only halfway — a HALF turn, tracing a half-moon
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A TURN is measured by comparing where you END UP with where you STARTED.**
>
> **Why the starting position matters so much: turning is not about where you are FACING, it is about HOW FAR YOU HAVE MOVED ROUND.** Two children facing the same way may have turned completely different amounts.

## 3.2 Adding turns

```
2 half turns in the same direction      =  a FULL turn
2 quarter turns in the same direction   =  a HALF turn
4 quarter turns in the same direction   =  a FULL turn
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **TURNS SIMPLY ADD UP, just like fractions of a whole.**
> ```
> 1/2 + 1/2 = 1        two halves make a whole turn
> 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2      two quarters make a half turn
> 1/4 × 4   = 1        four quarters make a whole turn
> ```
> **Why the fraction arithmetic works: A FULL TURN IS THE "WHOLE".**
>
> **A quarter turn is a quarter OF A FULL TURN**, in exactly the way a quarter chapati is a quarter of a whole chapati. **So everything you learned about fractions applies here without change.**

## 3.3 Naming the angles

**Hold one straw still and turn the other around, joined at a paper clip. Different amounts of turn have different names:**

| Turn | Angle |
|---|---|
| **less than 1/4 turn** | **ACUTE angle** |
| **exactly 1/4 turn** | **RIGHT angle** |
| **between 1/4 and 1/2 turn** | **OBTUSE angle** |
| **exactly 1/2 turn** | **STRAIGHT angle** |
| **more than 1/2 turn** (e.g. 3/4) | bigger still — a reflex turn |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **THE RIGHT ANGLE IS THE LANDMARK — everything else is described by comparison with it.**
> ```
> ACUTE   — SMALLER than a right angle
> RIGHT   — exactly a QUARTER turn
> OBTUSE  — BIGGER than a right angle but smaller than a straight angle
> STRAIGHT — exactly a HALF turn; the two arms lie in one straight line
> ```
> **Why a straight angle looks straight: after half a turn, the moving arm points exactly OPPOSITE to where it began** — so the two arms together make one straight line.

> [!TIP]
> **How to check an angle with nothing but a piece of paper: THE CORNER OF ANY PAGE IS A RIGHT ANGLE.**
>
> **Hold the corner against the angle.** If the angle is narrower than the corner it is **acute**; if wider, it is **obtuse**; if it fits exactly, it is a **right angle**.
>
> **This works because a page corner is made by two straight cuts meeting squarely** — you do not need a protractor to test one.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Can two acute turns together make another acute turn?**
>
> **SOMETIMES YES, SOMETIMES NO — and that is the interesting part.**
> ```
> a small acute + a small acute → may still be less than a quarter turn → ACUTE
> a large acute + a large acute → goes past a quarter turn → OBTUSE
> ```
> **So "acute plus acute" has no single answer.** It depends on how big each acute turn was — which is a good reminder that angle names describe a **range**, not a fixed size.

## 3.4 Turns in everyday objects

```
DOOR with a hinge — can swing well past a quarter turn
SCISSORS         — open to less than a quarter turn
CLOTHES CLIP     — opens only a little; much less than a quarter turn
TAP              — usually a half turn or more
FILE COVER       — can be opened right back, past a half turn
```

> [!TIP]
> **Why objects have different maximum turns: the turn is limited by what the object is FOR, and by what gets in the way.**
>
> **A door stops when it hits the wall. Scissors stop when the handles meet.** The limit is physical, not mathematical — **and noticing it is a good way to see angles outside a maths book.**

## 3.5 A tool for measuring turns

**Make one from tracing paper:**
```
1. Cut out a CIRCLE from tracing paper.
2. FOLD it to make 8 EQUAL PARTS.
3. Attach a straw at the centre and mark a starting point.
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Now every turn can be read off in EIGHTHS of a full turn:**
> ```
> 1/8 turn  — half of a quarter turn, so a small ACUTE angle
> 2/8 turn  = 1/4 turn — a RIGHT angle
> 3/8 turn  — more than a quarter, less than a half → OBTUSE
> 4/8 turn  = 1/2 turn — a STRAIGHT angle
> 8/8 turn  = a FULL turn
> ```
> **Why 8 parts rather than some other number: eighths let you show quarters AND halves exactly** — 2/8 is a quarter and 4/8 is a half — **while also giving you smaller steps in between.**
>
> **The equivalent fractions of Chapter 2 are doing the work here:** 2/8 = 1/4 and 4/8 = 1/2 are exactly why this tool can measure both.

## Summary

```
A TURN is measured from where you STARTED to where you ENDED UP

TURNS ADD like fractions, because A FULL TURN IS THE WHOLE
   2 half turns  = a full turn         1/2 + 1/2 = 1
   2 quarter turns = a half turn       1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2
   4 quarter turns = a full turn

NAMES — all measured against the RIGHT ANGLE
   ACUTE    less than a quarter turn
   RIGHT    exactly a quarter turn
   OBTUSE   more than a quarter but less than a half turn
   STRAIGHT exactly a half turn — the arms lie in one straight line,
            because the moving arm now points exactly OPPOSITE

TESTING an angle needs no protractor — THE CORNER OF A PAGE IS A RIGHT ANGLE
   narrower than the corner → acute;  wider → obtuse

TWO ACUTE TURNS may make an acute OR an obtuse turn — the names describe
   a RANGE, not one fixed size

A TRACING-PAPER CIRCLE folded into 8 EQUAL PARTS measures turns in eighths
   1/8 acute · 2/8 = 1/4 right · 3/8 obtuse · 4/8 = 1/2 straight · 8/8 full
   eighths work because 2/8 = 1/4 and 4/8 = 1/2 — equivalent fractions again
```
