<!-- ===== Objects and materials ===== -->

What is a **material**? Name five.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Any substance that is used to create an object.**
>
> Examples: paper, wood, cloth, glass, metal, plastic, clay.

---

Can one object be made of more than one material? Give an example.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** A **pen** is made of **plastic, metal and ink**.
>
> Equally, **one material can make many objects** — plastic makes bottles, rulers and chairs.

---

What decides which material is used to make an object?

> [!ANSWER]
> Its **properties** and the **purpose** the object must serve.
>
> A tumbler must **hold water**, so it needs a material capable of that — which rules out cloth and paper.

---

Why can't a tumbler be made of **cloth**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Cloth **cannot hold water** — the water would soak through and leak out.
>
> The material has to have the property the job demands.

---

Why are balls for different sports made of different materials?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because each sport needs different properties — especially **how high the ball bounces**, but also size, texture and hardness.
>
> A cricket ball and a tennis ball are the same idea but very different objects; you can't play tennis with a cricket ball.

---

<!-- ===== Classification ===== -->

What is **classification**, and on what basis is it done?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Arranging objects into groups.** It is done on the basis of a **common property** they share.

---

Why is grouping things useful?

> [!ANSWER]
> It helps us **study and observe patterns** in the properties of things.
>
> Everyday examples: a kitchen stores similar utensils together; a grocer keeps spices in one corner and pulses in another; a chemist arranges medicines by type. Each makes finding and understanding things faster.

---

Two students group the same objects differently — one by colour, one by material. Who is right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both.** They simply chose **different properties** to group by.
>
> Neither grouping is wrong; the useful question is **which property matters for your purpose**.

---

<!-- ===== Lustre ===== -->

What does **lustrous** mean? Which materials are usually lustrous?

> [!ANSWER]
> Having a **shiny surface**.
>
> Lustrous materials are usually **metals** — iron, copper, zinc, aluminium, gold. **Non-lustrous** materials include paper, wood, rubber and jute.

---

An old iron object looks dull. Has it stopped being a metal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Metals can **lose their lustre** because of the effect of **air and moisture** on their surface.
>
> That's why lustre is often visible only on a **freshly cut surface** — cut or polish it and the shine returns.

---

"All that glitters is not gold." What does this mean scientifically?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not everything shiny is a metal.**
>
> Some surfaces are made shiny by **polishing** or **coating** them with thin layers of plastic, wax or other materials — so shininess alone doesn't prove a material is a metal.

---

<!-- ===== Hardness ===== -->

How do you test whether a material is **hard** or **soft**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Press** it with your hand, or try to **scratch** its surface (a metal key works well).
>
> Materials that are **difficult to compress or scratch are hard**; those that compress or scratch easily are **soft**.

---

Is rubber hard or soft?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It depends what you compare it with — hardness is relative.**
>
> Rubber is **harder than sponge** but **softer than iron**.
>
> So "is it hard?" is an incomplete question; the real one is "**harder than what?**"

---

<!-- ===== Transparency ===== -->

Define **transparent, translucent and opaque**, with an example of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Transparent** — things can be seen **clearly** through it (glass, water, air, cellophane).
>
> **Translucent** — objects can be seen but **not clearly** (butter paper, frosted glass).
>
> **Opaque** — you **cannot see through** it at all (wood, cardboard, metals).

---

Sara hides behind a **frosted glass** door and Ghulan behind a **wall**. Who is better hidden, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Ghulan.** A wall is **opaque** — nothing can be seen through it.
>
> Frosted glass is only **translucent**: Sara's shape can still be made out, just not clearly.

---

<!-- ===== Solubility ===== -->

You stir sugar, salt, chalk powder, sand and sawdust into water. Which dissolve?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sugar and salt dissolve** — they disappear completely. They are **soluble** in water.
>
> **Chalk powder, sand and sawdust do not** — they stay visible however long you stir. They are **insoluble**.

---

Do all liquids mix with water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Some liquids mix completely, but others **do not mix and form a separate layer** when left undisturbed — **oil** is the everyday example.

---

Is any **gas** soluble in water? Why does it matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — oxygen dissolves in water.**
>
> **Why it matters:** it is **essential for the survival of animals and plants that live in water** — fish breathe the oxygen dissolved in it, not the oxygen in the water molecule itself.

---

Why is water so important in the working of our body?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **water can dissolve a large number of materials**.
>
> That lets it carry dissolved nutrients around the body and remove dissolved waste.

---

How would you make **ORS** at home, and what is it for?

> [!ANSWER]
> Mix **six teaspoons of sugar and half a teaspoon of common salt in one litre of boiled and cooled water**.
>
> **Oral Rehydration Solution** treats **dehydration** caused by diarrhoea or other illness. Ready-made packets (dissolved in 1 litre of water) are available at primary health centres and in the market.

---

<!-- ===== Mass and volume ===== -->

What is **mass**, and what units measure it?

> [!ANSWER]
> The property that tells how **heavy or light** something is — **more mass means heavier**.
>
> **Mass gives the quantity of matter.** Units: **gram (g)** and **kilogram (kg)**.

---

What is **volume**? Two identical tumblers have water at different levels — what does that show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The space occupied by a material is its volume.**
>
> The tumblers have the **same capacity**, but different water levels mean **different volumes** of water — the one with the lower level holds less.

---

What do "500 mL" and "1 L" on a bottle tell you? What is the SI unit of volume?

> [!ANSWER]
> They state the **volume** of liquid in the bottle.
>
> The **SI unit of volume is the cubic metre (m³)**, and `1 m³ = 1000 L`.

---

Correct these: **`7 kgs`** · **`500 ml`** · **`2m3`**

> [!ANSWER]
> **`7 kg`** — no "s" for plural, no full stop.
>
> **`500 mL`** — lowercase **m**, **capital L**, no space inside the symbol.
>
> **`2 m³`** — superscript 3, and **a space** between the number and the unit.

---

<!-- ===== Matter ===== -->

What is **matter**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Anything that occupies space and has mass.**

---

Which two properties are possessed by **all** materials, and why does that make them special?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mass and volume.**
>
> **Why special:** not every material is shiny, hard, transparent or soluble — those properties vary. But **every single material has mass and occupies space**, which is exactly why those two are used to define **matter**.

---

Is **air** matter? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Air **occupies space** (that's why you can't pour water into an already-full bottle — the air is in the way) and it **has mass**.
>
> Meeting both conditions is all it takes to be matter.

---

What is the difference between **matter** and a **material**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Matter** is the general term for anything with mass and volume.
>
> **Materials are the types of matter used to make objects** — wood, glass, plastic. So every material is matter, but "matter" also covers things we aren't using to build anything.
