<!-- ===== Stars and constellations ===== -->

How do stars produce their light?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Stars shine with their own light** — they produce it themselves, unlike planets and moons, which only reflect the Sun's light.

---

What is a **constellation**? How has the meaning changed?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Originally:** a group of stars forming a **pattern**.
>
> **Currently:** a **defined region of the sky** that often includes such a pattern.
>
> Because the star patterns are the most noticeable feature, the word is still commonly used for the groups of stars themselves.

---

How many constellations are officially listed, and by whom?

> [!ANSWER]
> **88**, defined by the **International Astronomical Union (IAU)** in the early twentieth century — dividing the **entire sky into 88 regions**.
>
> An agreed set was needed because **different cultures drew the boundaries differently**.

---

Why did ancient people identify star patterns at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> The imaginary shapes **helped them recognise stars in the sky**.
>
> More practically, **recognising stars was a useful skill for navigation** — it helped sailors and travellers find directions at sea or on land, **before the magnetic compass existed**. It is **still used in emergencies as a backup method**.

---

Describe the constellation **Orion**, and name the bright star near it.

> [!ANSWER]
> Orion is imagined as a **hunter**, with **three stars in the middle forming his belt**.
>
> Nearby is **Canis Major** (his dog), which contains **Sirius — the brightest star in the night sky**. Some imagine Orion and his dog battling a bull, the constellation **Taurus**.

---

Give the Indian names for: the **Big Dipper** · the **Pole Star** · **Betelgeuse** · the **Pleiades**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Big Dipper → **Saptaṛiṣhi** · Pole Star → **Dhruva tārā** · Betelgeuse → **Ārdrā** · Pleiades → **Kṛittikā**.
>
> (Aldebaran in Taurus is **Rohiṇī**. In Indian astronomy the term **nakṣhatra** denotes a star or group of stars.)

---

Which constellations contain the **Big Dipper** and the **Little Dipper**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Big Dipper → **Ursa Major** · Little Dipper → **Ursa Minor** (which contains the **Pole Star**).

---

Give two different Indian community stories about the **Big Dipper**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Tribes in **Central India** see the four stars that form a rectangle as a **"grandmother's cot"**, with the other three stars as **three thieves stealing it**.
>
> Fishermen along the **Konkan coast** see the four stars as a **boat**, with the last three as **the neck of the boat**.

---

<!-- ===== Sky watching ===== -->

What is **light pollution**, and how does it affect star gazing?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The presence of excessive artificial light at night time.**
>
> In a big city it means **only a few stars** can be seen; in villages and areas with less light pollution, **far more stars** are visible. Smoke, dust, tall buildings and trees also block the view.

---

Why is the **Pole Star not visible** from Australia?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **the Pole Star is not visible from the Southern Hemisphere** at all.
>
> **Not all stars and constellations are visible from all places on Earth**, or on all nights of the year.

---

List four preparations for a night sky watching session.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** Choose a **dark, open area** away from lights, buildings and trees — **with an adult**. **2.** Pick a night with **no Moon and no clouds**. **3.** **Wait about half an hour** for your eyes to adjust to the darkness. **4.** Carry a **sky-map app or printed constellation images**, a **magnetic compass** and a notebook.

---

Why must you **wait half an hour** in the dark before observing?

> [!ANSWER]
> So your **eyes get adjusted to the darkness**, which lets you **see the night sky much better**.
>
> Coming straight from bright light, you'd miss all the fainter stars — including the Pole Star, which is not very bright.

---

How do you **find the Pole Star** using the Big Dipper?

> [!ANSWER]
> Take the **two stars at the end of the Big Dipper's cup** and imagine a **straight line through them towards the north**.
>
> At about **five times the distance between those two stars**, the line reaches a **not-very-bright star** — that is the **Pole Star**.

---

When and how do you find **Orion** and then **Sirius**?

> [!ANSWER]
> In India, Orion is best viewed from **December to April**, after sunset.
>
> Look for the **three bright stars in a short straight line** (the belt) — the easiest way to spot Orion.
>
> Then imagine a **straight line through those three stars towards the east**, and it leads to **Sirius**.

---

<!-- ===== The Sun ===== -->

What is the Sun, and how big and far away is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The Sun is a star — the star closest to us** — an extremely hot **spherical ball of gases** giving out a huge amount of energy.
>
> It is about **100 times bigger than the Earth in diameter** and about **150 million km** away.

---

Why does the Sun look so much bigger than other stars?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it is **much closer** to us.
>
> Other stars are **so far away that they appear as points**, even though **some of them are much bigger than our Sun**.

---

Why can't we see other stars during the **day**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because of the **extreme brightness of the Sun**, which overwhelms their faint light.
>
> The stars are still there — they just can't be picked out against the bright sky.

---

What is an **astronomical unit**? How far is the nearest star after the Sun?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1 astronomical unit (au)** is approximately the **distance between the Sun and the Earth**.
>
> **Proxima Centauri**, the nearest star after the Sun, is about **269,000 au** away — 269,000 times our distance to the Sun.

---

<!-- ===== Planets ===== -->

What is **revolution**? Define a **planet**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Revolution** is the **movement of an object around the Sun**.
>
> A **planet** is a **large, nearly spherical object that revolves around the Sun**.

---

Name the eight planets in order of increasing distance from the Sun.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.**

---

Compare the **inner four** planets with the **outer four**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Inner four (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars):** **smaller**, with **solid surfaces** and rocks on them.
>
> **Outer four (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune):** **much larger** than Earth, mostly made of **gas and ice**, with **large flat ring-like structures** of dust particles and rocky material.

---

Why is Mars called the **Red Planet** and Earth the **Blue Planet**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mars** appears red because **the soil on Mars is reddish** in colour.
>
> **Earth** appears blue from space because **a large portion of its surface is covered with water**.

---

Venus is called the Morning Star and Evening Star. What's wrong with that name?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Venus is not a star at all — it's a planet.**
>
> It earns the name because it is **seen shining brightly at dawn and dusk**. After the Sun and the Moon, it is the **brightest object in the sky**.

---

**Venus is farther from the Sun than Mercury, yet hotter.** Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> In general, **planets get most of their energy from the Sun, so the farther away, the colder**.
>
> But **the presence of an atmosphere can trap heat**, significantly changing a planet's temperature. Venus's atmosphere traps enough heat to make it **hotter than Mercury** despite being farther away.

---

Why is **Pluto** no longer called a planet?

> [!ANSWER]
> When discovered it was called a planet, but **later, more similar small objects were found**.
>
> So in **2006 the IAU redefined the requirements** for an object to be a planet — and under that definition Pluto and the other small objects are **dwarf planets**. (Pluto is smaller than Earth's Moon.)

---

How can you tell a **planet** from a **star** with the naked eye?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Stars appear to twinkle a lot; planets do not.**
>
> Both look like shining dots — Mercury, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are all visible to the naked eye — so twinkling is the practical test.

---

Where and when should you look for **Venus**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **At dawn**, look near the **Eastern** direction **before sunrise**.
>
> **At dusk**, look near the **Western** direction **after sunset**.

---

<!-- ===== Moon and satellites ===== -->

What is a **satellite**? What are moons?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Objects that move around planets** — they are **smaller than planets**.
>
> **Moons are the natural satellites of planets.** Earth has **1**, Mars has **2**, while Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune have **large numbers**.

---

Give the Moon's distance, revolution time and size relative to Earth.

> [!ANSWER]
> About **384,000 km** away — our **nearest neighbour in space**.
>
> Takes about **27 days** for one revolution around the Earth.
>
> About **a quarter the size of the Earth** in diameter.

---

What are **craters**, how did they form, and why do they last so long?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Circular bowl-like structures** on the Moon's surface, mostly formed by the **impact of asteroids or rocks from space**.
>
> **Why they last:** the Moon has **hardly any atmosphere, no water and no life** — so there is **nothing to erode or wash them away**, and the features stay for a very long time.
>
> On Earth, wind and water would have worn them down long ago.

---

Summarise India's **Chandrayaan** missions.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Chandrayaan-1 (2008)**, **Chandrayaan-2 (2019)**, and **Chandrayaan-3 (launched July 2023)**, whose **Vikram lander carrying the Pragyan rover soft-landed on 23 August 2023**.
>
> With that, **India became the first country in the world to land near the Moon's little-explored south pole**. **23 August** is now celebrated as **National Space Day**.
>
> **Chandrayaan-4** is planned, aiming to **bring back soil and rock samples**.

---

<!-- ===== Asteroids and comets ===== -->

What are **asteroids**, and where is the **asteroid belt**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Small objects of the Solar System that are **rocky and irregular in shape** (about **10 m to 500 km** across).
>
> Many revolve around the Sun in paths **between those of Mars and Jupiter** — that region is the **asteroid belt**. Some asteroids occasionally pass very close to Earth.

---

What are **comets** made of, and how does a comet get its **tail**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Dust, gases, rocks and ice.**
>
> **As a comet approaches close to the Sun, the frozen material in it starts evaporating — and that evaporating material forms the tail.**
>
> That's why comets grow **dim as they move away from the Sun** and can no longer be seen with the naked eye: without the Sun's heat there is nothing to evaporate.
