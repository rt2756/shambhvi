<!-- ===== Natural & artificial ===== -->

What are **lodestones**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Naturally occurring magnets**, discovered in ancient times.
>
> The magnets used by early sailors were based on them. Magnets in pencil boxes, toys and laboratories today are **artificial magnets**, made by people.

---

Name three shapes magnets commonly come in.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Bar magnet, U-shaped magnet, ring magnet.**

---

<!-- ===== Materials ===== -->

What are **magnetic** and **non-magnetic** materials? Name three magnetic ones.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Magnetic materials are attracted towards a magnet; non-magnetic materials are not.**
>
> Magnetic: **iron, nickel and cobalt** (and some of their combinations with other metals).
>
> Non-magnetic: wood, rubber, plastic, glass, paper.

---

A copper coin is not attracted to a magnet. Does that mean copper isn't a metal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — copper is a metal, just not a magnetic one.**
>
> **"Metal" and "magnetic" are different properties.** Only iron, nickel, cobalt and some of their combinations are magnetic; copper, aluminium, gold and silver are not.

---

<!-- ===== Poles ===== -->

Iron filings are sprinkled on paper and a bar magnet placed on them. What do you see?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Most of the filings stick near the two ends** of the magnet, with very few along the middle.
>
> Those ends are the **poles** — the **North pole** and the **South pole** — and attraction is **strongest there**.

---

Is this true only for bar magnets?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Most iron filings stick to the **poles of a magnet of any shape** — bar, U-shaped or ring.

---

A bar magnet is broken in half. How many poles does each piece have?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Each piece still has two — a North and a South pole.**
>
> **A single pole cannot exist.** However small you break the pieces, North and South always exist **in pairs**.

---

<!-- ===== Direction ===== -->

A bar magnet is suspended freely by a thread and allowed to settle. Which way does it point, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> It comes to rest along the **north–south direction**, every time.
>
> **Why: the Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet**, and the suspended magnet lines up with it.

---

The same experiment is done with a plain **iron bar**. What happens, and what test does this give you?

> [!ANSWER]
> The iron bar can come to rest in **any direction** — it does not settle north–south.
>
> **The test:** suspend a piece of metal freely. **If it always settles north–south, it is a magnet**; if it rests in any direction, it isn't.

---

Which end of a magnet is called the **North pole**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The end that **points towards the north** when the magnet is freely suspended — the **North-seeking pole**.
>
> The other end, pointing south, is the **South-seeking pole (South pole)**.

---

<!-- ===== Compass ===== -->

Describe a **magnetic compass**.

> [!ANSWER]
> A small **circular box with a transparent cover**, containing a **needle-shaped magnet mounted on a pin** so that it can **rotate freely**, above a **dial marked with directions**.
>
> The end of the needle that rests towards north is usually **painted red**.

---

How do you **use** a compass to find directions?

> [!ANSWER]
> Place it where you want to know the directions and **wait for the needle to come to rest** along north–south.
>
> Then **gently rotate the box** until the **N and S marked on the dial line up with the needle**. All directions at that place now read correctly off the dial.

---

How do you turn an iron sewing needle into a magnet?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Stroke it with one pole of a magnet, 30–40 times, always in the same direction** — moving the magnet along the needle's length, **lifting it away** at the end of each stroke and starting again from the same end.
>
> **Test it:** bring iron filings or steel pins near. If they're attracted, the needle has become a magnet.

---

Why must you **lift the magnet away** at the end of each stroke instead of sliding it back?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because stroking **back and forth would undo** what the forward stroke achieved.
>
> Only repeated strokes in the **same direction with the same pole** build the magnetism up; a return stroke works against it.

---

How would you turn that magnetised needle into a working compass?

> [!ANSWER]
> Push the needle **horizontally through a cork**, then **float the cork in a bowl of water** so the needle stays above the water.
>
> Being free to turn, it settles along **north–south**. Rotate the cork and it returns to the same direction each time.

---

What was the **matsya-yantra**?

> [!ANSWER]
> An early Indian navigation device — a **magnetised fish-shaped iron piece kept in a vessel of oil**.
>
> It was used at sea long before the modern compass, and works on **exactly the same principle** as the floating-needle compass: a magnet free to turn settles north–south.

---

<!-- ===== Attraction & repulsion ===== -->

State the rule for how two magnets interact.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Unlike poles attract; like poles repel.**
>
> North–South attract. North–North, or South–South, repel.

---

You have two identical-looking bars. One is a magnet, one is plain iron. How can you tell **for certain** which is which?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Look for repulsion, not attraction.**
>
> **Why:** a plain iron bar is **attracted by both poles** of a magnet, so attraction proves nothing. But **only a magnet is pushed away**, and only by a like pole.
>
> **If the two objects repel each other, both must be magnets.** **Repulsion is the sure test for a magnet.**

---

The North pole of a bar magnet is brought near the North pole of a compass needle. What happens? And with the South pole?

> [!ANSWER]
> **North near North → the needle moves away** (like poles repel).
>
> **South near North → the needle moves closer** (unlike poles attract).

---

<!-- ===== Through materials ===== -->

A sheet of wood is placed between a magnet and a compass needle. What happens to the needle's deflection?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No appreciable change** — the needle deflects just as before.
>
> The same is true for **cardboard, plastic and glass**.

---

What does that experiment show, and where do you see it in daily life?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The magnetic effect can act through non-magnetic materials.**
>
> Everyday examples: a magnet holds a **pencil box or purse shut through its plastic lid**, a **fridge magnet** holds paper against the door, and a **magnetic duster** works on a writing board.
