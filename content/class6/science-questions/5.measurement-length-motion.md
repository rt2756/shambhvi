<!-- ===== Why standard units ===== -->

Five friends measure the same table in handspans and get different answers. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **their handspans are different sizes**.
>
> Body-part units — handspan, foot, fist, finger — **differ from person to person**, so the same table gives a different number for each person.

---

What are the **two parts** of any measurement?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **number** and a **unit**.
>
> "13 handspans" is the number **13** and the unit **handspan**. A number alone means nothing without the unit.

---

Why did the world adopt **standard units**?

> [!ANSWER]
> So that **measurements of the same length made by different people do not differ**.
>
> As people began travelling between places, the many different local systems caused confusion — so countries came together and adopted the **International System of Units (SI units)**.

---

Name two units of length used in ancient India.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Angula** (finger width) and **yojana** — also **dhanusa** and multiples of angula.
>
> They were used for artefacts, architecture and town planning, and the **angula is still used by traditional carpenters and tailors**. Objects with ruled markings that may be scales have been excavated from **Harappan Civilisation** sites.

---

<!-- ===== SI units ===== -->

What is the **SI unit of length** and its symbol? Give the three conversions.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **metre**, symbol **`m`**.
>
> `1 km = 1000 m` · `1 m = 100 cm` · `1 cm = 10 mm`

---

Which unit would you use for **the length of a railway track between two cities**, and for **the thickness of a page**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Railway track → **kilometres** (metres would give an awkwardly huge number).
>
> Thickness of a page → **millimetres** (metres would give a tiny fraction).
>
> Pick the unit that gives a **convenient-sized number**.

---

What is the smallest length you can measure with a **15 cm scale**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1 mm** — the smallest division marked on it.
>
> Each 1 cm section is divided into 10 equal parts, and `1 mm = 0.1 cm`.

---

Correct these: **`15 Cms.`** · **`9.4cm`** · **`5 Kms`**

> [!ANSWER]
> **`15 cm`** — lowercase, no "s" for plural, no full stop.
>
> **`9.4 cm`** — leave a **space** between the number and the unit.
>
> **`5 km`** — lowercase and no "s".

---

<!-- ===== Correct measuring ===== -->

Why can't you measure the **girth of a tree** with a metre scale?

> [!ANSWER]
> A metre scale is **rigid** and can't wrap around a curved surface.
>
> Use a **flexible measuring tape** (like a tailor's tape) instead — that's exactly what flexible measuring devices are for.

---

Where should your **eye** be when reading a scale, and why does it matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Directly above the point being read.**
>
> Looking from an **angle** makes the mark appear to line up with the wrong point, giving a **wrong reading**.

---

The zero end of your scale is **broken**. Can you still use it? An object runs from the 1.0 cm mark to the 10.4 cm mark.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Line one end of the object up with any clear **full mark**, then **subtract** that reading from the reading at the other end.
>
> `10.4 cm − 1.0 cm = ` **9.4 cm**

---

How do **visually challenged** students measure length?

> [!ANSWER]
> With **scales having raised markings** that can be felt by touching them.

---

How would you measure the length of a **curved** line, such as string lights along an arch?

> [!ANSWER]
> Use a **flexible measuring tape** — or lay a **thread** along the curve, mark the length, then **straighten the thread** and measure it with a metre scale.
>
> A rigid scale can't follow the curve, but a thread can — and a straightened thread is easy to measure.

---

<!-- ===== Reference point ===== -->

Deepa's friends disagree about whether the garden or the school is closer. Who is right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **All of them.** Each is measuring the distance **from their own house**, and their houses are in different places.
>
> If they all measured from the **same** point — say the bus stand — their answers would agree.

---

What is a **reference point**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A fixed object or point with respect to which a distance is stated.**
>
> Without agreeing on one, two people describing the same situation can give completely different — and both correct — answers.

---

A kilometre stone reads "Delhi 70 km", then the next reads "Delhi 60 km". What is happening, and what is the reference point?

> [!ANSWER]
> Padma's **position is changing** — she is getting **closer to Delhi**.
>
> The **reference point is Delhi**: each stone states her distance from it.

---

<!-- ===== Motion and rest ===== -->

When is an object said to be **in motion**? When is it **at rest**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In motion:** if its **position changes with respect to a reference point with time**.
>
> **At rest:** if its position does **not** change with respect to that reference point with time.

---

Deepa is on a moving bus. Are the other passengers moving or at rest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both answers are correct — it depends on the reference point.**
>
> Taking **the bus** as the reference point, the passengers are **at rest** — their positions don't change relative to it.
>
> Taking **a building outside** as the reference point, the passengers are **in motion** along with the bus.

---

Why must you always state a reference point before saying something is moving?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **motion is relative**. The same object can be truthfully described as moving *and* at rest, depending on what you compare it to.
>
> "Is it moving?" is an incomplete question — the full question is "**moving with respect to what?**"

---

<!-- ===== Types of motion ===== -->

Name the three types of motion with an example of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Linear** — motion along a **straight line** (a dropped eraser, a pushed box, a march-past).
>
> **Circular** — motion along a **circular path** (a merry-go-round, an eraser whirled on a thread).
>
> **Oscillatory** — motion **to and fro** about a fixed position (a swing, a pendulum).

---

An eraser tied to a thread is **whirled around**. What kind of motion is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Circular motion** — it moves along a circular path, just like a merry-go-round.

---

An eraser is hung on a thread, pulled to one side and released. What kind of motion is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Oscillatory motion** — it moves **to and fro** about its fixed hanging position, like a swing.

---

A metal strip is clamped at one end, and the free end is pressed and released. Classify the motion.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Oscillatory** — the free end moves **up and down** about its rest position.
>
> Oscillatory motion doesn't have to be side to side; any repeated to-and-fro about a fixed position counts.

---

What is **periodic motion**? Which of the three types are periodic?

> [!ANSWER]
> Motion that **repeats its path after a fixed interval of time**.
>
> **Both circular and oscillatory motion are periodic** — the circular one traces the same path round and round, and the oscillatory one repeats its to-and-fro.
>
> Linear motion in a straight line is **not** periodic; it doesn't come back.

---

Classify each: a **swing** · a **ball rolling straight down a slope** · the **hands of a clock**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Swing → oscillatory** (to and fro about a fixed point).
>
> **Ball down a slope → linear** (along a straight line).
>
> **Clock hands → circular** (round a fixed centre) — and also **periodic**, repeating every 12 hours.
