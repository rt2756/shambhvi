<!-- ===== General ===== -->

What is a **mixture**? What does every separation method rely on?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two or more substances mixed together.**
>
> Every method works by exploiting a **difference between the components** — in size, weight, solubility or some other property. Find the difference and you have your method.

---

<!-- ===== Handpicking ===== -->

What is **handpicking**, and on what differences does it work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Picking components out of a mixture by hand** — such as small stones and husk from wheat or rice.
>
> It works on differences in **size, colour and shape**.

---

When is handpicking a **good** choice, and when is it useless?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Good** when the particles to be removed are **present in small quantities** and can be easily picked up by hand.
>
> **Useless** when there is a huge amount to remove — handpicking husk from a whole harvest of grain would take forever.

---

<!-- ===== Threshing and winnowing ===== -->

What is **threshing**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The process of **separating grains from the harvested stalks** — traditionally by **beating the stalks** on a large wooden log.

---

Define **winnowing** and explain how it works.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Separating the heavier and lighter components of a mixture by wind or by blowing air.**
>
> Threshed grain is dropped from a **soop (bamboo tray)** into the wind: the **light husk is blown away** to one side, while the **heavier grains fall straight down** in a pile.

---

You rub roasted peanuts between your palms, then blow. What happens and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **light skins blow away**, while the **heavier peanuts stay**.
>
> This is **winnowing on a small scale** — blowing air separates the heavier and lighter components.

---

Valli cannot separate husk from rice in a **closed room**. Why, and what would you suggest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Winnowing needs moving air** to carry the light husk away, and a closed room has none.
>
> **Suggestions:** do it outdoors on a breezy day, stand in front of a **fan**, or **blow** gently across the tray yourself.

---

What does a modern **thresher** machine do?

> [!ANSWER]
> It performs **threshing and winnowing simultaneously** — separating grains from the stalks **and** from the husk in one operation.

---

<!-- ===== Sieving ===== -->

What is **sieving** used for, and what property does it use?

> [!ANSWER]
> Separating the components of a **solid–solid mixture whose particles differ in size**.
>
> **Fine particles pass through the holes** of the sieve; **bigger particles stay on top** — as when flour passes through while bran and small stones remain.

---

Would sieving work if the holes were **larger than every particle** in the mixture?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No** — everything would fall straight through and nothing would be separated.
>
> **The hole size must lie between the sizes of the two components**: small enough to stop one, large enough to pass the other.

---

Where else, outside the kitchen, is sieving used?

> [!ANSWER]
> At **construction sites**, to separate **pebbles and stones from sand**.

---

<!-- ===== Sedimentation and decantation ===== -->

Define **sedimentation** and **decantation**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sedimentation** — the **settling down of a heavier insoluble component** at the bottom of a liquid.
>
> **Decantation** — **removing the liquid by gently tilting or pouring** it off, leaving the settled solid behind.

---

Dada has no strainer. How can he still get most of the tea leaves out?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Leave the pan undisturbed for some time** so the tea leaves **settle at the bottom** (sedimentation), then **gently pour** the tea into the cup (decantation).

---

Why is decantation not a **proper** method of separation here?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it **does not separate completely** — a few tea leaves still get poured out with the tea.
>
> For a complete separation you need **filtration**.

---

Give two everyday uses of decantation.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Washing and cleaning rice and pulses** — the water is poured off, leaving the grains.
>
> **Separating oil from water** — oil doesn't mix and forms a separate layer that can be poured off.

---

<!-- ===== Filtration ===== -->

What is **filtration**? Name the two things it produces.

> [!ANSWER]
> Passing a mixture through a **filter whose pores are too small for the solid to pass**.
>
> **Residue** — what stays on the filter. **Filtrate** — the liquid that comes through.

---

Why does a piece of **cloth** work as a filter?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because there are **very small holes or pores between the woven threads**.
>
> Particles bigger than the pores are held back while the water passes through. People in ancient times used this same practice.

---

Malli tries to filter **muddy water** with a **tea strainer**. Will it work? What should he use?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** A tea strainer's holes are big enough to stop tea leaves but **far too large for mud particles**, which will pass straight through.
>
> He should use a **cloth**, or better still a **filter paper**, which has **very fine pores**.

---

What decides which filter you should use?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The size of the particles to be removed.**
>
> The filter's pores must be **smaller than the particles** you want to catch. That's why a strainer, a cloth and a filter paper each suit different jobs.

---

Name three materials other than filter paper that can be used as filters.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Cotton, charcoal and sand.**

---

<!-- ===== Evaporation ===== -->

Why can't **filtration** separate salt from salt water?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the salt is **dissolved** — it has broken up completely into the water and is no longer present as particles.
>
> There is nothing for a filter to catch; the salt passes through with the water. **Evaporation** is needed instead.

---

How is **common salt obtained from seawater**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Seawater — a mixture of salts and other substances **dissolved** in water — is kept in **shallow pits exposed to sunlight and air**.
>
> Over a few days the **water evaporates completely**, leaving the solid mixture behind. Common salt is then obtained from it by **further purification**.

---

Why are the pits **shallow** and left in **sunlight and air**?

> [!ANSWER]
> All three speed up **evaporation**: shallow pits give a **large exposed surface area**, sunlight raises the **temperature**, and open air allows **moving air** to carry the vapour away.
>
> The same three factors from the states-of-water chapter, put to practical use.

---

Salt solution is boiled in a china dish. What is left, and what happened to the water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The salt is left behind** in the dish — you can feel it by touch.
>
> The **water evaporated** away as water vapour.

---

Why do **white patches** appear on dark clothes after sweating in summer?

> [!ANSWER]
> Sweat contains **dissolved salt**. When the **water in the sweat evaporates**, the **salt is left behind** on the cloth as white patches.
>
> It's the seawater-to-salt process happening on your shirt.

---

Which method would you choose for each: **(a)** stones in a small bowl of rice · **(b)** husk from a large heap of grain · **(c)** bran from flour · **(d)** mud from water · **(e)** salt from salt water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a) Handpicking** — few stones, easily picked out.
>
> **(b) Winnowing** — the husk is much lighter and can be blown away.
>
> **(c) Sieving** — a solid–solid mixture with different particle sizes.
>
> **(d) Filtration** — mud is insoluble; a fine filter catches it. (Sedimentation and decantation would remove most of it first.)
>
> **(e) Evaporation** — the salt is dissolved, so only evaporating the water recovers it.
