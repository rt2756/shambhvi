<!-- ===== Same substance ===== -->

Thirav says ice and water must be **different substances** because ice is hard and water flows. Is he right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Leave an ice cube on a table and it **turns into water**; put water in a freezer and it turns into ice.
>
> They are **two states of the same substance** — the substance hasn't changed, only its state.

---

<!-- ===== The three states ===== -->

Compare **ice, water and water vapour** for shape, flow and spreading.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Ice (solid):** keeps its **own shape**, does **not** flow, does **not** spread.
>
> **Water (liquid):** takes the **shape of its container**, **flows** and **spreads** over a surface — but its **volume stays constant**.
>
> **Water vapour (gas):** **no fixed shape**, and **spreads out into the entire available space**.

---

Does water vapour exist in this room right now?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** **Water vapour exists even at room temperature** and is present in the air around us — it is simply **invisible**.
>
> Every puddle that dries and every wet floor that is mopped adds to it.

---

Why can you smell food cooking without entering the kitchen?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the smell **spreads through the air** and reaches your nostrils.
>
> **Gases spread out into the entire space available** to them — which is exactly why the smell doesn't stay put in the kitchen.

---

Name a substance other than water that shows all three states.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wax** (a candle) — also **oil** and **ghee**.
>
> Wax is solid, melts to a liquid when heated, and can be turned back to solid by cooling.

---

<!-- ===== Evaporation ===== -->

Define **evaporation** and give three everyday examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The conversion of water into its vapour state.**
>
> Examples: **drying of wet clothes**, a **mopped floor** drying, **sweat** drying on the body, puddles disappearing, sanitiser disappearing from your hands.

---

Does evaporation only happen when water is heated?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — evaporation takes place continuously, even at room temperature.**
>
> That's why clothes dry on an ordinary day and a puddle disappears without anyone boiling it.

---

A spoonful of water on a steel plate disappears. Has it seeped through the plate?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Watch the underside — **nothing comes through**.
>
> The water was **converted into water vapour** — it evaporated.

---

Is the "steam" you see above a hot pan actually water vapour?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Partly.** **Water vapour itself is invisible.**
>
> What you can see is the part that has already **condensed into tiny water droplets** — those droplets are what make the steam visible.

---

<!-- ===== Factors ===== -->

The same amount of water is put in a **bottle cap** and in a **flat plate**. Which evaporates first, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The plate.**
>
> Spreading the water out gives it a **larger area exposed to air**, and **more exposed surface area means faster evaporation**.

---

Why do clothes dry faster on a **sunny** day and on a **windy** day?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sunny:** higher **temperature** speeds up evaporation.
>
> **Windy:** increased **movement of air** carries the vapour away, so evaporation is faster.

---

Why do clothes dry **slowly** on a rainy day?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the air is already **humid** — it holds a lot of **water vapour**.
>
> **If the amount of water vapour in the air is already high, water evaporates slowly.** There's less room, so to speak, for more.

---

What is **humidity**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The amount of water vapour in the air.**
>
> Rainy days are **more humid**, which is exactly why washing takes longer to dry then.

---

You must dry clothes indoors on a rainy day. Using what you know, how would you speed it up?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Spread them out** (more exposed surface area), put them in a **warm** place, and use a **fan** to keep air moving over them.
>
> Each one targets a different factor: area, temperature, and air movement.

---

<!-- ===== Condensation ===== -->

Define **condensation**. When does it happen?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The conversion of water vapour into its liquid state.**
>
> It happens when **water vapour in the air comes into contact with a cold surface**.

---

Why do water droplets appear on the outside of a glass of iced water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Water vapour from the air touches the cold outer surface** of the glass and **condenses** into droplets.
>
> Small droplets deposit first and then **combine into bigger drops**.

---

Why are dew drops seen more in the **morning**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Overnight, surfaces such as leaves become **cold**. Water vapour in the air touching them **condenses** into dew.
>
> By later in the day the surfaces have warmed up, so condensation stops and the dew evaporates.

---

A covered glass of iced water is kept on a **digital weighing balance** for 30 minutes. What happens to the reading, and what does that prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The mass increases**, and the water level inside does **not** drop.
>
> **What it proves:** the water on the outside came **from the air** by condensation — it did **not** seep out of the glass.
>
> **Why the reasoning works:** if water were merely **seeping out**, the total mass would stay the **same** (the water just moved). Mass **going up** means water arrived from **outside**.

---

How does an **Atmospheric Water Generator** produce drinking water?

> [!ANSWER]
> It **cools humid air** so the **water vapour in it condenses** into liquid water.
>
> It's the glass-of-iced-water effect put to work on a large scale.

---

<!-- ===== Melting and freezing ===== -->

Define **melting** and **freezing**, and say what must be done in each case.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Melting** — conversion of a **solid into a liquid**; you must **supply heat**.
>
> **Freezing** — conversion of a **liquid into a solid**; you must **cool** it.

---

Name the change of state in each: **ice → water · water → water vapour · water vapour → water · water → ice**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Ice → water: melting** · **water → water vapour: evaporation** · **water vapour → water: condensation** · **water → ice: freezing**.

---

Give an example other than water of a substance that melts and freezes in daily life.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Candle wax** — it melts when the candle burns and solidifies again when it cools.
>
> **Coconut oil** is another: liquid in warm weather, but it **turns solid in winter**.

---

<!-- ===== Cooling effect ===== -->

Why does water stay cold in a **matka** but not in a stainless steel pot?

> [!ANSWER]
> Water **seeps through the porous surface** of the earthen pot and **evaporates** — and **evaporation causes cooling**, taking heat from the water left behind.
>
> A stainless steel pot is **not porous**, so no water reaches the outside to evaporate, and no cooling happens.

---

Why do you feel cooler sitting under a **fan**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The moving air makes the **sweat on your skin evaporate faster**, and that **evaporation cools you**.
>
> The fan doesn't cool the air — it speeds up evaporation from *you*.

---

Why is water sprinkled on floors and roofs in summer?

> [!ANSWER]
> The sprinkled water **evaporates**, and evaporation **takes heat away** from the surface — producing a **cooling effect**.

---

Why does hand sanitiser feel **cold** on your hands?

> [!ANSWER]
> It **evaporates quickly** from your skin, and evaporation **draws heat away** from your hand — which you feel as coldness.
