<!-- ===== Touch ===== -->

You hold your right hand in warm water and your left in ice-cold water, then put both into the same tap water. What do you feel?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **right hand feels the water cool**, while the **left hand feels the same water warm**.

---

What does that experiment prove, and why does it happen?

> [!ANSWER]
> **We cannot always rely on our sense of touch** to decide whether a body is hot or cold.
>
> **Why:** touch reports the **change** from whatever your hand was already used to, not the actual hotness. A hand coming from warm water senses a drop; one coming from cold water senses a rise — so the two disagree about identical water.

---

Can you always tell someone has a fever just by touching them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No** — touch is unreliable for exactly the reason above, and the difference between a normal temperature and a mild fever is small.
>
> A **thermometer** is needed to be sure.

---

<!-- ===== Temperature ===== -->

What is **temperature**, and what measures it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A reliable measure of the hotness (or coldness) of a body.** A hotter body has a **higher** temperature.
>
> It is measured with a **thermometer**.

---

Name the two kinds of thermometer and what each is for.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Clinical thermometers** — for measuring **human body temperature**.
>
> **Laboratory thermometers** — for **many other purposes**, over a much wider range.

---

<!-- ===== Scales ===== -->

Name the three temperature scales, their units and symbols. Which is the SI unit?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Celsius** — degree Celsius, **°C** · **Fahrenheit** — degree Fahrenheit, **°F** · **Kelvin** — kelvin, **K**.
>
> **The SI unit of temperature is the kelvin (K).**

---

Convert **37 °C** to the Kelvin scale.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Kelvin = Celsius + 273.15`
>
> `37 + 273.15 = ` **310.15 K**

---

What is 37.0 °C on the Fahrenheit scale? Is that scale still used in science?

> [!ANSWER]
> **98.6 °F.**
>
> The Fahrenheit scale is **no longer used in most scientific studies**.

---

Correct these: **`37°C.`** · **`300 °K`** · **`25 Degree celsius`**

> [!ANSWER]
> **`37 °C`** — leave a **space** between the number and the unit; **no full stop** after the symbol.
>
> **`300 K`** — **no degree sign is written with K**.
>
> **`25 degrees Celsius`** — **degree** is lowercase, **Celsius** is capitalised, and use the plural "degrees" for more than one.

---

<!-- ===== Clinical thermometer ===== -->

Why have **mercury** thermometers been replaced by **digital** ones?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mercury is extremely toxic** and **difficult to dispose of** if the thermometer breaks accidentally.
>
> Digital thermometers **pose no such risk**, and their displayed numbers are **easier to read**.

---

What is a **non-contact (infrared) thermometer**, and why was it valuable during COVID-19?

> [!ANSWER]
> A thermometer that measures temperature **without touching** a person's body, from a distance.
>
> **Why valuable:** no contact means it **reduces the risk of spreading disease** between the many people being checked.

---

List the steps for using a digital clinical thermometer.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** Wash your hands and the tip with soap and water. **2.** **Reset** it. **3.** Place it **under the tongue** and close the mouth. **4.** Wait for the **beep or flash**. **5.** Read the display. **6.** **Clean and dry** the tip again.

---

Why should you **not hold a thermometer by its tip**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The tip is the **sensing end**. Your hand is warm, so holding it there would **add your own body heat** to the reading and give a wrong result.

---

Why must the display be kept **out of the water** when washing it?

> [!ANSWER]
> The digital display and its electronics can be **damaged by water**. Only the **tip** needs cleaning.

---

What is normal body temperature? Your friend measures 36.8 °C — should they worry?

> [!ANSWER]
> **37.0 °C** is taken as normal.
>
> **No, they shouldn't worry.** "Normal" is the **average of a large number of healthy people** — a perfectly healthy person may read slightly above or below it.
>
> Body temperature is influenced by **age, time of day and activity level**.

---

Between what limits does human body temperature normally stay?

> [!ANSWER]
> It does **not normally go below 35 °C or above 42 °C**.

---

Why is the **armpit** used for small children and the elderly, and how does that affect the reading?

> [!ANSWER]
> It is **safer and easier** than placing a thermometer under the tongue for someone who may not hold it properly.
>
> But the armpit reading is about **0.5 °C to 1 °C lower** than the actual body temperature, so that must be allowed for.

---

How was fever detected before thermometers, and why wasn't it reliable?

> [!ANSWER]
> By the **pulse rate**, which fever affects — known in India in olden days.
>
> **Why unreliable:** several **other situations also affect the pulse rate**, so a raised pulse doesn't prove fever.

---

<!-- ===== Laboratory thermometer ===== -->

Describe a laboratory thermometer.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **long, narrow, uniform sealed glass tube** with a **bulb** at one end containing a liquid — usually **alcohol (coloured red so it can be seen easily) or mercury**.
>
> A **Celsius scale** runs along the tube. The liquid column **rises and falls with temperature**, and the mark the top of the column reaches is the reading.

---

Can a **clinical** thermometer measure the temperature of **boiling water** or of **ice**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Those temperatures are **outside its range** — a clinical thermometer only spans the narrow band of human body temperatures.
>
> A **laboratory** thermometer (typical range **−10 °C to 110 °C**) is needed instead.

---

Between 0 °C and 10 °C a thermometer has **10 small divisions**. What is the smallest value it can read?

> [!ANSWER]
> `10 °C ÷ 10 divisions = ` **1 °C** per division.
>
> So the smallest value it can read is **1 °C**.

---

Why should you check the **range and smallest division** before using any thermometer?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because they **differ from one instrument to another**.
>
> Using one whose range doesn't cover what you're measuring gives no reading at all — and not knowing the smallest division means you can't tell how precise your reading is.

---

Why must the bulb **not touch the bottom or sides** of the beaker?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because you would be measuring the temperature of the **glass beaker**, not of the liquid inside it.
>
> The glass may be hotter or cooler than the liquid — for instance if it's sitting on a burner.

---

Why must the temperature be read **while the thermometer is still immersed**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **as soon as you take it out, the liquid column begins to fall** — the thermometer starts adjusting to the cooler air.
>
> Lift it out and the reading you get is no longer the liquid's temperature.

---

Where should your **eye** be when reading a laboratory thermometer?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Directly in line with the level of the liquid column.**
>
> Viewing from above or below makes the column appear to line up with the wrong mark, giving a wrong reading.

---

Water is boiling and being heated continuously. What happens to its temperature? What about melting ice?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both stay constant** — the boiling water's temperature does not rise, and the melting ice's does not either.
>
> **Why:** the heat being supplied goes into **changing the state** (liquid to gas, solid to liquid) rather than into raising the temperature.
