## What data is

**Data = any collection of facts, numbers, measures or observations that conveys information about things.**

Your classmates' favourite colours, everyone's weight, the shoe sizes in a class — all data.

> [!TIP]
> **Not every question needs data collection.** "What is the capital of India?" and "When did India get independence?" have **known, fixed answers** — look them up. "What is the most popular TV show in my class?" and "How much water is wasted in my locality?" **depend on the particular people or place**, so you must go and collect.

## Organising data

A raw list ("Mehnoor – Kabaddi, Anaya – Kabaddi, Densy – Badminton…") is data, but you **can't see the answer in it**. Organising is what turns data into information.

### Tally marks & frequency

Mark `|` for each item, and for the **fifth**, draw a line **across the previous four**: `卌`.

- **Frequency** = how many times a value occurs.
- **Frequency table** = each category with its tally and frequency.

**Why bundle in fives:** you count finished bundles (5, 10, 15…) instead of counting strokes one by one — far faster and much harder to miscount.

> [!WARNING]
> A frequency table tells you **how many** chose each thing, but **not who chose what**. To hand each student their own sweet you'd need the original named list — organising data can **lose** information as well as reveal it.

### Arranging in order

Putting the shoe sizes in **ascending order** (3, 3, 3, 4, 4, … 7) makes the smallest and largest jump out immediately, and makes counting how many wear each size easy — identical values now sit together.

## Pictographs

**A pictograph represents data using pictures of objects.**

- Every pictograph needs a **key (scale)** saying what one symbol stands for — `= 1 student`, `= 10 children`.
- A **half symbol** represents half the key's value.
- Categories can run **horizontally or vertically**; symbols are drawn in each row or column according to that category's frequency.

**Reading one:** 5 symbols with a key of 10 → `5 × 10 = 50`. Two full symbols and a half → `20 + 5 = 25`.

**Choosing the key:** with a lot of data, `1 symbol = 1 item` takes forever to draw. A bigger key (1 symbol = 5, or 10) saves time and space.

> [!WARNING]
> **The trouble with pictographs:** they get awkward when frequencies **aren't neat multiples of the key**. With `1 symbol = 10`, showing 25 needs a half symbol — and showing **33 or 27** can't be done neatly at all.

## Bar graphs

When there's a lot of data, a pictograph is slow to draw. A **bar graph** shows the same information faster.

**The rules:**

- Bars have **uniform width** with **equal spacing** between them.
- The **length or height** of each bar represents the value.
- Bars may be drawn **vertically or horizontally**.
- A **scale** states what one unit length represents.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the width and gaps must be equal:** only the **length** is supposed to carry the information. If bars had different widths, a fat short bar might *look* bigger than a thin tall one, and the picture would mislead.

### Drawing one

1. Draw a **horizontal and a vertical line**.
2. Mark the **categories** along one line, **equally spaced**.
3. **Choose a scale** that fits the paper — the crucial step.
4. Draw each bar to the height its frequency demands.

**Choosing the scale:** with frequencies up to 6, use `1 unit = 1 student`. For runs up to 100, `1 unit = 1 run` would mean marking 100 steps — tedious. Use `1 unit = 10 runs`. For India's population in crores, `1 unit = 10 crores`.

> [!TIP]
> **Pick the scale from the biggest value.** Ask: "how tall would the largest bar be?" If the answer is silly (100 units, or half a unit), change the scale.

### Reading one

- **Longest bar = maximum**, **shortest bar = minimum**.
- To find a value, match the bar's top to the **scale**: 12 units at `1 unit = 100 vehicles` → **1200 vehicles**.
- To combine categories, read each and **add** — traffic in 8–9 (1000) plus 9–10 (800) = **1800 vehicles**.

> [!TIP]
> A graph invites you to ask **why**, not just **what**. Traffic being lightest 6–7 a.m. and heaviest 7–8 a.m. isn't a number to memorise — it's people leaving for school and work. Reading data well means explaining the shape, not just quoting it.
