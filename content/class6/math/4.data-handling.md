## Why learn this?

Suppose your class wants to pick one game for sports day. Thirty children call out thirty answers. Which game wins? You can't tell by listening — you need to **collect** the answers, **organise** them, and **show** them so the answer jumps out. That's this whole chapter: **collect → organise → present → interpret**.

## What is data?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Data is a collection of facts, numbers, measures, observations or other descriptions of things that conveys information about them.**

Your classmates' favourite games, everyone's shoe size, the number of cars passing your gate each hour — all data.

### Do you always need to collect data?

No. Some questions have **one fixed answer** that's already known — you just look it up. Others depend on **particular people at a particular time** — you have to go and ask or count.

| Question | Collect data? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| What is the most popular TV show in my class? | ✔ yes | depends on these classmates now |
| How much water is wasted in my locality? | ✔ yes | depends on this place, must be measured |
| When did India get independence? | ✘ no | fixed fact (1947) — look it up |
| What is the capital of India? | ✘ no | fixed fact (New Delhi) |

## Collecting and organising data

### The messy list

Naresh and Navya ask classmates their favourite game and write it down as they go: "Mehnoor – Kabaddi, Anaya – Kabaddi, Densy – Badminton, …". That's data, but you can't **see** the most popular game in it — you'd have to keep re-counting.

### Tally marks

Go through the list once. For each answer, put a stroke `|` next to that game. For every **fifth** one, draw a line **across** the four before it:

```
|||| (with a slash across)  = 5
```

> [!TIP]
> **Why bundle in fives?** You can count bundles quickly — 5, 10, 15, 20 — and then add the leftover strokes. Counting 23 single strokes is slow and easy to get wrong; counting four bundles and 3 strokes is quick and safe.

### Frequency table

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The **frequency** of a value is **how many times it occurs**. A **frequency table** lists each category with its tally marks and frequency.

| Game | Tally | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Kho-kho | `||||` | 4 |
| Hockey | `|||| |||` (one bundle + 3) | 8 |
| Kabaddi | `||||` slashed + 1 | 6 |

Now the most popular game is obvious: **hockey**, frequency 8 (as in the book).

### What a frequency table loses

Shri Nilesh needs sweets for a class: the table says jalebi 6, barfi 3, gujiya 13, rasgulla 7, gulab jamun 9. Total = 6 + 3 + 13 + 7 + 9 = **38** sweets. Great for **buying**.

But is it enough to **hand each child the right sweet**? **No.** The table only says *how many* chose each sweet, not *who* chose what. For distributing you need the original list of names (or a list grouped by sweet with names).

> [!WARNING]
> Organising data can **throw information away**. Keep the original list if you might need the details later.

### Arranging data in order

Shoe sizes of a class: 4, 5, 3, 7, 5, 4, … Write them in **ascending order** (smallest to largest): 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, …, 7, 7.

Now you can read off at once:
- **smallest** size → at the start (3)
- **largest** size → at the end (7)
- how many wear size 5 → all the 5s sit together, just count them
- how many wear **more than 4** → count everything after the last 4

**Why ordering helps:** equal values end up **next to each other**, so counting them is easy, and the extremes are at the two ends.

## Pictographs

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A pictograph shows data using pictures (symbols).** It always needs a **key** (also called the **scale**) that says what one symbol stands for, e.g. 📘 = 1 book, or 🧍 = 10 students.

### Reading a pictograph

1. Read the **key** first.
2. Count the symbols in the row.
3. **Multiply** by the key. A **half symbol** = half the key.

**Worked example.** Key: 1 symbol = 10 students. A row shows 3 full symbols and a half → 3 × 10 + 5 = **35** students.

**Worked example (book — kites).** Key: 1 kite symbol = 100 kites. Rani bought 300 kites → **3 symbols**. Poonam Ben bought 700 → 7 symbols. Rukhsana says Poonam bought **more than double** Rani's. Check: double of 300 = 600, and 700 > 600 → **she's right**.

**Worked example (book — library).** A pictograph of books borrowed each day: the day with the fewest symbols is the minimum; add all the days for the week's total. The busiest day was Saturday — a sensible reason: children borrow books to read over the Sunday holiday. Always look for a **reason** behind the pattern.

### Choosing a good key

If 1 symbol = 1 item and you have 500 items, you'd draw all day. A **bigger key** saves time and space.

**Worked example (book — Mudhol hounds).** Dogs in six villages: A 18, B 36, C 12, D 48, E 18, F 24.
- Every number is a **multiple of 6**, so a key of **1 symbol = 6 dogs** means no half symbols at all. 
- Village B: 36 ÷ 6 = **6 symbols**. Village D: 48 ÷ 6 = 8 symbols.
- Kamini says B and D together have more dogs than the other four villages together. B + D = 36 + 48 = **84**; A + C + E + F = 18 + 12 + 18 + 24 = **72**. 84 > 72 → **she is right**.

> [!TIP]
> **Choose the key so that the numbers divide by it** (or by half of it). Look for a number that goes into all the frequencies.

> [!WARNING]
> **Pictographs struggle with awkward numbers.** With 1 symbol = 10 students, 35 is fine (3½ symbols), but **33** or **27** can't be shown neatly — you'd need 3.3 or 2.7 of a picture. That's one reason we also use bar graphs.

## Bar graphs

When there's a lot of data, or the numbers are awkward, a **bar graph** is better.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In a bar graph:**
> - each category gets a bar;
> - all bars have the **same width** and **equal gaps** between them;
> - the **length (or height)** of the bar shows the value, measured against a **scale**;
> - bars can be **vertical** or **horizontal**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why must widths and gaps be equal?** Only the **length** is meant to carry information. If one bar were fatter, your eye would see "more area" and think it's bigger, even if it's shorter. Equal widths make sure we compare **just the lengths**.

### Reading a bar graph

**Worked example (traffic).** Scale: 1 unit = 100 vehicles. A bar 12 units tall → 12 × 100 = **1,200** vehicles.
- Longest bar → the busiest hour; shortest → the quietest.
- 8–9 a.m. had 1,000 and 9–10 a.m. had 800 → together **1,800**.
- Why is 7–8 a.m. busiest? People are heading to school and work. A good reader asks **why**, not just **what**.

**Worked example (book — school attendance).** The bar for Class 2 shows 5 absentees. The class with the tallest bar (Class 8) had the most absent. The class with **no bar** (Class 5) had **full attendance** — a missing bar means zero.

**Worked example (book — household spending).** Imran's family spends most on food, then rent. Education ₹800, food ₹3,400. Is education less than one-fourth of food? One-fourth of 3,400 is 3,400 ÷ 4 = 850. 800 < 850 → **yes**.

### Drawing a bar graph — step by step

1. Draw a horizontal line and a vertical line (the **axes**) meeting at 0.
2. Mark the **categories** along one axis, equally spaced.
3. **Choose a scale** for the other axis — the most important step.
4. Draw each bar to the length its value needs.
5. Give the graph a **title** and label both axes.

### Choosing the scale

Look at the **biggest value** and ask how tall its bar would be.

- Frequencies up to 6 → 1 unit = 1 student is fine.
- Runs up to 100 → 1 unit = 1 run means 100 marks — far too many. Use **1 unit = 10 runs** (tallest bar 10 units).
- A survey of 120 students where the biggest group is 45 → **1 unit = 5 students**; 45 ÷ 5 = 9 units.

**Worked example (book — tickets).** Vidisha's bar is 6 units and Jabalpur's is 5 units. Vidisha sold 24 tickets, Jabalpur 20. So 1 unit = 24 ÷ 6 = **4 tickets** (check: 5 × 4 = 20 ✓).

> [!TIP]
> If you know one bar's value and its length, **value ÷ length = scale**. Then every other bar is just length × scale.

### Totals from a frequency table — a common mistake

A table shows how many matches a bowler took 0, 1, 2, … 7 wickets in. To find the **total wickets**, you can't just add 0 + 1 + 2 + … + 7. Why not? If he took 3 wickets in **8** matches, that's 3 × 8 = **24** wickets, not 3.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Total = sum of (value × frequency).** Multiply each value by how many times it happened, then add those products.

**Mini example.** Wickets: 0 in 5 matches, 1 in 4, 2 in 3, 3 in 2. Total = 0×5 + 1×4 + 2×3 + 3×2 = 0 + 4 + 6 + 6 = **16** wickets.

## Artistic choices — and their dangers

### Vertical or horizontal bars?

Pick whichever **matches the real thing**:
- **Heights** (mountains, tallest child in each class) go **upward** → **vertical** bars feel natural.
- **Lengths along the ground** (rivers, distances between cities) → **horizontal** bars.

### Infographics

When a graph is decorated with pictures and colour to make it eye-catching, it's called an **infographic** (information graphic). They're great for grabbing attention — but they can **mislead**.

**Book example:** the seven highest peaks on the seven continents (Everest 8,848 m, Aconcagua 6,962 m, Denali 6,194 m, Kilimanjaro 5,895 m, Elbrus 5,642 m, Vinson Massif 4,892 m, Kosciuszko 2,228 m) drawn as **triangles** instead of bars. Looks lovely — but the taller triangles are also **wider**, which suggests taller mountains are bigger all round. That's extra "information" the data never gave.

In another picture, Everest **looks** about twice as tall as Elbrus. Check: 5,642 × 2 = 11,284, but Everest is only 8,848 m. **The picture lies.**

> [!WARNING]
> When reading or making a fancy graph, **check the numbers**, not just the picture. Equal widths, a clear scale, and honest heights matter more than decoration.

## In a nutshell

- **Data** = facts or observations that give information. Collect it only when the answer isn't already a known fact.
- **Tally marks** (bundles of 5) → **frequency table**. A table shows *how many*, not *who*.
- **Ordering** data puts equal values together and the extremes at the ends.
- **Pictograph:** symbols + **key**; count × key; half symbol = half the key; choose a key that divides the numbers.
- **Bar graph:** equal widths and gaps, length shows value, choose the **scale** from the largest value; scale = value ÷ length.
- **Total from a frequency table** = Σ value × frequency.
- Infographics are attractive but can **mislead** — compare numbers, not pictures.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** forget the key and you misread every pictograph · a half symbol is half the key, not 1 · unequal bar widths mislead · a missing bar means 0 · total wickets ≠ 0 + 1 + 2 + … — multiply by frequencies · a tall triangle isn't a fair bar.
