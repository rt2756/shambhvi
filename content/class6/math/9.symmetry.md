# 9. Symmetry

**A symmetry is a part or parts of a figure repeated in a definite pattern.**

A flower, a butterfly, a rangoli and a pinwheel all have it. A picture of **clouds** does not — nothing repeats in any regular way.

## Line (reflection) symmetry

**A line of symmetry cuts a figure into two parts that overlap exactly when folded along that line** — mirror halves.

- Fold a square **vertically, horizontally,** and along **each diagonal** → all four folds give matching halves. A square has **4 lines of symmetry**.
- A figure with one or more lines of symmetry is said to have **reflection symmetry**.

> [!WARNING]
> **A rectangle's diagonal is NOT a line of symmetry.** Fold a non-square rectangle along its diagonal and the two halves visibly overhang — they don't match. A rectangle has only **2** lines of symmetry (through the middles of opposite sides), not 4.

**Reflection in action:** label a square's corners A, B, C, D. Fold along the vertical line and B, C swap places with A, D — every point lands exactly where its mirror partner was. That swapping *is* the reflection.

### Lines of symmetry to know

| Shape | Lines |
|---|---|
| Equilateral triangle | **3** |
| Isosceles triangle | **1** |
| Scalene triangle | **0** |
| Square | **4** |
| Rectangle | **2** |
| Regular hexagon | **6** |
| **Regular polygon, n sides** | **n** |
| Circle | **infinite** |

> [!TIP]
> **You cannot draw a triangle with exactly 2 lines of symmetry.** Two lines of symmetry would force all three sides equal — which immediately gives you a *third* line. So a triangle has 0, 1 or 3 — never 2.

### Making symmetric shapes

- **Ink blot:** fold paper, drop ink on one half, press and open → the crease is the line of symmetry.
- **Paper folding and cutting:** fold, cut along a line, unfold → the cut is mirrored on both sides of the fold.
- **Punching:** each fold **doubles** the number of holes. One punch through paper folded twice gives **4** holes.

## Rotational symmetry

A **windmill** has no line of symmetry — fold it any way and the halves don't match. But **rotate it 90° about its centre** and it looks exactly the same. That's **rotational symmetry**.

- The fixed point it turns about is the **centre of rotation**.
- An angle that leaves the figure looking exactly the same is an **angle of symmetry**.

**For the windmill:** 90°, 180°, 270° and 360° — **4 angles of symmetry**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **360° is ALWAYS an angle of symmetry**, for every figure — a full turn brings anything back to where it started. So it is always counted, and a figure whose *only* angle of symmetry is 360° is said to have **no rotational symmetry**.

The number of angles of symmetry is the figure's **order of rotational symmetry**.

### The pattern in the angles

| Order | Angles of symmetry |
|---|---|
| 2 | 180°, 360° |
| 3 | 120°, 240°, 360° |
| 4 | 90°, 180°, 270°, 360° |

**Every angle of symmetry is a multiple of the smallest one.**

**Why:** if turning by angle *s* leaves the figure unchanged, doing it twice must too — so 2s works, and 3s, and so on. And the turns must land exactly on 360° to close the circle, which means **the smallest angle divides 360° exactly**.

> [!TIP]
> **If the smallest angle of symmetry is a whole number of degrees, it must be a FACTOR of 360.** That single fact answers a whole family of questions — 45° is possible (`360 ÷ 45 = 8`), but 17° is impossible (`360 ÷ 17` isn't a whole number).

### Figures with radial arms

For **n equally spaced radial arms** to have rotational symmetry, the angle between adjacent arms must be **360° ÷ n**.

- 3 arms → **120°** apart · 4 arms → **90°** · 5 arms → **72°** · 6 arms → **60°**

**Why:** rotating must carry each arm exactly onto the next, so the full turn of 360° has to be shared **equally** among the n gaps.

⚠️ Three arms at *unequal* angles have **no** rotational symmetry — only a full 360° turn restores them.

## The circle — the most symmetric of all

- **Every angle is an angle of symmetry** — turn a circle by any amount about its centre and it coincides with itself.
- **Every diameter is a line of symmetry** — infinitely many.

**Why:** every point on the rim is the same distance from the centre, so no rotation and no reflection through the centre can tell one position from another. The circle is the one figure with **no smallest** angle of symmetry.
