## What is symmetry?

Look at a butterfly. Its left wing is a mirror copy of its right wing. Look at a flower: the same petal repeats again and again around the middle. Look at a rangoli: the same design repeats in each direction. Now look at a cloud: nothing repeats in any regular way.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A figure has symmetry when a part of it is repeated in a definite pattern.**

The book's opening pictures: the **flower** has 6 lines of symmetry, the **rangoli** 4, the **butterfly** 1. The **pinwheel** has no line of symmetry (but it does have the turning kind — see below). The **cloud** has none.

There are two kinds of symmetry in this chapter:
1. **Line (reflection) symmetry** — fold it and the halves match.
2. **Rotational symmetry** — turn it and it looks the same.

## Line symmetry

### The folding test

Cut out a square of paper. Fold it in half so that the two halves lie **exactly** on top of each other. The crease is a **line of symmetry**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A line of symmetry divides a figure into two parts that match exactly when folded along that line** — each half is the **mirror image** of the other. A figure with a line of symmetry has **reflection symmetry**.

**How many ways can you fold a square so the halves match?**
- top to bottom (horizontal line through the middle),
- left to right (vertical line),
- along one diagonal,
- along the other diagonal.

That's **4 lines of symmetry**. There's no fifth way.

### A rectangle is different

Fold a non-square rectangle along its **diagonal**: the corners **stick out** — the halves don't match. So the diagonal is **not** a line of symmetry. A rectangle has only **2** lines of symmetry: the two lines through the middles of opposite sides.

> [!WARNING]
> **A rectangle's diagonal is NOT a line of symmetry** (unless the rectangle is a square). It cuts the rectangle into two equal *pieces*, but they don't land on each other when folded — one is the other turned round, not reflected.

### Reflection moves points

Label a square's corners A (top left), B (top right), C (bottom right), D (bottom left).
- **Reflect in the vertical line:** A ↔ B and D ↔ C swap places.
- **Reflect in the horizontal line:** A ↔ D and B ↔ C swap — D and C go to where A and B were.
- **Reflect in the diagonal AC:** **A and C stay put** (they're on the fold), while **B and D swap**.

**Why points on the line stay put:** folding along a line doesn't move the line itself. Every other point jumps to the spot the same distance away on the other side.

### Lines of symmetry of common shapes

| Shape | Lines of symmetry |
|---|---|
| Equilateral triangle (all sides equal) | **3** |
| Isosceles triangle (two sides equal) | **1** |
| Scalene triangle (no sides equal) | **0** |
| Square | **4** |
| Rectangle (not a square) | **2** |
| Regular pentagon | **5** |
| Regular hexagon | **6** |
| **Regular polygon with n sides** | **n** |
| Circle | **infinitely many** (every line through the centre) |

**Regular polygons (Chapter 1's shape sequence):** triangle 3, square 4, pentagon 5, hexagon 6, … — the lines of symmetry give the **counting numbers from 3**, the same as the number of sides.

**Why n lines for n sides:** for an odd number of sides, each line runs from a corner to the middle of the opposite side — one per corner. For an even number, half the lines join opposite corners and half join the middles of opposite sides — still n in all.

> [!TIP]
> **No triangle has exactly 2 lines of symmetry.** One line of symmetry makes two sides equal. A second line makes another pair equal — and then all three are equal, which brings a **third** line. So a triangle has **0, 1 or 3** lines, never 2.

### Paper folding, cutting and punching

**Punching holes:** fold paper, punch one hole through all the layers, then open it.
- Folded **once** → **2** holes, mirror images across the fold.
- Folded **twice** (in half, then in half again) → **4** holes, symmetric about **both** fold lines.

**Why:** each fold doubles the number of layers, and the punch goes through every layer.

**Working backwards:** if the opened paper has 4 holes arranged symmetrically about a vertical and a horizontal line, it was folded vertically **and** horizontally, then punched once.

**Cutting shapes:** fold a paper in half twice, then cut off the folded corner.
- Cut **straight across** the closed corner at a slant → when opened, a **diamond-shaped hole** (a square turned on its corner) in the middle.
- Cut a small **square notch** out of the closed corner → a **square hole** with sides parallel to the paper's edges.

> [!TIP]
> Before you cut, **predict** the shape: imagine the cut being reflected across every fold line. Then cut and check.

### Completing a figure

On squared paper, half a figure is drawn with a line of symmetry. To complete it, take each corner, count how many squares it is **from the line**, and mark a point the **same number of squares on the other side**. Join the points.

**Why counting squares works:** a reflection puts every point the **same distance** from the line, on the **opposite side**, along a line that crosses the mirror at a right angle.

With **two** lines of symmetry, reflect in one line, then reflect everything (old and new) in the other.

## Rotational symmetry

### The pinwheel

A pinwheel (windmill) has **no** line of symmetry — fold it any way and the blades don't match. But **turn** it a quarter turn (90°) about its centre, and it looks **exactly the same** as before.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A figure has **rotational symmetry** if it looks exactly the same after turning it by **less than a full turn** about a fixed point.
> - The fixed point is the **centre of rotation**.
> - Each turn that makes it look the same is an **angle of symmetry**.
> - The **number** of angles of symmetry (counting 360°) is the **order of rotational symmetry**.

**The pinwheel's angles of symmetry:** 90°, 180°, 270°, 360° → **order 4**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **360° is an angle of symmetry of every figure** — a full turn always brings things back to the start. A figure whose **only** angle of symmetry is 360° is said to have **no rotational symmetry**.

### Common angles

| Figure | Angles of symmetry | Order |
|---|---|---|
| Square | 90°, 180°, 270°, 360° | 4 |
| Rectangle (not square) | 180°, 360° | 2 |
| Equilateral triangle | 120°, 240°, 360° | 3 |
| Regular hexagon | 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°, 360° | 6 |
| Letter S, Z, N | 180°, 360° | 2 |
| Scalene triangle | 360° only | none |

### The key rule: multiples of the smallest angle

Look at the table: every angle of symmetry is a **multiple of the smallest one** (90, 180, 270, 360 are 1, 2, 3, 4 times 90).

**Why:** if turning by the smallest angle *s* leaves the figure looking the same, then turning by *s* **again** also does — so 2s works, and 3s, and so on until you've gone all the way round.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **If the smallest angle of symmetry is a whole number of degrees, it divides 360 exactly — it is a factor of 360.**
> And the order of rotational symmetry = **360° ÷ smallest angle**.

**Worked examples (book):**
- Smallest angle **60°** → the others are 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°, 360°.
- **60°** is an angle of symmetry, and there are **two** angles of symmetry **smaller** than 60°. Then 60° must be the **3rd** multiple of the smallest: smallest = 60 ÷ 3 = **20°**.
- Can the smallest angle be **45°**? 360 ÷ 45 = 8 ✓ → **yes**, order 8.
- Can it be **17°**? 360 ÷ 17 is not a whole number → **no**.

### Radial arms

A figure with **n equal arms**, spread **evenly** around a centre, has smallest angle of symmetry **360° ÷ n**.

- 3 arms → 120° · 4 arms → 90° · **5 arms → 72°** (angles 72°, 144°, 216°, 288°, 360°) · **6 arms → 60°**.
- **7 arms** → 360° ÷ 7 = **51 3/7°** — not a whole number! (7 × 51 = 357, leaving 3, so 51 and 3/7.) That's fine — a figure can still be built; its angle just isn't whole.

**Why even spacing matters:** a turn has to carry **every** arm exactly onto the next one. If the gaps were unequal, some arm would land in a gap.

### Colouring a circle's sectors

A circle split into 12 equal sectors (30° each). Colour them so the picture has:
- **3** angles of symmetry → repeat a pattern of 4 sectors, 3 times (smallest angle 120°).
- **4** angles of symmetry → repeat a pattern of 3 sectors, 4 times (90°).
- Possible orders: any number that divides 12 — **1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12**.

## Both kinds together

Many figures have **both** reflection and rotational symmetry: a square (4 lines, order 4), a regular hexagon (6 lines, order 6), a plus sign.

**Book's sketches:**
- A **triangle** with at least 2 lines and 2 angles of symmetry → only the **equilateral** triangle (3 lines, order 3).
- A triangle with **1** line but no rotational symmetry → an **isosceles** (non-equilateral) triangle.
- A quadrilateral with rotational symmetry but **no** line symmetry → a **parallelogram** that isn't a rectangle or rhombus (order 2: 180°, 360°).
- A quadrilateral with a line of symmetry but **no** rotational symmetry → a **kite** or an **isosceles trapezium** (1 line).

> [!NOTE]
> One kind doesn't need the other. The pinwheel has rotational but no line symmetry; a butterfly has line but no rotational symmetry.

**The new Parliament building** (book). Its outer boundary is a triangle-like shape with **3 lines of symmetry** and rotational symmetry with angles **120°, 240°, 360°**.

**Koch snowflake** (Chapter 1). The first shape, a triangle, has 3 lines; every later snowflake has **6 lines of symmetry**, and angles of symmetry 60°, 120°, …, 360° (order 6).

## The circle — the most symmetric of all

- **Every line through the centre** is a line of symmetry → infinitely many.
- **Every angle** is an angle of symmetry → turn it by any amount about its centre and it looks the same.

**Why:** every point on the rim is the same distance from the centre, so no turn or fold through the centre can tell one position from another. The circle is the only figure with **no smallest** angle of symmetry.

## In a nutshell

- **Symmetry** = a part repeated in a definite pattern.
- **Line symmetry:** fold along the line, halves match. Square 4, rectangle 2 (not the diagonals!), equilateral triangle 3, regular n-gon n, circle infinite. Triangles have 0, 1 or 3 — never 2.
- Reflection: points on the line stay; others jump to the same distance on the other side.
- Each fold **doubles** punched holes.
- **Rotational symmetry:** looks the same after a turn of less than 360°. Angles of symmetry are multiples of the smallest; the smallest (if whole) is a **factor of 360**; order = 360 ÷ smallest.
- n evenly spaced arms → smallest angle 360° ÷ n.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** a rectangle's diagonal is not a line of symmetry · 360° always counts but alone means "no rotational symmetry" · 17° can't be a smallest angle of symmetry · a pinwheel has rotational but no line symmetry · arms must be evenly spaced · regular n-gon has n lines, not 2n.
