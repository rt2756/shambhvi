## Numbers can tell us things

Children stand in a line and each says **how many of their neighbours are taller**.

- **0** — neither neighbour is taller · **1** — one is taller · **2** — both are taller.
- Someone at an **end** has only **one** neighbour, so they can never say 2.
- **Two neighbours can't both say 2**, since one of them must be the taller of the pair.

> [!TIP]
> Puzzles like this are really about **reasoning from constraints**, not calculation. Before trying arrangements at random, ask what each number *forces* to be true.

## Supercells

**A cell is a supercell if its number is greater than all its neighbouring cells** (left and right — and above and below, in a grid).

- The cell holding the **largest number** in a table is **always** a supercell — nothing can beat it.
- The cell holding the **smallest number** can **never** be one — every neighbour beats it.
- A table with no repeats **always has at least one supercell**, because there is always a largest number.

## Patterns in digits

### Digit sums

Add a number's digits: `68 → 6+8 = 14`, and `176 → 1+7+6 = 14` too.

- **Smallest number with digit sum 14:** **59** — to keep a number small, use **as few digits as possible** and put the **small digits first**.
- **Largest 5-digit number with digit sum 14:** **95000** — put the **big digits first** to make it large.

> [!TIP]
> **Consecutive-digit numbers** like 345 have a neat property: their digit sum is **3 × the middle digit** (`3+4+5 = 12 = 3 × 4`). It works because the outer digits are one less and one more than the middle, so the +1 and −1 cancel.

### Counting a digit

From **1 to 100**, the digit **7** appears **20 times** — 10 times in the units place (7, 17, 27, …, 97) and 10 times in the tens place (70–79). Note 77 contributes **twice**.

## Palindromes

**A palindrome reads the same forwards and backwards** — 66, 848, 575, 1111.

**Reverse-and-add:** take a number, add its reverse, and repeat until you get a palindrome.
`68 + 86 = 154` → `154 + 451 = 605` → `605 + 506 = ` **1111** ✓

> [!NOTE]
> **Starting from any 2-digit number, this always reaches a palindrome.** For **3-digit** numbers nobody knows — it's suspected that **196 never** produces one, but this has never been proved.

## Kaprekar's constant — 6174

**D.R. Kaprekar**, a maths teacher in Devlali, Maharashtra, found this in **1949**.

Take any **4-digit number with at least two different digits**:

1. Arrange its digits to make the **largest** number — call it **A**.
2. Arrange them to make the **smallest** number — call it **B**.
3. Work out **C = A − B**, and repeat with C.

**Starting from 6382:**
`8632 − 2368 = 6264` → `6642 − 2466 = 4176` → `7641 − 1467 = ` **6174**

And `7641 − 1467 = 6174` again — it **sticks**. Every valid starting number reaches **6174**, the **Kaprekar constant**.

> [!TIP]
> The 3-digit version settles on **495** in the same way.

## Mental maths & estimation

- **Digits and operations:** think about **how many digits an answer can have** before calculating. Two 5-digit numbers added give a 5- or 6-digit sum — **never** a 7-digit one, since the largest possible is `99999 + 99999 = 199998`.
- **4-digit + 2-digit can never give a 6-digit number** — the biggest is `9999 + 99 = 10098`, only 5 digits.
- **5-digit − 5-digit gives at most a 5-digit answer** — subtracting can only make things smaller.

> [!TIP]
> **Look for structure before adding.** A grid of thirty-two 32s isn't thirty-two additions — it's `32 × 32 = 1024`. Spotting the repeated number turns a long slog into one multiplication.

## The Collatz conjecture — an unsolved mystery

**The rule:** start with any whole number.
- If it's **even**, take **half** of it.
- If it's **odd**, **multiply by 3 and add 1**.
- Repeat.

`12 → 6 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1`
`21 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1`

Every number anyone has ever tried eventually reaches **1**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In **1937** the German mathematician **Lothar Collatz** conjectured that this happens for **every** whole number. Despite many mathematicians working on it, **nobody has been able to prove it — it is still unsolved today.**
>
> A good reminder that mathematics isn't a finished subject. Some questions simple enough for a Class 6 student to understand are still open.
