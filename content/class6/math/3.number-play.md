## What this chapter is about

This chapter isn't about learning a new kind of sum. It's about **thinking with numbers** — spotting what *must* be true, what *can't* be true, and why. Almost every question here can be solved with a little reasoning instead of a lot of calculating. That habit will save you hours in later classes.

## Numbers can tell us things

Some children stand in a line. Each one says **how many of their immediate neighbours (left and right) are taller than them**. So everyone says 0, 1 or 2.

- **0** — no neighbour is taller.
- **1** — exactly one neighbour is taller.
- **2** — both neighbours are taller.

Let's reason through the book's questions.

**Can a child at an end say 2?** No. A child at the end has **only one** neighbour, so at most one person can be taller.

**Can everyone say 0?** Yes — if all the children are the **same height**, nobody is taller than anybody.

**Five children, all different heights: can four say 1 and one say 0?** Yes — stand them **shortest to tallest**. Each child's right-hand neighbour is taller (say 1); the tallest at the end has nobody taller (says 0).

**Can it be 1, 1, 1, 1, 1?** No. With different heights, **the tallest child** has nobody taller next to them, so the tallest *must* say 0.

**Is 0, 1, 2, 1, 0 possible?** Yes — e.g. heights tall, medium, short, medium, tall.

**At most how many can say 2?** With 5 children, at most **2**. A child saying 2 is shorter than both neighbours, so **two "2"-children can't stand next to each other** — one of them would be taller than the other. And the ends can't say 2. So only positions 2 and 4 can say 2.

> [!TIP]
> These puzzles are about **constraints** — facts that force something to be true. Before trying random arrangements, ask "what *must* happen?" (e.g. "the tallest always says 0").

## Supercells

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A cell is a supercell if its number is greater than the numbers in all the cells next to it.** In a single row, neighbours are left and right. In a grid, they are left, right, above and below.

**Worked example.** In the row `6828 | 670 | 9435 | 3780 | 3708 | 7308 | 8000 | 5583 | 52`:
- 6828 > 670 → supercell (it has only one neighbour).
- 9435 > 670 and > 3780 → supercell.
- 8000 > 7308 and > 5583 → supercell.
- 3780 is not (9435 beats it), 5583 is not (8000 beats it).

So the supercells are **6828, 9435, 8000**.

### Facts about supercells — with reasons

1. **The cell with the largest number is ALWAYS a supercell.** Nothing anywhere is bigger, so certainly no neighbour is.
2. **The cell with the smallest number is NEVER a supercell** (with no repeats). Every neighbour is bigger than it.
3. **So every table (without repeats) has at least one supercell.** You can't fill it to have none — the biggest number will always be one.
4. **The second-largest number need not be a supercell** — put it right next to the largest. Example: `1 2 3 4 5 6 7 9 8` — 8 is second largest but 9 is beside it.

### How many supercells can you get?

Two supercells can never sit side by side (one of the pair must be smaller). So at best they **alternate**: super, not, super, not, …

- 9 cells in a row: positions 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 → **5 supercells** at most.
- In general: even number of cells n → n ÷ 2; odd number → (n + 1) ÷ 2.

**Example with 5 supercells:** `110 100 150 130 280 200 230 210 270` — 110, 150, 280, 230, 270 each beat their neighbours.

## Numbers on the number line

To place numbers like 2180, 2754, 9590 and 9950 on a line marked 1000, 2000, …, 10000, look at the **thousands** digit first (which gap it's in), then the **hundreds** (how far along the gap). 9590 and 9950 are both between 9000 and 10000, but 9950 is almost at 10000 while 9590 is a bit past the middle.

## Playing with digits

### How many numbers have a given number of digits?

| Digits | From | To | How many |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 9 | **9** |
| 2 | 10 | 99 | **90** |
| 3 | 100 | 999 | **900** |
| 4 | 1000 | 9999 | **9,000** |
| 5 | 10000 | 99999 | **90,000** |

**Why 90 two-digit numbers?** Count from 10 to 99: that's 99 − 10 + 1 = 90. Or: the first digit can be 1–9 (9 choices) and the second 0–9 (10 choices): 9 × 10 = 90. Each extra digit multiplies by 10.

### Digit sums

The **digit sum** is what you get by adding a number's digits. 68 → 6 + 8 = 14. 176 → 1 + 7 + 6 = 14. 545 → 14 too.

**Smallest number with digit sum 14?** **59**.

**Why:** fewer digits means a smaller number, and one digit can only reach 9, so we need two digits. With two digits, put the **smallest possible digit first**: the second digit can be at most 9, so the first must be at least 14 − 9 = 5. → 59.

**Largest 5-digit number with digit sum 14?** **95000**.

**Why:** to make a number big, make the **leftmost digit as big as possible**: 9. That leaves 14 − 9 = 5 for the rest; put it next: 95, then zeros → 95000.

**How big a number with digit sum 14?** As big as you like! 95, 9005, 900005, 90000005, … just keep adding zeros in the middle. There's no largest.

**Consecutive digits.** 123 → 6, 234 → 9, 345 → 12, 456 → 15, 567 → 18, 678 → 21, 789 → 24. The sums go up by 3 and are all **3 × the middle digit**.

**Why:** the digits are (middle − 1), middle, (middle + 1). The −1 and +1 cancel, leaving three middles.

### Counting a digit

**How many times does 7 appear from 1 to 100?**
- In the **units** place: 7, 17, 27, …, 97 → **10** times.
- In the **tens** place: 70, 71, …, 79 → **10** times.
- Total **20**. (77 counts twice — once in each place.)

From 1 to 1000: 100 times in units, 100 in tens, 100 in hundreds → **300**.

## Palindromes

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A palindrome is a number that reads the same forwards and backwards:** 66, 848, 575, 1221, 12321.

**3-digit palindromes using 1, 2, 3:** first and last digits must match: 111, 121, 131, 212, 222, 232, 313, 323, 333 — **9** of them (3 choices for the outside × 3 for the middle).

### Reverse and add

Take a number, add its reverse; repeat until you get a palindrome.
- 12 + 21 = **33** ✓ (one step)
- 47 + 74 = **121** ✓
- 68 + 86 = 154 → 154 + 451 = 605 → 605 + 506 = **1111** ✓

> [!NOTE]
> From **any 2-digit number** this always ends in a palindrome. For bigger numbers nobody knows for sure — **196** has been tried for millions of steps and never become a palindrome, but nobody has proved it never will.

### Puzzle: who am I?

*"I am a 5-digit palindrome. I am odd. My tens digit is double my units digit. My hundreds digit is double my tens digit."*

- Odd → units digit is odd: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9.
- Tens = 2 × units must be a single digit → units can only be 1, 3 (2 × 5 = 10 is too big). Hundreds = 2 × tens: if units = 3, tens = 6, hundreds = 12 ✗. So units = **1**, tens = **2**, hundreds = **4**.
- Palindrome → the first two digits mirror the last two: **1 2 4 2 1**.

Answer: **12,421**.

### Palindromic times and dates

Clock times like 4:44, 10:01, 11:11, 12:21. If it's 10:01 now, the next palindromic time is 11:11 — **70 minutes** later — and then 12:21, **140 minutes** after 10:01.

## The magic number 6174 (Kaprekar's constant)

**D. R. Kaprekar**, a school maths teacher from Devlali in Maharashtra, discovered this in **1949**.

Take any **4-digit number whose digits are not all the same**.
1. Arrange its digits to make the **largest** number.
2. Arrange them to make the **smallest** number.
3. Subtract: largest − smallest. Repeat with the answer.

**Worked example, starting from 6382:**
- 8632 − 2368 = 6264
- 6642 − 2466 = 4176
- 7641 − 1467 = **6174**
- 7641 − 1467 = 6174 … it's stuck!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Every such 4-digit number reaches **6174** within **7 rounds** — and then stays there. That's why it's called a **constant**.

> [!WARNING]
> If an answer has fewer than 4 digits (e.g. 999), **keep a leading zero** — treat it as 0999. Otherwise the method breaks.

**3-digit version:** the numbers settle on **495**. Example: 321 → 321 − 123 = 198 → 981 − 189 = 792 → 972 − 279 = 693 → 963 − 369 = 594 → 954 − 459 = **495** → 495 again.

## Mental maths: what's possible and what isn't

Before you calculate, ask **how many digits** the answer can have. The trick is to test the **extremes** — the biggest and smallest numbers allowed.

**Can 4-digit + 2-digit ever be 6 digits?** Biggest possible: 9999 + 99 = 10,098 — only 5 digits. **Never.**

**Is 5-digit + 5-digit always 5 digits?** No: 10,000 + 20,000 = 30,000 (5 digits) but 60,000 + 40,000 = 1,00,000 (6 digits). **Only sometimes.**

**Can 5-digit + 5-digit = 18,500?** The smallest 5-digit number is 10,000, so the smallest possible sum is 20,000. **Never.**

**Can 5-digit − 2-digit be a 3-digit number?** The smallest result is 10,000 − 99 = 9,901 (4 digits). **Never.**

**Can 5-digit − 5-digit = 91,500?** The biggest difference is 99,999 − 10,000 = 89,999. **Never.**

> [!TIP]
> To decide *always / sometimes / never*: try the **smallest** and **largest** possibilities. If both extremes work → always. If one works and one doesn't → sometimes. If even the most extreme case fails → never.

### Making thousands from 1500s and 400s

With bricks of 1500 and 400 (any number of each), can you make 1000? No — 1500 is too big, and 400s give 400, 800, 1200: you skip 1000. But 14,000 = 8 × 1500 + 5 × 400 = 12,000 + 2,000 ✓.

## Playing with number patterns

When you see a grid full of repeated numbers, **don't add one by one**. Count how many times each number appears, then multiply.

**Worked example.** A grid of 8 columns × 4 rows of 32: that's 32 numbers, each 32 → 32 × 32 = **1024**.

A grid of 4 × 4 of 64: 16 × 64 = **1024** too!

**Worked example.** Rows alternate: five 50s, four 40s, five 50s, four 40s, five 50s. Total = 15 × 50 + 8 × 40 = 750 + 320 = **1070**.

## The Collatz conjecture — an unsolved mystery

The rule:
- If the number is **even**, **halve** it.
- If it is **odd**, **multiply by 3 and add 1**.
- Repeat.

**Examples from the book:**
- 12 → 6 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → **1**
- 21 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → **1**
- 100 → 50 → 25 → 76 → 38 → 19 → 58 → 29 → 88 → 44 → 22 → 11 → 34 → 17 → 52 → 26 → 13 → 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → **1**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In **1937**, the German mathematician **Lothar Collatz** guessed (conjectured) that **every** starting number eventually reaches 1. Computers have checked enormous numbers and it always works — **but nobody has ever proved it**. It is still one of the most famous unsolved problems in maths.

**Why does it definitely work for powers of 2?** 64 is 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2. It's even, so halve: 32 — still a power of 2, still even. Keep halving: 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. You never meet an odd number until 1.

> [!NOTE]
> A **conjecture** is a statement that seems true but hasn't been proved. Maths isn't finished — some open questions are simple enough for a Class 6 student to understand!

## Simple estimation

Sometimes an exact count isn't needed — a good **estimate** is enough.

**Paromita's estimate (book):** her class sections have 32, 29 and 35 students → about 100 in Class 6. The school has Classes 6–10, each similar → about **500 students**.

**Sheetal says she has spent 13,000 hours in school.** Check: about 6 hours a day × about 200 school days = 1,200 hours a year. 13,000 ÷ 1,200 ≈ 11 years. But a Class 6 student has been in school maybe 7–8 years. So **13,000 is too high** — about 9,000–10,000 is more like it.

> [!TIP]
> Good estimating = break it into pieces you **can** guess (hours per day, days per year), multiply, then ask "does this make sense?"

## Games and winning strategies

### Game of 21

Players take turns. The first says 1, 2 or 3; each player then adds 1, 2 or 3 to the last number. **Whoever says 21 wins.**

**Work backwards.** If you say **17**, your opponent can only say 18, 19 or 20 — and from any of those you can reach 21. So 17 is a "winning number". By the same reasoning, so is 13 (it lets you reach 17), and 9, 5, **1**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Winning numbers: 1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 21** — they go up in **4s**.
> **Why 4?** Whatever your opponent adds (1, 2 or 3), you add the rest to make 4 (3, 2 or 1). So each pair of turns moves exactly 4.
>
> The **first player** wins by saying **1**, then always landing on the next winning number.

**Variation (book):** start at 0, add 1–3, first to **22** wins → winning numbers 22, 18, 14, 10, 6, **2**. First player says 2 and wins.

**Variation:** add 1–10, first to **99** wins. Now pairs of turns make **11**. Winning numbers: 99, 88, 77, …, 22, **11**. The first player must say 1–10 and can't reach 11 — so the **second player** wins by saying 11, then 22, 33, …

## In a nutshell

- Many number puzzles are solved by **reasoning about what must be true**, not by trial.
- **Supercell** = bigger than all neighbours; the largest is always one, the smallest never; at most every other cell.
- 9 one-digit, 90 two-digit, 900 three-digit, … numbers.
- **Digit sum:** smallest number → few digits, small digit first (59 for 14); largest → big digits first.
- **Palindromes** read the same both ways; reverse-and-add makes one from any 2-digit number.
- **Kaprekar:** big − small repeatedly → **6174** (4 digits) or **495** (3 digits).
- Test **extremes** to decide always / sometimes / never.
- **Collatz:** halve if even, 3n + 1 if odd — always reaches 1 so far, still **unproved**.
- **Game of 21:** say the numbers 1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 21 — steps of (largest move + 1).

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** smallest digit-sum number is 59, not 95 · 77 counts twice when counting 7s · keep the leading zero in Kaprekar (0999) · 5-digit − 2-digit can never be 3-digit · Collatz is a conjecture — "always works so far" is not a proof · in add-1-to-10 games the step is 11, not 10.
