## A fraction is a share

When a whole is **shared equally**, a fraction says how much each share is.

- 1 roti between **2** children → each gets **1/2**. Between **4** children → each gets **1/4**.
- **More people sharing the same thing → each share is SMALLER.**

> [!WARNING]
> **The commonest mistake:** thinking `1/9 > 1/5` because 9 > 5. It's the other way round — **1/9 < 1/5**. Nine people sharing one roti each get *less* than five people do. With unit fractions, **the bigger the bottom number, the smaller the share**.

- A **fractional unit** (or unit fraction) is one equal part of a whole: `1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, …`
- In `5/6`, **5 is the numerator** (how many parts) and **6 is the denominator** (what size each part is).

> [!TIP]
> Read `3/4` as **"3 times 1/4"**, not just "three quarters". That phrasing tells you both things at once: the fractional unit is `1/4`, and you have **3** of them. Most fraction questions become obvious once you say them this way.

> [!NOTE]
> Fractions have been named in India since ancient times — the **Rig Veda** calls 3/4 *tri-pada*, which survives as "teen paav" in Hindi and "mukkaal" in Tamil.

## Fractions on the number line

The distance from 0 to 1 is **one unit**. Cut it into equal parts and each part is a fractional unit.

- Cut into 3 → each part is `1/3`; the mark at two parts is `2/3`.
- **Infinitely many fractions lie between 0 and 1** — you can always cut into more parts.

## Mixed numbers

- **Numerator smaller than denominator** → the fraction is **less than 1** (`3/5`).
- **Numerator bigger than denominator** → **more than 1** (`7/3`).

A **mixed number** has a **whole part** and a **fractional part less than 1**.

**Fraction → mixed number:** how many whole units fit inside?
`8/3 = 3/3 + 3/3 + 2/3 = 1 + 1 + 2/3 = ` **2 2/3**

**Mixed number → fraction:** turn the wholes into fractional units.
`3 3/4` → each whole is `4/4`, so `(4 + 4 + 4 + 3)/4 = ` **15/4**

## Equivalent fractions

Fractions that give the **same share** are **equivalent** — the same amount named with different fractional units.

`1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 = 4/8 = …`

**Why they're equal (as shares):** 1 roti among 2 children, 2 rotis among 4 children, and 3 rotis among 6 children all give each child the same amount — double both the rotis and the children and nothing changes per child.

- To make an equivalent fraction, **multiply (or divide) the numerator and denominator by the same number**.
- `1/2 = (1×3)/(2×3) = 3/6` ✓

## Lowest terms (simplest form)

A fraction is in **lowest terms** when its numerator and denominator have **no common factor except 1**.

**Method:** divide both by their **highest common factor**.
`16/20` → both divide by 4 → **4/5** ✓ (4 and 5 share nothing)

**Or go in steps** — easier when the HCF isn't obvious:
`36/60` → ÷2 → `18/30` → ÷2 → `9/15` → ÷3 → **3/5**

Both routes reach the same answer. (Spotting that 36 = 3×12 and 60 = 5×12 gets there in one jump.)

## Comparing fractions

**Same denominator?** Just compare the numerators — the pieces are the same size, so more pieces means more. `2/6 < 3/6`

**Same numerator?** The one with the **bigger denominator is smaller** — same number of pieces, but each piece is smaller. `4/7 > 4/8`

**Neither?** Convert both to the **same fractional unit** (same denominator) first.

**Compare 4/5 and 7/9:**
Use `5 × 9 = 45` as the common denominator.
`4/5 = 36/45` · `7/9 = 35/45` → `36 > 35` → **4/5 > 7/9**

> [!TIP]
> Any common multiple of the denominators works — their **product** always works, and the **smallest** common multiple keeps the numbers smallest.

## Adding & subtracting — Brahmagupta's method

**Same fractional unit:** just add (or subtract) how many units you have; the unit doesn't change.

`4/7 + 6/7 = 10/7 = 1 3/7` · `6/7 − 4/7 = 2/7`

**Why the denominator stays the same:** you're counting sevenths. Four sevenths plus six sevenths is ten sevenths — exactly like 4 apples + 6 apples = 10 apples. The *size* of the piece never changed, only *how many*.

**Different fractional units → make them the same first.**

`1/4 + 1/3` → common denominator `4 × 3 = 12` → `3/12 + 4/12 = ` **7/12**

**The method (described in general by Brahmagupta in 628 CE):**

1. Find **equivalent fractions with a common denominator** (use a common multiple of the denominators).
2. **Add or subtract the numerators**, keeping the denominator.
3. **Express in lowest terms** if needed.

**Example:** `1/6 + 1/3` → smallest common multiple of 6 and 3 is 6 → `1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = ` **1/2** (simplified).

**Example:** `3/4 − 2/3` → common denominator 12 → `9/12 − 8/12 = ` **1/12**

> [!WARNING]
> **Never add the denominators.** `1/4 + 1/3` is **not** `2/7`. Quarters and thirds are different-sized pieces — you must rename them into the same size before you can count them together.
