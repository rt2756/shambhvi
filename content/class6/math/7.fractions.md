## Where fractions come from — sharing

One roti, two children, shared equally: each gets **half** a roti, written **1/2** ("one half", or "one upon two").

One roti, four children: each gets **1/4** (a quarter).

**Which is more, 1/2 or 1/4?** When more children share the same roti, each gets **less**. So 1/2 > 1/4.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A **fraction** tells us **how much each share is** when something is divided into **equal** parts.
> In 3/4: the bottom number (**denominator**) says **how many equal parts the whole is cut into**; the top number (**numerator**) says **how many of those parts we have**.

**Worked examples (book):**
- 3 guavas of the same size weigh 1 kg together → each is about **1/3 kg**.
- 1 kg of rice packed equally in 4 packets → each packet **1/4 kg**.
- 4 friends share **3** glasses of juice equally → each drinks **3/4** glass. (Each glass split into quarters → 12 quarters → 3 quarters each.)
- A fish of 1/2 kg and a fish of 1/4 kg → together **3/4 kg** (a half is two quarters, plus one quarter).

**In order, smallest to biggest:** quarter (1/4), half (1/2), three quarters (3/4), one and a quarter, one and a half, two and a half.

## Fractional units

A **fractional unit** is one equal part of the whole: 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, … (numerator 1).

**Equal parts can have different shapes.** Cut a chikki into 6 equal pieces by slicing across, or into a 2 × 3 grid — the pieces look different but each is **1/6** of the chikki, because each is one of 6 **equal** parts.

> [!WARNING]
> The parts must be **equal**. Cutting a chikki into 4 pieces of different sizes does **not** give quarters.

**Bigger denominator → smaller unit.** 1/8 is smaller than 1/6, because the same whole is cut into more pieces.

### Building fractions from units

3/4 means **three** pieces of size 1/4:

```
3/4 = 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4      (3 times a quarter)
```

The book's "table of 1/2":
- 1/2 (1 time), 1/2 + 1/2 = 1 (2 times), 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 = 3/2 (3 times), … 7 times a half = **7/2**.

5 times a quarter roti: 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = **5/4**. 9 times a quarter = 9/4 = **2 and 1/4** rotis.

## Fractions on the number line

Take the length from 0 to 1. Cut it into **3 equal parts**; each part is 1/3 long. The first mark is 1/3, the next is **2/3**, then 3/3 = 1.

```
0 ──── 1/3 ──── 2/3 ──── 1
```

Keep going past 1: 4/3, 5/3, 6/3 = 2, …

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A fraction is a number** — it has its own spot on the number line. 3/2 lies halfway between 1 and 2; 6/5 lies a little past 1.

**How many fractions between 0 and 1?** **Infinitely many.** Between any two you can always cut into smaller pieces and find more (1/2, 1/3, 1/4, …, 1/1000, …).

## Fractions bigger than 1 — mixed numbers

- **Numerator smaller than denominator** (3/5) → less than 1.
- **Numerator bigger** (7/3) → more than 1.
- **Equal** (4/4) → exactly 1.

A **mixed number** has a **whole number part** and a **fraction part less than 1**: 2 2/3 means 2 + 2/3.

### Fraction → mixed number

**How many whole units are in 7/2?** Each whole is 2/2. 7 halves = 2/2 + 2/2 + 2/2 + 1/2 = 3 wholes and a half → **3 1/2**.

Shortcut: **divide numerator by denominator**. 7 ÷ 2 = 3 remainder 1 → 3 1/2.

**Worked examples (book):** 9/2 = 4 1/2 · 9/5 = 1 4/5 · 21/19 = 1 2/19 · 47/9 = 5 2/9 (47 = 5 × 9 + 2) · 12/11 = 1 1/11 · 19/6 = 3 1/6.

> [!NOTE]
> **Not every fraction bigger than 1 becomes a mixed number.** 8/4 = 2 exactly — there's no fraction part left over, so it's just the whole number 2.

### Mixed number → fraction

**3 1/4 as a fraction?** Each whole is 4 quarters. Three wholes = 12 quarters, plus 1 quarter = **13/4**.

Shortcut: **whole × denominator + numerator**, over the same denominator: (3 × 4 + 1)/4 = 13/4.

**Book examples:** 7 2/3 = 23/3 · 9 4/9 = 85/9 · 3 1/6 = 19/6 · 2 3/11 = 25/11 · 3 9/10 = 39/10.

**Why the shortcut works:** "whole × denominator" turns each whole into that many fractional units; then you add the extra units.

## Equivalent fractions

Look at a **fraction wall** (strips cut into halves, thirds, quarters, sixths, …). The strip for 1/2 is exactly as long as **two** sixths-pieces plus one more: **1/2 = 3/6**. Also 2/3 = 4/6, and 3/6 = 4/8 = 5/10.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Equivalent fractions** are different names for the **same amount** (same point on the number line).
> **To make one, multiply (or divide) the numerator and denominator by the same number.**
> 2/3 = (2 × 2)/(3 × 2) = 4/6 · 4/6 = 2/3 = 6/9 = 8/12 = 10/15 …

**Why it works — the sharing story.** 1 roti for 2 children gives each 1/2. Now **double both**: 2 rotis for 4 children. Each child still gets the same amount! Multiplying the number of things and the number of sharers by the same number doesn't change anybody's share.

**Why it works — the cutting story.** Take 2/3. Cut **every** third into 2 equal pieces. The whole now has 6 pieces (denominator × 2), and your 2 thirds have become 4 pieces (numerator × 2). Same amount, smaller pieces: 4/6.

**Worked examples (book):**
- How many 1/6 pieces make 1/2? **3** (1/2 = 3/6). How many make 1/3? **2**.
- 5 glasses shared among 4 friends = **10** glasses among 8 friends → 5/4 = 10/8.
- 4 kg of potatoes in 3 bags = 12 kg in **9** bags → 4/3 = 12/9.
- 7 rotis among 5 children = 14 rotis among 10 children → 7/5 = 14/10.

### Division facts and fractions

3 rotis shared by 4 children → each gets 3/4. Three ways to say this:
- **Division:** 3 ÷ 4 = 3/4
- **Addition:** 3 = 3/4 + 3/4 + 3/4 + 3/4
- **Multiplication:** 3 = 4 × 3/4

> [!TIP]
> **a ÷ b = a/b.** A fraction *is* a division. 2 cakes shared among 5 children → each gets 2 ÷ 5 = **2/5** cake.

## Lowest terms

A fraction is in **lowest terms** (simplest form) when the numerator and denominator have **no common factor other than 1**.

**Method:** divide top and bottom by a common factor; repeat until none is left.

- 16/20 → both divide by 4 → **4/5**.
- 36/60 → ÷ 2 → 18/30 → ÷ 2 → 9/15 → ÷ 3 → **3/5**.
- 17/51 → 51 = 3 × 17 → ÷ 17 → **1/3**.
- 64/144 → ÷ 16 → **4/9**.
- 126/147 → ÷ 21 → **6/7**.
- 525/112 → ÷ 7 → **75/16** (75 = 3 × 5 × 5 and 16 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 share nothing).

**Why dividing doesn't change the value:** it's the reverse of making equivalent fractions — you're joining small pieces back into bigger ones.

> [!TIP]
> Using the **prime factorisations** from Chapter 5 shows every common factor at once: 126 = 2 × 3 × 3 × 7, 147 = 3 × 7 × 7 → common part 3 × 7 = 21.

## Comparing fractions

### Same denominator

Same-size pieces, so **more pieces = more**: 2/6 < 3/6, 4/7 < 5/7.

**Sharing view:** same number of children, more rotis → bigger share. 4 glasses among 7 children < 5 glasses among 7 children.

### Same numerator

Same number of pieces, but a **bigger denominator means smaller pieces**: 4/7 > 4/8, 1/5 > 1/6.

**Sharing view:** same rotis, more children → smaller share.

### Different numerators and denominators

**Rename both with the same fractional unit** (common denominator), then compare numerators.

**Worked example.** 4/5 or 7/9? Common denominator 5 × 9 = 45: 4/5 = 36/45, 7/9 = 35/45. 36 > 35 → **4/5 > 7/9**.

**Book examples:**
- 8/3 vs 5/2 → 16/6 vs 15/6 → **8/3 > 5/2**.
- 4/9 vs 3/7 → 28/63 vs 27/63 → **4/9 > 3/7**.
- 7/10 vs 9/14 → 49/70 vs 45/70 → **7/10 > 9/14**.
- 9/4 vs 5/2 → 9/4 vs 10/4 → **9/4 < 5/2**.
- 3 glasses among 4 (3/4 = 15/20) vs 7 glasses among 10 (7/10 = 14/20) → the first group gets more.

**Ordering (book):** ascending 2/5 < 7/10 < 11/15 (as thirtieths: 12, 21, 22). Descending 13/4 > 25/16 > 7/8 > 17/32 (as 32nds: 104, 50, 28, 17).

> [!TIP]
> Any **common multiple** of the denominators works. The product always works; the smallest one keeps numbers small. **Quick check against 1/2 or 1** often settles it without any working: 3/7 is less than 1/2 and 5/8 is more, so 3/7 < 5/8.

## Adding and subtracting fractions

### Same fractional unit

You're just **counting units**: 4 sevenths + 6 sevenths = 10 sevenths.

4/7 + 6/7 = **10/7** = 1 3/7 · 5/8 − 3/8 = 2/8 = **1/4** · 10/27 − 1/27 = 9/27 = **1/3**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the denominator stays the same:** it names the **size** of the pieces, and adding doesn't change the size — only how many you have. 4 apples + 6 apples = 10 apples, not 10 "double apples".

On the number line, 4/7 + 6/7 means: start at 0, jump 4 sevenths, then 6 more sevenths → land on 10/7.

### Different fractional units — Brahmagupta's method

1/4 + 1/3: quarters and thirds are **different-sized pieces**, so you can't just count them. First **rename them as the same unit**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Brahmagupta's method** (written down by the Indian mathematician **Brahmagupta in 628 CE**):
> 1. Rewrite the fractions as equivalent fractions with a **common denominator** (a common multiple of the denominators).
> 2. **Add or subtract the numerators**; keep the denominator.
> 3. Put the answer in **lowest terms**.

**Worked examples:**
- 1/4 + 1/3 → twelfths: 3/12 + 4/12 = **7/12**.
- 1/6 + 1/3 → sixths: 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = **1/2**.
- 3/4 − 2/3 → twelfths: 9/12 − 8/12 = **1/12**.
- 2/3 + 3/4 (Rahim's paint) → 8/12 + 9/12 = 17/12 = **1 5/12 litres**.
- 2/5 + 3/4 (Geeta's and Shamim's lace) → 8/20 + 15/20 = 23/20 = **1 3/20 m**. More than the 1 m border → **enough lace**.
- 13/4 − 10/3 … careful: the book asks **"subtract 13/4 from 10/3"**, which means 10/3 − 13/4 = 40/12 − 39/12 = **1/12**.
- Jaya's school is 7/10 km away; she rides 1/2 km → walks 7/10 − 5/10 = **2/10 = 1/5 km**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Never add the denominators.** 1/4 + 1/3 is **not** 2/7. (Check: 2/7 is less than 1/3 alone — adding something can't make it smaller!)

> [!WARNING]
> **"Subtract A from B" means B − A.** The order is reversed from how it's read.

### Mixed numbers

Turn them into fractions first (or add wholes and parts separately):
2 1/2 + 1 1/3 = 5/2 + 4/3 = 15/6 + 8/6 = 23/6 = **3 5/6**.

## A pinch of history

In ancient India a fraction was called **bhinna** (Sanskrit for "broken"), or **bhaga** / **ansha** ("part", "piece"). The way the whole world writes fractions today — one number **above** the other — **began in India**: the **Bakhshali manuscript** (around 300 CE) already wrote 1/2 that way. Aryabhata (499 CE), **Brahmagupta (628 CE)**, Sridharacharya and Mahaviracharya kept using it. The **line** between the numbers was added later by the Moroccan mathematician **Al-Hassar** (12th century), and the notation spread to Europe.

Egyptians and Babylonians also used fractions, but mostly **fractional units** (numerator 1); other fractions were written as sums of units — today called **Egyptian fractions**. **General fractions and their rules** (adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing) were first worked out in India — the Vedic **Sulba-sutras** already use them — and **Brahmagupta** wrote the rules down in the form we still use.

### Puzzle: different fractional units that add to 1

- **Two different units can't make 1.** The biggest unit is 1/2, and 1/2 + 1/2 = 1; swap either 1/2 for a smaller unit and the total drops below 1.
- **Three different units:** there is exactly **one** way — **1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1**. (Check in sixths: 3/6 + 2/6 + 1/6 = 6/6.) Starting from 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3, one third must grow — and the only bigger unit is 1/2 — then the rest must make up the other 1/2.

## In a nutshell

- Fraction = **equal share**. Denominator = parts in the whole; numerator = parts taken.
- **Fractional units** (1/n) build every fraction; bigger n → smaller unit.
- Fractions are **numbers on the number line**; infinitely many lie between 0 and 1.
- Mixed number ↔ fraction: divide (7/2 = 3 1/2) or whole × denominator + numerator (3 1/4 = 13/4).
- **Equivalent fractions:** multiply or divide top and bottom by the same number. **a ÷ b = a/b.**
- **Lowest terms:** divide out every common factor.
- **Compare:** same denominator → compare tops; same numerator → bigger bottom is smaller; otherwise use a common denominator.
- **Add/subtract:** same unit → count units; different units → Brahmagupta: common denominator, then add tops, then simplify.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** pieces must be equal · 1/8 < 1/6 even though 8 > 6 · never add denominators · simplify the final answer · 8/4 is a whole number, not a mixed number · "subtract A from B" = B − A.
