> [!NOTE]
> This is a **hands-on** chapter. Read it with a **ruler, compass, pencil and protractor** beside you, and actually draw each figure. Constructions only make sense once your own hand has made them.

## What "construction" means

To **construct** a figure is to draw it **exactly**, using only a few tools — mainly a **ruler** (for straight lines and lengths) and a **compass** (for circles, arcs and copying lengths). The goal isn't a pretty picture: it's a figure where every length and angle is **exactly** what it should be, and where you can explain **why**.

## The compass and the circle

**Think:** mark a point P. Now imagine marking **every** point that is exactly 4 cm from P — to the right, to the left, up, down, slanting… What shape do all those points make?

They make a **circle**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A circle is the set of all points at the same distance from a fixed point.**
> - The fixed point is the **centre**.
> - The fixed distance is the **radius**.

That is **exactly** what a compass does: its pointed tip stays still at the centre, and the pencil stays a fixed distance away while it swings round. A compass doesn't "guess" a circle — it **forces** every point drawn to be the same distance from the centre.

### Setting the radius

1. Put the compass point on the **0** of your ruler.
2. Open it until the pencil tip is on the length you want (say 4 cm).
3. Don't change the opening again until you're done with that length.

> [!TIP]
> **A compass is a distance-copier.** Its real power: it can carry an exact length from one place to another without measuring again. You'll use this in every construction below.

### Wavy waves (book's artwork)

The book's "Wavy Wave" is made of **half circles** sitting on a straight line, alternately above and below.

**Worked example (book).** The central line AB is 8 cm, and it holds **two** waves: a half circle above from A to X, then one below from X to B. The two waves are equal, so X is the **midpoint**: **AX = 4 cm**. A half circle's ends are a **diameter** apart, and the radius is half of that: **radius = 2 cm**.

> [!TIP]
> **A half circle of radius r covers 2r of the line.** So for any wave, radius = (length it covers) ÷ 2, and its centre is the middle of that stretch.

How to draw it: mark the midpoint of each segment as a **centre**, set the compass to half the segment, and draw half circles — one above the line, the next below.

## Squares and rectangles

### The defining properties

| Shape | Properties |
|---|---|
| **Rectangle** | **R1:** opposite sides are equal · **R2:** all four angles are 90° |
| **Square** | **S1:** all four sides are equal · **S2:** all four angles are 90° |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A square is a special rectangle** — it has right angles like any rectangle, plus the extra demand that **all four** sides (not just opposite pairs) are equal.

### Naming a four-sided figure

Name the corners **in order as you walk around the shape** — start anywhere, go either way.

For square PQRS (corners in order P → Q → R → S):
- **Valid:** PQRS, QRSP, RSPQ, SPQR (and backwards: PSRQ, SRQP, …).
- **Not valid:** **PQSR** — going P → Q → S jumps **across** the square instead of along a side.

### A turned square is still a square

Draw a square on dot paper, then a "tilted" one with corners on dots. The tilted one is **still a square**.

**Why:** turning a shape doesn't change any **side length** or any **angle**, and those are the only things the definition talks about.

> [!TIP]
> On a dot grid you can check a tilted square **without measuring**: if every side goes, say, "2 across and 1 up" (turned for each side), all sides are equal, and the turns between them are right angles.

> [!WARNING]
> Don't judge a shape by how it sits on the page. A "diamond" might be a square standing on a corner — check its **sides and angles**.

## Constructing a rectangle and a square

### Rectangle 6 cm × 4 cm (book)

1. Draw AB = 6 cm with the ruler.
2. At **A**, draw a line **perpendicular** to AB (use the protractor: 90°).
3. On it, mark **D** with AD = 4 cm (use the compass set to 4 cm).
4. At **B**, draw a perpendicular to AB and mark **C** with BC = 4 cm.
5. Join **DC**.

**Check when you're done:** DC should measure 6 cm, and the angles at C and D should be 90° — even though you never set them directly. If they come out right, your construction was accurate.

### Square of side 6 cm

Same steps, with every side 6 cm. **Keep the compass at 6 cm** the whole time.

**Why keep the same setting?** All four sides must be equal. One fixed compass opening guarantees that; re-measuring each side with a ruler lets small errors creep in, and then the figure won't close neatly.

### Can a figure have four right angles but unequal opposite sides?

**No.** Draw AB, raise perpendiculars at A and B, and mark D and C at the same height. Once all four corners are 90°, the top side DC is forced to be as long as AB, and AD as long as BC. **Four right angles drag equal opposite sides along with them.**

## An exploration in rectangles

Construct a rectangle **ABCD** with AB = 7 cm and BC = 4 cm. Let **X** slide along side AD and **Y** slide along side BC. How long is XY?

The book suggests recording results in a **table** instead of writing a sentence each time:

| X from A | Y from B | XY |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mm | 5 mm | 7 cm |
| 1 cm | 1 cm | 7 cm |
| 1 cm 5 mm | 1 cm 5 mm | 7 cm |
| 1 cm | 3 cm | about 7.3 cm |

**What we see:**
- When X and Y are the **same distance** from A and B, **XY = AB = 7 cm**, and ABYX is itself a **rectangle**. So the **closest** X and Y can be is **7 cm**.
- XY is **longest** when X and Y are at **opposite corners** (X at A and Y at C, or X at D and Y at B). Then XY is a **diagonal**: **XY = AC = BD**.

**Why the shortest is 7 cm:** the sides AD and BC are parallel, 7 cm apart. The straight-across path is the shortest; any slanted path is longer.

## Breaking rectangles into squares

**Construct a rectangle that splits into 2 identical squares.**

**Plan first with a rough sketch.** Call the rectangle ACDF with B on AC and E on FD, so ABEF and BCDE are squares.
- Each square has all sides equal: AF = AB = BE = FE and BE = BC = CD = ED.
- So **all the short lines are equal**, and **AC = 2 × AF**.

Mark equal sides on your rough figure with a small **"|"** tick — that's the convention.

**Build it.** Pick any AF, say 4 cm → AC = 8 cm → a 4 cm × 8 cm rectangle. Or skip the ruler: draw AF, raise a perpendicular, and use the compass (set to AF) to step off AB and then BC.

**For 3 identical squares:** the length must be **3 times** the breadth (e.g. 2 cm × 6 cm).

**Rectangles that can't be split this way:** 4 cm × 2.5 cm (4 is not 2 × 2.5) can't make two identical squares; 7 cm × 2 cm (7 is not 3 × 2) can't make three.

### Square with a hole

To put a circular hole exactly in the middle of a square, the circle's centre must be where the **two diagonals cross**. **Why:** that point is the same distance from all four corners and from all four sides.

## Diagonals of rectangles and squares

The **diagonals** join opposite corners (AC and BD in ABCD).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - In a rectangle, **the two diagonals are equal**.
> - A diagonal splits each corner it passes through into **two angles that add to 90°**.
> - Those two angles are **equal (45° and 45°) only when the rectangle is a square**.

**Why 45°–45° means a square:** the diagonal cuts the rectangle into two triangles. If the two parts of the corner are equal, the diagonal leans exactly "halfway" — and that only happens when the two sides at that corner are the **same length**. A long thin rectangle tilts the diagonal towards its long side, so the angle next to the long side is smaller.

**Worked example (book).** A rectangle whose diagonal splits a corner into **50° and 40°** (50 + 40 = 90 ✓). Draw one side, then use the protractor to draw the diagonal at 40° (or 50°) from it, and raise a perpendicular at the side's other end. Where they meet is the far corner.

### Rectangle from a side and a diagonal

**Construct a rectangle with one side 4 cm and diagonal 8 cm.**
1. Draw AB = 4 cm.
2. Raise a perpendicular at **B**.
3. Set the compass to **8 cm**, put the point on **A**, and draw an arc cutting the perpendicular at **C**. Now AC = 8 cm exactly.
4. Complete the rectangle: perpendicular at A, mark D with AD = BC, join CD.

**Why the arc works:** every point on the arc is 8 cm from A, and C must be on the perpendicular too — so the crossing is the one point that's both.

> [!NOTE]
> The diagonal must be **longer** than the side — a slanted line across a rectangle is always longer than its sides. With side 3 cm and diagonal 7 cm it works the same way.

## Points at the same distance from two points — the house

**Construct a house:** a square-ish base BCED with sides 5 cm, and a roof point **A** that is **5 cm from both B and C**.

**How do we find A without trial and error?**
- All points 5 cm from B lie on a **circle of radius 5 cm centred at B**.
- All points 5 cm from C lie on a **circle of radius 5 cm centred at C**.
- A must be on **both** circles → A is where the circles **cross**.

You don't need full circles — **two arcs** (radius 5 cm, from B and from C) crossing above BC are enough. Join AB and AC.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To find a point at given distances from two points, draw an arc from each point with that radius. Where the arcs cross is your point.** This is the key compass trick of the chapter.

Then, with the compass still at 5 cm and its point on A, draw the curved arc of the roof from B to C — it passes through both because both are 5 cm from A.

**Bigger house:** all sides 7 cm → same steps, compass at 7 cm.

## A four-sided figure with equal sides that isn't a square

**Yes — a rhombus.** All four sides equal, but the angles are not 90°. Push a square over sideways (like a hinged frame) and the sides stay equal while the angles change.

**How to construct one:** draw a side of 5 cm, draw a second 5 cm side at, say, 60° to it, then use the compass (5 cm) from the two free ends to find the fourth corner where the arcs cross.

**Why this matters:** "all sides equal" (S1) alone does **not** make a square; you need right angles (S2) too.

## In a nutshell

- **Circle** = all points at one distance (radius) from the centre; a compass enforces this. A compass also **copies lengths**.
- **Rectangle:** opposite sides equal + four right angles. **Square:** all sides equal + four right angles. Turning a shape doesn't change it.
- Name four-sided figures by walking round the corners in order.
- Construct rectangles/squares: side → perpendiculars → mark lengths with the compass → join → **check**.
- In rectangle ABCD, XY across is shortest (= AB) straight across and longest (= diagonal) corner to corner.
- Diagonals of a rectangle are equal; they split the corners equally only in a **square**.
- **Two arcs crossing** give the point at chosen distances from two points.
- Equal sides without right angles → **rhombus**, not a square.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** PQSR is not a name for square PQRS · a tilted square is still a square · keep the compass opening fixed when copying a length · a diagonal is always longer than a side · equal sides alone don't make a square · 45°/45° split happens only in a square.
