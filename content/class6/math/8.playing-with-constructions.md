## What this chapter is about

A **construction** is a drawing made with just two tools, a **ruler** (for straight lines and lengths) and a **compass** (for circles and arcs), sometimes with a protractor for right angles. In this chapter you will:

- learn what a compass really does: it finds **all the points at one distance** from a centre;
- draw pictures from circles and arcs (a person, waves, eyes, a house);
- construct **squares and rectangles** exactly, using their properties;
- explore rectangles: the distance between moving points, the **diagonals**, rectangles split into squares;
- use **two circles** to find a point at given distances from two points.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** A **circle** is all the points at the same distance from one point, its **centre**; that distance is the **radius**. An **arc** is a piece of a circle. Two lines are **perpendicular** if they meet at a right angle (90°). The **diagonals** of a rectangle join opposite corners.

## 8.1 Artwork with a compass

Look at these pictures made only of circles and straight lines. Try drawing them freehand first, then with a compass: the compass makes them neat.

![Artwork: two touching circles, circles with one common centre, three overlapping circles, a face with ears](assets/img/class6/math/8/artwork.svg)

### What a compass does

Mark a point P. Now mark many points, in different directions, that are each **4 cm** from P.

![Twelve points, each 4 cm from P, lying on a dashed circle round P](assets/img/class6/math/8/points_4cm.svg)

They all lie on a **circle** with centre P. To draw it: open the compass against a ruler so that the **pencil is 4 cm from the metal point**; put the point on P and turn the compass, **keeping the point still**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A circle is the set of all points at the same distance (the radius) from the centre.** So a compass is really a "same-distance machine": every point it draws is exactly one radius from where the metal point stands. This is why a compass can **copy a length** and **find a point at a given distance** from another point.

**Try this.** With a compass, make the artwork above: two circles that just touch (the centres must be exactly two radii apart: why?), and three circles with the same centre. Is it easier with the compass than freehand?

### Check yourself

**1.** Imagine marking **all** the points that are 4 cm from P. What do they look like?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **circle** with centre P and radius 4 cm. In every direction from P there is exactly one point at 4 cm, and together those points go all the way round P.

**2.** Take any point on that circle. Is its distance from P equal to 4 cm, less, or more? What about another point on the circle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Exactly 4 cm**, for every point on the circle. The compass's pencil stayed 4 cm from the point P all the way round, so every point it drew is 4 cm from P. (A point **inside** the circle is less than 4 cm from P; a point **outside** is more.)

### Drawing a person

![A person drawn with a compass: a circle for the head, a line for the neck, and a body whose top edge is an arc dipping in the middle](assets/img/class6/math/8/person_fig.svg)

The head is a circle. The body's top edge is an **arc that dips in the middle**: part of a circle whose centre is **above** it.

**Try this.** Put the compass point at the top of the neck line and try different openings until the arc passes through both shoulder points. Then try putting the point lower: what happens to the curve?

### Wavy wave

![A wave on an 8 cm line AB: a half circle above the first half and a half circle below the second half](assets/img/class6/math/8/wave.svg)

**Example 1: planning the wave.** The line AB is 8 cm long and X is its middle. The first wave is a **half circle** on AX, above the line; the second is a half circle on XB, below it.
1. A half circle on AX has AX as its **diameter** (the line across the circle through the centre), so its centre is the **middle of AX** and its radius is **half of AX**.
2. AX = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 cm, so the radius is 4 ÷ 2 = **2 cm**, with centre 2 cm from A.
3. The second half circle has centre 2 cm from B, radius 2 cm, drawn below the line.

### Check yourself

**1.** For the wave on AB = 8 cm, what radius do you set on the compass for the half circle? How long is AX?

> [!ANSWER]
> **AX = 4 cm** (X is the middle of AB, and 8 ÷ 2 = 4).
>
> **Radius = 2 cm.** The half circle stretches across AX, so AX is its diameter, and the radius is half of that: 4 ÷ 2 = 2 cm. Put the compass point at the middle of AX (2 cm from A).
>
> ![The first half circle: centre 2 cm from A, radius 2 cm, AX = 4 cm](assets/img/class6/math/8/wave_answer.svg)

**2.** Draw the wave on a central line of a different length.

> [!ANSWER]
> Say AB = **10 cm**.
> 1. Mark the middle X: AX = XB = 5 cm.
> 2. The radius is half of 5 cm: **2.5 cm**.
> 3. Put the compass point 2.5 cm from A, draw the half circle above AX. Put it 2.5 cm from B, draw the half circle below XB.
>
> **Rule:** radius = AB ÷ 4, with centres a quarter and three quarters of the way along.

**3.** Draw a wave whose two bumps are **smaller than half circles** (flatter), but still exactly alike.

> [!ANSWER]
> Use a **bigger radius** with the centre **further from the line**:
>
> ![Flatter waves: arcs of radius 3 cm through A and X (centre below the line) and through X and B (centre above)](assets/img/class6/math/8/wave_small.svg)
>
> 1. Take AB = 8 cm, X the middle. Choose a radius **more than 2 cm**, say 3 cm.
> 2. Find the centre for the first bump: it must be 3 cm from **both A and X**. Draw arcs of radius 3 cm from A and from X **below** the line; where they cross is the centre.
> 3. With that centre, draw the arc from A to X: it bulges **above** the line, flatter than a half circle.
> 4. Do the same for X and B, with the arcs **above** the line, and draw the arc bulging below.
>
> Both bumps match because they use the **same radius** and the same distances.

**4.** How do you draw the body of the person (the curved top edge)?

> [!ANSWER]
> ![The person with the compass point for the body's top edge marked above the shoulders](assets/img/class6/math/8/person_answer.svg)
>
> 1. Draw the head (a circle) and the neck (a short line).
> 2. Mark the two **shoulder points**, the top corners of the body, at the same height either side of the neck.
> 3. Put the compass point on the neck line, **above** the shoulders, and open it to reach one shoulder. Check it also reaches the other: since the point is exactly in the middle, it will.
> 4. Draw the arc from one shoulder to the other: it **dips** in the middle because the centre is above it.
> 5. Draw the sides and the bottom with a ruler.
>
> A centre **higher up** gives a flatter curve; a lower centre gives a deeper one.

**5.** How do you draw these eyes with a compass?

![Two eyes, each made of an upper and a lower curve meeting at two corners, with a round pupil](assets/img/class6/math/8/eyes.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Each eye is **two arcs** meeting at the corners, like two waves facing each other.
>
> ![Hint: a light helper line through the eye's corners; the compass point A for the upper curve is below the line and B for the lower curve is above, the same distance away](assets/img/class6/math/8/eyes_hint.svg)
>
> 1. Draw a light **helper line** and mark the two corners of the eye on it, say 4 cm apart.
> 2. For the **upper** curve, put the compass point at **A**, below the middle of the corners, opened so it reaches both corners; draw the arc above.
> 3. For the **lower** curve, put the point at **B**, the **same distance above** the middle, with the same opening; draw the arc below.
> 4. Because A and B are equally far from the line, the two curves are **mirror images**: the eye is symmetrical. Draw the pupil as a small filled circle in the middle, and repeat for the second eye.

## 8.2 Squares and rectangles

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A rectangle** has: **(R1)** opposite sides equal, and **(R2)** all four angles 90°.
> **A square** has: **(S1)** all four sides equal, and **(S2)** all four angles 90°.
> So **every square is a rectangle** (its opposite sides are certainly equal), but not every rectangle is a square.

![Rectangle ABCD with opposite sides AB and DC in blue; square SPQR](assets/img/class6/math/8/names_rect_square.svg)

**Naming.** A rectangle is named by its corners **in order going round** it, starting anywhere and going either way: ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC, or ADCB, DCBA, CBAD, BADC. **ABDC is not allowed**: going A → B → D jumps across the middle.

> [!WARNING]
> A tilted square is still a square, even though it "looks like a diamond". Shape names depend on **lengths and angles**, never on which way the shape is turned.

**Try this.** Cut a square from paper and a rectangle that is not a square. Turn each one on the table in different ways. Measure a side and an angle each time: do the measurements ever change?

**Turning a square.** A square sheet of paper, turned round, is still a square: turning doesn't change any length or angle. So a **tilted** square is still a square, and a tilted rectangle is still a rectangle.

![A rectangle with four equal small squares placed symmetrically around its corners, on dot paper](assets/img/class6/math/8/rect_four_squares.svg)

### Check yourself

**1.** Which of these is **not** a name for the square SPQR shown above: PQSR, SPQR, RSPQ, QRSP?

> [!ANSWER]
> **PQSR.** Going round the square the corners come in the order S, P, Q, R (and back to S). PQSR goes P → Q and then **jumps across** to S, skipping R. The others are all in order: SPQR, RSPQ (= R, S, P, Q) and QRSP (= Q, R, S, P).

**2.** Draw the rectangle-with-four-squares picture above on dot paper. How did you place the squares so they sit symmetrically round the rectangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw the rectangle first: here 6 dot-gaps long and 3 dot-gaps tall.
> 2. Each small square is 2 × 2 gaps.
> 3. Place each square **one gap away diagonally** from a corner of the rectangle: its nearest corner is 1 gap left (or right) and 1 gap up (or down) from the rectangle's corner.
>
> Doing the **same thing at every corner** makes the picture symmetrical: the squares are mirror images of each other across the middle lines of the rectangle.

**3.** Which shapes in this dot-grid collection are squares? Can you tell without measuring?

![Four 4-sided shapes A, B, C, D drawn on a dot grid, some tilted](assets/img/class6/math/8/grid_squares.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **A and D are squares; B and C are not.**
>
> **How to tell from the dots alone:** look at how each side "steps" across the grid.
> - **A:** one side goes 3 right, 2 up; the next goes **2 left, 3 up**. The steps are the same numbers swapped (3 and 2), so the sides are equal, and swapping with a turn (right → up, up → left) means the side has been turned by a **quarter turn**: a right angle. All four sides go this way, so A is a square.
> - **D:** sides go 2 right, 1 up and then 1 left, 2 up: the same pattern, so D is a (small) square.
> - **B:** sides go 3 right, 1 up and then 1 right, 3 up. They are equal in length, but the second is **not** the first turned a quarter turn (that would be 1 **left**, 3 up), so the corner isn't 90°. B is a rhombus-like shape, not a square.
> - **C:** sides go 3 right, 3 up and then 3 left, 2 up: different lengths, so not a square.

**4.** Draw at least 3 tilted squares and rectangles on a dot grid with corners on dots. Check that they have the right properties.

> [!ANSWER]
> Use the step trick from question 3:
> - **Tilted square:** pick a step, say "2 right, 1 up". The next side is "1 left, 2 up", then "2 left, 1 down", then "1 right, 2 down" back to the start. All sides are equal and every corner is 90°.
> - **Another square:** "3 right, 1 up", then "1 left, 3 up", and so on.
> - **Tilted rectangle:** use "2 right, 1 up" for the long side **twice over** ("4 right, 2 up"), and "1 left, 2 up" for the short side. Opposite sides match and the corners are still right angles.
>
> **Check:** measure the sides with a ruler and the corners with a protractor (or the corner of a page): squares have 4 equal sides, rectangles have equal **opposite** sides, and every corner is 90°.

## 8.3 Constructing squares and rectangles

**Example 2: a square PQRS with side 6 cm.**
1. Draw PQ = 6 cm.
2. At P, draw a line **perpendicular** to PQ (use a protractor: put its centre on P and mark 90°).
3. Mark S on it with **PS = 6 cm**, by a ruler or by a compass opened to 6 cm.
4. At Q, draw another perpendicular to PQ.
5. Mark R on it with QR = 6 cm (with the compass still open at 6 cm, this is quick).
6. Join S to R.

![Square PQRS with side 6 cm: right angles at P and Q, and compass arcs of 6 cm marking S and R](assets/img/class6/math/8/square_construction.svg)

> [!TIP]
> **Use the compass to copy lengths.** Once it is opened to 6 cm, it keeps that length: you can mark S and R without measuring again, which is quicker and more accurate.

**Try this.** Construct the 6 cm square both ways: once marking S and R with a ruler, once with the compass kept at 6 cm. Measure SR in each. Which one came out closer to exactly 6 cm, and why?

### Check yourself

**1.** Why must PS be 6 cm long?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because in a **square all sides are equal** (property S1). PQ is 6 cm, so PS, QR and RS must all be 6 cm too.

**2.** In the finished figure, how long is RS, and what are ∠R and ∠S?

> [!ANSWER]
> **RS = 6 cm, ∠R = 90°, ∠S = 90°.** Measure them to check. **Why they come out right:** PS and QR both stand straight up from PQ and both are 6 cm, so S and R are at the same height above PQ. The line SR is then parallel to PQ, the same length, and meets the upright sides at right angles.

**3.** Draw a rectangle with sides 4 cm and 6 cm. Check that it has both rectangle properties.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw AB = 6 cm.
> 2. Draw perpendiculars at A and B.
> 3. Mark D and C on them with AD = BC = **4 cm**.
> 4. Join DC.
>
> **Check:** AB = DC = 6 cm and AD = BC = 4 cm (R1: opposite sides equal), and all four angles measure 90° (R2).

**4.** Draw a rectangle with sides 2 cm and 10 cm. Check both properties.

> [!ANSWER]
> Same steps: PQ = 10 cm, perpendiculars at P and Q, PS = QR = **2 cm**, join SR.
>
> **Check:** PQ = SR = 10 cm, PS = QR = 2 cm, and ∠P = ∠Q = ∠R = ∠S = 90°. It is a long, thin rectangle, but it is still a rectangle.

**5.** Can you construct a 4-sided figure with **all angles 90°** but **opposite sides not equal**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Try: draw PQ and perpendiculars at P and Q. Both uprights stand at 90° to PQ, so they are **parallel**. If S and R were at different heights (PS ≠ QR), the top line SR would slope, and then the angles at S and R would **not** be 90° (one would be more, one less). The only way to get 90° at S and R too is to make PS = QR, and then SR = PQ as well. So four right angles **force** opposite sides to be equal.

## 8.4 An exploration in rectangles

Construct a rectangle ABCD with **AB = 7 cm** and **BC = 4 cm**. A point X can slide along side AD, and a point Y along side BC (including the corners).

![Four positions of X on AD and Y on BC in a 7 cm by 4 cm rectangle, with the length XY of each](assets/img/class6/math/8/xy_rect.svg)

**Keeping a record.** Instead of writing long sentences ("When X is 5 mm from A and Y is 3 cm from B, XY = …"), use a **table**: only the positions and the length change.

| Distance of X from A | Distance of Y from B | Length of XY |
|---|---|---|
| … | … | … |

**Try this.** Construct the 7 cm × 4 cm rectangle three or four times on a sheet. In each copy, mark X and Y at your own positions, measure XY and add it to the table. Which positions give the smallest XY?

### Check yourself

**1.** Where should X and Y be so that they are **closest** together? Farthest apart? Guess, then check.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Closest:** when X and Y are **at the same height**, the same distance from A and from B. Then XY goes straight across and **XY = 7 cm**.
> - **Farthest:** when they are at **opposite ends**: X at A and Y at C, or X at D and Y at B. Then XY is a **diagonal**, about **8.1 cm**.

**2.** How does the smallest distance XY compare with the length of AB?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It is equal to AB: 7 cm.** XY can never be shorter than the width of the rectangle (7 cm), because X and Y are on the two sides that are 7 cm apart; going straight across is the shortest way.

**3.** Find XY when (i) X is 5 mm from A and Y is 3 cm from B; (ii) X is 1 cm from A and Y is 1 cm from B; (iii) X is 2 cm from A and Y is 4 cm from B.

> [!ANSWER]
> Measure on your construction; you should get about:
>
> | X from A | Y from B | XY |
> |---|---|---|
> | 5 mm | 3 cm | **7 cm 4 mm** |
> | 1 cm | 1 cm | **7 cm** |
> | 2 cm | 4 cm | **7 cm 3 mm** |
>
> The further apart X and Y are in height, the longer XY is.

**4.** What is XY when X and Y are the **same** distance from A and B: 5 mm and 5 mm, 1 cm and 1 cm, 1 cm 5 mm and 1 cm 5 mm?

> [!ANSWER]
> **7 cm every time.**
>
> | X from A | Y from B | XY |
> |---|---|---|
> | 5 mm | 5 mm | 7 cm |
> | 1 cm | 1 cm | 7 cm |
> | 1 cm 5 mm | 1 cm 5 mm | 7 cm |

**5.** In those equal-distance cases, how does XY compare with AB, and what shape is ABYX?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **XY = AB** (both 7 cm).
> - **ABYX is a rectangle.** AX = BY (the same distance down each side), the sides AX and BY are both upright, and the angles at A and B are 90°. So, as in the square construction, XY is level and equal to AB, and all four angles of ABYX are right angles.

**6.** How does the farthest distance between X and Y compare with the diagonals AC and BD?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It equals them.** The farthest positions are X = A, Y = C (or X = D, Y = B), so XY **is** the diagonal AC (or BD). Both diagonals measure about **8.1 cm**: the two diagonals of a rectangle are equal.

### Breaking rectangles into squares

Can we construct a rectangle that splits into **2 identical squares**? **Plan first with a rough sketch.**

![Rough sketch: rectangle ACDF split into squares ABEF and BCDE; all six short sides carry one tick mark (they are equal)](assets/img/class6/math/8/two_squares.svg)

Since the two squares are identical and each has equal sides, **all the short lines are equal**: AF = AB = BC = BE = FE = ED. We mark equal lengths with a small tick "|". So the long side AC is **twice** the short side AF.

**Example 3: constructing it with a compass.** Draw AF (any length) and a perpendicular at A. Open the compass to the length AF (point at A, pencil at F). Keeping that opening, mark B on the perpendicular, then put the point on B and mark C. Complete the rectangle with perpendiculars.

> [!TIP]
> **Plan before you construct:** a quick rough sketch with the equal sides ticked shows what must be equal to what. Then the construction is just copying lengths with the compass.

### Check yourself

**1.** If AF = 4 cm, how long must AC be for the rectangle to split into two equal squares?

> [!ANSWER]
> **AC = 8 cm.** AC = AB + BC, and each is equal to AF = 4 cm (sides of the squares): 4 + 4 = 8 cm.

**2.** Construct a rectangle that can be divided into **three** identical squares.

> [!ANSWER]
> ![A rectangle 3 times as long as it is wide, made of three squares; compass arcs step the width along the top three times](assets/img/class6/math/8/three_squares.svg)
>
> 1. Draw the short side AF (any length, say 3 cm) and a perpendicular at A.
> 2. Open the compass to AF. Step it along the perpendicular **three times**, marking B, C and then the corner G: AG = 3 × AF.
> 3. Draw perpendiculars at B, C and G down to the level of F, and join along the bottom.
>
> The long side is **3 times** the short side, and the lines at B and C cut it into three equal squares.

**3.** Give side lengths of a rectangle that **cannot** be divided into two identical squares, and one that cannot be divided into three.

> [!ANSWER]
> A rectangle splits into 2 identical squares only if its long side is **exactly 2 times** its short side; into 3, only if it is **exactly 3 times**.
> - Not 2 squares: **5 cm by 3 cm** (5 is not 2 × 3 = 6). Also 4 cm by 2.5 cm.
> - Not 3 squares: **7 cm by 2 cm** (7 is not 3 × 2 = 6). Also 10 cm by 4 cm.

### Check yourself: construct with care

**4.** Construct an 8 cm × 4 cm rectangle with a square inside it, sharing the rectangle's centre as shown.

![An 8 cm by 4 cm rectangle with a 4 cm square in the middle; 2 cm gaps on each side; the diagonals meet at the common centre](assets/img/class6/math/8/square_in_rect.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Plan with a rough figure:** the square fits between the long sides, so its side is the rectangle's width, **4 cm**.
> 2. The rectangle is 8 cm long, so 8 − 4 = 4 cm is left over, shared equally: **2 cm** on each side.
> 3. Construct the rectangle. On both long sides, mark points **2 cm** and **6 cm** from the left end, and join them across.
>
> **Check:** the diagonals of the rectangle and of the square cross at the same point, the centre.

**5.** Construct the "falling squares": three 4 cm squares, each one starting at the top right corner of the one before. Then try squares of 7 cm, 5 cm and 3 cm the same way.

![Falling squares: three 4 cm squares climbing corner to corner; and squares of 7, 5 and 3 cm climbing the same way](assets/img/class6/math/8/falling_squares.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Construct the first 4 cm square (base, two perpendiculars, compass at 4 cm).
> 2. Its **top right corner** is the bottom left corner of the next square. Extend the top side to the right by 4 cm, and the right side upward by 4 cm; these are two sides of the next square. Complete it.
> 3. Repeat for the third.
>
> For 7, 5, 3: the 5 cm square starts at the top right corner of the 7 cm one, and the 3 cm square at the top right corner of the 5 cm one. Keep each new square's bottom side **along the line** of the previous square's top, so they line up as shown.

**6.** Construct the "shadings" picture: a big square split into four squares, three of which are split again into four small squares, each small square cut by a diagonal with one half shaded. Choose your own size.

![A large square in four quarters; three quarters are split into four small squares each, with a diagonal and a shaded half in every small square](assets/img/class6/math/8/shadings.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Choose a big square of side **8 cm**.
> 1. Construct the 8 cm square.
> 2. Mark the **middle** of each side (4 cm along) and join opposite middles: four 4 cm squares.
> 3. In three of them, mark the middles of their sides (2 cm along) and join: each splits into four 2 cm squares.
> 4. Draw one diagonal in each small square, all in the same direction, and shade the same half of each.
>
> Choosing a size that halves neatly (8 → 4 → 2) keeps every mark on a whole centimetre.

**7.** Construct a square with a circular hole whose centre is the centre of the square.

![A square with a circle in it; the circle's centre is where the diagonals cross](assets/img/class6/math/8/square_hole.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Construct the square (say 6 cm).
> 2. Draw both **diagonals**. They cross at the **centre** of the square.
> 3. Put the compass point there and draw a circle (radius less than 3 cm, so it stays inside: here 2 cm).
>
> **Why the diagonals:** the centre is the point exactly in the middle in every direction, and each diagonal passes through the middle of the square.

**8.** Construct a square with four holes, one in the middle of each quarter.

![A square split into four quarters, each with a circle at its centre](assets/img/class6/math/8/square_holes.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Construct the square (say 8 cm) and split it into four 4 cm squares by joining the middles of opposite sides.
> 2. In **each** small square, draw its diagonals lightly to find its centre.
> 3. Draw a circle about each centre with the same radius (say 1.5 cm).

**9.** Construct a square of side 8 cm whose four sides are replaced by arcs that curve inwards evenly.

![A square with four inward-curving arcs between its corners; one compass point, outside the square below the middle of the bottom side, is shown](assets/img/class6/math/8/square_curves.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Each arc must pass through **two neighbouring corners** and bulge inwards by the same amount.
> 1. Construct the 8 cm square.
> 2. For the bottom side, put the compass point **outside the square, on the line through the middle of that side**, and open it to reach both bottom corners. (With the opening at 8 cm, the point sits about 6.9 cm below the side.)
> 3. Draw the arc from one corner to the other: it bulges up into the square.
> 4. Do the same for the other three sides with the **same opening**, the point outside each side's middle.
>
> **Why it is even:** the point is equally far from both corners (it is on the middle line), and using the same opening for all four sides makes all four arcs identical.

## 8.5 The diagonals of a rectangle

![Rectangle PQRS with diagonals PR and QS; the eight angles they make at the corners are named a to h](assets/img/class6/math/8/rect_diagonals.svg)

The diagonals PR and QS split each corner's right angle into two smaller angles: 8 angles in all, named a to h.

**Try this.** Construct three rectangles: 6 cm × 4 cm, 8 cm × 3 cm and 5 cm × 5 cm. Draw the diagonals and record, in a table, the sides, the two diagonals and all 8 angles. What stays true every time?

### Check yourself

**1.** Compare the lengths of the two diagonals. Predict, then measure.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The two diagonals are equal.** In the 6 cm × 4 cm rectangle each measures about 7.2 cm.
>
> **Why:** the triangle PQR (half the rectangle on one side of PR) and the triangle QPS fit exactly on each other if you flip the rectangle over: they have the same two sides (6 cm and 4 cm) with a right angle between them. So their long sides PR and QS are equal.

**2.** The diagonal PR splits ∠R into g and h, and ∠P into c and d. Are g and h equal? Are c and d? Which pairs of the 8 angles are equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> In a rectangle that is **not** a square, **g ≠ h** and **c ≠ d**: the diagonal leans closer to the longer side. In the 6 × 4 rectangle each corner splits into about **34° and 56°** (they add up to 90°).
>
> **Equal pairs:** every angle that a diagonal makes with a **long** side is about 34°, and every angle with a **short** side is about 56°. So the 8 angles come in two groups of 4 equal angles. For instance, the angle PR makes with PQ at P equals the angle it makes with RS at R (the rectangle looks the same turned upside down).

**3.** How should the rectangle be made so that a diagonal divides the corners into **equal** parts?

> [!ANSWER]
> Make **all its sides equal**: a **square**. Then the diagonal splits each corner into **45° + 45°**, because it leans equally towards both sides.

**4.** What happens in the special case of a square?

> [!ANSWER]
> - The diagonals are still **equal**.
> - All **8 angles are 45°**.
> - The diagonals also cross at **right angles** (90°), which doesn't happen in other rectangles.

**5.** What general rules did you notice about rectangles' diagonals?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The two diagonals of a rectangle are **always equal**.
> 2. At each corner, the two parts of the right angle **add up to 90°**.
> 3. A diagonal makes the **same angle** with the two long sides (and with the two short sides).
> 4. The corners are split into **equal** parts only when the rectangle is a **square** (45° each).

**6.** How can you be sure these rules are **always** true, not just for the rectangles you drew?

> [!ANSWER]
> Measuring a few rectangles only shows they are true **for those**. To be sure, you need a **reason that works for every rectangle**, like the one in question 1: half the rectangle flipped over fits exactly on the other half, so the diagonals must be equal, whatever the sizes. (In later classes you will learn to write such reasons as proofs.)

### Constructing a rectangle from a diagonal's angle

**Example 4: a rectangle where a diagonal splits the corners into 60° and 30°.**
1. Draw AB (any length).
2. Draw a perpendicular to AB at B.
3. At A, draw a ray at **60°** to AB; where it meets the perpendicular is **C**. (Now ∠BAC = 60°, so the rest of ∠A is 90° − 60° = 30°.)
4. Find D: either draw perpendiculars at A and at C (D is where they meet), or use the compass to mark D with **AD = BC** and **CD = AB**.
5. Join to finish ABCD.

![Rectangle ABCD: the diagonal AC makes 60° with AB and 30° with AD; D is found by compass arcs](assets/img/class6/math/8/rect_60_30.svg)

### Constructing a rectangle from a side and a diagonal

**Example 5: one side 5 cm and a diagonal 7 cm.**
1. Draw DC = 5 cm.
2. Draw a line l perpendicular to DC at C. Corner B lies somewhere on l.
3. B is also **7 cm from D**. All points 7 cm from D lie on a **circle** of radius 7 cm about D. So draw that circle (or just an arc near l): **where it crosses l is B**.
4. Draw perpendiculars at D (up) and at B (across); they meet at A.

![Rectangle ABCD with DC = 5 cm and diagonal DB = 7 cm: the 7 cm circle about D cuts the perpendicular at C in B](assets/img/class6/math/8/rect_5_7.svg)

> [!TIP]
> **Instead of hunting for one point by trial and error, draw all the points that satisfy one condition** (a circle for "7 cm from D") and see where they meet the points for the other condition (the line l). The crossing is the answer, first time.

## 8.6 Points at given distances from two points

**Example 6: the house.** Every edge of the house's border is 5 cm, and the door is 1 cm by 2 cm.

![A house: walls DB and EC and floor DE of 5 cm, roof AB = AC = 5 cm, and an arc from B to C with centre A](assets/img/class6/math/8/house_fig.svg)

1. Draw the floor DE = 5 cm and the walls DB and EC, 5 cm each, at right angles; draw the door.
2. The roof top **A** must be **5 cm from B** and **5 cm from C**. All points 5 cm from B make a circle about B; all points 5 cm from C make a circle about C.
3. **The circles cross at A** (the crossing above the house): it is on both, so it is 5 cm from both.
4. Join AB and AC. Then, with the compass still at 5 cm and its point on A, draw the arc from B to C.

![The house with the two 5 cm circles about B and C crossing at A](assets/img/class6/math/8/house_circles.svg)

**Try this.** Draw a 6 cm line PQ. Using arcs, find a point 4 cm from P and 5 cm from Q. How many such points are there? (Two: one on each side of PQ, because the two circles cross twice.)

### Check yourself

**1.** Did you need to draw both full circles to find A?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Only the parts **near where A will be** matter. Draw a short arc of radius 5 cm from B and another from C, above the walls; they cross at A. This is quicker and keeps the drawing clean.

### 1. Construct a rectangle in which a diagonal divides the corners into 50° and 40°.

> 4. Mark D with the compass so that AD = BC and CD = AB (or with perpendiculars at A and C), and join.
>
> **Check:** all four corners are 90°, opposite sides are equal, and the diagonal AC splits ∠A (and ∠C) into 50° and 40°.

### 2. Construct a rectangle in which a diagonal divides the corners into 45° and 45°. What do you notice about its sides?

> [!ANSWER]
> ![Rectangle ABCD with diagonal AC at 45° to both AB and AD: it is a square](assets/img/class6/math/8/rect_45.svg)
>
> Same steps with a **45°** ray from A.
>

### 6. Draw the person, the wavy wave and the eyes again, this time using the "two arcs" idea from the house to find each compass point exactly.

> [!ANSWER]
> In each picture, a curve has to pass through **two known points** with a chosen radius. The compass point must be **that radius from both points**, so find it the house way: **two arcs, one from each point, crossing**.
> - **Person:** choose a radius (say 2.5 cm). Draw arcs of 2.5 cm from both shoulder points, above them; they cross at the compass point. Draw the body's top edge from there.
> - **Wavy wave:** for the flatter wave, draw arcs of the chosen radius from A and from X (below the line); their crossing is the first centre. Do the same from X and B above the line.
> - **Eyes:** with the chosen radius, arcs from the two eye corners cross **below** the helper line (point A, for the upper curve) and **above** it (point B, for the lower curve). The two crossings are equally far from the line, so the eye comes out symmetrical.
>
> No guessing is needed any more: the crossing gives the exact centre.

### 7. Is there a 4-sided figure with all sides equal that is **not** a square? If so, construct one.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes**: a **rhombus** that is not a square (its angles are not 90°).
>
> ![A rhombus ABDC with all sides 5 cm and a 60° angle at A, built with compass arcs](assets/img/class6/math/8/rhombus.svg)
>
> 1. Draw AC = 5 cm.
> 2. Find B, **5 cm from A and from C**: arcs of 5 cm about A and about C cross at B (the house idea).
> 3. Find D, **5 cm from B and from C**: arcs of 5 cm about B and about C cross at D (the crossing away from A).
> 4. Join A–B–D–C–A.
>
> All four sides are 5 cm, but the angle at A is **60°**, not 90°, so it is not a square.

## In a nutshell

- A **circle** is all the points at one distance (the **radius**) from the centre; a compass draws circles and **copies lengths**.
- To draw an arc through two points, put the compass point **equally far from both** (on the line through their middle).
- **Rectangle:** opposite sides equal and all angles 90°. **Square:** all sides equal and all angles 90°. Name them by going round the corners **in order**. Turning a shape doesn't change it.
- Construct a square or rectangle: base, **perpendiculars** at both ends, compass to mark the equal sides, join.
- In a 7 cm-wide rectangle, two points on opposite sides are **at least 7 cm apart**, exactly when at the same height; at most a **diagonal** apart.
- The **diagonals** of a rectangle are **equal**; they split the corners equally (45°) only in a **square**.
- To find a point at given distances from two points, draw **two arcs**; they **cross** at the point.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** keep the compass point **still** and don't let the opening slip · the radius is half the **diameter** (a half circle on 4 cm has radius 2 cm) · ABDC is **not** a name for rectangle ABCD (go round in order) · four right angles force opposite sides to be equal · a 4-sided figure with equal sides is a square **only if** its angles are 90° (otherwise a rhombus) · don't find points by trial and error with a ruler: use **arcs**.
