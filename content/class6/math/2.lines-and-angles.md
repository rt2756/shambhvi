## What this chapter is about

Geometry is built from a few simple ideas: **points**, **lines**, **line segments**, **rays** and **angles**. In this chapter you will:

- learn what each of these is, how to **name** it, and how to draw it;
- see an **angle** as an **amount of turning**, and compare angles by placing one on another;
- meet the special angles: **straight angles** and **right angles**, and the **acute**, **obtuse** and **reflex** angles;
- **measure** angles in **degrees** with a protractor (and make your own protractor by folding paper!), and **draw** an angle of any size.

> [!NOTE]
> **Symbols we will use.** Segment AB is written with a bar over AB, line AB with arrows at both ends over it, and ray AB with one arrow. In these notes we just say "segment AB", "line AB" and "ray AB". The sign **∠** means "angle": ∠ABC is read "angle ABC".

## 2.1 Point, line segment, line and ray

![A point P; a segment AB with two ends; a line AB going on for ever both ways; a ray AP with one end at A](assets/img/class6/math/2/basics.svg)

- **Point.** Press a sharp pencil on paper: the tiny dot is a model of a **point**. A point marks an **exact place**; it has **no length, breadth or height**. We name points with capital letters: point P, point Z. (The tip of a compass or a needle is a good model too.)
- **Line segment.** The **shortest path** between two points A and B is the **line segment AB** (or BA). A and B are its **end points**. A fold in paper gives a straight crease: a model of a segment.

![Many routes from A to B: wavy dashed routes are all longer than the straight segment AB in red](assets/img/class6/math/2/routes.svg)

- **Line.** Extend segment AB beyond A and beyond B **without ever stopping**: that is the **line AB**. A line has **no end points**, so we can never draw all of it; we draw a piece with arrows to show it goes on. A line can also be named with a small letter, like line l.
- **Ray.** A **ray** starts at one point and goes on for ever in **one direction**, like a beam of light from a torch or a lighthouse. Ray AP starts at A and passes through P. **The starting point is always written first.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Through two points there is exactly one line.** Why: a line is straight, so once it passes through A and is aimed at B, its direction is fixed; there is no room for a second, different straight line through both.

**Try this.** Mark two dots on paper. Draw as many different **straight** lines through both as you can with a ruler. Now mark just **one** dot and do the same. What do you notice?

**Example 1: naming.** In the picture of a ray AP above, the ray starts at A, so it is ray **AP**, never ray PA. The segment between A and B can be called segment AB **or** BA: a segment has no direction.

> [!ANSWER]

> [!ANSWER]

## 2.2 Angles

An **angle** is made by **two rays with the same starting point**. The common starting point is the **vertex**, and the two rays are the **arms**.

![An angle with vertex B and arms BD and BE, marked by a small curve](assets/img/class6/math/2/angle_parts.svg)

**Naming an angle.** We can call it ∠B, using the vertex. To be clear, use a point on each arm with the vertex **in the middle**: **∠DBE** or **∠EBD**. We mark an angle with a small curve near the vertex.

### The size of an angle is an amount of turning

Imagine Vidya opening the cover of her notebook. At first it is closed; she turns it a little, then more, then all the way back. **The more she turns it, the bigger the angle** between the cover and the page.

![The size of an angle: the amount one arm turns about the vertex from its starting position to its final position](assets/img/class6/math/2/turn.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The size of an angle is the amount of turn about the vertex needed to move one arm onto the other.** It has nothing to do with how long the arms are drawn.

Angles made by turning are everywhere: the two arms of a compass or divider (vertex at the hinge), the blades of open scissors (vertex at the screw), open spectacles, a wallet, a stapler, a box lid.

**Example 2.** In Vidya's book, which angle is bigger: when the cover is opened a little (case 1) or a bit more (case 2)? **Case 2**, because the cover has turned more.

> [!ANSWER]

> [!ANSWER]
> ![Three lines AB, BC and CA through three points, with the angles at A, B and C marked](assets/img/class6/math/2/three_points.svg)
>
> **3 lines: AB, BC, CA.** (Each pair of points gives one line: A and B, B and C, C and A.)
>
> **3 angles: ∠BAC, ∠ABC, ∠ACB** (one at each point, between the lines to the other two points).

**6.** Now mark four points A, B, C, D, no three on one line. Draw every line through two of them. How many lines? How many angles can you name using A, B, C, D?

> ∠BAC, ∠CAD, ∠BAD; ∠ABC, ∠CBD, ∠ABD; ∠BCA, ∠ACD, ∠BCD; ∠ADB, ∠BDC, ∠ADC.

## 2.3 Comparing angles

When animals open their mouths, the jaws make angles: the wider the mouth, the bigger the angle. Sometimes it's easy to see which of two angles is bigger; when they are close in size, it's hard. Then we need a method.

**Try this.** Draw four animals with open mouths (a snake, a fish, a crocodile, a bird), each mouth opened a different amount. Mark the vertex (where the jaws meet) and the two arms (the jaws) of each. Arrange the angles from smallest to largest. Then draw four angles of your own in different directions, label them ∠A, ∠B, ∠C, ∠D, and ask a friend to put them in order: which pairs are hard to decide by eye?

### Superimposition: putting one angle on top of the other

Trace one angle and place it on the other so that the **vertices match** and **one pair of arms lies along each other**. Then look at the other arms: the angle whose arm is further out is bigger.

![∠ABC of 30° and ∠PQR of 55°, then placed on top of each other with B on Q and BC along QR: QP lies outside BA](assets/img/class6/math/2/superimpose.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two angles are equal** if, when one is placed on the other with vertices together and one pair of arms together, the **other arms also lie on each other**. Why: then the same amount of turn takes one arm to the other in both angles.

### Using a see-through circle

Two cranes are arguing about who opens its beak wider. Put a transparent circle on the first angle with its **centre on the vertex**; mark A and B where the arms cross the circle. Move the circle onto the second angle, centre on the vertex and OA along one arm. If the other arm passes **beyond B**, the second angle is bigger.

![Comparing with a circle: crane 1's arms cross the circle at A and B; on crane 2, one arm passes through A and the other passes beyond B](assets/img/class6/math/2/circle_compare.svg)

**Example 3: superimposing.** In the superimposing picture (30° and 55°), ∠ABC is traced and placed on ∠PQR with B on Q and BC along QR. Arm BA falls **inside** ∠PQR, between QR and QP. So ∠ABC is smaller: **∠PQR is larger** (55° against 30°).

**Try this.** Make "rotating arms" from two straws joined by a paper clip. Make several with different openings. Cut an angle-shaped slit in cardboard by tracing one of them. Mix them up: which one passes through the slit? Only the one with the **same angle** goes through, however long its arms are (as long as they fit).

> [!WARNING]
> **Longer arms do not make a bigger angle.** Cutting the arms shorter (or drawing them longer) doesn't change the amount of turn between them.

### Check yourself

**1.** Is it always easy to compare two angles by looking?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** If one angle is much bigger, you can see it at once. But angles like 89° and 91° look almost the same, and long arms can make a smaller angle **look** bigger. Then we must **superimpose** them or **measure** them.

**2.** Where else do we compare things by placing one on top of the other?

> [!ANSWER]
> Comparing the **lengths** of two pencils or strips (put them side by side with one end together); comparing two **shapes** or tiles (place one on the other to see if they match exactly); comparing the size of two **coins** or **circles**; checking if two pages are the same size.

**3.** Which crane opened its beak wider in the circle method above?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The second crane.** With one arm along OA, its other arm crossed the circle **beyond B**, so it has turned further than the first crane's arm: its angle is bigger.

**4.** Fold a rectangular sheet of paper and draw along the crease. Name and compare the angles between the crease and the sides of the paper. Make other folds. Which is the largest angle you made? The smallest?

> [!ANSWER]
> Call the corners A, B (top) and C, D (bottom), and the crease EF with E on the top edge and F on the bottom edge. Four angles appear: **∠AEF, ∠BEF, ∠DFE, ∠CFE**.
> - If the crease is **slanting**, ∠AEF and ∠CFE are the same size and bigger, and ∠BEF and ∠DFE are the same size and smaller. (When you place the paper's halves on each other, the matching angles cover each other.)
> - If the crease goes **straight across** (fold one edge onto the opposite edge), all four angles are equal: they are **right angles**.
>
> The more slanted the crease, the bigger the large angles and the smaller the small ones; your largest and smallest angles depend on your folds.

![Rays from O: OA, OX, OY, and a ray along which B and then C lie](assets/img/class6/math/2/compare_q2.svg)

**Question 5.** In this figure, decide which angle is greater, and why.

**(a)** ∠AOB or ∠XOY

> [!ANSWER]
> **∠AOB.** ∠XOY lies completely **inside** ∠AOB (OX and OY are between OA and OB), so ∠AOB = ∠AOX + ∠XOY + ∠YOB, which is more than ∠XOY.

**(b)** ∠AOB or ∠XOB

> [!ANSWER]
> **∠AOB.** Both have arm OB. Starting from OB, you must turn further to reach OA than to reach OX, because OA is beyond OX.

**(c)** ∠XOB or ∠XOC

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are equal.** B and C lie on the **same ray** from O, so OB and OC are the same arm. ∠XOB and ∠XOC are the same angle with two names.

**6.** Which is greater: ∠XOY or ∠AOB? Give reasons.

![Two angles that overlap and look about the same size: ∠XOY (blue) and ∠AOB (black)](assets/img/class6/math/2/compare_q3.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **You can't be sure just by looking.** Neither angle sits inside the other, and they look nearly equal. Trace one and **superimpose**, or **measure** both. Measuring this drawing gives **∠XOY = 63°** and **∠AOB = 60°**, so **∠XOY is greater**, but only by 3°.

## 2.4 Special types of angles

### Straight angle

When Vidya opens her book flat on the table, the cover has made a **half turn**: the two arms point in **opposite directions** and make a straight line. This is a **straight angle**. (Turning the cover all the way round to the back is a **full turn**.)

### Right angle

Any ray OC from the vertex of a straight angle ∠AOB splits it into two angles, ∠AOC and ∠COB. Can we make them **equal**?

**Fold the paper so that OB lies on OA.** The crease is the ray OC we want.

![Straight angle AOB with the crease OC standing straight up, making two 90° angles](assets/img/class6/math/2/straight_bisect.svg)

**Why the two angles are equal:** when folded, ∠COB lies exactly on ∠COA (the vertex stays at O, OC stays put, and OB falls on OA). Folding **is** superimposing.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Each of the two equal halves of a straight angle is a **right angle**. A right angle is a **quarter turn** (half of a half turn). It looks like the corner of an "L", and we mark it with a small square. Two lines that meet at a right angle are **perpendicular**.

### Acute and obtuse angles

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - An **acute angle** is **less than a right angle** (less than a quarter turn). "Acute" means sharp.
> - An **obtuse angle** is **more than a right angle but less than a straight angle** (between a quarter turn and a half turn). "Obtuse" means blunt.

![Three groups of angles: acute ones (less than a right angle), right angles, and obtuse ones (between right and straight)](assets/img/class6/math/2/angle_groups.svg)

**Try this.** Use the corner of a page (a right angle) as a tester. Hold it against angles around you: if the angle fits inside the corner it is acute, if it matches it is right, and if it is wider it is obtuse.

### Check yourself

**1.** Can a ray OC divide a straight angle ∠AOB into two equal angles?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Fold the paper so that OB lands on OA; the crease is OC. The two angles ∠AOC and ∠COB then cover each other exactly, so they are equal: two **right angles**.

**2.** A straight angle is half of a full turn. How much of a full turn is a right angle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **One quarter (¼) of a full turn.** A right angle is half of a straight angle, and a straight angle is half of a full turn: ½ of ½ = ¼.

**3.** How many right angles do the windows of your classroom have? Where else in your classroom do you see right angles?

> [!ANSWER]
> A rectangular window frame has **4 right angles**, one at each corner. If the window has bars or panes, every place where a straight bar meets the frame or another bar makes more right angles (a cross of two bars makes 4 right angles where they meet).
>
> Other right angles: the corners of the blackboard, door, table top, book covers, floor tiles, and where a wall meets the floor.

![Two dot grids. Left: A and B on the same row with two dots between them. Right: A and B on a slant going down to the right](assets/img/class6/math/2/grids_question.svg)

**4.** Join A to other dots of each grid with a straight line to make a **straight angle** with AB. In how many ways can you do it?

> [!ANSWER]
> For a straight angle, the new line must go from A in **exactly the opposite direction** to B, along the same straight line.
>
> ![Answers in green: in the left grid, the dots to the left of A in the same row; in the right grid, the dots up and to the left of A on the same slant](assets/img/class6/math/2/grids_straight.svg)
>
> - Left grid: the dots to the **left of A in the same row**: **2 ways** (the next dot and the one after).
> - Right grid: the dot continuing the slant **up and to the left** of A: **1 way** (the next dot along would be off the grid).
>
> (All the ways in one grid give the same angle; they just stop at different dots.)

**5.** Join A to other dots to make a **right angle** with AB. In how many ways?

> [!ANSWER]
> The new line must split the straight angle at A into two equal parts: it must be **perpendicular** to AB.
>
> ![Answers in green: in the left grid, the dots straight above and below A; in the right grid, the dots on the slant through A going up to the right and down to the left](assets/img/class6/math/2/grids_right.svg)
>
> - Left grid (AB along a row): the dots **straight above and below A**: **5 ways** (2 above, 3 below in this grid).
> - Right grid (AB going one right, one down each step): the perpendicular goes **one right, one up** each step: **3 ways** (1 dot up-right, 2 dots down-left).
>
> **Why this slant is a right angle:** going "1 right, 1 down" and "1 right, 1 up" make the same angle with a row of dots on either side (half of a right angle each), so together they make a right angle.

**Question 6.** Make a slanting crease on a piece of paper. Then make another crease perpendicular to it.

![A slanting crease and a second crease across it, making four right angles](assets/img/class6/math/2/fold_perpendicular.svg)

**(a)** How many right angles do you have now? Why are they exactly right angles?

> [!ANSWER]
> **4 right angles.** The second fold was made by laying one part of the first crease **exactly on the other part**. So the second crease splits the straight angle on the first crease into two equal halves: two right angles. The same happens on the other side, so there are 4.

**(b)** Describe how you folded the paper so that someone else can follow your steps.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Fold the paper anywhere at a slant and press to make a crease. Unfold.
> 2. Pick a point on the crease. Fold the paper again **through that point** so that the crease lands **exactly on top of itself** (one half of the crease on the other half).
> 3. Press and unfold. The new crease crosses the first one at right angles.

**7.** Look back at the angles in this section's pictures. Find acute, right, obtuse and straight angles.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Straight:** ∠AOB in the folding picture (A, O, B on a line).
> - **Right:** ∠AOC and ∠COB in the same picture; the four angles at the perpendicular creases.
> - **Acute:** the first group in the classifying picture; the angles of the scissors blades; ∠QTR.
> - **Obtuse:** the third group; ∠PTR in the "name the angles" figure (it is more than a right angle).

**8.** Draw a few acute and a few obtuse angles, turned in different directions.

> [!ANSWER]
> Any angles work, as long as:
> - each **acute** angle is **smaller** than the corner of a page (fits inside it), and
> - each **obtuse** angle is **bigger** than the corner of a page but its arms don't yet form a straight line.
>
> Turning the page doesn't change the type: an angle is acute or obtuse because of its **size**, not its direction. (See the three groups in the picture above for examples in many directions.)

**9.** Acute means sharp and obtuse means blunt. Why were these words chosen?

> [!ANSWER]
> An **acute** angle has a narrow opening, so a shape with that corner is **pointed and sharp**, like the tip of a knife or a needle. An **obtuse** angle opens wide, so the corner is **blunt and rounded off**, like the corner of a big open book. The names describe how the corners feel.

**10.** Count the acute angles in each figure. What is the next figure, and how many acute angles will it have? Do you see a pattern?

![A triangle; the triangle with a smaller triangle joining its midpoints; then a third smaller triangle inside the middle one](assets/img/class6/math/2/acute_pattern.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **3, 12, 21**, and the next figure has **30**.
>
> 1. **First figure:** a triangle with all angles acute (60° each): **3**.
> 2. **Second:** the 3 corners still give 3. Each of the 3 new points in the middle of a side has **4 rays** (two along the side, two into the triangle), making three 60° angles that are acute (and some bigger ones): 3 × 3 = 9 more. Total **12**.
> 3. **Third:** the middle triangle gets 3 new side-points, each giving 3 more acute angles: 12 + 9 = **21**.
> 4. **Next:** draw a triangle joining the midpoints of the newest middle triangle: 21 + 9 = **30**.
>
> ![The fourth figure: one more triangle inside the middle; 30 acute angles](assets/img/class6/math/2/acute_pattern_next.svg)
>
> **Pattern:** start at 3 and **add 9** each time (3 new points × 3 acute angles each).

## 2.5 Measuring angles in degrees

To give an angle a **number**, mathematicians divided a full turn into **360 equal parts**. Each part is **1 degree**, written **1°**. The measure of an angle is **how many 1° parts fit inside it**.

![30 thin one-degree slices side by side make an angle of 30°](assets/img/class6/math/2/thirty_units.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Full turn = 360°.**
> - **Straight angle** = half turn = 360° ÷ 2 = **180°**.
> - **Right angle** = quarter turn = 180° ÷ 2 = **90°**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why 360?** Nobody knows for sure. The Rigveda describes a wheel with 360 spokes; old Indian, Persian, Babylonian and Egyptian calendars used a year of 360 days; and the Babylonians counted in 60s. The most useful reason: **360 can be divided exactly by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, …**: by every number up to 10 except 7. So a circle splits into many equal parts with a whole number of degrees each.

### Check yourself

![Ten circles divided into 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10 and 12 equal parts](assets/img/class6/math/2/circle_parts.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide the full turn, 360°, by the number of parts:
>

### The protractor

A **protractor** is a half circle divided into **180 one-degree parts**. There is a long mark every 10° and a medium mark every 5°, so you can count in 10s and 5s.

**How to measure an angle:**
1. Put the protractor's **centre point on the vertex**.
2. Turn it so that **one arm lies along the 0° line** (the straight edge's line).
3. Read the number where the **other arm** crosses the scale, using the scale that **starts at 0 on your first arm**.

> [!WARNING]
> A protractor has **two rows of numbers**, one counting from the right end and one from the left end, so that you can measure an angle opening either way. **Always use the row that has 0 on the arm you started from.** Reading the wrong row gives 180° minus the right answer.

**Example 4.** In the picture, ∠AOB has OB along the 0° line on the right, and OA crosses the scale at 80 (counting from the right). So **∠AOB = 80°**.

![A protractor with OB along the 0 line and OA crossing the scale at 80](assets/img/class6/math/2/protractor_aob.svg)

### Make your own protractor by folding

1. Cut out a paper circle (a full turn, 360°). Fold it in half and cut along the crease: a **semicircle**, half a turn = **180°**. Write 0° at the right corner and 180° at the left.
2. Fold the semicircle in half: the crease is a **quarter turn**. Mark **90°** at the top.
3. Fold in half again: the new creases make **45°** (and 180° − 45° = **135°** on the other side).
4. Fold in half once more: **22.5°** angles appear.

![A folded paper protractor: creases from O to points A to I, every 22.5°, labelled 0°, 22.5°, 45°, 67.5°, 90°, 112.5°, 135°, 157.5°, 180°](assets/img/class6/math/2/paper_protractor.svg)

Halving an angle by folding is called **bisecting** it, and the crease is the **angle bisector**.

**Example 5: a reflex angle on the paper protractor.** The angle from crease OA all the way round past OI, the long way, is the rest of the full turn after ∠AOI. ∠AOI is a straight angle, 180°, so the long way round is 360° − 180° = 180° too: a straight angle has the same size on both sides. But from OA to OB the long way round is 360° − 22.5° = **337.5°**, a reflex angle.

**Try this.** Make the paper protractor above. Use it to check: is the corner of your book exactly 90°? Is the angle of a set-square 45°?

**Question 17.** What went wrong in each case?

**(a)** ∠U = 35°

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake:** one arm goes **below** the 0 line, so neither arm is on 0. The student read only the upper arm (35°).
> **Right way:** turn the protractor so the lower arm lies on the 0 line. The angle is 35° above the line plus 25° below it: **60°**.

**(b)** ∠V = 80°

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake:** the protractor is tilted and **neither arm is on the 0 line**. One arm reads 80 and the other 15.
> **Right way:** either turn the protractor so one arm is on 0, or subtract the two readings: 80 − 15 = **65°**.

**(c)** ∠W = 70°

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake:** the **vertex is not at the centre** of the protractor (it is to the right of the centre point). The reading at the edge is then meaningless.
> **Right way:** put the centre point exactly on the vertex. The arms are a flat line and a line going straight up: a right angle, **90°**.

**(d)** ∠X = 150°

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake:** the **wrong row of numbers**. The first arm lies along the **left** 0, but the student read the row that starts at 0 on the right.
> **Right way:** count up from the 0 on the arm: the angle is **30°** (= 180 − 150). A quick check: the angle is clearly acute, so 150° can't be right.

**(e)** ∠Y = 120°

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake:** the angle opens **below** the protractor, but the protractor was placed above the line. The student read where the lower arm's **backward extension** (the dotted line) crosses the scale.
> **Right way:** turn the protractor over so it covers the angle, with one arm on 0. The angle is **60°** (= 180 − 120).

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake:** the marked angle (the curve goes round the outside) is the **reflex** angle, but the student measured the small angle between the arms.

**(a)** At 1 o'clock the angle between the hands is 30°. Why?

> [!ANSWER]

> [!ANSWER]

> - 4 o'clock: 4 × 30° = **120°**.

**19.** Can the amount a door is opened be described by an angle? What are its vertex and arms?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** The **vertex** is the **hinge line** (where the door is fixed to the frame, seen from above it is a point). One **arm** is the door's **closed position** (along the wall or the frame), and the other arm is the **door** in its open position. The more the door opens, the bigger the angle: up to about 90° for most doors.

**20.** Vidya notices that the bigger the angle she starts swinging from, the faster she goes. Where is the angle?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **vertex** is the point where the swing's ropes are tied to the branch. One **arm** is the rope hanging **straight down** (the swing at rest; you can't see this arm while she swings). The other arm is the rope at the **starting position**, pulled back. The angle between them is the starting angle: pull back further and the angle (and her speed) is bigger.

**21.** A toy has slanting slabs for balls to roll down. Can angles describe how steep the slabs are? What are the arms? Which arm can you see?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** For each slab, the angle is at the point where the slab starts. One arm is the **slab** itself (visible). The other is an imaginary **flat (horizontal) line** through that point (invisible). The steeper the slab, the bigger the angle between them, and the faster the ball rolls.

**22.** An insect sits on a flat line, and then in a turned (rotated) position. Can an angle describe how much it turned? What are the arms and vertex?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Look at the line touching the insect's body. The **vertex** is the point the insect turned about. One **arm** is that line in the **first position** (the flat line), and the other arm is the same line on the insect's body **after turning**. The angle between them is the amount of rotation: a quarter turn, for instance, is 90°.

## 2.7 Types of angles by their measures

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Type | Measure |
> |---|---|
> | **acute** | more than 0° and less than 90° |
> | **right** | exactly 90° |
> | **obtuse** | more than 90° and less than 180° |
> | **straight** | exactly 180° |
> | **reflex** | more than 180° and less than 360° |
> | **full turn** | 360° |

![Examples: acute angles 40°, 50°, 75°; obtuse angles 110°, 130°; reflex angles 220°, 270°](assets/img/class6/math/2/type_examples.svg)

**Why reflex angles exist:** two arms from a vertex always make **two** angles, one on each side, and together they make a full turn. If one is 100°, the other is 360° − 100° = 260°, which is a reflex angle.

**Example 7.** B, E, R lie on a straight line, ES is perpendicular to BR, and ∠TER = 80°. Find ∠BET and ∠SET.

![A straight line B E R; ES stands at a right angle; ET makes 80° with ER](assets/img/class6/math/2/bet.svg)

- ∠BET: ∠BER is a straight angle (180°) and ∠TER takes 80° of it, so **∠BET = 180° − 80° = 100°**.
- ∠SET: ∠SER = 90° (a right angle) and ∠TER = 80°, so **∠SET = 90° − 80° = 10°**.

### 5. Draw the letter Y so that the three angles are 150°, 60° and 150°.

> [!ANSWER]
> ![The letter Y: the two upper arms make 60°, and each makes 150° with the stem](assets/img/class6/math/2/letter_y.svg)
>
> 1. Draw the stem straight down from the joining point.
> 2. Draw the two upper arms making **60°** with each other, equally either side of straight up.
> 3. Check: 150° + 60° + 150° = **360°**, a full turn round the joining point, as it must be.

### 6. The Ashoka Chakra has 24 spokes. What is the angle between two neighbouring spokes? What is the largest acute angle between any two spokes?

> [!ANSWER]
> ![The 24-spoke Ashoka Chakra: neighbouring spokes are 15° apart; 75° is the largest acute angle](assets/img/class6/math/2/chakra.svg)
>
> 1. The 24 equal gaps share the full turn: 360° ÷ 24 = **15°**.
> 2. Angles between spokes are multiples of 15°: 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, **75°**, 90°, …
> 3. The largest that is still less than 90° is **75°** (5 gaps).

### 7. Puzzle: I am an acute angle. Double me: still acute. Triple me: still acute. Four times me: still acute! But five times me is obtuse. What can I be?

> [!ANSWER]
> Call the angle x.
> 1. **4x is acute**, so 4x < 90°, so x < 22.5°. (Then 2x and 3x are smaller, so they're acute too.)
> 2. **5x is obtuse**, so 5x > 90°, so x > 18°. (And 5x < 180° easily.)
>
> So **x is more than 18° and less than 22.5°**. In whole degrees: **19°, 20°, 21° or 22°**. (Check 22°: 4 × 22 = 88 acute ✓, 5 × 22 = 110 obtuse ✓. And 18° fails: 5 × 18 = 90 is right, not obtuse.)

## In a nutshell

- **Point:** an exact place, no size. **Segment AB:** the shortest path from A to B (two ends). **Line AB:** goes on for ever both ways (no ends). **Ray AB:** starts at A, goes on for ever through B (first letter = start).
- Through two points there is **exactly one line**; through one point, endlessly many.
- **Angle:** two rays from one point (vertex); its **size is the amount of turn**, not the arm length. Name it with the vertex in the middle: ∠ABC.
- Compare angles by **superimposing** (vertices and one arm together) or with a circle; **measure** them in **degrees**: full turn 360°, straight 180°, right 90°.
- **Types:** acute (< 90°), right (90°), obtuse (90°–180°), straight (180°), reflex (180°–360°).
- **Protractor:** centre on the vertex, one arm on 0, read from the 0 on that arm. Reflex angle = 360° − the other angle.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** ray AB ≠ ray BA (the first letter is the start) · longer arms do **not** make a bigger angle · read the protractor row that starts at **0 on your arm** (else you get 180° minus the answer) · the protractor's **centre** must sit on the vertex · a marked curve going round the **outside** means a reflex angle · "acute" means **less than** 90°, so 90° itself is right, not acute.
