## The building blocks

| Term | What it is | Written as |
|---|---|---|
| **Point** | a precise **location** — no length, breadth or height | `Z`, `P`, `T` |
| **Line segment** | the **shortest path** between two points, **including** both ends | `AB` or `BA` |
| **Line** | a segment **extended forever in both directions** | `AB` or `l`, `m` |
| **Ray** | starts at one point and goes on **endlessly in one direction** | `AP` (start first!) |

- **Through one point:** infinitely many lines can be drawn.
- **Through two points:** exactly **one** line. Any two points determine a **unique** line.

> [!WARNING]
> **A ray's name is not reversible.** `AP` starts at A and passes through P; `PA` would start at P and head the opposite way — a different ray. But a **line segment** `AB` = `BA`, and a **line** `AB` = `BA`, because neither has a starting point.

> [!TIP]
> If B is another point along the ray `AP`, then `AB` names the **same ray** — what matters is the **starting point** and the **direction**, not which point you pick further along.

## Angles

**An angle is formed by two rays sharing a common starting point.**

- The shared point is the **vertex**; the two rays are the **arms**.
- Named with **three letters, vertex in the middle**: `∠DBE` or `∠EBD`.

> [!WARNING]
> **Only use the single-letter name (`∠P`) when there's exactly one angle at that vertex.** If several angles meet at P, `∠P` is ambiguous — you must say `∠APB`, `∠APC`, and so on.

### The size of an angle is a TURN

**The size of an angle is the amount of rotation needed about the vertex to move the first arm onto the second.**

Opening a book cover further makes a bigger angle; opening scissors wider makes a bigger angle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Making the arms longer does NOT make the angle bigger.** The angle measures the *turn* between the arms, not how far they extend. Two angles with very different arm lengths can be exactly equal — a fact the "passing through a slit" activity proves: only the *angle* decides whether the rotating arms fit, never their length.

## Comparing angles

**By superimposition:** place one angle on the other so the **vertices coincide** and one pair of arms lines up. Whichever angle's second arm is further round is the larger.

- If the vertex **and both arms** coincide exactly, the angles are **equal** — the same amount of rotation is needed for each.

**Without superimposition:** lay a **transparent circle** centred on the vertex, mark where the arms cross the rim, and carry that mark to the other angle. This is the idea a **protractor** is built on.

## Special angles

Watch a book cover turn:

| Angle | Turn | Degrees |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute** | less than a quarter turn | `0° < a < 90°` |
| **Right angle** | exactly a **quarter** turn | **90°** |
| **Obtuse** | between a quarter and a half turn | `90° < a < 180°` |
| **Straight angle** | exactly a **half** turn — arms in a straight line | **180°** |
| **Full turn** | all the way round | **360°** |

- **A straight angle contains exactly two right angles**, so a right angle is **half** a straight angle.
- Two lines meeting at right angles are **perpendicular**.
- **Acute** means *sharp*, **obtuse** means *blunt* — the names describe how the corner looks.

> [!TIP]
> **Folding proves a right angle exactly.** Fold a straight angle `∠AOB` so that arm `OB` lands exactly on `OA` — the crease splits it into two angles that must be **equal** (superimposition shows it), and two equal angles making 180° are **90°** each. No measuring needed.

## Measuring in degrees

Divide the full turn at the centre of a circle into **360 equal parts**. Each part is **1 degree (1°)**.

**The measure of an angle is the number of 1° parts it contains.**

- Full turn **360°** · straight angle **180°** (half of 360) · right angle **90°** (half of 180).

> [!NOTE]
> **Why 360?** Nobody knows for certain, but the choice is ancient — the **Rigveda** speaks of a wheel with 360 spokes, and Indian, Persian, Babylonian and Egyptian calendars used 360-day years. The practical reason it stuck: **360 is the smallest number divisible by every number up to 10 except 7** — so a circle splits into 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 or 10 equal parts with a **whole number** of degrees each. It's also divisible by 12 (months) and 24 (hours).

### Using a protractor

1. Put the protractor's **centre on the vertex** of the angle.
2. Line one arm up with the **0° mark**.
3. Read where the **other arm** crosses the scale.

**Why two sets of numbers?** So you can measure from **either side** — an angle opening from the left uses one scale, from the right the other. **Pick the scale whose 0 sits on your chosen arm**, and stay on that scale.

> [!TIP]
> If neither arm is on 0, you can still measure: read **both** arms and **subtract**. Arms at 20 and 55 → the angle is `55 − 20 = 35°`. Lining up with 0 just saves you the subtraction.
