## Why start here?

Every shape you will ever study — triangles, squares, circles, the letter M, the Ashoka Chakra — is built out of a few very simple ideas: **points**, **lines** and **angles**. If these are clear, the rest of geometry is just putting them together. This chapter builds those bricks one at a time.

## Point

Press the sharp tip of a pencil on paper. You get a tiny dot. The sharper the pencil, the tinier the dot. Now imagine making it *so* sharp that the dot has **no size at all** — only a position. That is a **point**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A point marks an exact location. It has no length, no breadth and no height.**

Things that *model* a point: the tip of a compass, the sharpened end of a pencil, the point of a needle.

We name points with a **single capital letter**: point A, point P, point Z.

## Line segment

Mark two points A and B on paper. Fold the paper so that the crease passes through both. The crease between A and B is a **line segment**.

Now think of all the ways to travel from A to B along the paper — wiggly paths, curved paths, zig-zags. The straight one is the **shortest**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A line segment is the shortest path between two points.** It **includes** both points, called its **end points**. We write it as **AB** or **BA** — same segment either way, because it has no direction.

## Line

Take segment AB and imagine extending it beyond A **and** beyond B, on and on, forever. That's a **line**. We draw arrowheads at both ends to show it never stops.

- Named by two points on it — **line AB** — or by a small letter: **line l**, **line m**.
- Line AB = line BA (no start, no end, so no direction to worry about).

> [!NOTE]
> **How many lines through points?**
> - Through **one** point you can draw **as many lines as you like** — infinitely many, spinning round it like spokes.
> - Through **two** points you can draw **exactly one** line.
>
> **Why only one?** Try it: once the line has to go through A *and* B, its direction is fixed. Any other "line" through both would have to bend — and lines are straight. So **two points decide a line**.

## Ray

A torch beam starts at the torch and goes on in one direction. Sunlight starts at the Sun and streams outward. That's the idea of a **ray**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A ray starts at one point and goes on endlessly in ONE direction.** Ray **AP** starts at **A** and passes through **P**. The **starting point is always written first**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Ray AP is NOT ray PA.** AP starts at A and heads towards P; PA starts at P and heads towards A — the opposite way. Different starting points, different rays.
>
> But if B is another point on ray OA (beyond O, in A's direction), then **ray OB is the same ray as OA**: same start, same direction. What matters is **where it starts and which way it goes**, not which point you pick to name the direction.

### Summary

| Object | Ends | Can you swap the letters? | Picture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Line segment AB | 2 end points | yes, AB = BA | `A●──────●B` |
| Line AB | none | yes, AB = BA | `◀──A────B──▶` |
| Ray AB | 1 start (A) | **no** | `A●──────B──▶` |

**Worked example (from the book, Fig. 2.4).** Points L, M, P, Q, R are joined in a chain L–M–P–Q–R. The segments are **LM, MP, PQ, QR**. L and R are each on just **one** segment (they're the ends). M, P and Q are each on **two** segments (each joins two neighbours).

## Angle

Open a book slowly. The two covers start together and swing apart. Open scissors. Look at the hands of a clock. In each case two straight parts come out of **one shared point** and spread apart. That spread is an **angle**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An angle is formed by two rays that start from the same point.**
> - The common starting point is the **vertex**.
> - The two rays are the **arms**.

### Naming an angle

Angle with vertex B and arms BD and BE is written **∠DBE** or **∠EBD**.

> [!WARNING]
> **The vertex letter must be in the MIDDLE.** ∠BDE would mean an angle at D — a completely different angle.
>
> You may shorten it to **∠B** only if B is the vertex of **just one** angle. If several angles meet at B, "∠B" doesn't say which one you mean.

**Worked example — three points.** Mark A, B, C not on one line. Join every pair: you get **3 lines** AB, BC, CA, and you can name **3 angles**: ∠ABC, ∠BCA, ∠CAB (one at each point).

**Worked example — four points** (no three on a line). Joining every pair gives **6 lines**: AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD. At each point, three lines meet, making **3 angles** there (for A: ∠BAC, ∠CAD, ∠BAD). 4 points × 3 angles = **12 angles**.

> [!TIP]
> The 6 lines among 4 points is the complete-graph pattern from Chapter 1 — 1, 3, 6, 10 lines. Everything connects!

## The size of an angle — it's a turn

Think of an angle as **turning** one arm about the vertex until it lies on the other arm. A small turn → small angle. A big turn → big angle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The size of an angle is the amount of rotation about the vertex needed to move one arm onto the other.**

> [!WARNING]
> **Longer arms do NOT make a bigger angle.** Draw a tiny "V" and a huge "V" with the same opening — the angle is the same. The size is about how much you **turn**, not how far the arms reach. The book's slit activity shows this: two strips joined at a pin pass through a slit only when the *angle* between them fits the slit, whatever their length.

## Comparing angles

**Easy cases:** when one angle is clearly wider, you can just look. But 89° and 91° look almost the same — then you need a method.

### Method 1: superimposition

**Superimpose** means "place one on top of the other". Trace one angle on thin paper and lay it on the other so that:

1. the **vertices coincide** (sit exactly on top of each other), and
2. **one arm of each lines up**.

Now look at the second arms. Whichever angle's second arm lies **further round** is the **bigger** angle. If the second arms also line up exactly, the angles are **equal**.

**Why it works:** both angles start from the same arm and the same vertex, so the one that needs the **bigger turn** to reach its second arm must be the bigger angle — that's the definition of angle size.

> [!NOTE]
> Superimposition is used to compare lots of things — line segments (lay one along the other), squares, circles. If one covers the other exactly, they are the same size.

### Method 2: a transparent circle

If you can't move the angles, put a **transparent circle** with its centre on the vertex of the first angle. Mark where both arms cross the rim. Move the circle to the second angle and compare the marks. This is exactly the idea behind a **protractor**.

**Worked example (book).** ∠AOB is made of ∠AOX + ∠XOY + ∠YOB. So **∠AOB is bigger than ∠XOY** — a whole is bigger than any of its parts.

## Special angles

Open a book cover from closed all the way round:

| Name | Turn | Degrees |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute** | less than a quarter turn | more than 0°, less than 90° |
| **Right angle** | exactly **¼ turn** | **90°** |
| **Obtuse** | more than a right angle, less than a straight angle | between 90° and 180° |
| **Straight angle** | **½ turn** — the two arms form a straight line | **180°** |
| **Reflex** | more than a straight angle, less than a full turn | between 180° and 360° |
| **Full turn** | all the way round | **360°** |

> [!TIP]
> **Why the names?** *Acute* means **sharp** — a narrow angle makes a sharp point (think of a needle tip). *Obtuse* means **blunt** — a wide angle makes a blunt, rounded-looking corner. *Reflex* angles "bend back" past straight.

### Making a right angle by folding

Fold a sheet so you get a straight crease — that crease is a straight angle (180°) at any point on it. Now fold again so that one half of the crease falls **exactly** on the other half. The new crease makes two angles.

**Why they're exactly right angles:** the two halves lay **exactly** on top of each other, so (by superimposition) the two angles are **equal**. Two equal angles making up a straight angle must each be half of it — **a right angle**. No measuring needed!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - A **straight angle = 2 right angles** → a right angle is half a straight angle and a **quarter of a full turn**.
> - Two lines that meet at a right angle are **perpendicular**. Two perpendicular creases make **4 right angles** around the point where they cross.

## Measuring angles in degrees

To measure length we use a unit (cm). For angles we need a unit too.

Divide a full turn into **360 equal parts**. Each part is **one degree**, written **1°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - Full turn = **360°**
> - Straight angle (½ turn) = 360 ÷ 2 = **180°**
> - Right angle (¼ turn) = 360 ÷ 4 = **90°**
>
> **The degree measure of an angle is the number of 1° parts that fit inside it.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Why 360?** Nobody knows for sure, but it is very old. The **Rigveda** mentions a wheel with 360 spokes, and ancient Indian, Persian, Babylonian and Egyptian calendars had 360-day years. A good reason it survived: **360 can be divided exactly by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, …** — every number up to 10 except 7. So a circle splits into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths… with a whole number of degrees each.

**Worked example (book, "Think!").** A straight angle is cut into 8 equal angles. Each is 180° ÷ 8 = **22.5°**. (A half-degree is fine — degrees can be split too.)

### Using a protractor

A protractor is a half-circle marked from 0° to 180°.

1. Put the protractor's **centre point exactly on the vertex**.
2. Line up its **base line along one arm**, so that arm points to **0°**.
3. Read the number where the **other arm** crosses the scale.

**Why are there two rows of numbers?** One row counts 0 → 180 from the right, the other from the left. That way you can measure an angle that opens either way. **Use the row that reads 0 on the arm you lined up**, and stay on that row.

> [!TIP]
> **Arms not on 0? Subtract.** If one arm is at 20 and the other at 55, the angle is 55 − 20 = **35°**. Lining up on 0 just saves you this step. This is how the book reads all the angles in its fan figure: ∠POQ = 35°, ∠POR = 95°, so **∠QOR = 95 − 35 = 60°**.

### Measuring a reflex angle

A protractor only goes to 180°. To measure a reflex angle, measure the **small angle** on the other side and subtract from 360°.

**Worked example (book).** The non-marked angle is 100°. The marked reflex angle = 360° − 100° = **260°**.

**Why:** the two angles together go once round the vertex — a full turn of 360°.

## Angles in real life — worked examples from the book

**Clock hands.** The 12 numbers split the full circle into 12 equal gaps, so each gap is 360° ÷ 12 = **30°**.
- 1 o'clock → 1 gap → **30°**
- 2 o'clock → 2 gaps → **60°**
- 3 o'clock → **90°** (a right angle)
- 4 o'clock → **120°**
- 6 o'clock → **180°** (a straight angle)

**A door.** The vertex is the hinge line; the arms are the door edge and the wall (where the closed door was). Opening the door wider = bigger angle.

**A swing / a slide.** The angle is between the resting (straight-down) position and the position you swing to. One arm is often invisible (the imaginary vertical line).

**The Ashoka Chakra.** It has **24** equally spaced spokes, so the angle between neighbours = 360° ÷ 24 = **15°**. Angles between any two spokes are multiples of 15°: 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, … The **largest acute** one is **75°** (90° is not acute — it's a right angle).

**Letter shapes.** The letter Y with angles 150°, 60°, 150°: they add to 360° — a full turn around the centre point, as they must.

**Using a straight line (book's "Let's Explore").** If ∠TER = 80° and B, E, R lie on a straight line, then ∠BET = 180° − 80° = **100°**, because the two angles together make the straight angle.

### The puzzle

*"I am an acute angle. Double me — still acute. Triple me — still acute. Four times me — still acute. Five times me — obtuse!"*

**Solve it step by step:**
- 4 × me is acute → 4 × me < 90 → me < 22.5.
- 5 × me is obtuse → 5 × me > 90 → me > 18.
- So me is between 18° and 22.5°. In whole degrees: **19°, 20°, 21° or 22°**.

(Check 22: 4 × 22 = 88 acute ✓, 5 × 22 = 110 obtuse ✓. Check 18: 5 × 18 = 90 — a right angle, **not** obtuse ✗.)

## In a nutshell

- **Point** = exact location, no size. **Segment** = shortest path between two points, with two end points. **Line** = goes forever both ways. **Ray** = one start, goes forever one way (start letter first!).
- One point → infinitely many lines; two points → exactly one line.
- **Angle** = two rays from one vertex. Name it with the vertex in the middle.
- **Angle size = amount of turn**, not arm length. Compare by **superimposing**.
- Full turn **360°**, straight **180°**, right **90°**. Acute < 90° < obtuse < 180° < reflex < 360°.
- Protractor: centre on vertex, one arm on 0, read the other arm; for reflex angles use 360° − small angle.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** ray AP ≠ ray PA · vertex letter goes in the middle · long arms ≠ big angle · read the protractor scale that starts at 0 on your arm · 90° is right (not acute), 180° is straight (not obtuse) · a clock gap is 30°, not 5° or 60°.
