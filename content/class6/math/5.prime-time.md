## Common multiples & common factors

- **Multiples** of a number: what you get counting *up* in steps of it — multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, …
- **Factors (divisors)** of a number: the numbers that divide it **exactly** — factors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.
- A **common multiple** of two numbers is a multiple of **both**; a **common factor** divides **both**.

**The idli-vada game:** say "idli" on multiples of 3, "vada" on multiples of 5, and **"idli-vada" on multiples of both**. The first idli-vada is **15**, then 30, 45, 60 — the **common multiples** of 3 and 5.

**The jump game:** starting at 0, jumping in steps of size *j*, you land only on **multiples of j**. So you reach a treasure on 24 only if **j is a factor of 24** — that is, j = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 or 24.

- To reach treasures on **two** numbers with one jump size, that size must be a **common factor** of both. Treasures on 14 and 36: factors of 14 are 1, 2, 7, 14; factors of 36 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36 → common factors **1 and 2**, so only those jump sizes work.

> [!TIP]
> **Factors sit at or below the number; multiples sit at or above it.** A factor has to divide it, and a multiple has to contain it — that alone rules out most wrong answers.

## Prime & composite

**Arrange the number as a rectangle.** 12 figs can be laid out as 1×12, 2×6 or 3×4 — several ways. 7 figs can only be 1×7. The rows and columns of each rectangle are **factors**.

- **Prime number: exactly two factors** — 1 and itself. First few: **2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19**.
- **Composite number: more than two factors** — 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, …
- **1 is neither prime nor composite** — it has only **one** factor, itself, so it fails the "exactly two" test.

> [!WARNING]
> **2 is the only even prime.** Every other even number has 2 as a factor *in addition to* 1 and itself → three or more factors → composite.

### Sieve of Eratosthenes — finding all primes to 100

1. Cross out **1** (neither prime nor composite).
2. Circle **2**, cross out all later multiples of 2.
3. Next uncircled number is **3** — circle it, cross out all later multiples of 3.
4. Next is **5** — circle, cross out its multiples. Continue.

Everything circled is **prime**; everything crossed out (except 1) is **composite**.

**Why it works:** crossing out the multiples of a number is exactly crossing out everything that *has* that number as a factor — i.e. everything composite. Whatever survives has no factor other than 1 and itself, which is the definition of prime. Named after **Eratosthenes**, a Greek mathematician of about 2200 years ago.

> [!NOTE]
> **Twin primes** are primes differing by 2 — (3, 5), (5, 7), (11, 13), (17, 19), (29, 31), (41, 43), (59, 61), (71, 73).
> **Perfect number:** the sum of all its factors is **twice** the number. `28 = 1+2+4+7+14+28 = 56 = 2 × 28` ✓ — and **6** is the perfect number below 10.

## Co-prime numbers

**Two numbers are co-prime if their only common factor is 1.**

In the treasure game with jump size 1 banned, a pair is "safe" exactly when it's **co-prime** — no jump size can reach both.

- 4 and 9 → co-prime (safe). 12 and 26 → **not** (both even, jump size 2 reaches them).
- Co-prime numbers need **not** be prime themselves: 4 and 9 are both composite.

> [!TIP]
> **For a co-prime pair, the first common multiple is just the product.** 4 and 9 → 36 = 4 × 9. When the numbers share a factor, the first common multiple comes in **below** the product (6 and 8 share 2 → first common multiple 24, not 48).

## Prime factorisation

Keep breaking a number into factors until **only primes are left**.

`56 = 4 × 14 = (2 × 2) × (2 × 7) = ` **2 × 2 × 2 × 7**

- Those primes are the **prime factors**; the whole product is the **prime factorisation**.
- **Every number greater than 1 has one.** A prime's own factorisation is just itself (7 → 7). **1 has none** — no prime divides it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The prime factorisation is UNIQUE — only the order can change.** Break 36 down any way you like (2×18, 3×12, 4×9, 6×6) and you always end with **two 2s and two 3s**. That's why order doesn't matter: multiplication gives the same result whichever order you multiply in.

### Use 1 — checking co-prime properly

⚠️ **You cannot conclude anything from *one* factorisation.** Writing `56 = 14 × 4` and `63 = 21 × 3` shows no common factor — but it's wrong: `56 = 7 × 8` and `63 = 9 × 7` share **7**. Splitting a number one way doesn't reveal *all* its factors.

**Prime factorisation settles it, because it finds every prime factor there is:**

- `56 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 7` · `63 = 3 × 3 × 7` → share **7** → **not co-prime**.
- `80 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 5` · `63 = 3 × 3 × 7` → **no common prime** → **co-prime**.

**Why no common prime is enough:** if they shared any common factor bigger than 1, that factor's own prime factors would have to show up in *both* lists. Nothing shared means no such factor exists.

### Use 2 — checking divisibility without dividing

**One number is divisible by another when the second's prime factorisation is *contained inside* the first's.**

- **Is 168 divisible by 12?** `168 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 7`, `12 = 2 × 2 × 3`. The 12 sits inside → regroup as `(2 × 2 × 3) × (2 × 7) = 12 × 14` ✓ **yes**.
- **Is 75 divisible by 21?** `75 = 3 × 5 × 5`, `21 = 3 × 7`. **7 is missing** from 75 → **no**.
- **Is 42 divisible by 12?** `42 = 2 × 3 × 7`, `12 = 2 × 2 × 3`. Both primes appear — but 12 needs **two** 2s and 42 has only **one** → **no**.

> [!WARNING]
> Having the right primes isn't enough — you need **enough copies of each**. That's the trap in the 42 ÷ 12 example.

## Divisibility tests

| Divisible by | Test | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **10** | ends in **0** | multiples of 10 are 10, 20, 30… — always a 0 |
| **5** | ends in **0 or 5** | multiples of 5 alternate …0, …5 |
| **2** | ends in **0, 2, 4, 6, 8** | the even digits |
| **4** | **last two digits** form a multiple of 4 | everything above the last two digits is a multiple of 100, and **100 is divisible by 4** — so only the leftover two digits can spoil it |
| **8** | **last three digits** form a multiple of 8 | everything above is a multiple of 1000, and **1000 is divisible by 8** |

> [!WARNING]
> **The last digit alone can't test for 4.** 12 and 22 end alike, but only 12 is a multiple of 4; same for 14/24 and 16/26. You genuinely need two digits.

> [!NOTE]
> Tests for **3, 6, 7 and 9** come in later classes (they use digit sums, not last digits).

> [!TIP]
> **Leap years** are multiples of 4 — except century years, which must be divisible by **400**. So 2024 ✓, 1900 ✗, 2000 ✓.
