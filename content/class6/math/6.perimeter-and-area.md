## Perimeter — the distance around

**The perimeter of a closed plane figure is the distance covered going once around its boundary.** For a polygon, it's simply the **sum of all its side lengths**.

| Shape | Perimeter | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Rectangle** (l, b) | `2 × (l + b)` | opposite sides are equal, so l and b each appear **twice** |
| **Square** (side s) | `4 × s` | all four sides equal — add s four times, i.e. multiply by 4 |
| **Triangle** | sum of the three sides | no shortcut — the sides can all differ |
| **Equilateral triangle** | `3 × s` | all three sides equal |
| **Regular polygon**, n sides | `n × s` | a **regular** polygon has all sides equal |

**Worked:** a 12 cm × 8 cm rectangle → `2 × (12 + 8) = 2 × 20 = ` **40 cm**.

> [!TIP]
> **Read the question for the word "around."** Lace round a tablecloth, tape round a photo frame, fencing round a field, running round a track — all perimeter. Answers are in **cm / m**, never squared.

**Cost problems:** `cost = perimeter × rate per metre`. Fencing a 150 m × 120 m park at ₹40/m → perimeter `2 × 270 = 540 m` → `540 × 40 = ` **₹21,600**.

**Rounds:** distance = `perimeter × number of rounds`. Three rounds of a 75 m square park → `4 × 75 = 300 m` per round → `3 × 300 = ` **900 m**.

## Same wire, new shape

A wire bent into one shape and re-bent into another keeps **the same total length** — so the **perimeters are equal**.

A 5 cm × 3 cm rectangle of wire has perimeter `2 × 8 = 16 cm`. Bent into a square: `4s = 16` → **s = 4 cm**.

## Area — the region enclosed

**Area is the amount of region enclosed by a closed figure**, measured in **square units** (sq cm, sq m).

- **Area of a rectangle = length × width** · **Area of a square = side × side**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why area is measured in squares.** Squares tile a region **completely, with no gaps and no overlaps**, so counting them gives an exact measure. Circles can't — pack the same rectangle with circles two different ways and you get 42 one time and 44 the next, because of the gaps between them. A square's flat sides fit perfectly against its neighbours; that reliability is what makes it the right unit.

### Estimating area on grid paper

Trace the shape onto squared paper (1 square = 1 sq unit) and count:

1. A **full** square counts as **1 sq unit**.
2. **Ignore** portions **less than half** a square.
3. **More than half** a square counts as a **full 1 sq unit**.
4. **Exactly half** counts as **1/2 sq unit**.

**Why rules 2 and 3 are fair:** the bits you throw away (under half) and the bits you round up (over half) roughly cancel each other out across the whole shape — so the estimate lands close to the true area.

### Area by splitting

An irregular figure can be split into **rectangles (and triangles)**, each area worked out, then **added**.

**Leftover areas are subtractions:** a 5 m × 4 m floor with a 3 m × 3 m carpet →
`20 − 9 = ` **11 sq m** uncarpeted.

## Area of a triangle

**Cut a rectangle along its diagonal** → two triangles that **overlap exactly**. So each is **half** the rectangle.

> **Area of a triangle = half the area of the rectangle that encloses it**

**What if the triangle isn't a neat half?** Drop a line from the top vertex to split it into two right triangles. Each is half of its own little rectangle, so together they're **half of the whole rectangle** — the rule holds for *any* triangle, not just right ones.

> [!TIP]
> Two triangles that look completely different can have **the same area** — area doesn't depend on shape, only on how much region is enclosed.

## Same area, different perimeter

**These are independent — one does not fix the other.**

- Nine unit squares (area 9 sq units) can be arranged as a **3×3 square with perimeter 12**, or strung out in a **line with perimeter 20**.
- For a fixed area, the **most square-like rectangle has the LEAST perimeter**, and the **longest, thinnest one has the GREATEST perimeter**.
  - Area 24: `4 × 6` → perimeter 20 (least) · `1 × 24` → perimeter 50 (greatest).

**Why:** a long thin shape has lots of boundary hugging very little region; a compact shape wraps the same region in far less boundary.

> [!WARNING]
> **Equal areas do NOT mean equal perimeters, and equal perimeters do NOT mean equal areas.** This trips people up constantly — check which one the question actually asks for.

### Adding a square to a figure

Attach one unit square to a figure and the perimeter can **increase, decrease, or stay the same** — it depends how many of the new square's sides get *hidden* against the existing shape. Tucking it into a notch can even shorten the boundary.

## Non-standard units

When a figure has slanted edges, its perimeter can be recorded in **two kinds of unit** — e.g. **6s + 3d** meaning 6 straight units plus 3 diagonal units. A diagonal step is **longer** than a straight one, so you cannot simply call the total 9.
