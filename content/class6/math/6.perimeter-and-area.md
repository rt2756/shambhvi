## Two different questions about a shape

Imagine a rectangular garden.
- If you want to put a **fence around it**, you need to know **how long the boundary is**. That's the **perimeter**.
- If you want to **plant grass on it**, you need to know **how much ground is inside**. That's the **area**.

The same garden has both, but they measure completely different things — one is a **length**, the other is an amount of **surface**. Most mistakes in this chapter come from mixing them up.

## Perimeter — the distance around

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The perimeter of a closed figure is the distance covered when you go around its boundary once.** For a polygon (a closed shape made of straight sides), it's simply the **sum of all its side lengths**.

Perimeter is a length, so it's measured in **cm, m, km** — never "square" anything.

### Rectangle

A rectangle ABCD with length 12 cm and breadth 8 cm. Walk around it: AB + BC + CD + DA = 12 + 8 + 12 + 8 = **40 cm**.

Notice each length appears **twice** (opposite sides are equal). So:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Perimeter of a rectangle = 2 × (length + breadth)**
>
> **Why:** going round once you travel one length and one breadth, then another length and another breadth — the pair (length + breadth) twice.

Check: 2 × (12 + 8) = 2 × 20 = 40 cm ✓.

### Square

All four sides are equal, so adding side + side + side + side is the same as **4 × side**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Perimeter of a square = 4 × side**

### Triangles and regular polygons

- **Triangle:** add the three sides. No shortcut, because the sides can all be different.
- **Equilateral triangle** (all sides equal): **3 × side**.
- **Regular polygon** with *n* equal sides: **n × side**. (A regular hexagon: 6 × side.)

### Working backwards

Often you know the perimeter and need a side.

**Worked examples (book):**
- Rectangle, perimeter 14 cm, breadth 2 cm. Length + breadth = 14 ÷ 2 = 7, so length = 7 − 2 = **5 cm**.
- Square, perimeter 20 cm → side = 20 ÷ 4 = **5 cm**.
- Rectangle, perimeter 12 m, length 3 m → length + breadth = 6 → breadth = **3 m** (it's actually a square!).
- Triangle, perimeter 55 cm, two sides 20 cm and 14 cm → third side = 55 − 20 − 14 = **21 cm**.
- A 36 cm string made into a square → side 36 ÷ 4 = **9 cm**; an equilateral triangle → 36 ÷ 3 = **12 cm**; a regular hexagon → 36 ÷ 6 = **6 cm**.

### Same wire, new shape

A 5 cm × 3 cm rectangle is made of wire. Straighten it and bend it into a square. The wire's length doesn't change, so the **perimeter stays the same**: 2 × (5 + 3) = 16 cm. Square side = 16 ÷ 4 = **4 cm**.

### Perimeter in real life

> [!TIP]
> Look for words like **around, border, boundary, fence, lace, frame, rounds**. They all mean perimeter.

**Cost of fencing.** A park 150 m × 120 m, fence ₹40 per metre. Perimeter = 2 × (150 + 120) = 540 m. Cost = 540 × 40 = **₹21,600**.

**Several rounds.** A field 230 m × 160 m fenced with **3 rounds** of rope. One round = 2 × (230 + 160) = 780 m. Three rounds = **2,340 m**.

**Running tracks (book).** Akshi runs round a 70 m × 40 m track: one round = 2 × (70 + 40) = 220 m; 5 rounds = **1,100 m**. Toshi runs round a 60 m × 30 m track: one round = 180 m; 7 rounds = **1,260 m**. **Toshi ran further** — more rounds on a smaller track beat fewer rounds on a bigger one here.

Where is Akshi after 1,000 m? 4 rounds = 880 m, so she's **120 m** into her 5th round.

**Fair races (book's Deep Dive).** On square tracks of side 100 m and 150 m, runners start at different points (A and B) so that both run the same distance to a common flag: 100 + 100 + 100 + 50 = 350 m and 125 + 150 + 75 = 350 m. That's why runners on outer lanes start further ahead — **outer lanes have bigger perimeters**.

### Straight and diagonal units

On dot paper, some edges go straight (one side of a square) and some go **diagonally** (corner to corner of a square). A diagonal is **longer** than a side. So a triangle with 3 straight and 3 diagonal edges does **not** have perimeter 6 units — we write it as **3s + 3d** (s = straight unit, d = diagonal unit).

> [!WARNING]
> You can't add straight and diagonal units together as if they were the same — that's like adding centimetres and inches.

## Area — the region inside

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The area of a closed figure is the amount of region (surface) it encloses.** It's measured in **square units**: sq cm, sq m, sq units.

### Why squares?

To measure a length we use a small length (1 cm) and count how many fit. To measure a surface we need a small **surface** and count how many fit. The best choice is a **unit square** (1 cm by 1 cm = 1 sq cm).

**Why squares and not circles?** Squares fit together with **no gaps and no overlaps**, so counting them gives an exact answer. Circles leave gaps — pack a rectangle with coins one way and you might fit 42, another way 44. A unit that gives different answers isn't a good unit.

### Area of a rectangle

Draw a rectangle 5 cm long and 3 cm wide on squared paper. Count the squares: 3 rows of 5 = **15 sq cm**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a rectangle = length × width**
> **Area of a square = side × side**
>
> **Why:** the rectangle splits into *width* rows, each with *length* unit squares. Rows × squares per row = length × width.

**Worked examples (book):**
- A garden 25 m long has area 300 sq m. Width = 300 ÷ 25 = **12 m**. (Area ÷ one side = the other side.)
- A garden 50 m long, area 1,000 sq m → width **20 m**.
- Tiling a plot 500 m × 200 m at ₹8 per hundred sq m. Area = 1,00,000 sq m = 1,000 hundreds → cost 1,000 × 8 = **₹8,000**.
- A coconut grove 100 m × 50 m, each tree needs 25 sq m. Area = 5,000 sq m → 5,000 ÷ 25 = **200 trees**.

### Estimating area on squared paper

For a curvy shape (a leaf, a hand), trace it on squared paper and count:

1. A **full** square → 1 sq unit.
2. **More than half** a square → count as **1**.
3. **Exactly half** → count **½**.
4. **Less than half** → **ignore** it.

**Why this is fair:** the bits you round up (just over half) and the bits you throw away (just under half) roughly balance out over the whole shape. The estimate ends up close to the real area.

### Area by splitting

An L-shape or other "blocky" shape isn't a rectangle, but you can **cut it into rectangles**, find each area and **add**.

**Worked example.** An L-shape: a 6 m × 3 m rectangle with a 2 m × 5 m rectangle attached. Area = 18 + 10 = **28 sq m**.

**Subtracting works too.** A floor 5 m × 4 m with a square carpet of side 3 m. Uncarpeted area = 20 − 9 = **11 sq m**.

**Worked example (book).** A garden 15 m × 12 m has four flower beds, each 2 m × 1 m, dug in the corners. Lawn area = 180 − 4 × 2 = 180 − 8 = **172 sq m**.

### House plans (book)

Charan's house is on a 35 ft × 30 ft plot → area **1,050 sq ft**, perimeter 2 × 65 = **130 ft**. Sharan's is 42 ft × 25 ft → area **1,050 sq ft** too, but perimeter 2 × 67 = **134 ft**. **Same area, different perimeters!** Inside, you find missing room sizes by subtracting known lengths from the total length along that wall.

### Area maze puzzles

In these, a big rectangle is cut into smaller ones with some areas and sides given. Use **area ÷ side = other side** again and again. E.g. a rectangle with area 30 sq cm and one side 6 cm → other side 5 cm; a neighbouring rectangle sharing that 5 cm side with length 3 cm has area 15 sq cm.

## Area of a triangle

Draw a rectangle and its diagonal. You get two triangles that fit **exactly** on top of each other (cut and check!). So each triangle is **half the rectangle**.

**What about a triangle that isn't a right triangle?** Put it inside a rectangle with the same base and the top corner touching the opposite side. Draw a line straight down from the top corner. Now the rectangle splits into two smaller rectangles, and the triangle into two right triangles — **each right triangle is half of its small rectangle**. Add them: the triangle is half of the whole rectangle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a triangle = half the area of the rectangle drawn around it** (same base, same height).

**Worked example.** A triangle sits in a 6 cm × 4 cm rectangle, touching both long sides. Its area = ½ × 24 = **12 sq cm**.

> [!TIP]
> Two triangles that look completely different can have the **same area**, as long as they fit into the same rectangle this way. Area cares about how much space, not about the shape.

### Tangram pieces (book)

The seven tangram pieces come from one big square. By laying pieces on top of each other you find:
- the two **large triangles** have equal area; the two **small triangles** have equal area;
- the **square**, the **parallelogram** and the **medium triangle** can each be covered exactly by the two small triangles → each is **twice** a small triangle;
- a large triangle is **four** small triangles.

So in "small triangles": 4 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = **16**. The big square is 16 times a small triangle. Rearranging the same 7 pieces into a rectangle keeps the **same area (16)** — nothing is added or removed — but the **perimeter can change**, because a different outline is exposed.

## Area and perimeter are independent

Take **9 unit squares**.
- Arranged as a 3 × 3 square → perimeter **12** (the smallest possible).
- Arranged in a line, 9 × 1 → perimeter **20** (the largest possible).
- In between, e.g. an L or zig-zag, you can get 14, 16, 18…

All have area **9**. So **the same area can have different perimeters**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> For a fixed area, **the more compact (square-like) the shape, the smaller the perimeter**; the longer and thinner, the bigger the perimeter.
>
> **Why:** a long thin strip has lots of edge wrapped around very little inside. A compact square keeps its squares huddled together, hiding many edges against each other.

**Rectangles of area 24:** 1 × 24 (perimeter 50), 2 × 12 (28), 3 × 8 (22), 4 × 6 (**20**, the least).

**Area A smaller but perimeter bigger (book).** Shape A: 18 × 1 rectangle → area 18, perimeter 38. Shape B: 5 × 4 rectangle → area 20, perimeter 18. A has less area but more perimeter.

> [!WARNING]
> **Equal area does NOT mean equal perimeter, and equal perimeter does NOT mean equal area.** Always check which one the question asks for.

### Adding a square

Stick one more unit square onto a shape. The perimeter can go **up, down, or stay the same**. It depends on how many sides of the new square touch the shape: each touching side hides one old edge **and** doesn't add a new one. Touch on 1 side → perimeter +2. Touch on 2 sides (in a corner notch) → no change. Touch on 3 sides (filling a gap) → perimeter −2.

### Folding a square in half (book)

Fold a square of side 2 units and cut along the fold: two rectangles of 2 × 1.
- Each rectangle's area (2) is **half** the square's (4) — not larger.
- Square perimeter = 8; each rectangle's perimeter = 6; together = 12 = **1½ × 8**. This is the statement that's always true, whatever the size.

**Why:** cutting creates **two new edges** (one on each piece), each as long as a side. So the total perimeter grows from 4 sides to 6 sides — 1½ times as much.

### Combining rectangles (book)

Find one rectangle with the same area as 5 m × 10 m plus 2 m × 7 m. Total area = 50 + 14 = 64 sq m. Any pair of sides multiplying to 64 works: **8 × 8**, 16 × 4, 32 × 2.

## In a nutshell

- **Perimeter** = distance around (cm, m). Rectangle 2 × (l + b); square 4 × side; regular polygon n × side; triangle = sum of sides.
- **Area** = surface inside (sq cm, sq m), measured with unit squares because they tile with no gaps. Rectangle l × w; square side × side.
- Work backwards: side = perimeter ÷ 4 (square); other side = area ÷ known side.
- Irregular shapes: split into rectangles, add (or subtract). Curvy shapes: count squares, more than half = 1, less than half = 0.
- **Triangle = half its surrounding rectangle.**
- Same area ≠ same perimeter. Compact shapes have the least perimeter.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** perimeter is in cm, area in sq cm · rectangle perimeter is 2 × (l + b), not l × b · diagonal units are longer than straight ones · "₹8 per hundred sq m" means divide the area by 100 first · cutting a shape adds perimeter but not area · count rounds × one round's perimeter.
