<!-- ===== What symmetry is ===== -->

What is a **symmetry**? Why is a picture of **clouds** not symmetrical?

> [!ANSWER]
> A symmetry is **a part or parts of a figure repeated in some definite pattern**.
>
> A flower, butterfly, rangoli and pinwheel all repeat parts in a regular way. Clouds have **no repeating pattern at all** — nothing about them comes back onto itself by folding or turning.

---

Define a **line of symmetry**.

> [!ANSWER]
> A line that **cuts a figure into two parts that overlap exactly when folded along it** — mirror halves.
>
> A figure that has one is said to have **reflection symmetry**.

---

<!-- ===== Counting lines ===== -->

How many lines of symmetry does a **square** have? Describe the folds.

> [!ANSWER]
> **4** — fold **vertically**, **horizontally**, and along **each of the two diagonals**. Every one gives two exactly matching halves.

---

Is a **rectangle's diagonal** a line of symmetry?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Fold a non-square rectangle along its diagonal and the two halves **overhang** — they don't match.
>
> A rectangle has only **2** lines of symmetry, through the middles of opposite sides. (A square is the special case where the diagonals *do* work, because its sides are all equal.)

---

How many lines of symmetry does each have: **equilateral triangle · isosceles triangle · scalene triangle**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Equilateral: 3** (one through each vertex) · **Isosceles: 1** (through the apex) · **Scalene: 0**.
>
> The more equal sides, the more mirror lines.

---

Draw a triangle with exactly **two** lines of symmetry. What happens?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It's impossible.**
>
> Two lines of symmetry would force **all three sides to be equal** — and an equilateral triangle immediately has a **third** line of symmetry too.
>
> So a triangle has **0, 1 or 3** lines — never exactly 2.

---

How many lines of symmetry does a **regular hexagon** have? State the general rule for a regular polygon.

> [!ANSWER]
> **6.**
>
> **General rule: a regular polygon with n sides has exactly n lines of symmetry** — because all its sides and angles are identical, so each side (and each vertex) gives its own mirror line.

---

How many lines of symmetry does a **circle** have?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Infinitely many** — **every diameter** is a line of symmetry.
>
> Every point on the rim is the same distance from the centre, so folding along *any* diameter matches the two halves perfectly.

---

<!-- ===== Making symmetric shapes ===== -->

You fold a paper in half, drop ink on one side, press and open. Is the result symmetric, and where is the line of symmetry?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes** — the pressing prints an exact mirror copy of the blot onto the other half.
>
> The **crease (fold line) is the line of symmetry**.

---

A square sheet is folded **twice** and a **single** hole is punched. How many holes appear when unfolded, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **4 holes.**
>
> **Why:** each fold **doubles** the layers, so one punch goes through `2 × 2 = 4` layers. Unfolding spreads those 4 holes out symmetrically.
>
> (One fold → 2 holes; three folds → 8.)

---

A paper is folded vertically and a cut is made. What can you say about the resulting hole?

> [!ANSWER]
> The hole is **symmetric about the fold line** — whatever is cut on one side is mirrored on the other.
>
> This is why paper folding and cutting is a reliable way to *generate* symmetric shapes: the fold guarantees the symmetry before you even cut.

---

<!-- ===== Rotational symmetry ===== -->

The windmill has **no line of symmetry** yet still looks symmetrical. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> Fold it any way and the halves don't overlap — so no reflection symmetry.
>
> But **rotate it 90° about its centre** and it looks exactly the same. That is **rotational symmetry**.
>
> The fixed point it turns about is the **centre of rotation**.

---

What is an **angle of symmetry**? List the windmill's.

> [!ANSWER]
> An angle through which a figure can be rotated so it looks **exactly the same**.
>
> The windmill's: **90°, 180°, 270°, 360°** — **4** angles of symmetry.

---

Why is **360°** always an angle of symmetry?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **full turn** brings *any* figure back to exactly where it started — so it always leaves the figure looking the same.
>
> Because it's automatic, a figure whose **only** angle of symmetry is 360° is said to have **no rotational symmetry**.

---

A strip returns to its original look only after a full 360° turn. Does it have rotational symmetry?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 360° is its only angle of symmetry, and that one is automatic for every figure — so it counts as having none.

---

List the angles of symmetry for figures of order **2, 3 and 4**. What pattern do you see?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Order 2:** 180°, 360° · **Order 3:** 120°, 240°, 360° · **Order 4:** 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°.
>
> **The pattern: every angle is a multiple of the smallest one.**
>
> **Why:** if a turn of *s* leaves the figure unchanged, doing it twice must too — so 2s works, then 3s, and so on until you reach a full 360°.

---

The smallest angle of symmetry of a figure is **60°**. What are the others?

> [!ANSWER]
> The multiples of 60 up to a full turn: **120°, 180°, 240°, 300°, 360°**.
>
> So the figure has **order 6**.

---

Can a figure have a smallest angle of symmetry of **(a) 45°** or **(b) 17°**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a) Yes** — `360 ÷ 45 = 8`, a whole number, so eight equal turns close the circle exactly.
>
> **(b) No** — `360 ÷ 17` is not a whole number, so repeated 17° turns would never land back on 360°.
>
> **The rule: a whole-number smallest angle of symmetry must be a FACTOR of 360.**

---

**60°** is an angle of symmetry of a figure, and it has **exactly two** angles of symmetry smaller than 60°. Find its smallest angle.

> [!ANSWER]
> The angles are multiples of the smallest angle **s**, and 60 must be one of them.
>
> Two angles lie below 60, so those are **s and 2s**, making 60 the **third** → `3s = 60` → **s = 20°**.
>
> Check: 20°, 40° are the two below 60° ✓ and `60 = 3 × 20` ✓

---

<!-- ===== Radial arms ===== -->

A figure has **3 radial arms** at unequal angles. Does it have rotational symmetry? How would you fix it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No** — only a full 360° turn restores it, because a partial turn moves each arm to a gap of a different size.
>
> **Fix:** space the arms **equally**, at `360 ÷ 3 = ` **120°** apart. Then a 120° turn carries each arm exactly onto the next.

---

What angle must separate the arms of a figure with **5 radial arms** for it to have rotational symmetry? With **6**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **5 arms:** `360 ÷ 5 = ` **72°** · **6 arms:** `360 ÷ 6 = ` **60°**.
>
> **Why:** the rotation must carry each arm exactly onto the next, so the full 360° has to be shared **equally** among the gaps.

---

A figure with radial arms has exactly **7** angles of symmetry. Find its smallest angle.

> [!ANSWER]
> `360 ÷ 7 = ` **51 3/7 degrees** — not a whole number.
>
> This is a case where the smallest angle of symmetry is a **fraction** of a degree, which is perfectly allowed. (The "must be a factor of 360" rule only applies when the angle *is* a whole number.)

---

<!-- ===== The circle ===== -->

What are the angles of symmetry of a **circle**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every angle is an angle of symmetry** — rotate a circle by any amount about its centre and it coincides with itself.
>
> That makes the circle the one figure with **no smallest** angle of symmetry, since there's always a smaller turn that still works.

---

Which shape has **both** infinite reflection symmetry and infinite rotational symmetry?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **circle** — infinitely many lines of symmetry (every diameter) **and** every angle an angle of symmetry.
>
> **Why:** every point of the rim is the same distance from the centre, so nothing distinguishes one position from another.

---

<!-- ===== Combining the two ===== -->

Name a shape with **both** reflection and rotational symmetry, and state both counts.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **square**: **4 lines** of symmetry, and **4 angles** of symmetry (90°, 180°, 270°, 360°).
>
> An **equilateral triangle** works too: **3 lines**, **3 angles** (120°, 240°, 360°).
>
> For a **regular polygon with n sides** the two counts always match at **n**.

---

Sketch a quadrilateral with **rotational symmetry but no reflection symmetry**.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **parallelogram** (that is not a rectangle or rhombus).
>
> **Rotational:** a 180° turn about its centre maps it onto itself → order 2.
>
> **No reflection:** fold it any way — along a diagonal, or through the middles of opposite sides — and the halves never match, because its angles are not right angles.

---

Sketch a shape with **reflection symmetry but no rotational symmetry**.

> [!ANSWER]
> An **isosceles triangle** (not equilateral), or a **kite**.
>
> It has **1 line** of symmetry, but only a full **360°** turn brings it back — so no rotational symmetry.
>
> This pair of examples shows the two kinds of symmetry are **independent**: a figure can have either, both, or neither.
