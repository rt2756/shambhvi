<!-- ===== What maths is ===== -->

In one sentence, what is mathematics largely the search for?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The search for patterns — and for the explanations as to *why* those patterns exist.**
>
> Finding the pattern is only half of it; the **explanation** is the other half.

---

Why does the *explanation* of a pattern matter as much as the pattern itself?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because an explanation can be **reused far beyond where it was discovered**.
>
> Understanding patterns in the motion of stars and planets led to the **theory of gravitation** — and so to satellites and rockets to the Moon and Mars. Understanding patterns in **genomes** helped in diagnosing and curing diseases.
>
> A pattern you can only observe is a curiosity; one you can explain becomes a tool.

---

Which branch of mathematics studies patterns in **whole numbers**? In **shapes**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Whole numbers → **number theory**. Shapes → **geometry**.

---

<!-- ===== The sequences ===== -->

Give the next three numbers and the rule: **1, 3, 5, 7, 9, …**

> [!ANSWER]
> **11, 13, 15.** Rule: **start at 1 and add 2 each time** — the **odd numbers**.

---

Give the next three and the rule: **1, 3, 6, 10, 15, …**

> [!ANSWER]
> **21, 28, 36.**
>
> Rule: the **gaps grow by one each time** — add 2, then 3, then 4, then 5… These are the **triangular numbers**.

---

Give the next three and the rule: **1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …**

> [!ANSWER]
> **21, 34, 55.**
>
> Rule: **each number is the sum of the previous two** (`8 + 13 = 21`). These are the **Virahānka numbers**.

---

Give the next two and the rule: **1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, …** and **1, 3, 9, 27, 81, …**

> [!ANSWER]
> **64, 128** — **powers of 2**, doubling each time.
>
> **243, 729** — **powers of 3**, tripling each time.

---

Why are **1, 3, 6, 10, 15** called *triangular* numbers, and **1, 4, 9, 16** called *squares*?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because of the **shape their dots make**.
>
> 1, 3, 6, 10 dots can be arranged into a perfect **triangle** (rows of 1, 2, 3, 4 …).
>
> 1, 4, 9, 16 dots can be arranged into a perfect **square grid** (`2×2`, `3×3`, `4×4`).
>
> Likewise **cubes** — 8, 27, 64 dots stack into a **cube**.

---

**36** is special. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **36 is both a triangular number and a square number** — 36 dots arrange perfectly into a triangle *and* into a 6 × 6 square.
>
> It shows the same number can be represented differently and **play different roles depending on the picture** you draw of it.

---

<!-- ===== Odd numbers → squares ===== -->

What do you get when you add up the odd numbers starting from 1? Show the first five results.

> [!ANSWER]
> You get the **square numbers**:
>
> `1 = 1` · `1+3 = 4` · `1+3+5 = 9` · `1+3+5+7 = 16` · `1+3+5+7+9 = 25`

---

**Explain with a picture** why adding odd numbers gives squares.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take a **square grid of dots** and peel it into **L-shaped layers**.
>
> The corner is **1** dot. The L wrapping round it is **3**. The next L is **5**, then **7**, and so on — **each L is the next odd number**, and together they rebuild the whole square.
>
> **Why it holds forever:** you can draw that picture for a square of **any** size, so the pattern never breaks.

---

Without adding them up, find the sum of the first **10** odd numbers, and of the first **100**.

> [!ANSWER]
> The sum of the first **n** odd numbers is **n × n**.
>
> First 10 → `10 × 10 = ` **100**. First 100 → `100 × 100 = ` **10,000**.
>
> The picture does the work — no addition needed.

---

The sum of the first *n* odd numbers is 400. How many odd numbers were added?

> [!ANSWER]
> We need `n × n = 400` → **n = 20**.
>
> So the first **20** odd numbers (1 up to 39) were added.

---

<!-- ===== Up and down ===== -->

What is `1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1`? And `1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1`? What sequence appears?

> [!ANSWER]
> `1+2+3+2+1 = 9` and `1+2+3+4+3+2+1 = 16`.
>
> The **square numbers** again — adding the counting numbers **up and then back down** gives squares.

---

Find `1 + 2 + 3 + … + 99 + 100 + 99 + … + 3 + 2 + 1`.

> [!ANSWER]
> The pattern goes up to 100 and back, so the answer is `100 × 100 = ` **10,000**.
>
> **Why:** picture the square tilted as a diamond and count its **diagonals** — they contain 1, 2, 3, …, 100, …, 3, 2, 1 dots, which is exactly this sum.

---

<!-- ===== More relations ===== -->

What sequence do you get by adding up the **All 1's** sequence (1, 1+1, 1+1+1, …)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1, 2, 3, 4, …** — the **counting numbers**.
>
> **Why:** each step adds exactly one more 1, so the running total goes up by 1 every time.

---

What do you get by adding up the **counting numbers** (1, 1+2, 1+2+3, …)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1, 3, 6, 10, 15, …** — the **triangular numbers**.
>
> **Why:** building a triangle of dots row by row, each new row holds one more dot than the last — so the running total is exactly the triangular sequence.

---

Add **pairs of consecutive triangular numbers**: 1+3, 3+6, 6+10, 10+15. What do you get, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> `4, 9, 16, 25` — the **square numbers**.
>
> **Why:** a triangular number is a triangle of dots. Take two consecutive triangles, turn one upside down, and **they slot together into a perfect square**.

---

Add up powers of 2: **1, 1+2, 1+2+4, 1+2+4+8**. Then add 1 to each answer. What happens?

> [!ANSWER]
> The sums are `1, 3, 7, 15`. Adding 1 to each gives `2, 4, 8, 16` — the **powers of 2** again.
>
> **Why:** each running total falls **exactly one short** of the next power of 2. Doubling always jumps just past the sum of everything before it — by precisely 1.

---

Multiply the triangular numbers by **6 and add 1**. What sequence appears?

> [!ANSWER]
> `1×6+1 = 7`, `3×6+1 = 19`, `6×6+1 = 37`, `10×6+1 = 61` → with 1 at the front: **1, 7, 19, 37, 61** — the **hexagonal numbers**.
>
> **Why:** a hexagon can be built from **six triangles arranged around one central dot** — hence six times a triangular number, plus the middle one.

---

Add up the **hexagonal numbers**: 1, 1+7, 1+7+19, 1+7+19+37. What do you get?

> [!ANSWER]
> `1, 8, 27, 64` — the **cube numbers**!
>
> **Why:** the hexagonal numbers are exactly the sizes of the successive **shells of a cube**, so stacking them builds a cube layer by layer.

---

<!-- ===== Shape sequences ===== -->

Count the **sides** in the sequence of regular polygons. Which number sequence appears? What about the **corners**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Sides: **3, 4, 5, 6, 7, …** — the **counting numbers starting at 3**.
>
> Corners: **the same sequence**.
>
> **Why they match:** every side **ends at a corner** where the next side begins, so sides and corners pair up exactly one-for-one.

---

What does **regular** mean in "regular polygon"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **All sides are of equal length AND all angles are equal** — the sides look the same as each other, and so do the corners.
>
> (A rectangle has equal angles but unequal sides, so it is **not** regular; a square is.)

---

Count the **lines** in the complete graphs K2, K3, K4, K5. Which sequence appears?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1, 3, 6, 10** — the **triangular numbers**.
>
> **Why:** adding one new point means joining it to **every point already there** — so the *n*th point adds *n−1* new lines, growing the total by 1, then 2, then 3… exactly how triangular numbers are built.

---

How many little triangles are in each **stacked triangle**? Which sequence is it, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1, 4, 9, 16** — the **square numbers**.
>
> **Why:** the rows contain **1, 3, 5, 7, …** little triangles — the **odd numbers**. And we already know that adding up odd numbers gives squares.
>
> A nice example of one pattern explaining another.

---

In the **Koch snowflake**, each line segment is replaced by a "speed bump". The counts are 3, 12, 48. What is the rule and the next number?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each step turns **one** segment into **four**, so the total is **multiplied by 4** every time.
>
> `3, 12, 48, ` **192** — that is, **3 × powers of 4**.

---

Which sequence would you get from the number of **little squares** in the stacked-squares sequence?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1, 4, 9, 16, …** — the **square numbers**, since each shape is a full `n × n` grid of little squares.
