<!-- ===== Fractions as shares ===== -->

Three guavas of the same size together weigh **1 kg**. Roughly what does each weigh?

> [!ANSWER]
> One whole kilogram shared equally among 3 → each is **1/3 kg**.

---

Which is greater, **1/5 or 1/9**? Beni says 1/9, "because 9 is bigger than 5." Is he right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — 1/5 is greater.**
>
> Think of shares: 1 roti among **5** children gives each `1/5`; among **9** children gives each `1/9`. **More people sharing the same roti means a smaller share each.**
>
> So `1/9 < 1/5`. With unit fractions, **a bigger bottom number means a smaller piece**.

---

What is a **fractional unit**? Name the top and bottom numbers of `5/6`.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **fractional unit** (unit fraction) is **one equal part** of a whole: `1/2, 1/3, 1/4, …`
>
> In `5/6`: **5 is the numerator**, **6 is the denominator**.

---

Read **3/4** in the way that shows its size, and explain why that reading helps.

> [!ANSWER]
> Read it as **"3 times 1/4"**.
>
> It tells you both things at once: the **fractional unit is 1/4**, and there are **3** of them. "Three quarters" hides that structure — and most fraction questions become easy once you see the unit and the count separately.

---

Four friends share **3 glasses** of juice equally. How much does each get?

> [!ANSWER]
> 3 glasses among 4 → each gets **3/4 glass**.
>
> Division fact: `3 ÷ 4 = 3/4`.

---

Arrange from smallest to biggest: **one and a half, three quarters, one and a quarter, half, quarter, two and a half**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **quarter (1/4) < half (1/2) < three quarters (3/4) < one and a quarter (1¼) < one and a half (1½) < two and a half (2½)**
>
> The first three are all below 1; the last three are above it.

---

<!-- ===== Number line ===== -->

The distance from 0 to 1 is divided into **5 equal parts**. What fraction does the mark at the **third** part show?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each part is `1/5`, so three parts is **3/5**.

---

How many fractions lie **between 0 and 1**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Infinitely many.**
>
> You can always cut the unit into more equal parts — halves, thirds, quarters, hundredths — and every new cut creates new fractions between 0 and 1. There is no "last" one.

---

<!-- ===== Mixed numbers ===== -->

How can you tell at a glance whether a fraction is **less than or greater than 1**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Numerator smaller than denominator → less than 1** (`3/5`).
>
> **Numerator bigger than denominator → greater than 1** (`7/3`).
>
> **Why:** the denominator says how many units make a whole. If you have fewer than that many, you haven't reached one whole yet.

---

How many whole units are in **7/3**? Write it as a mixed number.

> [!ANSWER]
> `3/3 = 1`, so group the thirds: `7/3 = 3/3 + 3/3 + 1/3 = 1 + 1 + 1/3`
>
> = **2 1/3** — two whole units and a third left over.

---

Write **11/4** as a mixed number.

> [!ANSWER]
> `4/4 = 1`, and 11 contains **two** fours with 3 left over:
>
> `11/4 = ` **2 3/4**

---

Write **3 3/4** as a single fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each whole is `4/4`, so three wholes are `4/4 + 4/4 + 4/4 = 12/4`, plus the `3/4`:
>
> `= (12 + 3)/4 = ` **15/4**
>
> (The shortcut `(3 × 4 + 3)/4` is the same calculation written compactly.)

---

Write **2 5/6** as a single fraction.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(2 × 6 + 5)/6 = (12 + 5)/6 = ` **17/6**

---

<!-- ===== Equivalent fractions ===== -->

Show that **1/2, 2/4 and 3/6** are equivalent using the idea of sharing.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1 roti among 2** children · **2 rotis among 4** children · **3 rotis among 6** children.
>
> In every case each child gets the **same amount** — doubling or tripling *both* the rotis and the children leaves each share unchanged.
>
> So `1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6` — **equivalent fractions**.

---

How do you make an equivalent fraction? Write two fractions equivalent to **2/6**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Multiply or divide the numerator and denominator by the same number.**
>
> `2/6 = 4/12` (×2) · `2/6 = 1/3` (÷2).
>
> **Why it's allowed:** multiplying top and bottom by the same number is multiplying by 1 in disguise — the amount doesn't change, only how it's written.

---

**5 glasses of juice shared among 4 friends** is the same as **how many glasses shared among 8 friends**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Doubling the friends means doubling the juice to keep each share the same:
>
> `5/4 = 10/8` → **10 glasses among 8 friends**.

---

<!-- ===== Lowest terms ===== -->

When is a fraction in **lowest terms**? Reduce **16/20**.

> [!ANSWER]
> When the numerator and denominator have **no common factor except 1**.
>
> 16 and 20 share the factor **4**: `16 ÷ 4 = 4`, `20 ÷ 4 = 5` → **4/5**.
>
> Check: 4 and 5 share nothing but 1 ✓ — so it's fully reduced.

---

Reduce **36/60** to lowest terms, going in steps.

> [!ANSWER]
> Both even → ÷2 → `18/30`. Both even again → ÷2 → `9/15`. Both multiples of 3 → ÷3 → **3/5**.
>
> Now 3 and 5 share nothing but 1 ✓
>
> **Or in one jump:** `36 = 3 × 12` and `60 = 5 × 12`, so dividing by 12 gives 3/5 straight away. Both routes agree — steps are just easier when the HCF isn't obvious.

---

Reduce **144/180**.

> [!ANSWER]
> HCF of 144 and 180 is **36**: `144 ÷ 36 = 4`, `180 ÷ 36 = 5` → **4/5**.
>
> In steps if you prefer: ÷2 → 72/90, ÷2 → 36/45, ÷9 → 4/5.

---

<!-- ===== Comparing ===== -->

Compare **2/6 and 3/6**. Why is this pair easy?

> [!ANSWER]
> **3/6 > 2/6.**
>
> The denominators are the same, so both count the **same-sized pieces** (sixths). Three pieces beat two pieces — just compare the numerators.

---

Compare **4/7 and 4/8**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **4/7 > 4/8.**
>
> Same number of pieces (4), but sevenths are **bigger pieces** than eighths — sharing among 7 gives more than sharing among 8.
>
> With the same numerator, the **bigger denominator makes the smaller fraction**.

---

Compare **4/5 and 7/9**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Different units — convert both to a common denominator. `5 × 9 = 45` works:
>
> `4/5 = 36/45` · `7/9 = 35/45`
>
> `36 > 35` → **4/5 > 7/9** (only just!).

---

Compare **7/9 and 17/21**.

> [!ANSWER]
> A common multiple of 9 and 21 is **63**:
>
> `7/9 = 49/63` · `17/21 = 51/63`
>
> `49 < 51` → **7/9 < 17/21**.

---

Write in **ascending order**: **7/10, 11/15, 2/5**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Common denominator **30**:
>
> `7/10 = 21/30` · `11/15 = 22/30` · `2/5 = 12/30`
>
> `12 < 21 < 22` → **2/5 < 7/10 < 11/15**

---

<!-- ===== Adding & subtracting ===== -->

Find **4/7 + 6/7**, and explain why the denominator doesn't change.

> [!ANSWER]
> `4/7 + 6/7 = 10/7 = ` **1 3/7**
>
> **Why the 7 stays:** you are counting **sevenths**. Four sevenths plus six sevenths is ten sevenths — just like 4 apples + 6 apples = 10 apples. The *size* of each piece never changed, only **how many** you have.

---

Meena eats **1/2** of a chikki and her brother eats **1/4**. How much did they eat together?

> [!ANSWER]
> Different units, so rename: `1/2 = 2/4`.
>
> `2/4 + 1/4 = ` **3/4** of the chikki.
>
> (And `1/4` is left.)

---

Add **1/4 + 1/3** using Brahmagupta's method.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Step 1 — common fractional unit.** A common multiple of 4 and 3 is `4 × 3 = 12`:
> `1/4 = 3/12` · `1/3 = 4/12`
>
> **Step 2 — add the numerators:** `3/12 + 4/12 = 7/12`
>
> **Step 3 — lowest terms:** 7 and 12 share nothing → **7/12** is the answer.

---

Add **1/6 + 1/3**, and simplify.

> [!ANSWER]
> Smallest common multiple of 6 and 3 is **6** — so only the second fraction changes: `1/3 = 2/6`.
>
> `1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6` → simplify by ÷3 → **1/2**

---

Why is **1/4 + 1/3 NOT equal to 2/7**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **you never add denominators**. The denominator names the **size** of the piece, not a quantity to be counted.
>
> Quarters and thirds are different-sized pieces, so they can't be combined until both are renamed into the **same** size (twelfths). The right answer is **7/12** — and note `7/12` is bigger than either piece, while `2/7` is *smaller* than `1/3`, which is impossible when you add something positive.

---

Rahim mixes **2/5 litre** of yellow paint with **3/10 litre** of blue. How much green paint?

> [!ANSWER]
> Common denominator **10**: `2/5 = 4/10`.
>
> `4/10 + 3/10 = 7/10` → **7/10 litre**.

---

Find **5/8 − 3/8**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Same fractional unit, so subtract the numerators: `(5 − 3)/8 = 2/8`
>
> Simplify by ÷2 → **1/4**

---

Find **3/4 − 2/3**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Common denominator **12**: `3/4 = 9/12` and `2/3 = 8/12`.
>
> `9/12 − 8/12 = ` **1/12**
>
> (We multiplied the first by 3 and the second by 4 — whatever each denominator needed to reach 12.)

---

Subtract **1/3 from 5/6**.

> [!ANSWER]
> "From" means `5/6 − 1/3`. Common denominator **6**: `1/3 = 2/6`.
>
> `5/6 − 2/6 = 3/6 = ` **1/2**
>
> ⚠️ Watch the order — "subtract A from B" means **B − A**, not A − B.

---

Geeta buys **2/5 m** of lace and Shamim **3/4 m**. A tablecloth border needs **1 m**. Do they have enough?

> [!ANSWER]
> Common denominator **20**: `2/5 = 8/20` and `3/4 = 15/20`.
>
> `8/20 + 15/20 = 23/20 = ` **1 3/20 m**
>
> `23/20 > 1` → **yes, enough**, with `3/20` m to spare.
