<!-- ===== Perimeter ===== -->

What is the **perimeter** of a closed plane figure, and how do you find it for a polygon?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **distance covered going once around its boundary**.
>
> For a polygon it is simply the **sum of the lengths of all its sides**.

---

Find the perimeter of a rectangle **12 cm by 8 cm**, and explain the formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> `2 × (12 + 8) = 2 × 20 = ` **40 cm**
>
> **Why ×2:** opposite sides of a rectangle are equal, so the length appears **twice** (top and bottom) and the breadth **twice** (left and right). Adding one of each and doubling is the same as adding all four.

---

Perimeter of a rectangle is **14 cm** and its breadth is **2 cm**. Find the length.

> [!ANSWER]
> `2 × (l + 2) = 14` → `l + 2 = 7` → **l = 5 cm**
>
> Check: `2 × (5 + 2) = 14` ✓

---

A square has perimeter **20 cm**. Find its side.

> [!ANSWER]
> `4s = 20` → **s = 5 cm**.

---

A triangle has perimeter **55 cm** and two sides of **20 cm** and **14 cm**. Find the third side.

> [!ANSWER]
> The three sides add to the perimeter:
>
> `55 − (20 + 14) = 55 − 34 = ` **21 cm**

---

A string **36 cm** long is bent into (a) a square, (b) an equilateral triangle, (c) a regular hexagon. Find each side length.

> [!ANSWER]
> The string's length is the perimeter every time — only the number of equal sides changes.
>
> **(a) Square:** `36 ÷ 4 = ` **9 cm** · **(b) Triangle:** `36 ÷ 3 = ` **12 cm** · **(c) Hexagon:** `36 ÷ 6 = ` **6 cm**
>
> More sides sharing the same total → each side is shorter.

---

A wire forms a **5 cm × 3 cm** rectangle. Straightened and bent into a square, what is the square's side?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The wire's length doesn't change**, so the perimeters are equal.
>
> Rectangle perimeter = `2 × (5 + 3) = 16 cm`. For the square: `4s = 16` → **s = 4 cm**.

---

What would it cost to fence a rectangular park **150 m by 120 m** at **₹40 per metre**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Fencing goes **around** → perimeter: `2 × (150 + 120) = 2 × 270 = 540 m`.
>
> Cost = `540 × 40 = ` **₹21,600**.

---

Usha takes **three rounds** of a square park of side **75 m**. How far does she travel?

> [!ANSWER]
> One round = perimeter = `4 × 75 = 300 m`.
>
> Three rounds = `3 × 300 = ` **900 m**.

---

A farmer fences a **230 m × 160 m** field with **3 rounds** of rope. What length of rope is needed?

> [!ANSWER]
> One round = `2 × (230 + 160) = 2 × 390 = 780 m`.
>
> Three rounds = `3 × 780 = ` **2340 m**.

---

Akshi runs 5 rounds of a **70 m × 40 m** track; Toshi runs 7 rounds of a **60 m × 30 m** track. Who runs further?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Akshi:** one round = `2 × (70 + 40) = 220 m` → `5 × 220 = ` **1100 m**.
>
> **Toshi:** one round = `2 × (60 + 30) = 180 m` → `7 × 180 = ` **1260 m**.
>
> **Toshi runs further**, by 160 m — more rounds of a shorter track beat fewer rounds of a longer one here.

---

A figure's perimeter is written as **6s + 3d** units. Why can't we just call it 9 units?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **s (straight) and d (diagonal) are different lengths** — a diagonal step across a square is longer than a straight one along its side.
>
> Adding 6 and 3 would be adding two different units together, like adding 6 metres to 3 kilometres and calling it 9.

<!-- ===== Area ===== -->

What is **area**, and what units is it measured in?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **amount of region enclosed** by a closed figure.
>
> Measured in **square units** — sq cm, sq m — because we count how many unit **squares** cover the region.

---

A rectangular garden is **25 m** long with an area of **300 sq m**. Find its width.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Area = length × width` → `300 = 25 × w` → **w = 12 m**.

---

A floor is **5 m × 4 m**. A square carpet of side **3 m** is laid on it. Find the uncarpeted area.

> [!ANSWER]
> Floor area = `5 × 4 = 20 sq m`. Carpet area = `3 × 3 = 9 sq m`.
>
> Uncarpeted = `20 − 9 = ` **11 sq m** — the leftover region is a **subtraction**.

---

Four square flower beds of side **4 m** sit in the corners of a **12 m × 10 m** plot. Find the remaining area.

> [!ANSWER]
> Whole plot = `12 × 10 = 120 sq m`.
>
> One bed = `4 × 4 = 16 sq m` → four beds = `4 × 16 = 64 sq m`.
>
> Remaining = `120 − 64 = ` **56 sq m**.

---

A coconut grove is **100 m × 50 m** and each tree needs **25 sq m**. What is the maximum number of trees?

> [!ANSWER]
> Grove area = `100 × 50 = 5000 sq m`.
>
> `5000 ÷ 25 = ` **200 trees**.

---

What does it cost to tile a **500 m × 200 m** plot at **₹8 per hundred sq m**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Area = `500 × 200 = 100,000 sq m`.
>
> Number of hundreds = `100000 ÷ 100 = 1000`.
>
> Cost = `1000 × 8 = ` **₹8000**.

---

Why is area measured using **squares** rather than circles?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because squares **tile a region completely — no gaps, no overlaps** — so counting them measures the region exactly.
>
> **Circles can't.** Packing the same rectangle with circles two different ways gives **42 one time and 44 the next**, because of the gaps left between them. A square's flat sides sit perfectly against its neighbours, and that reliability is what a unit of measurement needs.

---

State the four rules for estimating area on grid paper, and why rules 2 and 3 are fair.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** A full square = **1 sq unit** · **2.** Ignore portions **less than half** · **3.** **More than half** counts as a full **1** · **4.** **Exactly half** counts as **1/2**.
>
> **Why fair:** the small bits thrown away (rule 2) and the bits generously rounded up (rule 3) roughly **cancel out** over the whole shape, so the estimate lands close to the true area.

<!-- ===== Triangle ===== -->

Cut a rectangle along its diagonal. What do you notice about the two pieces, and what does it tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> The two triangles **overlap each other exactly** — they have the **same area**.
>
> Together they make the whole rectangle, so **each triangle is half the rectangle**.

---

A triangle sits inside a rectangle **8 cm × 6 cm**, with the same base and height. Find the triangle's area.

> [!ANSWER]
> Rectangle area = `8 × 6 = 48 sq cm`.
>
> Triangle = **half** of it = **24 sq cm**.

---

The half-a-rectangle rule was shown with a right triangle. Why does it work for a triangle whose top vertex is *not* above a corner?

> [!ANSWER]
> Drop a line from the top vertex straight down to the base — this splits the triangle into **two right triangles**.
>
> Each of those is **half of its own small rectangle**. Adding them gives **half of one small rectangle + half of the other = half of the whole rectangle**.
>
> So the rule holds for **any** triangle, not just right-angled ones.

---

Two triangles look completely different but sit in the same rectangle. Can their areas be equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Both are half of the same rectangle, so both have the same area.
>
> **Area doesn't depend on shape** — only on how much region is enclosed. Two very different-looking figures can enclose exactly the same amount.

---

<!-- ===== Area vs perimeter ===== -->

Using **9 unit squares**, what is the smallest possible perimeter, and what is the largest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Smallest: 12 units** — arrange them as a **3 × 3 square** (`2 × (3+3)`).
>
> **Largest: 20 units** — lay all nine in a **single line** (`2 × (9+1)`).
>
> Same area (9 sq units) both times, but very different perimeters.

---

List the rectangles with whole-number sides and area **24 sq units**. Which has the greatest and least perimeter?

> [!ANSWER]
> `1×24, 2×12, 3×8, 4×6`.
>
> Perimeters: `50, 28, 22, 20`.
>
> **Greatest: 1 × 24** (perimeter 50) · **Least: 4 × 6** (perimeter 20).
>
> **The pattern:** for a fixed area, the **longest, thinnest** rectangle has the **greatest** perimeter and the **most square-like** one the **least** — a thin shape has a lot of boundary hugging very little region.

---

True or false: **"Two figures with the same area must have the same perimeter."**

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.** The 9-square example settles it: area 9 with perimeter **12** as a square, or perimeter **20** in a line.
>
> The reverse is false too — equal perimeters don't force equal areas. **Area and perimeter are independent**; always check which one the question wants.

---

A unit square is attached to the edge of a figure. Can the perimeter go **down**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** It depends how many of the new square's sides get **hidden** against the existing shape.
>
> Stuck on a flat edge, one side is hidden and three are added → perimeter **rises by 2**. Tucked into a **notch** where two or three sides are hidden, the perimeter can **stay the same or fall**.
