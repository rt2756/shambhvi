<!-- ===== Building blocks ===== -->

Describe a **point**. Does it have any size?

> [!ANSWER]
> A point determines a **precise location**, and has **no length, breadth or height**.
>
> The dot we draw is only a model — a real point must be imagined as invisibly thin. A sharper pencil gives a better picture of one.

---

What is a **line segment**, and how does it differ from a **line** and a **ray**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Line segment:** the **shortest path** between two points, **including** both end points — it stops at both ends.
>
> **Line:** that segment **extended forever in both directions** — no ends at all.
>
> **Ray:** starts at **one** point and goes on **endlessly in one direction** — one end, one infinite side.

---

Rihan marks **one** point. How many lines can he draw through it? Sheetal marks **two** points — how many through both?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Rihan: infinitely many** — a line through a single point can point in any direction, and there are endless directions.
>
> **Sheetal: exactly one.** Two points pin down both a position *and* a direction, so **any two points determine a unique line**.

---

Can the ray **AP** also be written as **PA**? Why or why not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** `AP` **starts at A** and goes through P. `PA` would start at P and head the **opposite way** — a completely different ray.
>
> The starting point must come **first** in a ray's name.

---

A ray starts at O, passes through A, and also passes through B further along. Can it be named **OB** as well as **OA**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** A ray is fixed by its **starting point** and its **direction** — not by which point along it you happen to name.
>
> Since O is still the start and the direction is unchanged, `OA` and `OB` name the **same ray**.

---

Why can a **line segment** be written either `AB` or `BA`, but a ray cannot be reversed?

> [!ANSWER]
> A segment has **two end points and no starting point** — neither end is special, so either may be named first.
>
> A ray has **one starting point**, which is genuinely different from the rest of it. Reversing the letters would claim a different starting point, and so a different ray.

---

<!-- ===== Angles ===== -->

What is an **angle**? Name its parts.

> [!ANSWER]
> An angle is formed by **two rays sharing a common starting point**.
>
> The shared point is the **vertex**; the two rays are the **arms**.

---

How do you name an angle formed by rays `BD` and `BE`? Give two correct names.

> [!ANSWER]
> **∠DBE** or **∠EBD** — the **vertex letter (B) always goes in the middle**.
>
> Reading the name traces the path: start on one arm, pass through the corner, end on the other arm.

---

Why can `∠APB` **not** be shortened to `∠P`?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **more than one angle has its vertex at P** in that figure (`∠APB`, `∠APC`, `∠BPC`…).
>
> `∠P` would be **ambiguous** — it wouldn't say which pair of arms you mean. The single-letter name is only safe when exactly one angle sits at that vertex.

---

<!-- ===== Size as rotation ===== -->

What actually determines the **size** of an angle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The amount of rotation (turn)** needed about the vertex to move the first arm onto the second.
>
> Opening a book cover further, or scissors wider, means more turning — and so a bigger angle.

---

Two angles have the same opening, but one is drawn with much **longer arms**. Which is bigger?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neither — they are equal.**
>
> The angle measures the **turn between the arms**, not how far the arms extend. Extending an arm adds length but no rotation.
>
> The "slit" activity proves it: a pair of rotating arms passes through an angle-shaped slit **only if the angles match**, regardless of arm length.

---

You are challenged to "reduce the angle" by cutting the arms shorter. Does it work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — the angle stays exactly the same.**
>
> Shortening the arms changes the drawing, not the amount of rotation between them. This is the single most common misunderstanding about angles.

---

<!-- ===== Comparing ===== -->

How do you compare two angles by **superimposition**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Place one on top of the other so the **vertices coincide** and **one pair of arms lines up**.
>
> Whichever angle's second arm has turned **further** is the larger one.

---

Two angles are superimposed and the vertex **and both arms** coincide exactly. What does this mean, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The angles are equal in size.**
>
> **Why:** if both arms land on top of each other, then exactly the **same amount of rotation** is needed to carry the first arm to the second in each angle — and that rotation *is* the size of the angle.

---

How can you compare two angles **without** superimposing them?

> [!ANSWER]
> Place a **transparent circle** with its **centre on the vertex**, and mark where the arms cross the rim. Carry that marked circle to the second angle, again centring it on the vertex with one arm aligned.
>
> Now you can see directly which arm falls further round.
>
> This is exactly the idea a **protractor** is built on — a circle used as a fixed reference for turns.

---

<!-- ===== Special angles ===== -->

Name the angle for each turn: a **quarter** turn, a **half** turn, a **full** turn.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Quarter turn → right angle (90°)** · **half turn → straight angle (180°)** · **full turn → 360°**.

---

What is a **straight angle**, and how many right angles does it contain?

> [!ANSWER]
> An angle whose **arms lie in a straight line** — a **half turn**, **180°**.
>
> It contains **exactly two right angles**, so a right angle is **half** a straight angle.

---

Fold a straight angle so one arm lands on the other. Why must the crease give exactly a **right angle**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Folding makes the two parts **superimpose exactly**, so the two angles are **equal**.
>
> They together make the straight angle, 180° — and two equal angles totalling 180° must be **90° each**.
>
> That's a proof, not a measurement: no protractor is needed.

---

Classify: **35°, 90°, 120°, 180°**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **35° acute** (less than a quarter turn) · **90° right** · **120° obtuse** (between a quarter and a half turn) · **180° straight**.

---

**Acute** means *sharp* and **obtuse** means *blunt*. Why are these good names?

> [!ANSWER]
> An **acute** angle makes a narrow, pointed corner — it looks and feels **sharp**, like a needle's tip.
>
> An **obtuse** angle makes a wide, flattened corner — visibly **blunt**.
>
> The words describe exactly what the corner looks like.

---

What are **perpendicular** lines?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two lines that **meet at right angles (90°)**.
>
> The classroom is full of them — window corners, door frames, the corners of a page.

---

<!-- ===== Degrees ===== -->

How is **1 degree** defined?

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide the full turn at the centre of a circle into **360 equal parts** — each part is **1 degree (1°)**.
>
> The measure of any angle is then simply **how many of those 1° parts it contains**.

---

Given a full turn is 360°, derive the measures of a **straight angle** and a **right angle**.

> [!ANSWER]
> A straight angle is **half** a full turn → `360 ÷ 2 = ` **180°**.
>
> A right angle is **half** a straight angle → `180 ÷ 2 = ` **90°**.
>
> Each follows from the one before — nothing needs to be memorised separately.

---

Why did mathematicians choose **360** parts rather than, say, 100?

> [!ANSWER]
> The most practical reason: **360 is the smallest number divisible by every number from 1 to 10 except 7**.
>
> So a circle can be split into 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 or 10 equal parts and each part is still a **whole number of degrees**. It's also divisible by **12** (months) and **24** (hours).
>
> The choice is ancient too — the **Rigveda** mentions a wheel with 360 spokes, and several ancient calendars used 360-day years.

---

A circle is divided into **8** equal parts. What is each angle? Into **5** parts?

> [!ANSWER]
> **8 parts:** `360 ÷ 8 = ` **45°** · **5 parts:** `360 ÷ 5 = ` **72°**.
>
> Both come out whole — one of the reasons 360 is such a convenient choice.

---

<!-- ===== Protractor ===== -->

List the three steps for measuring an angle with a protractor.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** Put the protractor's **centre on the vertex**. **2.** Line **one arm up with the 0° mark**. **3.** Read where the **other arm** crosses the scale.

---

Why does a protractor carry **two sets of numbers**?

> [!ANSWER]
> So you can measure an angle opening from **either direction** — one scale runs left-to-right, the other right-to-left.
>
> **Use the scale whose 0 sits on your chosen arm**, and stay on that same scale for the reading. Mixing the two is the usual source of wrong answers.

---

An angle's arms cross the protractor at **20** and **55**. What is the angle?

> [!ANSWER]
> `55 − 20 = ` **35°**.
>
> You don't have to align an arm with 0 — reading **both** arms and **subtracting** works just as well. Aligning to 0 simply saves you the subtraction step.
