<!-- ===== Taller neighbours ===== -->

Children in a line each say **how many of their neighbours are taller**. Can a child standing at an **end** say "2"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** A child at an end has **only one neighbour**, so the most they can ever say is **1**.
>
> Saying 2 would require two taller neighbours, and they simply don't have two.

---

Can **two children standing next to each other** both say "2"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** If they are neighbours, **one of the two must be taller than the other**.
>
> The shorter one may say 2, but the taller one cannot count the shorter as taller — so at most one of any adjacent pair says 2.

---

Can all children in a line say **0**? What arrangement does that need?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only if the line is arranged so nobody has a taller neighbour** — which is impossible for more than one child of differing heights.
>
> Consider the **shortest** child: whoever stands next to them **is** taller, so that child must say at least 1. So all-zeros can't happen (with all heights different).

---

<!-- ===== Supercells ===== -->

What makes a cell a **supercell**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Its number is **greater than all its neighbouring cells** — left and right in a row, and also above and below in a grid.
>
> A cell at the end of a row has only one neighbour to beat.

---

Will the cell holding the **largest** number in a table always be a supercell? Can the **smallest** ever be one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Largest: always yes** — nothing in the table beats it, so it certainly beats its neighbours.
>
> **Smallest: never** — every neighbour is bigger than it, so it fails immediately.

---

Can you fill a table with **no repeated numbers** so that it has **no supercells at all**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** With no repeats there is always a single **largest** number, and that cell is automatically a supercell.
>
> So every such table has **at least one**.

<!-- ===== Digit sums ===== -->

What is the **digit sum** of 176? Name another number with the same digit sum.

> [!ANSWER]
> `1 + 7 + 6 = ` **14**.
>
> Others with digit sum 14: **68**, **545**, **95**, **860** — many possibilities.

---

What is the **smallest** number whose digit sum is **14**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **59.**
>
> **Why:** to make a number small, use **as few digits as possible**, and put the **smaller digit first**. Two digits are enough (max digit sum 18), and 59 beats 68, 77, 86, 95.

---

What is the **largest 5-digit** number whose digit sum is **14**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **95000.**
>
> **Why:** to make a number large, load the **biggest digits into the leftmost places**. Put 9 first, then as much as possible next (5), leaving zeros: `9 + 5 = 14` ✓

---

Find the digit sum of **345**, **456** and **567**. What is the pattern for three **consecutive** digits?

> [!ANSWER]
> `3+4+5 = 12` · `4+5+6 = 15` · `5+6+7 = 18`.
>
> Each is **3 × the middle digit**.
>
> **Why:** the outer digits are one **less** and one **more** than the middle, so the −1 and +1 **cancel** — leaving three copies of the middle digit.

---

<!-- ===== Digit detectives ===== -->

How many times does the digit **7** appear when writing the numbers **1 to 100**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **20 times.**
>
> **Units place:** 7, 17, 27, 37, 47, 57, 67, 77, 87, 97 → **10** times.
>
> **Tens place:** 70, 71, …, 79 → **10** times.
>
> Total **20** — and note **77 is counted twice**, once for each of its 7s, which is correct since we're counting *digits*, not numbers.

<!-- ===== Palindromes ===== -->

What is a **palindrome**? Give three examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> A number that **reads the same from left to right and from right to left**.
>
> Examples: **66, 848, 1111** (also 575, 797).

---

Write all the 3-digit palindromes that use only the digits **1, 2 and 3**.

> [!ANSWER]
> A 3-digit palindrome has the form **aba**, where the first and last digits match.
>
> `111, 121, 131, 212, 222, 232, 313, 323, 333` — **9 of them** (3 choices for a × 3 for b).

---

Apply **reverse-and-add** to **68** until you reach a palindrome.

> [!ANSWER]
> `68 + 86 = 154` — not a palindrome.
>
> `154 + 451 = 605` — not a palindrome.
>
> `605 + 506 = ` **1111** ✓ — a palindrome after **3 rounds**.

---

Does reverse-and-add **always** produce a palindrome?

> [!ANSWER]
> **For 2-digit starting numbers, yes — always.**
>
> **For 3-digit numbers, nobody knows.** It is suspected that starting from **196** you never reach a palindrome, but this has **never been proved**.
>
> A reminder that easily-stated questions can still be unanswered.

---

I am a **5-digit palindrome**. I am **odd**. My tens digit is **double** my units digit, and my hundreds digit is **double** my tens digit. Who am I?

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the units digit be **u**. Being a palindrome and odd, u is odd.
>
> Tens = `2u`, hundreds = `4u`. For hundreds to stay a single digit, `4u ≤ 9` → u can only be **1**.
>
> So units = 1, tens = 2, hundreds = 4, and the palindrome `u t h t u` reads **12421**.

<!-- ===== Kaprekar ===== -->

Describe the **three steps** in Kaprekar's process for a 4-digit number.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** Arrange the digits to make the **largest** number, **A**. **2.** Arrange them to make the **smallest** number, **B**. **3.** Work out **C = A − B**, then repeat with C.
>
> (The starting number must have **at least two different digits** — otherwise A − B = 0.)

---

Carry out Kaprekar's process on **6382**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `8632 − 2368 = 6264`
>
> `6642 − 2466 = 4176`
>
> `7641 − 1467 = ` **6174**
>
> Three rounds to reach it.

---

What happens if you apply the process **again** to 6174? What is 6174 called?

> [!ANSWER]
> `7641 − 1467 = ` **6174** — it stays put.
>
> That's why it's called the **Kaprekar constant**: every valid 4-digit starting number eventually lands here and then never moves.
>
> Discovered by **D.R. Kaprekar**, a maths teacher from Devlali, Maharashtra, in **1949**.

---

What is the equivalent constant for **3-digit** numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **495** — the same process on 3-digit numbers always settles there.

---

<!-- ===== Digits and operations ===== -->

Can a **4-digit number + a 2-digit number** ever give a **6-digit** answer?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.**
>
> The largest possible is `9999 + 99 = 10098` — only **5 digits**. There's no way to reach six.
>
> **Reasoning about the size of the answer before calculating** saves a lot of pointless arithmetic.

---

Can a **5-digit − 5-digit** subtraction give a **6-digit** answer?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.** Subtracting makes a number **smaller**, so the answer can't have more digits than the number you started from.
>
> The most it can be is a 5-digit result (e.g. `99999 − 10000 = 89999`).

---

"5-digit + 5-digit gives a 5-digit number" — always, sometimes, or never true?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes.**
>
> `10000 + 10000 = 20000` ✓ (5 digits), but `90000 + 90000 = 180000` ✗ (6 digits).
>
> It depends on whether the sum crosses 100,000.

---

Find the sum of a grid of **thirty-two 32s** without adding them one by one.

> [!ANSWER]
> Thirty-two copies of 32 is `32 × 32 = ` **1024**.
>
> **Spot the structure first** — a repeated number turns a long addition into a single multiplication.

---

<!-- ===== Collatz ===== -->

State the **Collatz rule** and apply it to **12**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **If the number is even, halve it. If it is odd, multiply by 3 and add 1. Repeat.**
>
> `12 → 6 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1`

---

Apply the Collatz rule to **21**.

> [!ANSWER]
> 21 is odd → `3 × 21 + 1 = 64`, then keep halving:
>
> `21 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1`

---

What did **Lothar Collatz** conjecture, and is it proved?

> [!ANSWER]
> In **1937** he conjectured that the sequence **always reaches 1**, whatever whole number you start from.
>
> **It is still unproved.** Despite many mathematicians working on it, nobody knows whether it's true for every number — it remains **one of mathematics' famous unsolved problems**.
>
> Worth noticing: the rule is simple enough for a Class 6 student, yet the question defeats the experts. Mathematics is not a finished subject.
