<!-- ===== Common multiples & factors ===== -->

In the idli-vada game (idli on multiples of 3, vada on multiples of 5), what is the **first** number where players say **"idli-vada"**, and what are such numbers called?

> [!ANSWER]
> **15** — the first number that is a multiple of **both** 3 and 5.
>
> They are the **common multiples** of 3 and 5: 15, 30, 45, 60, …

---

Playing idli-vada from 1 to 60, at which numbers is **"idli-vada"** said?

> [!ANSWER]
> The common multiples of 3 and 5 up to 60: **15, 30, 45, 60** — four times.
>
> (Every 15th number, because 15 is the first common multiple and the rest are its multiples.)

---

Jumpy starts at 0 and jumps in equal steps. Which **jump sizes** land him exactly on **24**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Jumping in steps of *j* lands only on **multiples of j**, so he reaches 24 only if **j is a factor of 24**:
>
> **1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24**.

---

Treasures sit on **14 and 36**. Which jump sizes reach **both**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Factors of 14: **1, 2, 7, 14**. Factors of 36: **1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36**.
>
> Shared: **1 and 2** — the **common factors**. Only those jump sizes reach both treasures.
>
> (A jump of 7 lands on 14 but then 21, 28, 35, 42 — it skips right over 36.)

---

Find the **common factors** of **20 and 28**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Factors of 20: 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20. Factors of 28: 1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28.
>
> Common: **1, 2, 4**.

---

Find three numbers that are multiples of **25** but **not** multiples of 50.

> [!ANSWER]
> **25, 75, 125** (also 175, 225, …).
>
> **Why these:** multiples of 25 alternate — 25, 50, 75, 100, 125 — and every *second* one is also a multiple of 50. Taking the odd ones out (25 × 1, 25 × 3, 25 × 5) avoids 50 every time.

---

I am a number **less than 40**. One of my factors is **7**. My digits add to **8**. Who am I?

> [!ANSWER]
> Multiples of 7 under 40: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35.
>
> Digit sums: 7, 5, 3, **10**, 8 → only **35** gives 8.
>
> **I am 35.**

---

Find the smallest number that is a multiple of **all the numbers from 1 to 10**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take enough of each prime to cover every number up to 10: `8 = 2³` needs three 2s, `9 = 3²` needs two 3s, plus 5 and 7.
>
> `2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 = ` **2520**
>
> Check: 2520 ÷ 4 = 630, ÷ 6 = 420, ÷ 8 = 315, ÷ 9 = 280 ✓ — all whole.

<!-- ===== Prime & composite ===== -->

12 figs can be laid in a rectangle several ways, but 7 figs only one way. What does that tell you about the two numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> The rows and columns of each rectangle are **factors**.
>
> **12** has many rectangles (1×12, 2×6, 3×4) → **many factors** → **composite**.
>
> **7** has only 1×7 → **exactly two factors** → **prime**.

---

Define **prime** and **composite**. Which is 1?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Prime: exactly two factors** — 1 and itself. **Composite: more than two factors.**
>
> **1 is neither** — it has only **one** factor (itself), so it fails the "exactly two" requirement.

---

Is there an even prime other than 2?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — 2 is the only even prime.**
>
> Every other even number has **2** as a factor as well as 1 and itself → at least three factors → composite.

---

Which of these are prime: **23, 51, 37, 26**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **23 — prime.** **37 — prime.**
>
> **51 — composite:** `51 = 3 × 17` (its digits 5+1 = 6 hint at a factor of 3).
>
> **26 — composite:** even, so `26 = 2 × 13`.
>
> 51 is the trap — it looks prime until you test 3.

---

Explain how the **Sieve of Eratosthenes** finds all primes up to 100, and why it works.

> [!ANSWER]
> Cross out 1. Circle 2 and cross out all later multiples of 2; the next uncrossed number is 3 — circle it and cross out its multiples; then 5, and so on. Circled = prime, crossed = composite.
>
> **Why it works:** crossing out the multiples of a number removes exactly the numbers that **have it as a factor** — that is, the composites. Anything left standing has no factor besides 1 and itself, which *is* the definition of prime.

---

Find **seven consecutive composite numbers** below 100.

> [!ANSWER]
> **90, 91, 92, 93, 94, 95, 96.**
>
> Check each: 90 even · 91 = 7×13 · 92 even · 93 = 3×31 · 94 even · 95 = 5×19 · 96 even. The primes 89 and 97 sit either side of this gap.

---

List the **twin primes** below 50.

> [!ANSWER]
> Twin primes differ by **2**: **(3, 5), (5, 7), (11, 13), (17, 19), (29, 31), (41, 43)**.

---

**28** is a *perfect number*. Show why, then find the perfect number between 1 and 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> A perfect number's factors add to **twice** the number.
>
> 28: `1 + 2 + 4 + 7 + 14 + 28 = 56 = 2 × 28` ✓
>
> Between 1 and 10: **6** — `1 + 2 + 3 + 6 = 12 = 2 × 6` ✓

---

True or false: **"Prime numbers have no factors."**

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.** Every prime has **exactly two** factors — 1 and itself.
>
> The correct statement is that a prime has **no factors other than 1 and itself**. Saying "no factors" would leave a number with nothing dividing it, which is impossible.

<!-- ===== Co-prime ===== -->

What does **co-prime** mean? Are 4 and 9 co-prime?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two numbers are **co-prime if their only common factor is 1**.
>
> Factors of 4: 1, 2, 4. Factors of 9: 1, 3, 9. Only 1 is shared → **yes, co-prime**.
>
> Note neither number is prime itself — **co-prime is about the pair, not the numbers**.

---

Are **12 and 26** co-prime? Are **18 and 35**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **12 and 26 — no.** Both are even, so **2** is a common factor.
>
> **18 and 35 — yes.** `18 = 2 × 3 × 3`, `35 = 5 × 7` — nothing shared.

---

For a co-prime pair, where does the **first common multiple** fall?

> [!ANSWER]
> Exactly at their **product**. For 4 and 9 it is `4 × 9 = 36`.
>
> When the numbers **do** share a factor, the first common multiple comes in **below** the product — 6 and 8 share a 2, and their first common multiple is 24, not 48.

---

Guna says "any two prime numbers are co-prime." Is he right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** A prime's only factors are 1 and itself. Two *different* primes therefore share nothing but 1 → co-prime.
>
> (The only exception would be a prime paired with itself — 7 and 7 share 7 — but that isn't two different numbers.)

---

<!-- ===== Prime factorisation ===== -->

Find the prime factorisation of **64** and of **105**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `64 = 2 × 32 = 2 × 2 × 16 = ` **2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2** (six 2s, i.e. 2⁶).
>
> `105 = 5 × 21 = ` **3 × 5 × 7**.

---

Break **36** down in two different ways and compare the results.

> [!ANSWER]
> Via 4 × 9: `(2 × 2) × (3 × 3)`. Via 6 × 6: `(2 × 3) × (2 × 3)`.
>
> Both give **two 2s and two 3s** — the same primes, possibly in a different order.
>
> **This is the key fact:** a number's prime factorisation is **unique** except for order. Order doesn't matter because multiplying in any order gives the same product.

---

A number's prime factorisation has **one 2, two 3s and one 11**. What is the number?

> [!ANSWER]
> Multiply them: `2 × 3 × 3 × 11 = 2 × 9 × 11 = ` **198**.

---

Anshu writes `56 = 14 × 4` and `63 = 21 × 3`, sees no common factor, and says they're co-prime. What's wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **One way of splitting a number doesn't reveal all its factors.**
>
> Written differently: `56 = 7 × 8` and `63 = 9 × 7` — they **share 7**, so they are **not** co-prime.
>
> Only **prime factorisation** is safe, because it digs down to every prime factor there is: `56 = 2×2×2×7`, `63 = 3×3×7` → the shared 7 is unmissable.

---

Use prime factorisation to decide whether **80 and 63** are co-prime.

> [!ANSWER]
> `80 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 5` · `63 = 3 × 3 × 7`
>
> **No common prime → co-prime.**
>
> **Why that's conclusive:** any shared factor bigger than 1 would have its own prime factors appearing in **both** lists. Nothing is shared, so no such factor can exist.

---

Are **121 and 1331** co-prime?

> [!ANSWER]
> `121 = 11 × 11` · `1331 = 11 × 11 × 11`
>
> They share **11** → **not co-prime**. (In fact 1331 is a multiple of 121.)

---

Is **168 divisible by 12**? Use prime factorisation.

> [!ANSWER]
> `168 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 7` · `12 = 2 × 2 × 3`
>
> Everything 12 needs (two 2s and a 3) sits inside 168's list → regroup: `(2 × 2 × 3) × (2 × 7) = 12 × 14` → **yes**.

---

Is **42 divisible by 12**? Careful — both have the primes 2 and 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> `42 = 2 × 3 × 7` · `12 = 2 × 2 × 3`
>
> **No.** 12 needs **two 2s**, but 42 has only **one**.
>
> **The trap:** having the right primes isn't enough — you need **enough copies of each one**.

---

Is **999 divisible by 99**?

> [!ANSWER]
> `999 = 3 × 333 = 3 × 3 × 111 = 3 × 3 × 3 × 37` · `99 = 3 × 3 × 11`
>
> 99 needs an **11**, which 999 doesn't have → **no**.

---

Find the prime factorisation of **56 × 25** without multiplying them out first.

> [!ANSWER]
> Factorise each piece and put them together: `56 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 7` and `25 = 5 × 5`.
>
> `56 × 25 = ` **2 × 2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 7**
>
> This works because the factorisation of a product is just the two factorisations combined — much easier than factorising 1400 from scratch.

---

What is the **smallest** number whose prime factorisation has **three different primes**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Use the three **smallest** primes, once each: `2 × 3 × 5 = ` **30**.
>
> Any other choice swaps in a bigger prime and makes the number larger.

<!-- ===== Divisibility tests ===== -->

State the tests for divisibility by **10, 5 and 2**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **10:** ends in **0** · **5:** ends in **0 or 5** · **2:** ends in **0, 2, 4, 6 or 8**.
>
> All three depend on the last digit alone, because 10 itself is built from 2 and 5.

---

Why does the **last digit alone fail** as a test for divisibility by 4?

> [!ANSWER]
> Counter-examples: **12 and 22** end in the same digit, but only 12 is a multiple of 4. Same with 14/24 and 16/26.
>
> So the last digit carries too little information — you need the **last two digits**.

---

State the test for **4**, and explain why it works. Is **8536** divisible by 4?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Test:** the number formed by the **last two digits** must be divisible by 4.
>
> **Why:** everything above the last two digits is a multiple of **100**, and **100 is divisible by 4** — so that whole part is automatically fine. Only the leftover two digits can spoil it.
>
> 8536 → last two digits **36**, and `36 ÷ 4 = 9` ✓ → **yes, divisible by 4**.

---

State the test for **8** and the reason behind it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The last three digits** must form a multiple of 8.
>
> **Why:** everything above them is a multiple of **1000**, and **1000 ÷ 8 = 125** exactly — so that part never affects the answer. Only the last three digits matter.

---

Guna checked **14560** against just **two** of {2, 4, 5, 8, 10} and declared it divisible by all five. Which two, and why is that enough?

> [!ANSWER]
> Checking **8 and 10** is enough.
>
> **Why:** divisibility by **8** brings 4 and 2 free (since 4 and 2 divide 8), and divisibility by **10** brings 5 and 2 free. Between them the whole list is covered.
>
> (Check: last three digits 560 ÷ 8 = 70 ✓, and it ends in 0 ✓.)

---

Which of **572, 5600, 6000** are divisible by all of 2, 4, 5, 8 and 10?

> [!ANSWER]
> Test 8 and 10 as above.
>
> **572** — doesn't end in 0, so fails 10 (and 5). ✗
>
> **5600** — ends in 0 ✓; last three digits 600, `600 ÷ 8 = 75` ✓ → **yes**.
>
> **6000** — ends in 0 ✓; `000 ÷ 8 = 0` ✓ → **yes**.

---

Find the remainders when **173** is divided by 10, by 5 and by 2.

> [!ANSWER]
> **÷10 → remainder 3** (the last digit itself).
>
> **÷5 → remainder 3** (`170` is a multiple of 5, leaving 3).
>
> **÷2 → remainder 1** (173 is odd).

---

2024 is a leap year. State the leap-year rule, and say whether **1900** and **2000** were leap years.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Leap years are multiples of 4 — except century years, which must also be divisible by 400.**
>
> **1900:** divisible by 100 but `1900 ÷ 400` isn't whole → **not** a leap year.
>
> **2000:** divisible by 400 ✓ → **leap year**.

---

Find the **largest 4-digit palindrome** that is divisible by 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> A 4-digit palindrome looks like **abba**. For divisibility by 4 the last two digits **ba** must form a multiple of 4.
>
> Start from the largest, a = 9: the number is `9bb9` — but it ends in 9, which is odd, so it can never be divisible by 4. So a must be **even**.
>
> Try a = 8: `8bb8`, needing **b8** divisible by 4 → b8 could be 08, 28, 48, 68, 88. Largest b is **8** → **8888** (`88 ÷ 4 = 22` ✓).
