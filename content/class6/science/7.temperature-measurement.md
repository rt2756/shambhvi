> *"Wrong measurements are worse than no measurements at all."* — **Anna Mani**

## Touch is not reliable

**The three-container experiment:** put your **right hand in warm water** and your **left hand in ice-cold water** for a minute or two. Now put **both hands into the same container of tap water**.

- The **right hand feels it cool**; the **left hand feels the same water warm**.

> **We cannot always rely on our sense of touch to decide whether a body is hot or cold.**

**Why:** touch reports a **change** from what your hand was already used to, not the actual hotness. Two hands starting from different conditions therefore disagree about the same water.

## Temperature

> **A reliable measure of the hotness (or coldness) of a body is its temperature.** A hotter body has a higher temperature.

- A device that measures temperature is a **thermometer**.
- **Clinical thermometers** measure human body temperature; **laboratory thermometers** are used for many other purposes.

## Temperature scales

| Scale | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| **Celsius** | degree Celsius | **°C** |
| **Fahrenheit** | degree Fahrenheit | **°F** |
| **Kelvin** | kelvin | **K** — **the SI unit** |

- `Temperature in Kelvin = Temperature in Celsius + 273.15`
- **37.0 °C = 98.6 °F.** The Fahrenheit scale is no longer used in most scientific work.
- All three are **named in honour of the scientists** who developed them.

> [!WARNING]
> **Writing them correctly:** scale names (Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin) take a **capital**; in the unit names, **degree** is lowercase but **Celsius/Fahrenheit** are capitalised; the unit **kelvin** is lowercase. All **symbols are capitals (°C, °F, K)** — and **no degree sign is written with K**. No full stop after the symbol, and **leave a space** between the number and the unit.

## The clinical thermometer

**Digital clinical thermometers** run on batteries, use **heat sensors**, and show the temperature **digitally**.

- They have **replaced mercury thermometers**, because **mercury is extremely toxic** and hard to dispose of safely if the thermometer breaks. Digital displays are also easier to read.
- **Non-contact (infrared) thermometers** measure temperature **without touching** — widely used during the COVID-19 pandemic because they **reduce the risk of spreading disease**.

**How to use one:** wash your hands and the tip with soap and water → **reset** it → place under the tongue and close your mouth → wait for the **beep or flash** → read the display → clean and dry the tip again.

> [!WARNING]
> **Precautions:** read the instruction manual first · **wash the tip before and after use**, keeping the display **out of water** · **do not hold the thermometer by the tip** (your hand's warmth would affect the reading).

### Normal body temperature

**The normal temperature of a healthy human body is taken as 37.0 °C.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **But not everyone reads exactly 37.0 °C, and that's fine.** "Normal" is the **average body temperature of a large number of healthy people** — a perfectly healthy person may sit slightly above or below it.
>
> Body temperature is influenced by **age, time of day and activity level**.

- Human temperature does **not normally go below 35 °C or above 42 °C**.
- Measuring in the **armpit** (used for small children or the elderly) reads about **0.5 °C to 1 °C lower** than the actual body temperature.

> [!NOTE]
> Before thermometers, fever was detected from the **pulse rate**, which was known in India in olden days. But other situations also affect pulse rate, so **pulse alone is not a reliable indicator of fever**.

## The laboratory thermometer

A **long, narrow, uniform sealed glass tube** with a **bulb** at one end containing a liquid — generally **alcohol (coloured red to be seen easily) or mercury**. A **Celsius scale** is marked along the tube; the liquid column rises and falls with temperature, and the mark it reaches is the reading.

- A typical range is **−10 °C to 110 °C**.
- **A clinical thermometer cannot measure boiling water or ice** — those temperatures are **outside its range**. This is what the laboratory thermometer is for.

### Finding the smallest value it can read

Look at the gap between two big marks and count the small divisions in it.

**Example:** between 0 °C and 10 °C the difference is 10 °C, and there are **10 divisions** → each small division is `10 ÷ 10 = ` **1 °C**. So the smallest value this thermometer can read is **1 °C**.

> [!TIP]
> **Always check the range and the smallest division of the particular thermometer you are about to use** — they differ from one instrument to another.

### Using it correctly

- The bulb must **not touch the bottom or sides** of the beaker (you'd be measuring the glass, not the liquid).
- Hold it **vertically**, not tilted.
- **Read it while it is still immersed** — the liquid column starts falling the moment you lift it out.
- Keep your **eye directly in line with the top of the liquid column**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Temperature stays constant while water is boiling, and constant while ice is melting** — even though heating continues the whole time. The heat is going into *changing the state*, not raising the temperature.
