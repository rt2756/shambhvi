> *"Wrong measurements are worse than no measurements at all."* — Anna Mani

## Lambok feels feverish

**Lambok** and his elder sister **Phiban** live in **Shillong**. Back from school, Lambok says he feels feverish. Phiban touches his forehead — he *might* have a fever. To be sure, she takes out the **thermometer**, **washes its tip with soap and water**, and measures. **Normal!** She washes and dries the tip again and puts it away.

**Question:** can you always tell a fever just by touching? Let's test our sense of touch.

## Can we trust touch?

**Activity 7.1:** three containers — **A warm water, B tap water, C ice-cold water.**

1. Keep your **right hand in A** (warm) and **left hand in C** (ice-cold) for 1–2 minutes.
2. Then put **both hands into B** at the same time.

| Hand | Was in | Feels B as |
|---|---|---|
| Right | warm A | **cool** |
| Left | ice-cold C | **warm** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The SAME water feels cool to one hand and warm to the other.**
> **Why:** your skin judges hot or cold **compared with what it was just touching**, not on a fixed scale. So **we cannot rely on touch** to decide how hot something is.

## Temperature

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **TEMPERATURE = a reliable measure of how hot or cold a body is.**
> - A **hotter** body has a **higher** temperature.
> - The **difference** in temperature tells how much hotter one body is than another.
>
> A **THERMOMETER** is a device that measures temperature. Two common kinds: **clinical** (body temperature) and **laboratory** (everything else).

## Clinical thermometer

The **digital clinical thermometer** shows the temperature as **numbers on a display**, **runs on batteries**, and senses temperature with a **heat sensor** when touching the body. It uses the **Celsius scale**, unit **degree Celsius (°C)**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Why mercury thermometers are being replaced:** **mercury is extremely toxic** and hard to dispose of if the thermometer breaks. **Digital thermometers** have no such risk and their numbers are **easier to read**.

> [!NOTE]
> **During COVID-19**, **non-contact (infrared) thermometers** measured temperature **from a distance** — without touching, so there was **less risk of spreading disease**.

### Using a digital thermometer (Activity 7.2)

1. **Wash** your hands and the thermometer **tip** with soap and water.
2. **Reset** it with the reset button.
3. Place it **under the tongue** and close your mouth.
4. Wait for the **beep or flashing light**.
5. Take it out and **read** the display; record it.
6. **Clean** the tip again and dry it.

> [!WARNING]
> **Precautions:** read the instruction manual · wash the tip **before and after** use · keep the **display out of water** while washing · **don't hold it by the tip** (your fingers would warm or dirty it).

### What is "normal" body temperature?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Normal temperature of a healthy human = 37.0 °C (= 98.6 °F).**
>
> **But not everyone reads exactly 37.0 °C.** "Normal" is the **average** of a large number of healthy people. A perfectly healthy person may be slightly above or below — it changes with **age, time of day and activity**.
>
> Human temperature normally stays **between 35 °C and 42 °C**.

> [!TIP]
> **Small children or old people:** the thermometer can go in the **armpit** instead. The reading is about **0.5 °C to 1 °C LOWER** than the actual body temperature (the armpit is less enclosed than the mouth).

> [!NOTE]
> **Before thermometers**, fever was guessed in India from the **pulse rate**. But other things (running, fear) change the pulse too — so the pulse alone is **not a reliable** fever test.

> [!WARNING]
> **A clinical thermometer can't measure boiling water or ice** — those temperatures are **outside its range**.

## Temperature scales

| Scale | Unit | Symbol | Human body | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Celsius** | degree Celsius | **°C** | 37.0 °C | everyday & clinical |
| **Fahrenheit** | degree Fahrenheit | **°F** | 98.6 °F | some thermometers; not in most science now |
| **Kelvin** | kelvin | **K** | 310.15 K | science — **SI unit** |

All three are named after the **scientists who developed them**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SI unit of temperature = kelvin (K).**
> **Kelvin = Celsius + 273.15** → 0 °C = 273.15 K; 37 °C = 310.15 K.

> [!TIP]
> **Komal says she has a fever of "101 degrees". Celsius or Fahrenheit?** → **Fahrenheit.** 101 °C is hotter than **boiling water** — impossible for a body. (Normal is 98.6 °F, so 101 °F is a mild fever.)

> [!WARNING]
> **Writing temperature units:**
> - **scale names** capitalised: Celsius scale, Fahrenheit scale, Kelvin scale
> - **"degree" lowercase**, "Celsius" capital: degree Celsius; unit **kelvin** all lowercase
> - **symbols** are capitals: °C, °F, K — and **no degree sign with K** (`300 K`, not `300 °K`)
> - **a space** between number and unit: `37.0 °C`; plural "degrees" for the full name

## Laboratory thermometer

A **long, narrow, uniform sealed glass tube** with a **bulb** at one end holding a liquid — usually **alcohol coloured red** (easy to see), or **mercury**. A **Celsius scale** is marked along the tube. The liquid column **rises when hot, falls when cold**; the mark at the **top of the column** is the reading.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Usual RANGE: −10 °C to 110 °C** (lowest to highest it can read).
>
> **Smallest value it can read (Activity 7.4):**
> **smallest division = (temperature between two big marks) ÷ (number of small divisions between them)**
> - 10 °C between big marks, **10** divisions → 10 ÷ 10 = **1 °C**
> - 0 °C to 100 °C with **50** divisions → 100 ÷ 50 = **2 °C** each
>
> Thermometers differ — **always check the range and the smallest division** of the one you use.

### Using it correctly

> [!IMPORTANT]
> 1. **Bulb fully in the liquid**, but **not touching the bottom or sides** of the beaker (they may be hotter or colder than the liquid).
> 2. Hold it **vertical**, not tilted.
> 3. **Read it while still in the liquid** — the moment you take it out, the column starts to **fall**.
> 4. **Eye level with the top of the liquid column.**
> 5. Wait till the column **stops rising** — but don't wait too long, or the water itself cools.
>
> **Precautions:** handle with care (glass breaks); **don't hold it by the bulb** (your hand's warmth changes the reading).

> [!WARNING]
> **Why not use a lab thermometer for body temperature?** It must stay **in** what it measures while you read it — you can't read it inside your mouth. And its column **falls as soon as it's removed**, so you can't take it out to read.

### Ice and boiling water

Under the teacher's supervision: **melting ice stays at about 0 °C** and **boiling water stays at about 100 °C** — **the temperature stays constant** while ice melts and while water boils.

**Phiban's class in Shillong** measured boiling water: **97.8, 98.0, 97.9, 98.0, 98.1 °C**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why are the readings slightly different?** Some students may not have followed the **correct method** (tilted thermometer, eye not level, bulb touching the beaker). (And Shillong is **high up in the hills**, where water boils a little **below 100 °C**.)

## Air temperature

- **Room thermometers** on walls of labs, clinics and hospitals give an **approximate room temperature**.
- **Weather reports** give the day's **maximum and minimum air temperature**. Over 10 days they **change** — weather depends on many factors; temperatures **rise towards summer** and **fall towards winter**.
- Weather stations all over the world record air temperature to make **weather forecasts**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Anna Mani (1918–2001)** — the **"Weather Woman of India"** — designed and built many **weather instruments**, so India no longer had to depend on other nations for them. She also explored **wind and solar energy**, helping India become a leader in **renewable energy**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Extremes:** the **Sun's core** reaches about **15 million °C** — and there's **no upper limit** to temperature. But there **is a lowest** possible: **−273.15 °C = 0 K, absolute zero.**

## Think-it-through questions

1. **Normal body temperature** → **37.0 °C** (not 98.6 °C!). **37 °C = 98.6 °F.**
2. **Ice-cold water can't be measured by a ___ thermometer.** → **clinical** (range only about 35–42 °C).
3. **Vaishnavi's record:** highest **40.0 °C**, on **Day One at 7 pm**; back to normal on **Day Three** (down to 37.0 °C and below).
4. **To measure 22.5 °C**, pick a thermometer whose **range includes 22.5 °C** and that has **0.5 °C divisions** (or finer) — otherwise you can't read the ".5".
5. **Draw a scale with 0.5 °C divisions from 10 °C to 20 °C** → 10 °C ÷ 0.5 °C = **20 small divisions** between the two big marks.

## In a nutshell

- Touch is **unreliable**; **temperature** measures hotness reliably, with a **thermometer**.
- Scales: **Celsius (°C), Fahrenheit (°F), Kelvin (K)**; **SI unit = kelvin**; K = °C + 273.15.
- **Clinical (digital)**: body temperature; normal **37.0 °C = 98.6 °F**; armpit reads 0.5–1 °C lower.
- **Laboratory**: range **−10 °C to 110 °C**; smallest division = gap ÷ divisions; read it **vertical, in the liquid, eye level, bulb not touching**.
- Melting ice and boiling water keep a **constant** temperature.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 98.6 is °F, not °C · no degree sign with K · don't hold a thermometer by its bulb or tip · read a lab thermometer while it's still in the liquid · a clinical thermometer can't measure boiling water.
