## Stars & constellations

- **Stars shine with their own light.** Some are bright, some dim.
- Groups of stars appear to form **patterns** resembling familiar things. Ancient peoples named them after animals, objects or story characters — **different cultures gave different names to the same stars**.
- **Why they bothered:** the patterns **helped them recognise stars in the sky**, and **recognising stars was a useful skill for navigation** — sailors and travellers used them to find direction at sea or on land, long before the magnetic compass. **It is still used in emergencies as a backup.**

> **A constellation was originally a pattern of stars; today the term means a defined REGION of the sky** that usually contains such a pattern.

> [!NOTE]
> The **International Astronomical Union (IAU)** defined an agreed set of boundaries in the early twentieth century, officially listing **88 constellations** and dividing the **entire sky into 88 regions**.

### Constellations to know

| Constellation | Imagined as | Notable star |
|---|---|---|
| **Orion** | a **hunter** — three stars in the middle form his **belt** | Betelgeuse (**Ārdrā**) |
| **Canis Major** | Orion's **dog** | **Sirius — the brightest star in the night sky** |
| **Taurus** | a **bull** | Aldebaran (**Rohiṇī**); Pleiades (**Kṛittikā**) |
| **Ursa Major** | contains the **Big Dipper** (**Saptaṛiṣhi**) | — |
| **Ursa Minor** | contains the **Little Dipper** | **Pole Star / Polaris (Dhruva tārā)** |

- **The Pole Star appears nearly stationary in the North**, which is what makes it useful for finding the north direction **in the Northern Hemisphere**.

> [!NOTE]
> In Indian astronomy, **nakṣhatra** denotes a star or group of stars. Indian communities have their own stories too: tribes in Central India see four stars of the Big Dipper as a **"grandmother's cot"** with three thieves stealing it; fishermen along the **Konkan coast** see the same four as a **boat** with the other three as its neck.

## Night sky watching

- **Light pollution** = the presence of **excessive artificial light at night**. In cities it hides most stars; villages and dark areas show far more.
- **Not all stars and constellations are visible from all places or on all nights** — the **Pole Star is not visible from the Southern Hemisphere** at all.

**How to observe well:**

- Choose a **dark, open area**, away from lights, tall buildings and trees — **with an adult**.
- Pick a night with **no Moon and no clouds**, especially for the Pole Star, which is not very bright.
- **Wait about half an hour for your eyes to adjust to the darkness.**
- Take a **sky-mapping app** (Sky Map, Stellarium), printed constellation images, a **magnetic compass** and a notebook.

**Finding the Pole Star:** locate the **Big Dipper**, take the **two stars at the end of its cup**, and imagine a straight line through them towards the north. At about **five times the distance between those two stars** you reach a not-very-bright star — the **Pole Star**.

**Finding Orion and Sirius:** best seen in India from **December to April** after sunset. Spot the **three bright stars in a short straight line** (the belt) — that's Orion. Extend a line through those three towards the **east** to reach **Sirius**.

## The Sun

- **The Sun is a star — the star closest to us.** An extremely hot spherical ball of **gases** giving out a huge amount of energy.
- **About 100 times bigger than the Earth in diameter**, and about **150 million km** away.
- **1 astronomical unit (au)** ≈ the Sun–Earth distance.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the Sun looks huge and other stars look like dots:** the Sun is **much closer** to us. Other stars are **so far away that they appear as points**, even though **some are much bigger than our Sun**. And during the day, the Sun's extreme brightness makes the others impossible to see.
>
> The nearest star after the Sun, **Proxima Centauri**, is about **269,000 au** away — 269,000 times our distance to the Sun.

## The Solar System

**The Earth, the Sun and many other objects together form our Solar System.** Most of these objects move around the Sun — **the movement of an object around the Sun is called revolution**.

### Planets

> **A planet is a large, nearly spherical object that revolves around the Sun.**

**In order of increasing distance from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.**

- **Inner four (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars)** — **smaller**, with **solid rocky surfaces**.
- **Outer four (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune)** — **much larger**, mostly **gas and ice**, with **large flat ring-like structures** of dust particles and rocky material.

**Nicknames and why:**
- **Venus — "Morning Star" / "Evening Star"** (though it's **not a star**), seen shining brightly at dawn and dusk.
- **Mars — the "Red Planet"**, because **its soil is reddish**.
- **Earth — the "Blue Planet"**, because a large portion of its surface is **covered with water**, so it appears blue from space.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Planets get most of their energy from the Sun, so in general the farther away, the colder.** But **an atmosphere can trap heat** — which is why **Venus is hotter than Mercury**, even though Venus is farther from the Sun.

> [!NOTE]
> Traditional Indian names: **Budha** (Mercury), **Śhukra** (Venus), **Pṛithvī** (Earth), **Mangala** (Mars), **Bṛihaspati/Guru** (Jupiter), **Śhani** (Saturn).
>
> **Pluto** was called a planet when discovered, but after more similar small objects were found, the **IAU redefined "planet" in 2006** — Pluto and the others are now **dwarf planets**.

### Telling planets from stars

> [!TIP]
> **Stars appear to twinkle a lot; planets do not.** That's the easiest way to tell them apart with the naked eye.

- **Venus is the easiest planet to identify** — after the Sun and Moon it is the **brightest object in the sky**. Look **east before sunrise** or **west after sunset**.
- Mercury, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are also visible to the naked eye, as shining dots.
- **Binoculars or a telescope** show objects brighter and larger, and reveal dim objects the naked eye cannot see.

## Natural satellites & the Moon

- **Objects that move around planets are called satellites**; they are **smaller than planets**. **Moons are the natural satellites of planets.**
- **Earth has 1 Moon; Mars has 2**; Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune have **large numbers**.

**The Moon:**
- About **384,000 km** from Earth — our **nearest neighbour in space**.
- Takes about **27 days** to complete one revolution around the Earth.
- About **a quarter the size of the Earth** in diameter.
- **Hardly any atmosphere.**
- Its surface has circular bowl-like **craters**, mostly formed by **asteroids or rocks from space hitting it**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why the craters survive so long:** with **no atmosphere, no water and no life** on the Moon, there is nothing to erode or wash them away — so the features stay for a very long time. On Earth, wind and water would have worn them down.

> [!NOTE]
> **India's Chandrayaan missions:** **Chandrayaan-1** (2008), **Chandrayaan-2** (2019), and **Chandrayaan-3** (launched July 2023) whose **Vikram lander with the Pragyan rover soft-landed on 23 August 2023** — making **India the first country to land near the Moon's little-explored south pole**. That date is now celebrated as **National Space Day**. **Chandrayaan-4** is planned, aiming to **bring back soil and rock samples**.

## Asteroids & comets

- **Asteroids** are small, **rocky and irregularly shaped** objects (about **10 m to 500 km**). Many revolve around the Sun between the paths of **Mars and Jupiter** — the **asteroid belt**. Some occasionally pass close to Earth.
- **Comets** are made of **dust, gases, rocks and ice**. As a comet approaches the Sun, its **frozen material starts evaporating**, and **that evaporating material forms the tail**. Moving away from the Sun they grow dim and can no longer be seen with the naked eye.
