## Deepa's new uniform

**Deepa**, 11, from a town in **Haryana**, has grown taller and needs a new uniform. At the cloth shop:

- her mother asks for **two metres** of cloth — the shopkeeper measures it with a **metal measuring rod**
- the tailor measures Deepa with a **flexible measuring tape**
- her mother tells him to add **"char angula"** — **four fingers' width** — to the length

Deepa's friends **Anish, Hardeep, Padma and Tasneem** chip in: grandmothers measure cloth by the **length of the arm**; farmers measure field beds by counting **strides**, or the length of their **feet**.

## Why we need standard units

The friends measure their classroom table in **handspans (balisht)**:

| Student | Handspans |
|---|---|
| Anish | slightly more than 13 |
| Padma | 13 |
| Tasneem | slightly less than 13 |
| Deepa | between 13 and 14 |
| Hardeep | 14 |

**Same table — five different answers!** Putting their hands side by side shows why: **their handspans are different sizes.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A measurement has TWO parts: a NUMBER and a UNIT.** In "13 handspans", **13** is the number and **handspan** is the unit.
>
> **Body-part units** (handspan, arm, foot, fist, finger) **differ from person to person** — so the same length gives different numbers. **We need units for which measurements of the same length by different people do NOT differ.**

> [!NOTE]
> **India's long history of measurement.** Ancient Indian texts mention units like the **angula** (finger width) and its multiples, the **dhanusa** and the **yojana**, used for artefacts, **architecture and town planning**. Traditional **carpenters and tailors still use the angula**. Objects with **ruled markings** that may have been scales have been excavated from **Harappan** sites.

### SI units

As people travelled between places using different systems, there was confusion. So countries agreed on standard units — the **International System of Units (SI units)**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The SI unit of length is the METRE, symbol `m`.**
>
> | Unit | Relation |
> |---|---|
> | **kilometre (km)** | **1 km = 1000 m** |
> | **metre (m)** | **1 m = 100 cm** |
> | **centimetre (cm)** | **1 cm = 10 mm** |
> | **millimetre (mm)** | **1 mm = 0.1 cm** |
>
> - **Big lengths** (a railway track between cities) → **km**.
> - **Small lengths** (a page's thickness, a coin's thickness) → **mm or cm**.

### Reading a 15-cm scale

- Big marks **0 to 15** — each gap between two big marks is **1 cm**.
- Each centimetre is split into **10 small parts — each 1 mm**.
- **1 mm is the smallest length you can measure with this scale.**
- Some scales also show **inches**: **1 inch = 2.54 cm** (older units like the **inch** and **foot** are still used by some people).

> [!TIP]
> **Conversions:** 1.5 km = 1.5 × 1000 = **1500 m**. A friend 1.42 m tall = **142 cm** = **1420 mm**. Multiply going to smaller units; divide going to bigger ones.

> [!WARNING]
> **Writing units correctly:**
> - **lowercase**: km, m, cm, mm (capital only at the start of a sentence)
> - **never add "s"** for plural — `5 cm`, not `5 cms`
> - **no full stop** after the symbol (unless the sentence ends)
> - **a space** between number and unit — `9.4 cm`, not `9.4cm`

## Measuring correctly

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **1. Choose the right instrument.**
> - pencil → **15-cm scale**
> - height of a room → **metre scale** or **measuring tape**
> - girth of a tree, size of your chest → **flexible tape** (a tailor's tape) — a stiff scale **can't wrap around** a curved thing
>
> **2. Place the scale in contact with the object, along its length** — not tilted away or at an angle.
>
> **3. Keep your eye DIRECTLY ABOVE the point you're reading.** Looking from the side gives a wrong reading.

**Broken scale or unclear zero?** Start from any **full mark** — say **1.0 cm** — and **subtract**:

> Reading at one end **1.0 cm**, at the other end **10.4 cm** → length = 10.4 − 1.0 = **9.4 cm**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Visually challenged students** use scales with **raised markings** they can feel by touch.

> [!TIP]
> **Why do friends get slightly different lengths for the same pen?** Small differences in **where the eye is**, **how the scale is placed**, or **reading between marks** — that's why careful method matters.

## Measuring a curved line

Anish's family hang **string lights along the arches** of their verandah. How much string do they need?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A rigid scale can't follow a curve.** Use a **flexible measuring tape**, or **lay a thread along the curve**, then **straighten the thread** and measure it with a metre scale.

> [!TIP]
> **Measuring the curved base of a tumbler** works the same way — wrap a thread round it, then straighten and measure.

> [!NOTE]
> **Thickness of one page?** Too thin to measure directly. Measure the thickness of **100 pages** pressed together and **divide by 100**.

## Describing position — the reference point

The teacher asks everyone to come straight to a **garden** for a trip. Is it closer than school?

- **Tasneem and Padma:** the garden is closer.
- **Deepa and Anish:** school is closer.
- **Hardeep:** about the same.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **They're ALL right** — each is measuring from **their own house**. Measure from **one shared point** (say the **bus stand**) and they'd all agree.
>
> **REFERENCE POINT** = the **fixed object or point** from which a distance is stated.

- Marking a **kabaddi court** with chuna (limestone) powder: they first choose a **reference point** on the ground to measure all lines from.
- **Kilometre stones:** Padma's bus passes **"Delhi 70 km"**, then **"Delhi 60 km"**. **Delhi is the reference point** — her **position** (distance from Delhi) is **changing** — so she knows she's getting **closer**.

## Motion and rest

**Activity 5.2:** list things in motion and at rest — and justify each. (A grazing cow — moving; a tree — at rest.)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An object is in MOTION if its position changes with respect to a reference point, with time.**
> **It is at REST if its position does NOT change with respect to the reference point, with time.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Whether something is moving depends on the reference point you choose.**
>
> In a moving bus, Deepa sees the seated passengers still in their seats a minute later:
> - **Reference: the bus (or herself)** → the passengers are **at rest**.
> - **Reference: a building outside** → the passengers (and the bus) are **in motion**.
>
> **Both are correct.** Motion is always **relative to something**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Think it over:** on a ship moving **steadily in a straight line** on a calm sea, with **no windows**, you can't tell whether it's moving — there's no outside reference point to compare with.

## Types of motion

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear** | moves **along a straight line** | a dropped eraser, an orange falling from a tree, a **march-past**, a **pushed box**, a car on a straight road |
| **Circular** | moves **along a circular path** | a **merry-go-round**, an eraser **whirled on a thread** |
| **Oscillatory** | moves **to and fro about a fixed position** | a **swing**, an eraser hanging on a thread (**pendulum**), a **metal strip** pressed down at one end and released |

**Activity 5.6:** hold one end of a **50 cm** metal strip down on a table with books or a brick. Press the free end and let go — it moves **up and down**: oscillatory.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **PERIODIC motion** = motion that **repeats its path after a fixed interval of time**.
> **Both circular and oscillatory motion are periodic** — one goes round the same circle again and again, the other repeats its to-and-fro.

> [!TIP]
> **In a children's park:** slide — **linear**; merry-go-round, giant wheel — **circular**; swing, see-saw — **oscillatory**.
>
> **A ball on a rollercoaster** can show **several kinds**: straight stretches are **linear**, loops are **circular** (one motion can change type along its path).

## Make your own tools

- **A metre scale** — use **plywood or steel**, not **cloth, paper or stretchable rubber**. **Why:** a scale must **not stretch or bend**, or its length changes and every measurement goes wrong.
- **Bicycle distance-meter:** clip a flexible metal strip to a spoke so it **clicks once per turn**. Measure the wheel's rim (thread method), count the clicks, and **multiply**: **distance = rim length × number of turns**. Road-race distances are measured like this with a **Jones Counter**.
- **Height record:** mark your height on a wall **every three months**.

## Think-it-through questions

1. **Match the unit:** Delhi to Lucknow → **km**; thickness of a coin → **mm**; length of an eraser → **cm**; length of a school ground → **m**.
2. **Which is NOT a standard unit: mm, cm, km, handspan?** → **Handspan.**
3. **True or false: 1 km = 100 cm.** → **False** — 1 km = 1000 m = **100 000 cm**.
4. **Why are some measuring devices flexible?** → To **wrap around curved** things (a chest, a tree trunk).
5. **Leaves from the same tree differ in length and breadth.** → Leaves grow at different times and get different light and space — living things vary.

## In a nutshell

- A measurement = **number + unit**; body units vary, so we use **standard SI units**.
- **SI unit of length: metre (m).** 1 km = 1000 m · 1 m = 100 cm · 1 cm = 10 mm.
- **Measure correctly:** right instrument, scale along the object, eye directly above; with a broken scale, start at a full mark and subtract.
- Curved lines: **thread** or **flexible tape**.
- **Reference point** = fixed point distances are stated from. **Motion** = position changing with time relative to it.
- **Linear, circular, oscillatory** motion; circular and oscillatory are **periodic**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** never write "cms" or "9.4cm." · eye directly above the mark · broken scale → subtract the starting mark · rest/motion depend on the reference point · 1 km = 1000 m, not 100.
