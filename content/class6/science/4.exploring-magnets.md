## Natural & artificial magnets

- **Lodestones** are **naturally occurring magnets**, discovered in ancient times and used by early sailors.
- Later people found that magnets could be made from **pieces of iron**. Today's magnets — in pencil boxes, stickers, toys, laboratories — are **artificial magnets**.
- Common shapes: **bar magnet, U-shaped magnet, ring magnet**.

## Magnetic & non-magnetic materials

- **Magnetic materials** are **attracted towards a magnet**: **iron, nickel and cobalt**, plus some of their combinations with other metals.
- **Non-magnetic materials** are **not attracted**: wood, rubber, plastic, glass, paper.

> [!WARNING]
> **Not every metal is magnetic.** Iron, nickel and cobalt are — but copper, aluminium, gold and silver are **not**. "Metal" and "magnetic" are different things.

## Poles

Sprinkle **iron filings** on paper and place a bar magnet on it: **most filings stick near the two ends**, and very few along the middle.

- Those ends are the **poles** — the **North pole** and the **South pole**.
- **Most iron filings stick to the poles of a magnet of any shape** — the attraction is strongest there.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A single pole cannot exist.** Break a magnet into smaller pieces and **every piece still has both a North and a South pole** — however small. Poles always come in pairs.

## Finding directions

**A freely suspended magnet always comes to rest along the north–south direction.**

- The end pointing north is the **North-seeking pole (North pole)**; the other is the **South-seeking pole (South pole)**.
- **Why it happens: the Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet.**

> [!TIP]
> **This gives a test for whether a piece of metal is a magnet.** Suspend it freely: a **magnet** always settles north–south, while a plain **iron bar** can come to rest in **any** direction.

### The magnetic compass

A small circular box with a **transparent cover**, containing a **needle-shaped magnet mounted on a pin** so it can **rotate freely**, above a **dial** marked with directions. The north-pointing end is usually **painted red**.

**To use it:** place it where you want directions, let the needle settle north–south, then **gently rotate the box** until the N and S on the dial line up with the needle. All directions then read correctly off the dial.

**Making your own:** stroke an iron sewing needle **30–40 times** with **one pole** of a magnet, always **in the same direction** (lift the magnet away at the end of each stroke and start again from the same end). Test it by seeing whether it picks up pins or iron filings. Then push it through a **cork** and float it in a bowl of water.

> [!NOTE]
> Long before the modern compass, Indians navigated at sea with the **matsya-yantra** (machchh-yantra) — a **magnetised fish-shaped iron piece floating in a vessel of oil**. Exactly the same principle as the floating-needle compass.

## Attraction & repulsion

> **Unlike poles attract. Like poles repel.**

- North–South → **attract** · North–North or South–South → **repel**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Repulsion is the sure test for a magnet.**
>
> **Why:** a plain iron bar is **attracted by both** the North and South poles of a magnet — so attraction alone proves nothing. Only another **magnet** will be **pushed away**, and only by a like pole. **If two objects repel, both must be magnets.**

**With a compass:** bring a magnet's **North** pole near the compass needle's **North** pole and the needle **moves away**; bring the **South** pole near and the needle **moves closer**.

## Magnetic effect passes through non-magnetic materials

Put a sheet of **wood, cardboard, plastic or glass** between a magnet and a compass needle — and there is **no appreciable change in the deflection**.

**The magnetic effect can act through non-magnetic materials.** That's why a magnet holds a pencil box shut through its plastic lid, and why a fridge magnet works through paper.
