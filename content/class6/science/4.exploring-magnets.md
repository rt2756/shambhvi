## Reshma's stuck story

**Reshma**, who lives in a coastal town in **Kerala**, is writing a story for her grandmother's 60th birthday about a **ship carrying spices** in olden days. Sailors found their way at night **by the stars** — but in her story a **storm** covers the sky. How can they find direction now?

Searching books and the internet, she learns travellers used a **magnetic compass**. She'd seen magnets in **pencil boxes, purses** and a **whiteboard duster**, but never looked closely. Now she's curious.

## Natural and artificial magnets

- **Lodestones** are **naturally occurring magnets**, discovered in ancient times — the old sailors' magnets were based on them.
- Later people found magnets could be **made from pieces of iron**. Magnets in labs, **pencil boxes, stickers and toys** are **artificial magnets**.
- Common shapes: **bar, U-shaped, ring** — also **disc, cylindrical, spherical**.

> [!NOTE]
> **However a magnet is shaped, its poles always come in pairs.** Different shapes are made for different jobs.

## Magnetic and non-magnetic materials

**Activity 4.1:** predict, then test, which objects stick to a magnet.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **MAGNETIC materials** are **attracted** to a magnet — **iron, nickel and cobalt**, and some of their mixtures with other metals.
> - **NON-MAGNETIC materials** are **not** — **wood, rubber, plastic, glass, paper**, and many metals.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Metal" does NOT mean "magnetic".** **Copper, aluminium, gold, silver** are metals but **not** magnetic. Only iron, nickel, cobalt (and some mixtures) are.

## The poles of a magnet

**Activity 4.2:** sprinkle **iron filings** (tiny pieces of iron) on paper, put a bar magnet on top and tap the paper.

- **Most filings cluster near the two ENDS**; very few stick in the middle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The two ends where attraction is strongest are the POLES — the North pole and the South pole.** Most iron filings stick to the poles of a magnet **of any shape**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A single pole cannot exist.** Break a magnet into pieces — **every piece, however small, still has a North and a South pole.** Poles always come **in pairs**.

> [!TIP]
> **Rolling a bar magnet over a heap of steel U-clips:** the **two ends** pick up many clips; the **middle** picks up very few (e.g. **10 – 2 – 10**).

## Finding directions

**Activity 4.3:** hang a bar magnet from a thread tied at its **middle** so it hangs **level**. Give it a gentle spin and let it stop. Mark where its ends point. Spin it again — **it stops along the same line every time.**

Use where the **Sun rises (east)** and **sets (west)** to work out the directions: the magnet's line is **north–south**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A freely suspended magnet always comes to rest along the NORTH–SOUTH direction.**
> - The end pointing **north** = the **North-seeking pole (North pole)**.
> - The end pointing **south** = the **South-seeking pole (South pole)**.
>
> **Why:** **the Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet**, and a freely hanging magnet lines up with it.

> [!TIP]
> **Is this metal bar a magnet? Hang it freely.**
> - A **magnet** always settles **north–south**.
> - A plain **iron bar** can stop in **any** direction.
>
> This also tells you **which end is North** on an unmarked magnet — no second magnet needed.

### The magnetic compass

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A **MAGNETIC COMPASS** is a small round box with a **transparent cover**. Inside, a **needle-shaped magnet** balanced on a **pin** can **rotate freely** above a **dial** marked with directions. The **north-pointing end is usually painted red**.
>
> **How to use it:** put it where you want to know directions, wait for the needle to settle **north–south**, then **gently turn the box** until **N and S on the dial line up with the needle**. Now every direction on the dial is correct for that place.

### Make your own compass (Activity 4.4)

1. Lay an **iron sewing needle** on a wooden table.
2. Put **one pole** of a bar magnet at one end of the needle and **slide it along to the other end**. **Lift it up**, bring the **same pole** back to the **same starting end**, and repeat — **30 to 40 times**.
3. **Test:** bring iron filings or steel pins near the needle. If they're attracted, it's now a **magnet**.
4. Push it **horizontally through a cork** and **float** the cork in a bowl of water (needle above the water). When it stops, it points **north–south**. Turn it gently — it returns to the same direction.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why always the same pole, the same direction, lifting at the end?** Each stroke lines up the needle's tiny magnetic parts **the same way**. Rubbing back and forth, or switching poles, would **undo** the work of the previous stroke.
>
> **Why float it?** Floating lets the needle **turn freely** — just like a hanging magnet — so it can line up with the Earth's magnetism.

> [!NOTE]
> **Long before the modern compass**, Indians navigated at sea with the **matsya-yantra (machchh-yantra)** — a **magnetised fish-shaped iron piece floating in a vessel of oil**. The same idea as your floating needle!

> [!TIP]
> **A compass points to the Earth's magnetic poles.** The needle's **North pole** points towards geographic **north** — and since **unlike poles attract**, the Earth's magnet must have its **South pole** near the geographic North.

## Attraction and repulsion

**Activity 4.5:** lay magnet A on **5–6 round pencils** (so it can roll). Bring one end of magnet B near one end of A (without touching), then the **other** end of B.

- Sometimes A **rolls towards** B; sometimes it **rolls away**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **UNLIKE poles ATTRACT** (North–South).
> **LIKE poles REPEL** (North–North, South–South).
>
> | Poles facing | Result |
> |---|---|
> | N – N | **repulsion** |
> | N – S | **attraction** |
> | S – N | **attraction** |
> | S – S | **repulsion** |

**Now replace one magnet with an iron bar:** **both ends** of the iron bar are attracted by **both** poles of the magnet — it's never repelled.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **REPULSION is the sure test for a magnet.**
>
> **Why attraction isn't enough:** a magnet attracts plain iron too, whichever pole you use — so "it was attracted" can't tell you whether the other bar is a magnet. **Only a magnet can be pushed away**, and only by a like pole. **If two objects repel, both must be magnets.**

> [!TIP]
> **Reshma has three identical bars — two magnets, one plain iron.** Bring the bars together in pairs. The pair that **repels** (at some ends) must be **the two magnets**. The bar that only ever **attracts** is the iron.
>
> **Finding the poles of an unmarked magnet with a marked one:** bring the marked **North pole** near one end. If it **repels**, that end is **North**; if it attracts, it's **South**.

### A compass needle is a magnet too (Activity 4.6)

- Bring a magnet's **North pole** near the compass needle's **North end** → the needle **moves away** (repulsion).
- Bring the magnet's **South pole** near it → the needle **moves closer** (attraction).

> [!TIP]
> **Keep compasses away from magnets** — a nearby magnet's pull is stronger than the Earth's and will make the needle point the wrong way.

## Magnetism works through things

**Activity 4.7:** put a sheet of **wood, cardboard, plastic or glass** between a magnet and a compass.

- The needle's deflection **barely changes**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The magnetic effect can act through NON-magnetic materials.**
> That's why a magnet holds a **pencil box shut through its plastic**, and a **fridge magnet works through paper**.

## Fun with magnets

Reshma sets up stalls at the school fair:
- **Magnetic garland** — steel pins/clips hang in a chain from a magnet.
- **Maze** — steer **steel balls** through a cardboard maze by moving a magnet **underneath**.
- **Paper clip in water** — lift it out with a magnet held **outside the glass**, keeping your fingers dry (magnetism works through the glass and water).
- **Matchbox-magnet cars** with **like poles facing** → they **push each other away**.
- **Hopping frog** — ring magnets glued in **alternating N–S** order along a scale; a frog with a ring magnet below "hops" as it slides over them (pushed up by like poles, pulled down by unlike ones).

> [!TIP]
> **A mechanic keeps dropping steel screws.** Rub the screwdriver tip with a magnet (like the needle method) to **magnetise** it — the screws will cling to it.
>
> **Two ring magnets on a stick, the top one floating and won't come down?** **Like poles are facing** and repel. **Flip the top magnet over** — now unlike poles face each other and it drops onto the lower one.

> [!NOTE]
> **Marking poles:** some magnets say **N and S**; some mark North with a **white dot**; some paint **North red and South blue**.

## Taking care of magnets

> [!WARNING]
> *"Store me properly"*, says the magnet:
> - store magnets **in pairs with unlike poles side by side**, a **piece of wood between them**, and **soft iron pieces across the ends**
> - **don't heat, drop or hammer** them (they lose their magnetism)
> - keep them **away from mobile phones and remote controls**

## Think-it-through questions

1. **Fill in:** unlike poles **attract**; like poles **repel**; magnetic materials are **attracted** to a magnet; a compass needle rests **north–south**; a magnet always has **two** poles.
2. **True or false?**
   - *A magnet can be broken to get a single pole.* → **False.**
   - *Similar poles repel.* → **True.**
   - *Iron filings mostly stick to the middle of a bar magnet.* → **False** — to the ends.
   - *A freely suspended bar magnet always aligns north–south.* → **True.**
3. **Three bar magnets in a U-shape with one end marked N.** → Work from the known pole: wherever two magnets **touch end to end and stay together**, those ends must be **unlike** (N next to S). Each magnet's two ends are opposite.

## In a nutshell

- **Lodestones** are natural magnets; most magnets today are **artificial**, in many shapes.
- **Magnetic materials** (iron, nickel, cobalt) are attracted; **non-magnetic** ones aren't — not every metal is magnetic.
- A magnet has a **North and a South pole**; poles always come in **pairs**.
- A **freely suspended magnet** rests **north–south** because the **Earth is a giant magnet** — the basis of the **magnetic compass** (and the ancient **matsya-yantra**).
- **Like poles repel, unlike poles attract**; **repulsion** is the sure test for a magnet.
- Magnetism works **through** non-magnetic materials.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** metal ≠ magnetic · iron filings cling to the ends, not the middle · attraction can't prove a magnet — repulsion can · stroke a needle with one pole, one direction · never heat or drop magnets.
