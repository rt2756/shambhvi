# 1. Numbers

## Natural & whole numbers

- **Natural numbers (N)** = the **counting numbers**: 1, 2, 3, 4, … They start at **1** and go on forever (no largest).
- **Whole numbers (W)** = the natural numbers **plus 0**: 0, 1, 2, 3, … So **whole = natural + 0**, and the smallest whole number is **0**.
- Zero is the only difference: every natural number is also a whole number, but **0 is whole and not natural**.

## Integers

- **Integers (Z)** = the whole numbers **together with their negatives**: …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …
- **Positive integers:** 1, 2, 3, … (right of 0) · **negative integers:** −1, −2, −3, … (left of 0).
- **0 is neither positive nor negative** — it is the only integer that is neither.
- On the number line, further **right = bigger**

> [!TIP]
> −3 > −8 (it is nearer 0 on the right)

## Integer operations

### Add & subtract

#### Imagine a number line
- `+` moves you **right**, `−` moves you **left**. The answer is just wherever you land after the moves.
- 5 + 3. Start from 5. **+ means move right**. +3 means move right by 3 steps. So 3 steps right from 5 lands us at 8. So 5 + 3 = 8.
- 5 - 3. Start from 5. **- means move left**. -3 means move left by 3 steps. So 3 steps left from 5 lands us at 2. So 5 − 3 = 2.
- -8 + 3. Start from -8. **+ means move right**. +3 means move right by 3 steps. So 3 steps right from -8 lands us at -5. So -8 + 3 = -5.
- 3 - 5. Start from 3. **- means move left**. -5 means move left by 5 steps. So 5 steps left from 3 lands us at -2. So 3 -5 = -2.

#### The algorithm — just check the signs

**First, look at the signs.** That puts every sum into one of three cases:

**Case 1 — both positive** (`+` and `+`): no problem → **add** the two numbers and keep a **`+`**.
- `+3 + 5 = 8` · `+5 + 3 = 8`

**Case 2 — both negative** (`−` and `−`): no problem → **add** the two numbers and put a **`−`** in front.
- `−5 − 3 = −8` · `−3 − 5 = −8`

**Case 3 — a mix of `+` and `−`:** two steps.
1. **Put the positive number on the left**. Now it reads `A − B`.
   - `−8 + 3` → `3 − 8` 
   - `+5 − 8` stays `5 − 8`
   - `−5 + 8` → `8 − 5`
2. **Compare the two numbers:**
   - **Larger − Smaller** → easy, just subtract → `8 − 5 = 3`
   - **Smaller − Larger** → 
      - do **Larger − Smaller** first
      - then **put a `−` at the end** 
      - `5 − 8`: do `8 − 5 = 3`, then `−3`

#### Special case
`8 - (-2)`

**Bracket means multiplication**
So 8 - (-2) = 8 -1 X -2 = 8 + 2 = 10

`-8 - (-2)`

**Bracket means multiplication**
So -8 - (-2) = -8 -1 X -2 = -8 + 2 = -6

### Multiply & divide — the sign rule

Two easy steps, every single time:

1. **Size:** multiply or divide the numbers **as normal, ignoring the signs**.
2. **Sign:** **count the negative (minus) signs** → **even count → +** , **odd count → −**.

**Why it works:** negatives cancel in **pairs** — every two minuses combine into a plus (`(−) × (−) = +`). So pair the minuses off: if **none is left over** the answer is **positive (even)**; if **one is left over** it stays **negative (odd)**.

**The 2 building blocks** (everything else is just these, repeated):
- **Same signs → positive:** `(−4) × (−3) = 12` · `(−12) ÷ (−3) = 4`
- **Opposite signs → negative:** `(−4) × 3 = −12` · `12 ÷ (−3) = −4`

**Just count the minuses:**
- **1 minus** → odd → **−** : `(−7) × 2 = −14`
- **2 minuses** → even → **+** : `(−7) × (−2) = 14`
- **3 minuses** → odd → **−** : `(−2) × (−3) × (−5) = −30`
- **4 minuses** → even → **+** : `(−1) × (−2) × (−1) × (−5) = 10`
- **division, 2 minuses** → even → **+** : `(−20) ÷ (−4) = 5`
- **mix of × and ÷, 3 minuses** → odd → **−** : `(−8) × (−3) ÷ (−6) = −4`

> [!TIP]
> The **size** never depends on the signs — work the number out first, then pick + or − just by counting the minuses. (This counting trick is for **× and ÷ only**.)

> [!WARNING]
> Minus × minus = plus (`(−2) × (−3) = 6`), but in **adding / subtracting**, minus then minus just moves further left (`−2 − 3 = −5`).

### More rules
- `a × 0 = 0` · `0 ÷ a = 0` · **`a ÷ 0` is not defined**.
- **Distributive shortcut:** `a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c` → `(−6) × 102 = (−6) × 100 + (−6) × 2 = −612`.

## Even & odd numbers

- **Even** = exactly divisible by 2 — ends in **0, 2, 4, 6, 8** (2, 4, 6, 8, …).
- **Odd** = not divisible by 2 — ends in **1, 3, 5, 7, 9** (1, 3, 5, 7, …).
- Just check the **last digit** to decide. 
- **0 is even.**

## Factorisation & factors

- **Factorisation** = breaking a number down into **equal parts** (equal-sized components).
  - **32** can be broken into **2** equal parts of **16**, or **4** equal parts of **8**, or **16** equal parts of **2**. The parts must always be **equal to each other**.
- **Factor** = any number that can break a bigger number into **equal parts** — in other words, a number that **divides it exactly, leaving no remainder**.
  - **2**, **4** and **16** each break 32 into equal parts (16 parts, 8 parts, 2 parts), so **2, 4 and 16 are factors of 32**.

> [!NOTE]
> **0 and 1 can't be factorised — so they are neither prime nor composite.**
> - **0** is already zero — there is nothing to break into smaller equal parts.
> - **1** can't be broken into any smaller whole-number parts either.
>
> Because factorisation doesn't apply to them, **0 and 1 are neither prime nor composite**.

## Prime & composite numbers

Every number **except 0 and 1** has at least **two** factors — **1** and **the number itself** (1 is a factor of every number, and every number is a factor of itself).

- **Prime** = a number whose **only** factors are **1 and itself** — it won't break into equal parts any other way.
  - **3** only breaks into **3 equal parts of 1** (or stays whole as **1 part of 3** — itself); nothing else divides it. So its only factors are **1 and 3**.
  - Primes: **2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, …** 
  - **2 is the only even prime**.
- **Composite** = a number with **more than two** factors — it can be broken into equal parts in at least one extra way.
  - **4** breaks into **2 equal parts of 2** → **2** is a factor. **6** breaks into **3 equal parts of 2** and **2 equal parts of 3** → **2** and **3** are factors.
  - Composites: **4, 6, 8, 9, 10, …**

## Factors & multiples

### How to find all factors of a number

**Use the prime factorisation.** With a bigger number like **300**, a system really matters — follow these 4 steps and you get **every** factor, with none missed and none repeated.

- **Step 1 — Prime factorise.** Keep dividing by the **smallest prime that fits**, until you reach 1.
  - `300 ÷ 2 = 150` → `150 ÷ 2 = 75` → `75 ÷ 3 = 25` → `25 ÷ 5 = 5` → `5 ÷ 5 = 1`
  - *(2 won't divide 75, so move up to 3; then 3 won't divide 25, so move up to 5.)*
  - So **`300 = 2² × 3¹ × 5²`**
- **Step 2 — List the powers of each prime**, always starting from the power **⁰ (= 1)**:
  - 2 → `2⁰=1`, `2¹=2`, `2²=4` → **1, 2, 4**
  - 3 → `3⁰=1`, `3¹=3` → **1, 3**
  - 5 → `5⁰=1`, `5¹=5`, `5²=25` → **1, 5, 25**
- **Step 3 — Combine one prime at a time.** *This is the trick that stops you missing any.* Keep a **running list** and multiply the **whole list** by each power of the next prime.
  - Start with the powers of 2: **1, 2, 4**
  - Multiply the whole list by each power of **3**:
    - `×1` → 1, 2, 4  ·  `×3` → 3, 6, 12
    - running list: **1, 2, 4, 3, 6, 12**
  - Multiply the whole list by each power of **5**:
    - `×1` → 1, 2, 4, 3, 6, 12
    - `×5` → 5, 10, 20, 15, 30, 60
    - `×25` → 25, 50, 100, 75, 150, 300
  - Sort them → **factors of 300: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25, 30, 50, 60, 75, 100, 150, 300**
- **Step 4 — Count-check.** Add **1** to each power and multiply: `(2+1) × (1+1) × (2+1) = 3 × 2 × 3 = ` **18**. So there must be **18** factors — count your list, and there are. ✓
  - **Why it works:** building a factor is just a set of **independent choices** — *how many 2s do I take?* `2⁰`, `2¹` or `2²` → **3** options · *how many 3s?* `3⁰` or `3¹` → **2** options · *how many 5s?* `5⁰`, `5¹` or `5²` → **3** options. Every option for one prime can pair with every option for the others, and independent choices **multiply**, so the total is `3 × 2 × 3 = 18`.
  - That is why the rule is **(power + 1)**: the **+ 1** counts the `⁰` option — taking **none** of that prime. That "none" option is what lets a factor **skip** a prime (e.g. **3** takes zero 2s and zero 5s; **4 = 2²** takes zero 3s and zero 5s).
  - It is the *same* 18 the running list reaches in **Step 3**: folding in a prime with *n* powers makes the list *n* times longer — start with 3 (the powers of 2), `×2` for the 3s → 6, then `×3` for the 5s → **18**.

> [!TIP]
> **Second check — factor pairs.** Line the sorted factors up and pair them **from the outside in**; every pair should multiply to the number:
> `1×300, 2×150, 3×100, 4×75, 5×60, 6×50, 10×30, 12×25, 15×20` — **9 neat pairs, no gaps** → nothing missed.

- **Multiple** = a number's times-table. To find multiples of a number, multiply the number by 1, 2, 3, 4, …
    - Example: multiples of **4** → `4 × 1 = 4`, `4 × 2 = 8`, `4 × 3 = 12`, `4 × 4 = 16`, …

> [!TIP]
> **1 is a factor of every number**
> Every number is a factor and a multiple of **itself**.
> Every number is a multiple of each of its factors.

### How to find common factors & HCF (highest common factor)

- **Common factor** = a number that divides **both** numbers exactly.
- **HCF** (Highest Common Factor) = the **largest** common factor.
- Steps to get HCF:
  1. Write each number as a product of **prime powers**.
  2. For every prime that appears in **both** numbers, take the **smaller** power.
  3. Multiply those primes together — that is the HCF.
- Example: HCF of **18** and **60**
  - `18 = 2¹ × 3²`
  - `60 = 2² × 3¹ × 5¹`
  - Common primes: **2** and **3** (ignore **5** — it is only in 60)
  - Smaller powers: `2¹` (not `2²`) and `3¹` (not `3²`)
  - HCF = `2¹ × 3¹ = 2 × 3` = **6**

> [!NOTE]
> **Co-prime numbers** = two numbers whose **only** common factor is **1** — that is, their **HCF = 1**.
> - Example: **16** and **25**. `16 = 2⁴` and `25 = 5²` — they share **no prime factor at all**, so the only number that divides both is **1**. HCF(16, 25) = **1**, so 16 and 25 are **co-prime**.
> - **They needn't be prime themselves.** 16 and 25 are both *composite*, yet still co-prime — what matters is that they have **no prime in common**, not that either one is prime.
> - **Useful consequence:** for co-prime numbers the **LCM is simply their product** (nothing overlaps to trim away) — e.g. `LCM(16, 25) = 16 × 25 = 400`, and `LCM(5, 7) = 5 × 7 = 35`.

### How to find common multiples & LCM (lowest common multiple)

- **Common multiple** = a number that appears in **both** times-tables.
- **LCM** (Lowest Common Multiple) = the **smallest** common multiple (used to add or compare unlike fractions).
- Steps to get LCM:
  1. Write each number as a product of **prime powers**.
  2. For every prime that appears in **either** number, take the **larger** power.
  3. Multiply those primes together — that is the LCM.
- Example: LCM of **18** and **60**
  - `18 = 2¹ × 3²`
  - `60 = 2² × 3¹ × 5¹`
  - All primes: **2**, **3**, **5**
  - Larger powers: `2²`, `3²`, `5¹`
  - LCM = `2² × 3² × 5¹ = 4 × 9 × 5` = **180**

> [!TIP]
> **HCF** = Highest Common **Factor** (the small answer).
> **LCM** = Lowest Common **Multiple** (the big answer).
> **HCF × LCM = product of the two numbers** (e.g. `6 × 180 = 18 × 60 = 1080`).
> If you already know one, you can find the other: **LCM = (a × b) ÷ HCF** or **HCF = (a × b) ÷ LCM**.

### Divisibility shortcuts

- **2:** last digit is **0, 2, 4, 6, or 8** (e.g. 48, 130)
- **3:** **sum of digits** is divisible by 3 (e.g. 123 → `1 + 2 + 3 = 6`)
- **4:** **last two digits** form a number divisible by 4 (e.g. 1324 → `24 ÷ 4 = 6`)
- **5:** last digit is **0 or 5** (e.g. 35, 120)
- **6:** divisible by **both 2 and 3** (e.g. 126 — even, and `1 + 2 + 6 = 9`)
- **8:** **last three digits** form a number divisible by 8 (e.g. 5024 → `24 ÷ 8 = 3` ✓; 5026 → `26 ÷ 8` leaves a remainder ✗)
- **9:** **sum of digits** is divisible by 9 (e.g. 729 → `7 + 2 + 9 = 18`)
- **11:** count from the **right** — sum digits in **odd** places, sum digits in **even** places; if their **difference is 0 or divisible by 11**, the number is divisible by 11.
  - **11** → odd: 1, even: 1 → `1 − 1 = 0` ✓
  - **121** → odd: 1 + 1 = 2, even: 2 → `2 − 2 = 0` ✓
  - **1331** → odd: 1 + 3 = 4, even: 3 + 1 = 4 → `4 − 4 = 0` ✓
  - **1463** → odd: 3 + 4 = 7, even: 6 + 1 = 7 → `7 − 7 = 0` ✓
  - **1234** → odd: 4 + 2 = 6, even: 3 + 1 = 4 → `6 − 4 = 2` (not 0 or divisible by 11) ✗
