# 10. Adverbs

*Shows up in: class 5, 6, 7.*

- *She sings.* → *She sings **sweetly**.* → *She sings **very** sweetly.*

An **adverb** adds to (*ad-*) a **verb** — and also to an **adjective** or **another adverb**.
It answers **how? when? where? how often? how much?**

| Kind | Answers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Manner** | how? | slowly, carefully, well, fast, bravely |
| **Time** | when? | now, yesterday, soon, already, later |
| **Place** | where? | here, there, outside, everywhere, upstairs |
| **Frequency** | how often? | always, usually, often, sometimes, never |
| **Degree** | how much? | very, too, quite, almost, extremely, enough |

- Modifies a verb: *He ran **quickly**.* · an adjective: *She is **very** tall.* · another adverb: *He ran **too** quickly.*

## Adjective or adverb? Ask what it describes

- *She is a **careful** driver.* — describes the driver (noun) → adjective.
- *She drives **carefully**.* — describes how she drives (verb) → adverb.

**Why the -ly:** most manner adverbs are built from adjectives by adding *-ly* — *careful → carefully*.
So *-ly* is a flag saying "I'm describing an action, not a thing".

> [!WARNING]
> Not every -ly word is an adverb: *friendly, lovely, lonely, ugly, silly* are **adjectives** (*a friendly dog*).
> Not every adverb has -ly: *fast, hard, late, well, soon*.

**good vs well:** *good* is the adjective, *well* is the adverb. *She is a **good** singer. She sings **well**.*
(Exception: *I feel **well*** = I am healthy — there *well* is an adjective.)

**hard / hardly, late / lately:** different meanings!
- *He works **hard*** (with effort) · *He **hardly** works* (almost not at all).
- *She came **late*** (not on time) · *I haven't seen her **lately*** (recently).

## Degrees of comparison

- Short adverbs: *fast – faster – fastest, hard – harder – hardest, soon – sooner – soonest.*
- -ly adverbs: ***more** slowly – **most** slowly.*
- Irregular: *well – better – best, badly – worse – worst, much – more – most, little – less – least.*

## Where adverbs go

- **Frequency** adverbs go **before** the main verb but **after** *am/is/are/was/were*:
  *She **always** walks.* · *She is **always** late.*
- With a helping verb, after the first helper: *I have **never** seen it.*
- Manner–place–time order at the end: *He sang **beautifully** (how) **at the concert** (where) **last night** (when).*

**Why "always" sits after *is***: *be* is a linking verb, so the adverb attaches to the description that follows it.

## Three traps

- **only** limits the word right after it: ***Only** Ravi ate the cake* (no one else) · *Ravi ate **only** the cake* (nothing else).
- **hardly, scarcely, seldom, never** are already negative — don't add *not/never*: *He **hardly ever** goes out* (not *hardly never*).
- When one of them **starts** the sentence, the helper jumps before the subject, like a question: ***Seldom have I** seen such a sight.* **Why:** moving the negative word to the front is for emphasis, and English marks that by flipping the order.
- After a **linking verb** (look, smell, taste, feel, sound) use the **adjective**: *The flowers smell **sweet*** — the sweetness belongs to the flowers, not to any action.

## too vs very, enough

- **very** = to a high degree: *The tea is **very** hot* (but you can drink it carefully).
- **too** = more than is good — something can't happen: *The tea is **too** hot **to drink**.*
- **enough** comes **after** an adjective/adverb: *He is tall **enough** to reach.* (not *enough tall*)
