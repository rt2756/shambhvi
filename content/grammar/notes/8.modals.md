# 8. Modals

*Shows up in: class 6, 7, 8, 9; a fixed class 9–10 exam item.*

**Modals** are helping verbs that add a **mood** to the main verb — ability, permission,
possibility, duty, advice. *I swim* (fact) → *I **can** swim* (ability) → *I **must** swim* (duty).

**can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, ought to** (+ *need, dare, used to* as semi-modals)

## Three grammar rules for every modal

1. **Always followed by V1** (base form): *She can **sing*** — never *can sings / can to sing* (except *ought **to***).
2. **No -s** with he/she/it: *He **can** swim* (not *cans*).
3. **Questions and negatives without *do***: ***Can** you swim? I **cannot** swim.*

**Why:** modals already carry the "helping" job (tense/mood), so they don't need *do*, and the main verb
stays plain — the same way *does* forces *goes* back to *go*.

## What each one means

| Modal | Main meanings | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **can** | ability now; informal permission | *She **can** speak French. **Can** I go out?* |
| **could** | past ability; polite request; possibility | *I **could** swim at five. **Could** you help me?* |
| **may** | formal permission; possibility (50%); wish | ***May** I come in? It **may** rain. **May** you live long!* |
| **might** | weaker possibility | *He **might** come, but I doubt it.* |
| **shall** | future with I/we; offers & suggestions | ***Shall** I open the window? **Shall** we go?* |
| **will** | future; willingness; promise | *I **will** help you.* |
| **would** | polite request; past habit; imagined result | ***Would** you pass the salt? As a child he **would** read for hours.* |
| **should** | advice; what is right | *You **should** see a doctor.* |
| **ought to** | moral duty (stronger than should) | *We **ought to** respect our elders.* |
| **must** | strong necessity/order; certainty (logical) | *You **must** wear a helmet. He has run 10 km — he **must** be tired.* |
| **need not / needn't** | no necessity | *You **needn't** come; I can manage.* |
| **used to** | past habit that no longer happens | *I **used to** play cricket.* |

**Why polite = past form:** *could, would, might* are "softer" — stepping one step back in time puts
distance between you and the request, so *Could you…?* sounds gentler than *Can you…?*

## The tricky pairs

- **must not** ≠ **need not**. *You **mustn't** shout* = it's forbidden. *You **needn't** shout* = it isn't necessary (I can hear you).
- **must** has no past form → use **had to**: *Yesterday I **had to** walk home.*
- **can** → **be able to** for other tenses: *I **will be able to** come.*
- **may** (possible) vs **might** (less likely).

## Modal + have + V3 = looking back at the past

- *He **must have** left* — I'm almost sure he left.
- *She **might / may have** forgotten* — possibly she forgot.
- *You **should have** told me* — you didn't, and that was a mistake.
- *He **can't have** done it* — I'm sure he didn't.
- *I **could have** won* — it was possible, but it didn't happen.
- *You **needn't have** bought milk* — you did buy it, but it wasn't necessary.

> [!WARNING]
> **used to + V1** (past habit: *I used to swim*) ≠ **be used to + -ing** (be accustomed to: *I am used to swimming in cold water*).
> In the second one *to* is a preposition, and prepositions are followed by *-ing*.
> *Would you mind + -ing* is the most polite request of all: *Would you mind **closing** the door?*

**Why *have + V3*:** modals have no past tense of their own, so English borrows the perfect (*have + V3*)
to push the guess or advice back into the past.
