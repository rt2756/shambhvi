## Changes are everywhere

Four students notice four changes in one day:

- an **ice cube** has become water
- a rose **bud** has opened into a flower
- a bottle of **cold water** isn't cold any more
- a banana's **brown spots** have spread, with a strong smell

Melting ice, chopping vegetables, boiling water, **popcorn** from corn, cutting paper, beetroot colouring water, **burning wood**, drying clothes, rolling dough into chapatis — something changes in every one. The change may be in **size, shape, colour, smell or another property**, and we notice it with our **senses** — sight, smell, touch, hearing, taste.

The question this chapter answers: **can we sort all these changes into groups — and what's the one test that sorts them?**

## The one question that separates them

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Is a NEW SUBSTANCE formed?**
>
> - **No → PHYSICAL change.** Only **physical properties** like **shape, size or state** change. The substance is still the same stuff.
> - **Yes → CHEMICAL change.** **One or more new substances** are formed, by a **chemical reaction**, which can be written as a **chemical equation**.

### Physical changes — same stuff, new look

**Activity 5.2** tries three things:

- **Fold paper** into a boat or a fan → unfold it → it's the **same paper**.
- **Inflate a balloon** and let the air out → the **same uninflated balloon**. Or prick it with a pin → the rubber is torn, but it's **still rubber**.
- **Crush chalk** into powder → every grain is **still chalk**.

Water changing between **ice, liquid and vapour** (Class 6) is the same kind of change — the **state** changes, the substance doesn't.

### Chemical changes — something new appears

**Activity 5.3 — the lime water test:**

| Blow through a straw into… | What you see | Change |
|---|---|---|
| **Tap water** (tumbler A) | bubbles only, water stays clear | **physical** |
| **Lime water** (tumbler B) | bubbles, liquid turns **milky**; a **white solid settles** at the bottom | **chemical** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why lime water turns milky:** the **carbon dioxide** in the air you breathe out **reacts** with lime water and forms **calcium carbonate** — a new **white** substance that **does not dissolve** in water, so the liquid looks milky.
>
> **Calcium hydroxide (lime water) + Carbon dioxide → Calcium carbonate (insoluble) + Water**
>
> **"Lime water turns milky" is the standard TEST for carbon dioxide.**

> [!WARNING]
> **Blow, never suck,** through the straw.

**Activity 5.4 — kitchen chemistry:** a pinch of **baking soda** (sodium hydrogen carbonate) into **vinegar or lemon juice** → **fizzing, bubbling**. Lead the gas into lime water → it turns **milky** → the gas is **carbon dioxide**.

**Vinegar + Baking soda → Carbon dioxide + Other substances** — a new substance, so a **chemical change**.

> [!TIP]
> **Baking soda in plain water** gives no bubbles — it just dissolves. No new substance → that one is **physical**.

## More chemical changes you already know

- **Rusting** (Chapter 4): a new brown substance, **iron oxide (rust)**, forms → **chemical**.
- **Burning magnesium** (Chapter 4): white **magnesium oxide** forms, with **heat and light** → **chemical**.
  **Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium oxide + Heat + Light**
- **Curdling of milk, cooking food, ripening fruit, compost forming, burning wood** → all **chemical** (new substances with new taste, smell or colour).

## Combustion & the fire triangle

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **COMBUSTION** = a chemical reaction in which a substance **reacts with oxygen** and gives out **heat and/or light**.
> Substances that do this are **combustible**: **wood, paper, cotton, kerosene**…

### Is oxygen needed? (Activity 5.5)

Two identical lit candles; cover **one** with a glass tumbler.
- The **uncovered** candle keeps burning.
- The **covered** candle **goes out** after a while.

**Why:** under the tumbler there's **no fresh supply of air** — the part of air that supports burning, **oxygen**, gets used up. Put a little **lime water** in the dish and it turns **milky** → **carbon dioxide** was formed from the **carbon in the wax** and the **oxygen in the air**. So **oxygen is required** for combustion.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **If someone's clothes catch fire: wrap them in a blanket or thick cloth.** It cuts off the **air**, so the fire goes out.
>
> ⚠️ **Never use a synthetic blanket** — it can **melt and stick to the skin**.

### Is fuel + air enough? (Activity 5.6)

Paper can lie in air **forever** without catching fire. So something else is needed.

- A **lit matchstick** touched to paper → catches fire **at once**.
- **A magnifying glass** focusing **sunlight** on paper → it **smokes, then catches fire** — **with no flame at all!**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the magnifying glass works:** the focused sunlight keeps **heating** that tiny spot. Its temperature rises until it's hot enough to burn.
>
> **IGNITION TEMPERATURE** = the **lowest temperature at which a substance catches fire.**
>
> The match lit the paper instantly because the flame is **already hotter** than paper's ignition temperature.

### The fire triangle

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Three things are needed for combustion — remove ANY ONE and the fire stops:**
> 1. **Fuel** — a combustible substance
> 2. **Oxygen**
> 3. **Heat** — enough to bring the fuel to its **ignition temperature**

| Way to put out a fire | Which side of the triangle it removes |
|---|---|
| blanket on burning clothes, tumbler over candle | **oxygen** |
| water on burning wood | mainly **heat** (cools it below ignition temperature) |
| clearing trees ahead of a forest fire | **fuel** |

> [!NOTE]
> **Fireflies** glow in the evening because of a **chemical change** — but **without heat**. Light made by living things like this is called **bioluminescence**. So a chemical change doesn't always mean fire.

## A burning candle — both kinds at once

Students argue about a burning candle (Activity 5.7). Who's right? **All of them.**

- The wax **melts** → **physical**.
- Liquid wax **flows** down and **solidifies** in new shapes → **physical**.
- Melted wax is **drawn up the wick** and **evaporates** in the heat → **physical**.
- The **wax vapour burns** to make the flame → **chemical** (new substances: carbon dioxide and water).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **One process can contain BOTH physical and chemical changes.** The candle is the classic example.

> [!NOTE]
> **Michael Faraday** gave a famous series of lectures in the nineteenth century called ***The Chemical History of a Candle***. He thought the candle was the perfect object to start studying science, because **melting, vaporisation and combustion** all happen in one small flame.

## Three different ways to sort changes

A change can be sorted by more than one question. **Keep the questions separate.**

### 1. New substance? → physical or chemical (above)

### 2. Can it be reversed?

| Change | Get the original back? |
|---|---|
| melting ice | **yes** — refreeze it |
| boiling / evaporating water | **yes** — condense it |
| folding paper, rolling a mat, inflating a balloon | **yes** |
| chopping vegetables | **no** |
| making popcorn | **no** |
| crushing chalk | **no** (it's still chalk, but you can't remake the stick) |
| burning wood, cooking, curd | **no** |

> [!WARNING]
> **Don't use "can I undo it?" to decide physical vs chemical.**
>
> - **Chopping vegetables** and **crushing chalk** can't be undone — but they're **physical** (no new substance).
> - Most **chemical** changes can't be undone, but that's not what *makes* them chemical — the **new substance** is.

### 3. Is it wanted?

- **Desirable:** milk → curd, ripening fruit, cutting fruit, cooking food.
- **Undesirable:** rusting of iron, **food decaying** in storage.
- **The same change can be both**, depending on the situation: food decaying in the cupboard is unwanted; food waste decaying into **compost** is useful.

> [!NOTE]
> **Changes with a long-term cost:** burning more fuel in cars, trains and aeroplanes is **adding carbon dioxide** to the air. **Paint drying** on walls and furniture releases substances by evaporation, **polluting the air**.

## Slow natural changes

### Weathering — rocks breaking down in place

Heaps of **sand, soil and stones** at the foot of mountains are **sediments**. How do they form?

- **Physical weathering** — rock breaks into smaller pieces because of:
  - **temperature changes** (heating and cooling)
  - **growing tree roots** pushing into cracks
  - **water freezing** in cracks (ice takes up more room and splits the rock)
- **Chemical weathering** — water or chemicals in it **react** with the rock. **Black basalt** contains **iron**; long exposure to water and moist air turns its surface into **red iron oxide** — a new substance.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **WEATHERING** = the physical and chemical changes that break down rocks. **It eventually forms soil.**

### Erosion — carrying the pieces away

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **EROSION** = rock pieces, soil and sediment being **moved from one place to another** by **wind and flowing water**. It's a **physical** change — a **landslide** is an example.

- **Why river pebbles are smooth:** flowing water keeps rolling and rubbing them, wearing off the sharp edges.
- **Where it ends:** when water or wind **slows down** (in a lake or the ocean), the carried material **settles** at the bottom. Over time it **hardens into new rock**.
- These changes take **thousands of years** and **cannot be reversed**.

> [!TIP]
> **Weathering breaks it; erosion carries it.**

## Think-it-through questions

1. **Water → ice and water → steam: physical or chemical?** → **Physical.** Only the **state** changes; it's still water, and both can be reversed.
2. **Is curdling of milk physical or chemical?** → **Chemical.** Curd is a **new substance** — different taste, texture and smell — and it can't be turned back into milk.
3. **Wind and rain help turn rocks into soil. Physical or chemical?** → **Both.** Breaking and wearing down is physical; reactions like iron turning to red iron oxide are chemical.
4. **True or false: "Collecting water vapour by condensing is a chemical change."** → **False** — vapour → liquid is a change of **state**, so **physical**.
5. **True or false: "Melting of wax is necessary for burning a candle."** → **True** — the wax must melt and rise up the wick before its vapour can burn.
6. **Which mixtures turn lime water milky: vinegar + baking soda, lemon juice + vinegar, vinegar + common salt, lemon juice + baking soda?** → Only the two with **baking soda**. An **acid + baking soda** makes **carbon dioxide**; the others make no gas.
7. **Prithvi chops vegetables (?), puts peels in a pot (?), they rot into compost (?), seeds sprout and grow (?).** → physical, physical, **chemical**, **chemical**.
8. **Yeast + sugar water in a bottle with a balloon on top.** → The balloon **inflates**; its gas turns lime water **milky**, so it's **carbon dioxide** made by the yeast — a **chemical** change. (That's how yeast makes bread fluffy.)
9. **Invisible ink in lemon juice, then warmed with an iron.** → The letters turn **dark brown** — heating changes the lemon juice chemically. **Can't be reversed.**

## In a nutshell

- **Physical change:** only physical properties (shape, size, state) change; **no new substance**.
- **Chemical change:** **new substance(s)** form by a chemical reaction; written as a chemical equation.
- **Lime water turning milky** is the test for **carbon dioxide**.
- **Combustion** needs **fuel + oxygen + heat (ignition temperature)** — the **fire triangle**.
- A **burning candle** has both physical and chemical changes.
- Changes can be **reversible or not**, and **desirable or not** — separate questions.
- **Weathering** (physical + chemical) breaks rocks into soil; **erosion** (physical) moves them.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** "can't undo it" ≠ chemical · condensing is physical · fire can start without a flame (magnifying glass) · synthetic blankets melt · chemical change isn't always fire (fireflies).
