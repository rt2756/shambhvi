## Why start with a torch?

Nihal's class is visiting the **Bhakra Nangal Dam**, where **falling water** spins machines to make electricity (a **hydroelectric** power house). Before the trip they list every use of electricity they can find — cooking, lighting, TV, trains, lifts, phones, fans, fridges, water pumps… the list never ends.

Electricity can be made by **wind (windmills), sunlight (solar panels), falling water, natural gas or coal** (Class 6, *Nature's Treasures*). It reaches our homes through wires to the wall sockets.

But wall electricity is **dangerous** to experiment with. So the book studies electricity using the **safest, smallest source we have: the cells in a torch.**

> [!WARNING]
> **Never experiment with mains electricity** — wall sockets, poles, even portable generators. The danger signs on poles mean it. Use only **cells/batteries** like those in torches, clocks, radios and remotes.

## Inside a torch

Slide a torch's switch one way — the lamp glows; slide it back — it goes dark. Open it up and you find **two or more electric cells**. Three parts make it work: **cells, a lamp, and a switch.** Let's take each one.

### The electric cell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An electric cell is a PORTABLE source of electrical energy.** It has **two terminals**:
> - the small **protruding metal cap = positive (+)**
> - the **flat metal disc = negative (−)**
>
> The **+** and **−** signs are usually printed on it too.

### The battery — cells joined the right way

**Activity 3.3:** take the two cells out of a torch, put them back in different orders, even flip one. The lamp glows only one way.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A battery = two or more cells joined so that the + terminal of one touches the − terminal of the next.**
>
> **Why that order matters:** each cell "pushes" the current in one direction. Joined + to −, both cells push the **same way**, so their pushes add up — like two people pushing a cart from the same side. Flip one cell and it pushes **against** the other — the pushes cancel and the lamp stays dark.

- **More cells → energy for a longer time and/or more energy.** That's why big torches use more cells.
- In devices where cells sit **side by side**, look inside: a **thick wire or metal strip** connects the **+ of one to the − of the next**. The **+/−** marks printed in the compartment show you how to place them.
- **Everyday language** calls even a *single* cell a "battery" — like the one in a mobile phone.

### Two kinds of lamp

**1. The incandescent lamp (an old-style bulb)**

- Inside the glass is a **very thin wire — the filament**. When current passes through it, it gets **very hot and glows**.
- The filament is held up by **two thicker wires**. One joins the **metal case** at the base; the other joins the **metal tip** at the centre of the base. **Case and tip are the lamp's two terminals.** An **insulator** keeps them from touching.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why the two terminals must never touch:** if they did, current would take the shortcut from one to the other and **skip the filament** — so the filament wouldn't glow.

- **A "fused" lamp** won't glow even in a good circuit — usually because its **filament is broken**. A broken filament is a **gap**, and current can't cross a gap.
- **It glows either way round.** It doesn't matter which terminal goes to + — current heats the filament whichever way it flows.

**2. The LED (Light Emitting Diode)**

- Most torches today use **LEDs**. An LED has **no filament**.
- It has two wires of **different lengths**: **longer wire = positive (+)**, **shorter wire = negative (−)**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An LED lets current flow in ONE direction only.**
>
> It glows only when **its longer (+) wire goes to the + of the battery** and its shorter (−) wire to the −. Connected the other way, it stays dark — no current flows at all.

> [!TIP]
> **Incandescent lamp vs LED in one line:** *the bulb doesn't care which way round; the LED does.*

## The electrical circuit

**Activity 3.6:** connect a cell and a lamp in six different ways (the book's Table 3.1). Only **two** arrangements make the lamp glow. What do they have in common?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The lamp glows only when ONE terminal of the lamp is joined to ONE terminal of the cell, and the OTHER terminal of the lamp to the OTHER terminal of the cell.**
>
> That makes a **complete, unbroken path** from the cell, through the lamp, and back to the cell. This is an **ELECTRICAL CIRCUIT**. The lamp glows **only while current flows round it**.

- **Why a complete loop is needed:** current must be able to go **all the way round and back to the cell**. A gap *anywhere* stops it *everywhere* — like one broken bridge closing a whole ring road.
- **Why both wires to the same terminal doesn't work:** then both ends of the lamp sit at the same point of the cell — there's no path *through* the cell, so no current flows through the lamp.
- **Direction of current:** taken as **from the + terminal, round the circuit, to the − terminal** of the cell.

**Practical tips from the activity:**
- Strip about **1 cm of plastic** off each end of a wire so the bare metal touches.
- In a cell holder, put the **− terminal towards the spring**.
- No holder? Fix wires to the cell with **electrical tape**.
- In a two-cell holder, the end joined to the **+ of one cell** is the battery's **+**; the end joined to the **− of the other cell** is its **−**.

## The switch

**Activity 3.8 — build your own:** two **drawing pins** on a piece of **cardboard**, a **safety pin** (or paper clip) that can swing on one of them, and a wire from each drawing pin.

| Safety pin | Gap? | Circuit | Lamp |
|---|---|---|---|
| touching **both** drawing pins | no gap | **closed** — **ON** | glows |
| touching **one** only | **air gap** | **open** — **OFF** | dark |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A switch is a simple device that completes (ON) or breaks (OFF) a circuit.**
>
> - It is **not** a source of current — the **cell** is.
> - **It can be placed anywhere in the circuit** — breaking the loop at any one point stops the current everywhere.
> - Home switches work the same way, just better designed.

## Circuit diagrams — drawing circuits with symbols

Drawing every wire, torch and bulb realistically is slow and confusing. So we use **standard symbols**.

| Component | How its symbol works |
|---|---|
| **Electric cell** | two parallel lines: **long line = +**, **short line = −** |
| **Battery** | several cell symbols in a row |
| **Electric lamp** | a circle with a cross/filament inside |
| **LED** | a **triangle pointing the way current can flow**, with a bar, plus **two small arrows** meaning "light comes out" |
| **Switch ON** | a closed line (no gap) |
| **Switch OFF** | a line lifted at one end (a gap) |
| **Wire** | a straight line |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A representation of an electrical circuit using symbols is called a CIRCUIT DIAGRAM.**
>
> **Why the whole world uses the same symbols:** organisations like the **IEC** (International Electrotechnical Commission), **ANSI** and **IEEE** set them, so an engineer anywhere can read a circuit drawn by anyone else.

**Reading an LED in a diagram:** current leaves the **long line (+)** of the cell. If the LED's triangle **points along that direction**, it's connected the right way and will glow.

## Conductors and insulators

**Why are wires made of metal and wrapped in plastic?** Build a **conduction tester** and find out.

**Activity 3.11:** a cell and a lamp, with **two wire ends left free**. Touch the free ends together → the lamp glows, so the tester works. Now touch both free ends to each object in turn (keeping the wires apart).

| Material | Lamp glows? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Metal key, spoon, coin, pin, needle, aluminium foil | **yes** | **conductor** |
| Pencil lead (graphite) | **yes** | **conductor** |
| Wood, plastic scale, glass bangle, paper, wax candle, rubber eraser, cork, cardboard | no | **insulator** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Conductors** (good conductors) — materials through which **current flows easily** — **all metals** (and graphite).
> **Insulators** (poor conductors) — materials through which **current cannot pass** — plastic, rubber, glass, wood, ceramics, paper.
>
> **Why the lamp is a good judge:** if the object completes the gap, the circuit is closed and the lamp lights. If it blocks current, the gap stays open.

- **Best conductors:** **silver, copper and gold**. Wires are mostly **copper** — nearly as good as silver, much **cheaper and plentiful**.
- **Why wires are metal *inside* and plastic *outside*:** the copper **carries** the current; the plastic or rubber covering **stops it reaching your hand**. Each material does the job it's best at.
- **Plugs, switches and sockets** are the same: **metal** parts to carry current, **plastic/rubber/ceramic** covers to protect people.

> [!WARNING]
> **Our body is a conductor.** Current through the body can cause severe injury or death. So:
> - never touch switches or plugs with **wet hands**
> - don't use electrical devices in **wet areas**
> - never use wires with **damaged insulation** or **broken plugs**.

## AC and DC

> [!NOTE]
> **Cells and batteries give DIRECT CURRENT (DC)** — it flows one way. They run **small** devices.
> **Power plants send ALTERNATING CURRENT (AC)** to wall sockets — it can run **larger appliances**. (Its details come in higher classes.)

> [!NOTE]
> **Cells come in many shapes:** **cylindrical** (torches, clocks, remotes, toys), **button cells** (watches, hearing aids), and **rechargeable** batteries (phones, laptops, electric vehicles). A **solar panel** can also be a source of electricity — for example to run a toy fan.

## Troubleshooting — why won't my lamp glow?

A circuit that should work but doesn't almost always has **a gap or a wrong way round**. Check in this order:

1. **Cells** — the right way round? not used up (dead)?
2. **Connections** — all tight? the **plastic stripped** off the wire ends? (A wire still covered in plastic touches with **insulator**, so no current — the lamp won't glow.)
3. **Switch** — really in the ON position?
4. **LED** — long wire to **+**?
5. **Incandescent lamp** — filament **broken (fused)**?

> [!TIP]
> **To test which of six cells A–F work:** build one circuit with a lamp (or LED) and swap the cells in **one at a time**, keeping everything else the same. The ones that light the lamp are working. Changing only the cell is what makes the test fair.

## Think-it-through questions

1. **Which statement is wrong: "a switch is the source of current"?** → **Wrong.** The **cell** is the source; the switch only opens or closes the path.
2. **Two lamps in one loop; one filament breaks. Does the other glow?** → **No.** The broken filament is a gap in the only path, so current stops everywhere.
3. **The + and − marks on a battery have rubbed off. How do you find them?** → Connect an **LED**: when it glows, the wire on its **longer leg** is touching the battery's **+**. (An incandescent lamp can't help — it glows either way.)
4. **An LED needs two cells. Tanya connects them + to +. Will it glow?** → **No** — the cells push against each other. Rejoin them **+ to −**, and put the LED's long leg on the battery's +.
5. **Circuit with a lamp and switch S1 next to the cell, lamp L2 also controlled by S2.** → Any lamp glows only if **every switch on its path** is ON. Trace each lamp's loop back to the cell and check each switch on it.

## In a nutshell

- A **cell** is a portable source of electrical energy with **+ (cap)** and **− (disc)** terminals. A **battery** is cells joined **+ to −**.
- An **incandescent lamp's filament** gets hot and glows; it works **either way round**. A broken filament = **fused** lamp.
- An **LED** has **long leg +, short leg −** and lets current through **one way only**.
- A **circuit** is a **complete path**; current flows **from + to −** outside the cell.
- A **switch** opens/closes the circuit and can be **anywhere** in it.
- A **circuit diagram** uses standard symbols.
- **Conductors** (metals, graphite) let current pass; **insulators** (plastic, rubber, glass, wood) don't.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** the switch is not the source · LED direction matters, bulb direction doesn't · a covered wire end = no contact · cells must go + to − · graphite is a non-metal that conducts.
