## Why any clock works: something that repeats

Early humans noticed **events in nature repeat after definite intervals** — sunrise and sunset, phases of the Moon, the seasons — and built timekeeping on those cycles. A **day** was defined by one cycle of the Sun rising and setting.

**Every clock, ancient or modern, is built on some process that repeats continuously**, marking equal intervals.

| Device | What repeats / flows |
|---|---|
| **Sundial** | position of a shadow cast by the Sun |
| **Water clock** | water flowing out of a marked vessel — or a bowl with a hole that fills and sinks |
| **Hourglass** | sand flowing from one bulb to another |
| **Candle clock** | a marked candle burning down |
| **Pendulum clock** | swings of a pendulum |
| **Quartz / atomic clock** | very rapid vibrations of a quartz crystal or of atoms |

- **Accuracy has improved enormously:** Huygens' early pendulum clocks gained or lost about **10 seconds a day**; modern **atomic clocks lose one second in millions of years**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Indian timekeeping:** shadow-based time appears in Kautilya's **Arthasastra**; **Varahamihira** (~530 CE) gave an accurate expression for time from a vertical stick's shadow. The outflow water clock was described in the Arthasastra, but grew inaccurate as the water level dropped and the flow slowed — which led to the sinking-bowl **Ghatika-yantra**, first mentioned by **Aryabhata**. Its bowl took **24 minutes** to fill and sink; that unit was the **ghati**, and a day was **60 ghatis**. Each sinking was announced by drums, conch shells or a gong.

> [!NOTE]
> The **Samrat Yantra** at Jantar Mantar, Jaipur — built ~300 years ago, **27 m** tall — is the world's largest stone sundial. Its shadow moves about **1 mm per second** and its scale reads intervals as short as **2 seconds**.

## The simple pendulum

A **bob** (small heavy ball) hung from a rigid support by a long thread.

- **Mean position:** where it hangs at rest. **Extreme positions:** the two ends of its swing.
- **One oscillation** = mean → extreme A → extreme B → back to mean. (Equally: extreme A → extreme B → back to A.)
- **Time period** = the time taken for **one oscillation**. Its motion is **periodic** — it repeats after a fixed interval.

**The two results that matter:**

- The time period **depends on the LENGTH** of the pendulum.
- The time period does **NOT depend on the MASS** of the bob.
- So all pendulums of the same length at the same place have the **same time period** — and that constancy is exactly what makes a pendulum usable as a clock.

> [!TIP]
> **Measure 10 oscillations and divide by 10**, rather than timing one. Your reaction-time error gets divided by 10 too, so the answer is far more accurate.

> [!NOTE]
> **Galileo** noticed a swinging lamp in a church and timed it **with his own pulse**, finding each swing took the same time. **Christiaan Huygens** turned that into the **pendulum clock** — invented 1656, patented 1657.

## SI unit of time

- **The SI unit of time is the second, symbol `s`.** Larger units: **minute (min)** and **hour (h)**.
- `60 s = 1 min` · `60 min = 1 h`

> [!WARNING]
> **Writing units correctly:** lowercase (`s`, `min`, `h`), **singular**, **no full stop**, and **a space between the number and the unit** — `4 m/s`, not `4m/s.`
> **`sec` and `hrs` are incorrect** — write `s` and `h`.

## Speed

**Who is faster?** Whoever covers **more distance in the same time**. To compare races over *different* distances, we need speed.

> **Speed = total distance covered ÷ total time taken**

- **SI unit: metre/second (m/s)**; also **kilometre/hour (km/h)**.
- Rearranged: **distance = speed × time** · **time = distance ÷ speed**.

**Example:** school is 3.6 km away, reached in 15 min. Speed = `3600 m ÷ 900 s = ` **4 m/s**.

**Example:** a bus at 50 km/h for 2 h covers `50 × 2 = ` **100 km**.

**Example:** a train at 90 km/h covering 360 km takes `360 ÷ 90 = ` **4 h**.

- **This is really *average* speed** — an object rarely moves at one steady speed the whole way. Dividing total distance by total time smooths all the speeding up and slowing down into one number. (In this book, "speed" means average speed.)

> [!NOTE]
> A **speedometer** shows a vehicle's speed in km/h; an **odometer** measures the distance it has travelled in km.

## Uniform & non-uniform linear motion

**Linear motion** = motion along a straight line.

- **Uniform linear motion:** straight line at **constant speed** → covers **equal distances in equal intervals of time**.
- **Non-uniform linear motion:** speed **keeps changing** → covers **unequal distances in equal intervals of time**.

**The train example:** a train leaves station A slowly, speeds up, runs at a steady speed from **B to C**, then slows to a halt at D.
- **B → C: uniform.** **A → B and C → D: non-uniform.**

> [!TIP]
> To test a table of readings, check the **distance covered in each equal time interval**. All equal → uniform. Varying → non-uniform.

> [!WARNING]
> **Uniform linear motion is an idealisation** — in everyday life almost nothing keeps a truly constant speed for long. That's exactly why we use **average speed**.
