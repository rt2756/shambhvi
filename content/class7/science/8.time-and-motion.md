## Prerna's question

**Prerna** is her district's fastest girl in the **100 m sprint** and dreams of running for India. Watching Olympic reruns, she's amazed that timing is so precise a winner can be picked even when two sprinters seem to cross the line **together**.

Around her, time is measured in many ways: her coach's **stopwatch**, her mother's **wristwatch**, her sister's **mobile phone**, her uncle's **Braille watch** and **talking watch**, the big **wall clock** at school.

Then she wonders: **how did people measure time before clocks and watches existed?**

## Why any clock works: something that repeats

Long ago, people noticed that **many events in nature repeat after definite intervals** — the **rising and setting of the Sun**, the **phases of the Moon**, the **changing seasons**. They used those cycles to keep time:

- First came **calendars**. A **day** was one cycle of the Sun rising and setting.
- Then people wanted to measure **smaller intervals within a day** — and invented devices.

| Device | What it uses |
|---|---|
| **Sundial** | the **changing position of a shadow** cast by the Sun |
| **Water clock — outflow type** | water **flowing out** of a vessel marked with times |
| **Water clock — sinking bowl** | a bowl with a **fine hole** floats on water, **fills in a fixed time and sinks**; then it's lifted and floated again |
| **Hourglass** | **sand flowing** from one bulb to the other |
| **Candle clock** | a candle with **markings** showing time passed as it burns |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every clock — ancient or modern — is built on some process that REPEATS continuously, marking equal intervals of time.** A sundial uses the Sun's daily path; a pendulum clock uses swings; a quartz watch uses a crystal's vibrations.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Samrat Yantra** at **Jantar Mantar, Jaipur** (a **UNESCO World Heritage site**), built about **300 years ago**, is the **world's largest stone sundial** — **27 m** tall. Its shadow moves about **1 mm every second** across a scale that marks intervals as short as **2 seconds**. Like every sundial it shows **local "solar" time**, which needs a correction to give **Indian Standard Time**.

**Activity 8.1 — make a water clock:** cut a plastic bottle in half, pierce a **small hole in the cap**, put the top half **upside down** over the bottom half, fill it with (coloured) water, and **mark the water level in the bottom part every minute**. Pour the water back up to reuse it — each time the level reaches a mark, another minute has passed.

> [!NOTE]
> **Timekeeping in ancient India.**
> - Shadow-based time first appears in **Kautilya's *Arthasastra*** (composed between the 2nd century BCE and 3rd century CE). **Varahamihira** (~**530 CE**) gave an accurate expression for time using the **shadow of a vertical stick**.
> - The **outflow water clock** was described in the *Arthasastra* and other texts — but it **wasn't accurate**: **as the water level dropped, the water flowed out more slowly**, so later "minutes" took longer.
> - That led to the **sinking-bowl water clock — the *Ghatika-yantra***, first mentioned by **Aryabhata**. It was used in **Buddhist monasteries, palaces and town squares**; each sinking was announced by **drums, conch shells or a gong**.
> - Its bowl took **24 minutes** to fill and sink — a unit called the **ghatika** or **ghati**. A **24-hour day = 60 ghatis**. It remained a standard unit until the late 19th century, when **pendulum clocks** replaced it.

> [!TIP]
> **Why the sinking bowl beat the outflow clock:** the bowl sits on top of a big body of water, so the push of water into the hole stays the **same** every time — each sinking takes the **same** 24 minutes.

## The pendulum

As people began to travel far, accurate time became **critical**. From the **14th century**, mechanical clocks driven by **weights, gears and springs** appeared. The **pendulum clock** (17th century) was the big breakthrough.

> [!NOTE]
> **Galileo Galilei** (1564–1642), sitting in a church, noticed a **lamp swinging** from the ceiling. Timing it with his own **pulse**, he found **each swing took the same time**. Experimenting further, he concluded that **a pendulum of a given length always takes the same time for one swing**. Inspired by this, **Christiaan Huygens** (1629–1695) **invented the pendulum clock in 1656** and **patented it in 1657**.

### The simple pendulum

A **small metal ball — the bob** — hung from a **rigid support** by a **long thread**.

- **Mean position (O):** where it hangs **at rest**.
- **Extreme positions (A and B):** the two ends of its swing.
- Its motion is **oscillatory** and **periodic** — it repeats its path after a **fixed interval of time**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ONE OSCILLATION** = **O → A → B → O** (mean to one end, across to the other end, back to mean). Equally: **A → B → A**.
>
> **TIME PERIOD** = the **time taken for ONE oscillation.**

**Activity 8.2:** a 100 cm pendulum. Pull the bob slightly aside and **release it gently** (don't push; keep the string taut). Time **10 oscillations**, repeat 3–4 times, and **divide by 10**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why time 10 swings and divide by 10?** Your **reaction time** in starting and stopping the watch adds a small error. Time one swing and that error is huge compared to the swing; time ten and the same error gets **shared across ten swings** — your answer is far more accurate.

### What controls the time period? (think like Galileo)

| Change | Time period? |
|---|---|
| **Length** of the pendulum | **CHANGES** — a longer pendulum swings more slowly (longer time period) |
| **Mass** of the bob | **does NOT change** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The time period of a simple pendulum depends on its LENGTH, not on the bob's MASS.**
> **All pendulums of the same length have the same time period at a given place** — so the time period of a given pendulum is **constant**. **That constancy is exactly why a pendulum can measure time.**

> [!TIP]
> **A swing in the playground is a pendulum.** Children of different **weights** on the same swing take **about the same time** per swing — mass doesn't matter. A **longer** swing takes **longer**.

### Clocks today

**Quartz clocks** use the tiny, very fast vibrations of a **quartz crystal**; **atomic clocks** use the vibrations of particular **atoms**. Accuracy has improved enormously: **Huygens' early pendulum clocks gained or lost about 10 seconds a day; today's atomic clocks lose only one second in millions of years.**

## The SI unit of time

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SI unit of time: the SECOND, symbol `s`.** Larger units: **minute (min)** and **hour (h)**.
>
> **60 s = 1 min · 60 min = 1 h** (so **1 h = 3600 s**)

> [!WARNING]
> **Writing units correctly:**
> - unit names and symbols in **lowercase**: second **s**, minute **min**, hour **h**
> - symbols are **singular** — `5 s`, not `5 ss`
> - **no full stop** after the symbol (unless the sentence ends)
> - **a space** between number and unit — `45 s`, not `45s`
> - **`sec` and `hrs` are wrong** — write `s` and `h`

- The smallest interval an ordinary **wall clock** can show is **1 second**.
- But tiny fractions matter: **race timers** record **hundredths or thousandths of a second (a millisecond)**; **ECG** heart monitors read **millisecond** changes in heartbeats; **computers and phones** work in **microseconds** (millionths of a second).

> [!NOTE]
> We can't see or feel time — we can only measure the **interval between events**. *"What is time?"* is still a tricky question with no easy answer.

## Slow or fast — speed

In a **100 m race** everyone starts together; soon some are ahead. **Whoever is ahead at an instant has covered more distance in the same time — they're faster.**

But how do you compare runners in **different-length** races? You need the **distance covered per unit time** — the **speed**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Speed = Total distance covered ÷ Total time taken**
>
> - **SI unit: metre per second (m/s).** Also **kilometre per hour (km/h)**.
> - Rearranged: **Distance = Speed × Time** and **Time = Distance ÷ Speed**.

**Worked examples (from the book):**

- **Swati's school is 3.6 km away; she cycles there in 15 min. Speed in m/s?**
  3.6 km = **3600 m**; 15 min = 15 × 60 = **900 s**. Speed = 3600 ÷ 900 = **4 m/s**.
- **Raghav's bus goes at 50 km/h for 2 h. How far?** Distance = 50 × 2 = **100 km**.
- **A train at 90 km/h covers 360 km. How long?** Time = 360 ÷ 90 = **4 h**.

> [!TIP]
> **Converting units:** always change to matching units **before** dividing — km and min don't mix with m and s.
> **km/h → m/s:** ×1000 (km → m) and ÷3600 (h → s), i.e. **× 5/18**. So **36 km/h = 10 m/s**, **72 km/h = 20 m/s**.

**More practice (the book's end-of-chapter style):**

| Question | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| A car goes **150 m in 10 s**. Speed in km/h? | 150 ÷ 10 = 15 m/s; 15 × 18/5 | **54 km/h** |
| Runners do **400 m in 50 s** and **in 45 s**. | 400 ÷ 50 = 8 m/s; 400 ÷ 45 ≈ 8.9 m/s | the second is faster by **about 0.9 m/s** |
| A train at **25 m/s** covers **360 km**. Time? | 360 km = 360 000 m; ÷ 25 = 14 400 s | **4 h** |
| A train goes **180 km in 3 h**. | 180 ÷ 3 = 60 km/h = 60 × 5/18 m/s; in 4 h: 60 × 4 | **60 km/h, about 16.7 m/s, 240 km** |
| A horse at **18 m/s** vs a train at **72 km/h**. | 72 km/h = 20 m/s | the **train is faster** |
| A car does **60, 70, 50 km** in three hours. | total 180 km ÷ 3 h | **not uniform; average 60 km/h** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The speed we calculate is really the AVERAGE speed.** An object rarely keeps one speed the whole way — it speeds up and slows down. **Total distance ÷ total time** smooths all of that into one number. (In this book, "speed" means average speed.)

> [!WARNING]
> **Average speed is NOT the average of the speeds.** A vehicle goes 500 m at 10 m/s (50 s) and 500 m at 5 m/s (100 s) — that's 1000 m in 150 s. **Always divide total distance by total time.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Speedometer** — shows a vehicle's **speed** in **km/h**. **Odometer** — measures the **distance** it has travelled in **km**.

## Uniform and non-uniform linear motion

**Linear motion** = motion **along a straight line** (Class 6).

A train runs on a straight track from station **A** to station **D**: it **starts slowly**, **speeds up**, runs **at a steady speed from B to C**, then **slows down and stops** at D.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **UNIFORM linear motion** — straight line, **constant speed** → covers **equal distances in equal intervals of time**. (**B → C**.)
> - **NON-UNIFORM linear motion** — straight line, **speed keeps changing** → covers **unequal distances in equal intervals of time**. (**A → B** and **C → D**.)

> [!TIP]
> **To test a table of readings:** work out the **distance covered in each equal time interval**. All the same → **uniform**. Different → **non-uniform**.
>
> Train X covers the same distance every 10 minutes → uniform. Train Y covers different distances → non-uniform.

- **A car on an empty straight highway** at a steady speed → nearly **uniform**. **A car in city traffic**, stopping and starting → **non-uniform**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Uniform linear motion is an idealisation.** In everyday life objects **seldom** keep a constant speed for long — so **non-uniform motion is far more common**, and that's why we use **average speed**.

## In a nutshell

- Clocks of every kind count a **repeating process**: sundials, water clocks, hourglasses, candle clocks, pendulums, quartz, atoms.
- **One oscillation** of a pendulum: O → A → B → O. **Time period** = time for one oscillation.
- **Time period depends on length, not mass**, and is **constant** for a given pendulum at a place.
- **SI unit of time: second (s).** 60 s = 1 min, 60 min = 1 h.
- **Speed = total distance ÷ total time** (m/s or km/h) — this is **average** speed.
- **Uniform motion:** equal distances in equal times; **non-uniform:** unequal.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** time 10 swings not 1 · mass doesn't change the time period · write `s` and `h`, never `sec`/`hrs` · convert units before dividing · average speed = total ÷ total.
