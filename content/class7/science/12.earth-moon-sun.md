# 12. Earth, Moon & the Sun

<!-- NCERT Class 7 "Curiosity" Chapter 12 — Earth, Moon, and the Sun. -->

## Rotation — why we have day and night

- **Rotation** = an object turning about an imaginary line through itself, the **axis of rotation**. The Earth's axis passes through the **North and South Poles**.
- The Earth completes **one rotation in about 24 hours**, spinning **West to East** (anti-clockwise seen from above the North Pole).
- **The Sun doesn't move across the sky — we do.** Like sitting on a merry-go-round: as *you* turn anti-clockwise, the trees appear to sweep past you clockwise. Because the Earth turns West→East, the Sun **appears** to rise in the East and set in the West.
- **Day and night:** sunlight falls on **half** the Earth at a time. The lit half has day, the other half night. As your location rotates into the light it is **sunrise**; rotating into darkness is **sunset**.
- Stars and the Moon appear to rise and set for the same reason.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Pole Star (Dhruva Tara) stays put.** The Earth's axis points very close to it, so it appears nearly **stationary** while all other stars appear to circle around it. Long-exposure photographs record this as curved **star trails**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Aryabhata** explained this in the *Aryabhatiya* (~5th century CE): *just as a man in a moving boat sees stationary objects moving backwards, so people see the stationary stars moving west.* His value for one rotation — about **23 h 56 min 4.1 s** — is impressively close to today's accepted figure.
> A **22-metre Foucault pendulum** hangs in the Constitution Hall of India's new Parliament building; Léon Foucault used such a pendulum to give the first simple demonstration of the Earth's rotation.

## Revolution — why the night sky changes

- **Revolution** = the motion of one object **around another**. The path is its **orbit**.
- The Earth completes one revolution around the Sun in about **365 days and 6 hours**. Seen from above, its orbit is **nearly circular**.
- **Why different stars appear in different months:** as the Earth moves around the Sun, the night side faces a **different direction in space** each month — so a different part of the sky is on view after sunset.

> [!NOTE]
> The **Bhil and Pawara** communities of the Tapi Valley used the appearance of certain star patterns as markers for the **arrival of the monsoon**.

## Seasons — the tilt, not the distance

**Seasons happen because the Earth's axis is TILTED, combined with the Earth's spherical shape.** The Earth keeps this same tilt all the way round its orbit.

**In June:** the Northern Hemisphere is tilted **towards** the Sun →
- sunrays are **concentrated into a smaller area** (more intense heating), and
- the hemisphere gets sunlight for **more than 12 hours** a day.
→ **Summer in the north.** In December everything reverses → **winter in the north**.

- The Southern Hemisphere always has the **opposite** season — which is why a December cricket tour of Australia needs **summer clothes**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Two popular explanations that are WRONG:**
> ✗ "The tilted hemisphere is closer to the Sun." ✗ "The Earth's oval orbit brings it nearer the Sun in summer."
> Both distance differences are far too small to matter — and in fact **the Earth is closest to the Sun in January**, during the northern winter. **It is the tilt.**

### Solstices & equinoxes (Northern Hemisphere)

| Date | Name | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| ~**21 June** | **summer solstice** | **longest day** |
| ~**22 December** | **winter solstice** | **shortest day, longest night** |
| ~**21 March** | **spring equinox** | day = night = 12 hours |
| ~**23 September** | **autumn equinox** | day = night = 12 hours |

- **At the poles:** the North Pole has **six months of continuous sunshine** (Sun rises 21 March, sets 22 September) then six months of darkness. The South Pole is the opposite.
- **At the equator:** always about **12 hours** of light and 12 of darkness, with little change in intensity — which is why **seasons are not very prominent in the southern states of India**.

## Eclipses

### Why the small Moon can hide the huge Sun

**Apparent size** depends on **both** an object's real size **and** its distance from you. Your thumb can cover a friend's head at 5 metres — not because it's bigger, but because it's much closer.

The Moon is far smaller than the Sun but also **far closer to us**, so their **apparent sizes in our sky are similar** — close enough for the Moon to cover the Sun exactly.

- Mercury and Venus are much **bigger** than the Moon, but so far away that their apparent size is tiny — Venus crossing the Sun appears only as a **small black dot** (a rare **Transit of Venus**).

### Solar eclipse — the Moon blocks the Sun

- Order: **Sun → Moon → Earth**. The **Moon's shadow falls on a small area** of the Earth.
- **Total solar eclipse** in that small area — it goes dark for a few minutes. **Partial solar eclipse** in the surrounding regions where only part of the Sun is hidden.
- It lasts only minutes because the Earth's rotation and the Moon's orbital motion keep the shadow **sweeping across** the surface.

> [!WARNING]
> **NEVER look at a solar eclipse directly** — the Sun is still intense enough to **damage the eyes and cause blindness**. Do **not** use sunglasses, binoculars or telescopes either.
> **Safe ways:** proper solar-eclipse goggles, projecting the Sun's image with a mirror onto a wall, or attending a viewing event run by a planetarium or astronomy club.

### Lunar eclipse — the Earth blocks the Sun's light

- Order: **Sun → Earth → Moon**. The **Earth's shadow falls on the full Moon**.
- **Total lunar eclipse:** the Moon is entirely in the Earth's shadow and turns a **dark red** colour. **Partial lunar eclipse:** only part of it is shadowed.
- **A lunar eclipse is safe to watch with the naked eye** — you're looking at the dim Moon, not the Sun.

> [!TIP]
> **Why lunar eclipses are seen by far more people:** the Earth's shadow is large and falls on the *whole* Moon, so anyone on the night side of the Earth can see it. The Moon's shadow is small and covers only a narrow strip of the Earth — so a *total solar* eclipse is visible from only a tiny region.

> [!NOTE]
> An eclipse is **grahan** in Sanskrit. The **Surya Siddhanta** gives calculations to predict eclipses in rhythmic shlokas. Once feared and surrounded by superstition, eclipses are now prized by scientists, who travel the world to study what only an eclipse reveals.

> [!NOTE]
> The **Kodaikanal Solar Observatory** (Palani hills, established **1899**, run by the Indian Institute of Astrophysics) has recorded data about the Sun for over a century. **M.K. Vainu Bappu**, the father of modern Indian astronomy, set up telescopes at Manora Peak (Nainital) and Kavalur (Tamil Nadu), studied stars, and discovered a comet.
