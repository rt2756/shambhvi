## Rashmika's puzzle

In **Kanniyakumari, Tamil Nadu**, 12-year-old **Rashmika** notices that **coconut-tree shadows are long in the morning and shorter in the afternoon**. She guesses it's because **the Sun moves across the sky**. But in Class 6 she learnt that **the Earth moves around the Sun**. So which is it — **does the Sun move, or does the Earth?**

## Rotation — why the Sun seems to move

**Activity 12.1 — the merry-go-round.** Sit facing outwards on a merry-go-round turning **anti-clockwise**. Everything around you — trees, buildings — **seems to swing past in the opposite direction, clockwise**. A tree comes into view on your **left** and disappears on your **right**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The Sun appears to move across the sky because we are watching it from the Earth, which is itself turning.** We're on the merry-go-round; the Sun is the tree.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ROTATION** = motion in which **all parts of an object move in circles around an imaginary line through it** — the **AXIS OF ROTATION**. (Like a spinning **top**, **fan** or **ball**.)
>
> - **The Earth's axis passes through its geographic North and South Poles.**
> - **The Earth completes one rotation in about 24 hours.**
> - Seen from **above the North Pole**, it rotates **anti-clockwise — from WEST to EAST**.

**Activity 12.2 — globe and sticker.** Put a sticker on your location on a globe. Turn the globe **anti-clockwise** (seen from above the North Pole) and watch your sticker go round and come back — **one rotation**.

### Day and night

Now shine a **torch** (the Sun) on the globe from about **1.5 m** in a dark room:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Light falls on only HALF the globe at a time.** The **lit half has day**; the **other half has night**.
>
> As the Earth turns **west to east**, your location moves **into** the light (**sunrise**) and later **out** of it (**sunset**). **The Earth's rotation causes the day-and-night cycle.**
>
> **Why exactly half:** the Earth is a **ball**, and light travels in **straight lines** — it can only reach the side facing the Sun.

- **In India, the Sun rises first in the EAST** (Arunachal Pradesh) and later in the west — because the Earth turns west-to-east, carrying eastern places into sunlight first. So **sunrise in Jharkhand comes before sunrise in Gujarat**.
- **When it's day in India, it's night in the USA** — the USA is on the **other side** of the globe, in Earth's shadow.

**A girl standing on the Equator, facing North**, through one day:

1. **Morning:** the Sun appears on her **right** — in the **East**.
2. **Noon:** the Earth has turned so the Sun is **overhead**.
3. **Evening:** she's turned further; the Sun **disappears on her left, in the West**. Stars come out.

> [!TIP]
> **So:** the Sun, the **Moon** and the **stars** all **appear to rise in the east and set in the west** — all for the same reason: the Earth spins **west to east** underneath them.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Foucault pendulum.** In the mid-19th century **Léon Foucault** used a very long pendulum to give the **first simple demonstration that the Earth rotates**. A **22-metre Foucault pendulum** hangs from a skylight in the **Constitution Hall of the new Parliament building in New Delhi** — symbolising the link between the idea of India and the vastness of the cosmos.

### The stars move too — except one

**Activity 12.3:** on an early evening between **March and May**, find the **Big Dipper (*Saptarishi*)** and the **Pole Star (*Dhruva Tara*)**. Sketch the Big Dipper's position, note the time, and repeat **every two hours** the same night.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The Big Dipper appears to move round the Pole Star.**
> **Why the Pole Star stays (nearly) still:** the Earth's **axis points very close to the Pole Star** (in the Northern Hemisphere). Stars near the axis barely move as the Earth spins — just as the centre of a spinning wheel barely moves. **All the other stars appear to circle around it.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Star trails** — astrophotographers keep a camera's shutter open for a **long time**. The stars' apparent motion shows up as **arcs of circles** (one famous photo was taken at **Mahuli, Maharashtra**).

> [!NOTE]
> **Aryabhata** explained this in his ***Aryabhatiya*** (around the **5th century CE**), Verse 9 of *Golapada*: *just as a man in a boat moving forward sees stationary objects on the shore moving **backwards**, so the stationary stars are seen by people at Lanka (the equator) as moving **westwards**.*
>
> His value for one rotation of the Earth — about **23 hours 56 minutes 4.1 seconds** — is **impressively close** to today's accepted value.

## Revolution — why the night sky changes over the year

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **REVOLUTION** = the motion of one object **around another**. The path is its **ORBIT**.
>
> - While rotating, the Earth also **revolves around the Sun**.
> - Seen from above, Earth's orbit is **nearly circular**. (In Class 6 it looked stretched because that picture was a **side view**.)
> - **One revolution takes about 365 days and 6 hours.**

> [!TIP]
> **Rotation vs revolution in one line:** *rotation* = spinning on your own axis (**day**); *revolution* = going round something else (**year**).

**Why different stars are seen in different months:** each evening after sunset the night side of the Earth faces outward. As the Earth moves round the Sun, **the night side faces a different direction in space each month** — so a **different set of stars** rises at sunset. Look at a star pattern at the **same time** of night a month apart and it will have shifted.

> [!NOTE]
> The **Bhil and Pawara**, indigenous communities of the **Tapi Valley** in western India, used the appearance of certain **star patterns** as signs that the **monsoon** was coming.

> [!TIP]
> **Because of revolution, a star rises about 4 minutes earlier each night.** So if Orion is overhead at 8 pm today, tomorrow it's overhead a few minutes **before** 8 pm. A group of stars rising at midnight on **21 June** will rise at midnight again on **21 June next year** — after one full revolution.

## Seasons — the tilt, not the distance

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The Earth's axis is NOT upright compared to its orbit — it is TILTED, and keeps the same tilt all the way round the Sun.**
>
> **Seasons happen because of this TILT combined with the Earth's SPHERICAL SHAPE.**

**In June** the **Northern Hemisphere** (where India is) is **tilted towards the Sun**:

1. **More intense sunlight** — the same amount of sunlight falls on a **smaller area** (it hits more directly), so that area is **heated more**.
2. **Longer days** — places get sunlight for **more than 12 hours** of the 24.

→ **Summer in the Northern Hemisphere.** At the **North Pole the Sun shines for all 24 hours** in June.

**In December** everything **reverses** — the Northern Hemisphere is tilted **away**: sunlight is **spread over a bigger area** (weaker) and days are **shorter** → **winter**. The North Pole is **dark for all 24 hours**.

> [!TIP]
> **Torch on a table:** shine it straight down — a **small bright** circle. Tilt it — the same light spreads into a **big dim** oval. That's the difference between summer and winter sunlight.

- **The Southern Hemisphere always has the opposite season.** It's **winter there in June** and **summer in December** — so the **Indian cricket team touring Australia in December** should pack **summer clothes**!

> [!WARNING]
> **Two popular explanations that are WRONG:**
> - ✗ *"The tilted hemisphere is closer to the Sun."*
> - ✗ *"Earth's oval orbit brings it closer to the Sun in summer."*
>
> Both distance differences are **far too small to matter**. (Draw circles of radius 14.7 cm and 15.2 cm, at 1 cm = 10 million km — Earth's closest and farthest distances. They're almost the same circle!) In fact **the Earth is CLOSEST to the Sun in JANUARY — during the northern winter.** It's the **tilt**.

### Solstices and equinoxes (Northern Hemisphere)

| Date | Name | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| around **21 June** | **summer solstice** | **longest day** |
| around **22 December** | **winter solstice** | **shortest day, longest night** |
| around **21 March** | **spring equinox** | day = night = **12 hours** |
| around **23 September** | **autumn equinox** | day = night = **12 hours** |

After the summer solstice, days get **shorter** and nights **longer**, until the winter solstice.

- **At the poles:** at the North Pole the Sun **rises on 21 March and stays up for six months**, setting around **22 September** — then **six months of darkness**. The **South Pole** is the opposite.
- **At the Equator:** always about **12 hours of light and 12 of darkness**, and the sunlight's strength hardly changes through the year. **That's why seasons aren't very noticeable in the southern states of India** (close to the equator) — so even in Chennai the summer solstice isn't a dramatic "longest day". (Local features and nearness to the sea also affect climate.)

> [!TIP]
> **If the Earth's axis weren't tilted**, every place would get the same sunlight and day length all year — **no seasons**. A **bigger tilt** would mean **more extreme** seasons (the planet **Uranus** is tilted much more).

## Eclipses

The two planets between us and the Sun, **Mercury and Venus**, look **tiny** next to the Sun — they can never block its light. But the **Moon**, Earth's natural satellite, **can**.

### Why the small Moon can hide the huge Sun

**Activity 12.4:** a friend stands **5 m** away. Close one eye and hold up your **thumb** at arm's length — you can **cover their whole head**!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **APPARENT SIZE** (how big something looks) depends on **both its real size AND its distance from you.**
>
> The **Moon is much smaller** than the Sun, but **much closer** to us — so their **apparent sizes in our sky are about the same**. That's why the Moon can exactly cover the Sun.

> [!NOTE]
> **Mercury and Venus are much bigger than the Moon** but **much farther away**, so they look tiny. When **Venus** passes between the Earth and the Sun, it's just a **small black dot** crossing the Sun's face — a rare **Transit of Venus**.

### Solar eclipse — the Moon blocks the Sun

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SOLAR ECLIPSE:** the **Moon comes between the Sun and the Earth** and blocks sunlight.
> **Order: Sun → Moon → Earth.**
>
> - The **Moon's shadow falls on a SMALL area** of the Earth. There, **no part of the Sun is visible** → **total solar eclipse**. For a few minutes it gets **dark in the daytime**.
> - Around that area, the Moon covers only **part** of the Sun → **partial solar eclipse**.
> - As the Moon moves off, a brilliant **"diamond ring"** is seen, then daylight returns.
> - **Why only a few minutes:** the **Earth's rotation** and the **Moon's motion in its orbit** keep the shadow **sweeping across** the surface.

> [!WARNING]
> **NEVER look at a solar eclipse directly** — even then, the Sun is strong enough to **damage your eyes and cause blindness**. **Not through sunglasses, binoculars or telescopes either.**
>
> **Safe ways:** proper **solar eclipse goggles**; **projecting the Sun's image** onto a wall with a mirror (teacher's set-up — a small mirror on a **sand-filled hollow ball** resting on a tape ring so it can be turned easily); or going to an **eclipse-viewing event** run by a **planetarium or astronomy club** (like the one in **Ooty, Tamil Nadu**).

> [!NOTE]
> **Eclipses and superstition.** Before their cause was known, eclipses were **feared** — blocking the Sun, even briefly, seemed alarming — and **superstitions** grew up about not eating, cooking or going out. Now that we understand them, there's nothing to fear (as long as we don't look at the Sun). **Scientists travel the world to see eclipses** — they reveal things that can't be studied otherwise.
>
> An eclipse is called **grahan** in Sanskrit. Ancient Indian texts gave **calculations to predict eclipses** — best known is the ***Surya Siddhanta***, written in rhythmic Sanskrit shlokas.

### Lunar eclipse — the Earth's shadow on the Moon

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **LUNAR ECLIPSE:** the **Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon** and blocks sunlight from reaching the Moon. We see the **Earth's shadow on the full Moon**.
> **Order: Sun → Earth → Moon.**
>
> - **Total lunar eclipse:** the Moon is **completely** in Earth's shadow — it turns **dark red** until it moves out.
> - **Partial lunar eclipse:** only **part** of the Moon is in shadow.
> - **Safe to watch with the naked eye** — you're looking at the dim Moon, not the Sun.

> [!TIP]
> **Why lunar eclipses are seen by many more people than total solar eclipses:** the Earth's shadow is **big** and covers the **whole Moon**, so **everyone on the night side** of the Earth sees it. The Moon's shadow on Earth is **small** — only a **narrow strip** of Earth gets a total solar eclipse.

> [!NOTE]
> The free **Stellarium** app (computer version) shows upcoming solar and lunar eclipses visible from your location.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Kodaikanal Solar Observatory** in the **Palani hills**, set up in **1899**, has recorded data on the Sun for **over 100 years**. It's run by the **Indian Institute of Astrophysics (IIA), Bengaluru**.
>
> **M.K. Vainu Bappu** — the **father of modern Indian astronomy** — set up telescopes at **Manora Peak near Nainital** (Uttarakhand) and **Kavalur** (Tamil Nadu; the observatory there is named after him). He studied **stars**, **discovered a comet**, and travelled the world to study **solar eclipses**.

> [!NOTE]
> **A deeper truth:** the planets actually move around a point **very close to, but not exactly at**, the Sun's centre — and the Sun itself wobbles slightly around that point. Scientists use tiny wobbles like this in **other stars** to discover **exoplanets** around them!

   - *Seasons occur due to the tilt of Earth's axis and its spherical shape.* → **True.**
   - *Earth's revolution causes day and night.* → **False** — **rotation** does.

## In a nutshell

- The Earth **rotates** on its axis (through the poles) in about **24 hours**, **west to east** — causing **day and night** and the **apparent motion** of the Sun, Moon and stars.
- The **Pole Star** stays nearly still because Earth's axis points almost at it.
- The Earth **revolves** round the Sun in about **365 days 6 hours**, in a nearly circular orbit — so different stars appear in different months.
- **Seasons** come from the **tilt** of Earth's axis plus its **spherical shape** — not from distance.
- **Solstices:** 21 June (longest day), 22 December (shortest). **Equinoxes:** 21 March, 23 September (12 h each).
- **Solar eclipse:** Sun → Moon → Earth. **Lunar eclipse:** Sun → Earth → Moon.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** the Sun only *appears* to move · rotation = day, revolution = year · seasons are NOT about distance (closest in January!) · Southern Hemisphere seasons are reversed · never look at a solar eclipse · a lunar eclipse is safe to watch.
