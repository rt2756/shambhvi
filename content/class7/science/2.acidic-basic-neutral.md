## The puzzle the chapter opens with

At a science fair on **National Science Day (28 February)**, Ashwin and Keerthi are handed a **blank white sheet**. A volunteer sprays a liquid on it — and the words *"Welcome to the Wonderful World of Science"* suddenly appear.

Nothing was printed on the paper. Something written on it was **invisible**, and the spray **reacted** with it to make a colour. By the end of this chapter you'll be able to explain exactly how — and make your own.

> [!TIP]
> Keep this question in mind the whole way through: **what could be written on the paper, and what could be in the spray bottle?** Every section below gives you one more piece of the answer.

## The three families

Every substance dissolved in water around us falls into one of three groups:

| Family | Taste / feel (only for safe, edible things) | Everyday examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Acidic** | **sour** | lemon juice, amla juice, tamarind water, vinegar, curd |
| **Basic** | **bitter**, feels **soapy / slippery** | soap solution, baking soda solution, lime water, washing powder solution |
| **Neutral** | neither | tap water, sugar solution, salt solution |

- **Why "sour" is a clue to acids:** every edible acidic thing in the list tastes sour. The sourness *is* the acid being detected by your tongue.
- **Why bases feel slippery:** rub baking soda solution between your fingers — it feels soapy. That slippery feel is typical of bases (soap itself is a base).

> [!WARNING]
> **Two rules before you ever use taste or touch:**
> 1. **Never taste or touch an unknown substance.** Some acids and bases burn skin and are poisonous. That's the whole reason indicators exist.
> 2. **Bitter does NOT prove basic.** Bitter gourd (*karela*) tastes bitter but is **not** basic. Taste is a hint, never a test.

### Acids hiding in your kitchen

| Food | Main acid in it |
|---|---|
| Lemon | **citric acid** |
| Orange | **citric acid** + **ascorbic acid (vitamin C)** |
| Amla | **ascorbic acid (vitamin C)** |
| Tamarind | **tartaric acid** |
| Tomato / spinach | citric acid and **oxalic acid** |
| Vinegar | **acetic acid** |
| Curd | **lactic acid** |
| Ant sting | **formic acid** |

## Indicators — testing without tasting

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An acid–base indicator is a substance that shows a DIFFERENT colour in an acidic solution than in a basic one.**
>
> **Why that's useful:** the colour does the "tasting" for you. You can find the nature of any liquid — even a dangerous one, even a floor-cleaner — just by watching a colour change.

### 1. Litmus — the standard indicator

- **Litmus is a natural dye obtained from lichens.** It comes as a **solution** or as **paper strips** in two colours, **blue** and **red**.
- **Lichens** are two living things living together — a **fungus and an alga**. They grow on rocks and tree trunks where there's **plenty of rain and clean air** (so finding lichens near you is a sign of clean air).

| Put a drop on… | Acidic | Basic | Neutral |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Blue litmus** | turns **RED** | stays blue | stays blue |
| **Red litmus** | stays red | turns **BLUE** | stays red |

> [!TIP]
> **Memory hook:** **B**lue → **R**ed = **A**cid ("**BRA**"). The other change (red → blue) is then automatically the base.
>
> **Why you need BOTH colours of litmus to find a neutral:** a neutral changes *neither*. With only blue litmus you can spot an acid — but a base and a neutral both leave blue litmus blue, so you can't tell them apart. Red litmus splits them.

**The classroom activity (Activity 2.1)** — drops of 11 samples on pieces of blue and red litmus — sorts everything into three groups:

- **Group A** (blue → red): lemon juice, amla juice, tamarind water, vinegar → **acidic**
- **Group B** (red → blue): soap solution, baking soda solution, lime water, washing powder → **basic**
- **Group C** (no change to either): tap water, sugar solution, salt solution → **neutral**

> [!NOTE]
> **Lime water is not lemon (lime) juice!** Lime water is **calcium hydroxide** in water: mix *chuna* (lime, calcium oxide) in water, leave it an hour, filter it. It's **basic** — the opposite of lime-the-fruit, which is acidic.

### 2. Red rose extract — make your own indicator

**How (Activity 2.3):** collect **fallen** red rose petals (don't pluck), wash them, crush them, cover with **hot water** (adult help), wait 5–10 minutes until the water is coloured, **filter**. The liquid that comes through (the **filtrate**) is your indicator.

| Rose extract in… | Colour |
|---|---|
| **Acid** | a shade of **red** |
| **Base** | **green** |
| **Neutral** | no change |

- **How we know it works:** the samples that turned it red are *exactly* Group A from the litmus test; the ones that turned it green are exactly Group B. Two different indicators agreeing is strong evidence.
- **Other flowers and vegetables work too:** beetroot, **purple cabbage**, **jamun**, **red hibiscus (gudhal)**, and turmeric.

> [!NOTE]
> **Hydrangea — a plant that is its own indicator.** It grows in cool Himalayan and North-eastern regions. In **acidic soil its flowers are blue**; in **basic soil, pink or red**. So a gardener can change the flower colour by changing the soil's nature.

### 3. Turmeric — the indicator with a blind spot

**How (Activity 2.5):** make turmeric (*haldi*) paste, dip filter paper in it, dry it, cut into strips — **turmeric paper**.

| Turmeric paper in… | Colour |
|---|---|
| **Base** | turns **RED** |
| **Acid** | stays **yellow** |
| **Neutral** | stays **yellow** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Turmeric can find a BASE, but it CANNOT tell an acid from a neutral.**
>
> **Why:** both acids and neutrals leave it yellow — so a yellow strip only tells you "not basic". To split the remaining two you need another indicator (blue litmus or rose extract).

- **Why a curry stain on a white shirt turns red when you use soap:** the curry has turmeric; soap is a base; turmeric turns red with a base. The stain is acting as an indicator!
- Turmeric is called the **"golden spice"** — a member of the ginger family, used widely in Ayurvedic home remedies.

### 4. Olfactory indicators — test by smell

**Olfactory** means *to do with smell*. These substances **change their smell** (not colour) in an acid or base.

**Activity 2.6:** keep cotton strips overnight in a closed box with chopped **onion**. Put tamarind water (acid) on one strip, baking soda solution (base) on another, and sniff:

- With the **base**, the **onion smell disappears**.
- With the **acid**, the onion smell **stays**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why this matters:** someone who **cannot see** colours can still test a liquid. That's the answer to the textbook question about blindfolded Manya — she should use an **olfactory indicator** like onion.

## Neutralisation — when acid meets base

**Activity 2.7:** a drop of lemon juice in water + blue litmus solution → the liquid turns **red** (acidic). Now add **lime water** drop by drop, swirling…

- For a while nothing seems to change — the lime water is being **used up** cancelling the acid.
- Then the colour flips **red → blue**: the acid has been cancelled and now there's a little extra base.
- Add **one more drop of lemon juice** and it can flip back to red.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When an acid and a base are mixed in suitable amounts, each cancels the other's effect. This is NEUTRALISATION.**
>
> **Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat**
>
> - **Salt** here means a family of substances — common salt is just one of them.
> - **Heat is released** — the mixture gets slightly warm. (We say heat is "evolved".)

> [!WARNING]
> **"Adding base to acid always makes it neutral" is FALSE.** It depends on the **amounts**. Too little base → still acidic. Too much → now basic. Neutral only happens when they're matched. That's exactly why the litmus in Activity 2.7 can flip back and forth.

## Neutralisation in daily life

The pattern is always the same: **too much of one nature → add something of the opposite nature.**

| Problem | Why it happens | Fix — and why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Red ant bite / bee sting** | the ant injects **formic acid** into the skin | rub **moist baking soda** — a **base**, it neutralises the acid |
| **Soil too acidic** | **too much chemical fertiliser** | add **lime** (quicklime / slaked lime) — a **base** |
| **Soil too basic** | — | add **organic matter** — manure, composted leaves — which release **acids** as they rot |
| **Acidic factory waste killing lake fish** | acid released into the lake | **neutralise the waste with a base before** it is released |

> [!NOTE]
> **Soil can be neutral and plants can still grow badly** — because of **missing nutrients**, not because of acidity. Neutralisation fixes only the acid/base problem, not every problem. (A real example from the **Farmer's Portal** of the Department of Agriculture.)

## Solving the welcome-message mystery

**Write with a base, spray with an indicator that changes colour in a base.** The book's suggestion:

- **Writing liquid:** **soap solution** (basic) — dries invisible.
- **Spray:** **turmeric solution** (yellow).
- **Result:** wherever the soap is, turmeric turns **red** → red letters appear on yellow paper.

**Other combinations that work** — and why:

| Write with | Spray with | Letters appear | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| soap / baking soda (base) | turmeric | **red** | turmeric turns red in base |
| baking soda (base) | rose extract | **green** | rose extract turns green in base |
| lemon / vinegar (acid) | rose extract | **red** | rose extract turns red in acid |
| lemon juice (acid) | turmeric | **nothing!** | turmeric doesn't react to acid |

> [!TIP]
> **Ashwin's Guru Purnima card:** paper painted with turmeric and dried, message written with a **basic** solution → the words show up red. **Keerthi's secret message in orange juice** (acidic) won't show with turmeric — her grandmother needs an indicator that reacts to **acids**, like **blue litmus** or **rose extract**.

## Worked "think it through" questions

These mirror the textbook's own end-of-chapter questions — the point is the *reasoning*.

1. **A solution turns red litmus blue. Adding too much of which liquid reverses it — lime water, baking soda, vinegar or salt solution?** → **Vinegar.** The solution is basic; only an **acid** can neutralise it and push it back.
2. **Grape juice + rose extract looks reddish. What happens if baking soda is added?** → Grape juice is acidic (red tint). Baking soda (base) first neutralises it, and with enough of it the colour turns **green**.
3. **Using only turmeric paper, can you identify vinegar, baking soda solution and sugar solution?** → **Only the baking soda** (turns it red). Vinegar and sugar solution both leave it yellow, so they can't be told apart with turmeric alone.
4. **Liquid X turns rose extract green. Then lots of amla juice is added.** → X is **basic**. Excess amla juice (an acid) neutralises it and then makes it acidic → the colour goes to **red**.
5. **A liquid: red litmus — no change, blue litmus — turns red, turmeric — no change.** → **Acidic.** Blue → red is the acid sign; turmeric staying yellow agrees (not a base).
6. **Vinegar on eggshell bubbles, soap solution doesn't. Why?** → Eggshell (and marble) is a **carbonate**. An **acid** reacts with it and gives off **carbon dioxide gas** — the bubbles. Soap is a base, so no reaction.

## In a nutshell

- Substances are **acidic, basic or neutral**.
- **Indicators** show different colours in acids and bases: **litmus** (from lichens), **red rose**, **red hibiscus**, **purple cabbage**, **turmeric**, and more.
- **Acid: blue litmus → red. Base: red litmus → blue.**
- **Rose extract: red in acid, green in base.**
- **Turmeric: red in base; stays yellow in acid AND neutral.**
- **Olfactory indicators** (onion) change smell, not colour.
- **Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat** — neutralisation — used for ant bites, soil and factory waste.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** bitter ≠ basic · turmeric can't spot acids · lime water is basic, lime juice is acidic · neutral needs the *right amount* of base · neutral soil can still lack nutrients.
