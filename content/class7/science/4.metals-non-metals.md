## At the ironsmith's furnace

Yashwant and Anandi, from a village in Rajasthan, visit **Sudarshan uncle, an ironsmith**. He makes **tawas, buckets, chimtas (tongs)**, and farm tools — **spades (phawras), axes (kulhadis), trowels (khurpis), rakes (jelees)**. He uses **iron** for the tools, **wood** for handles, and **coal** in his furnace.

He heats an iron block **red hot** and **beats it with a hammer** into an axe. Anandi asks: *can other metals be beaten flat too?* That question opens the whole chapter — how do we tell a **metal** from a **non-metal**, and why does it matter?

> [!NOTE]
> **Why heat the iron first?** Hot iron is **softer**, so hammering shapes it far more easily. (This is the textbook's last question.)

## The five tests that spot a metal

**Activity 4.1** — look at, and then hammer, a piece of **copper**, **aluminium**, an **iron nail**, a lump of **coal**, a pea-sized lump of **sulfur (*gandhak*)**, and a **block of wood**:

| Property | What it means | Metals | Non-metals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lustre** | shines when polished — **metallic lustre** | **shiny** | **dull** |
| **Hardness** | hard to scratch or squeeze | **hard** (mostly) | **softer** |
| **Malleability** | can be **beaten into thin sheets** | ✅ | ✗ — they **break** (brittle) |
| **Ductility** | can be **drawn into wires** | ✅ | ✗ |
| **Sonority** | makes a **ringing** sound when struck | ✅ **sonorous** | ✗ dull thud |
| **Conducts heat** | heat passes through it | **good** conductor | **poor** |
| **Conducts electricity** | current passes through it | **good** conductor | **poor** |

### Malleability — beaten into sheets

- Copper, aluminium and iron **flatten** when hammered → **malleable**.
- **Silver foil (*varq*)** on sweets and **aluminium foil** for food are both made possible by malleability.
- **Gold and silver are the most malleable metals.**
- Coal and sulfur **break into pieces** when hammered → **brittle**.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Not malleable" does NOT automatically mean "brittle".** **Wood** neither flattens **nor** breaks into pieces — so wood is **neither malleable nor brittle**. Check both behaviours separately.

### Ductility — drawn into wires

- Copper and aluminium **wires** in electrical fittings; **bangles, necklaces, earrings**; the strings of the **veena, sitar, violin and guitar**; a wire **tea strainer**.
- **Gold is so ductile that 1 gram can be drawn into a 2 km wire!**
- Nobody has ever seen a wire of coal or sulfur — they aren't ductile.

> [!NOTE]
> **Steel wire ropes** hold up **suspension bridges** and lift loads on **cranes**. Steel is a mixture of a metal (**iron**) and a non-metal (**carbon**) — a mixture like this is called an **alloy**.

### Sonority — the ringing sound

**Activity 4.2:** drop a metal spoon, a coin, a piece of coal and a wooden block. The **spoon and coin ring**; **coal and wood thud**.

- The **school bell** and the **ghungroos** ring because metals are **sonorous**.
- A visually challenged person can tell **metal from wood** by the sound their **walking stick** makes.

### Conducting heat

**Activity 4.3:** a **metal spoon** and a **wooden spoon** of the same size stand in the same hot water for the same time. Touch the tops — the **metal one is much hotter**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Conduction** = heat passing from one point of a material to another. **Metals are good conductors of heat; wood is a poor conductor.**
>
> **Why the test is fair:** same water, same temperature, same time, same size — only the **material** differs, so the difference must come from the material.

- **That is why cooking vessels are metal** (heat must reach the food quickly) **but their handles are wood or plastic** (heat must *not* reach your hand). More in *Heat Transfer in Nature*.

### Conducting electricity

**Activity 4.4:** use the **conduction tester** from the electricity chapter on aluminium foil, an iron nail, sulfur, copper wire, coal, dry wood, a stone, a rubber eraser and a nylon rope.

- Bulb glows → **aluminium, iron, copper** — **all metals**.
- Bulb stays dark → sulfur, coal, wood, stone, rubber, nylon.

> [!TIP]
> **Why electricians use plastic-handled screwdrivers, rubber gloves and rubber shoes:** plastic and rubber are **poor conductors** of electricity, so current can't pass through them into the electrician's body.

## Exceptions worth remembering

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The "rules" above are **generally** true — science always checks for exceptions:
> - **Not all metals are hard:** **sodium and potassium** are so soft they can be **cut with a knife**.
> - **Not all metals are solid:** **mercury** is a **liquid** at room temperature (once used in thermometers).
> - **Not every non-metal is a poor conductor:** **graphite** (a form of carbon, in pencil lead) **conducts electricity**.

## Rusting — needs air AND water, both

You've seen iron left outside develop **brown deposits**. Is it caused by **dry air**, **water**, or **both**? **Activity 4.5** answers it with three bottles of clean iron nails, left for **8–10 days**:

| Bottle | Set-up | Air? | Water? | Rust? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | nail + **silica gel**, capped | dry air only | ✗ | **no** |
| **B** | nail in **freshly boiled, cooled water** with an **oil layer** on top, capped | ✗ | ✅ | **no** |
| **C** | nail **partly** in water, bottle **open** | ✅ | ✅ | **YES** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Only bottle C rusted → BOTH air and water (moist air) are needed for iron to rust.**
>
> **Why each odd-looking step is there:**
> - **Silica gel** soaks up moisture → bottle A has air but **no water**. (It's the stuff in the little packets inside shoe boxes and medicine bottles.)
> - **Boiling** the water drives out the **dissolved air**; the **oil layer** stops air dissolving back in → bottle B has water but **no air**.
> - Each bottle removes **just one** ingredient. If either one alone could cause rust, A or B would have rusted. Neither did — so both are needed together.
>
> **Rust** = the brown deposit; **rusting** = the process of its formation on iron.

- **Corrosion** is the general name: the gradual wearing away of a metal surface by **air, water or other substances**. Iron → **brown rust**; copper → **green coating**; silver → **black coating**.
- **Rusting costs India an enormous amount of money every year** in repairing and replacing iron structures.
- **How to stop it:** **painting, oiling, greasing**, and **galvanisation** (coating iron with a thin layer of **zinc**). **Why they all work:** each puts a barrier between iron and the **air and water** it needs to rust.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Iron Pillar of Delhi** — made more than **1600 years ago** in the time of **Chandragupta II**, about **8 m** tall, weighing more than **6000 kg** — has **barely any rust** despite centuries of wind and rain. It shows how advanced metal technology was in ancient India.

> [!NOTE]
> **Iron and Indian history.** The **Harappans** used **copper and gold** for utensils and jewellery, but there's hardly any evidence they used **iron**. Once iron came into wide use, its strength made far better **ploughs** and tools — a big step in India's progress. (Why copper before iron? Copper is easier to get from its ore and work with.)

## Metal oxides are basic, non-metal oxides acidic

This links straight back to the indicators of Chapter 2.

**Magnesium (a metal) — Activity 4.6 (teacher demo, wear eye protection):**
- Clean a **3–4 cm magnesium ribbon** with sandpaper, hold it in **tongs**, light it.
- It burns with a **dazzling white flame** and leaves a **white powder — magnesium oxide** (magnesium + oxygen from the air).
- Mix the powder with a little warm water and test → **red litmus turns blue** → **basic**.

**Sulfur (a non-metal) — Activity 4.7 (teacher demo, in a well-ventilated place):**
- Burn powdered sulfur in a **deflagrating spoon** (a long-handled metal spoon for burning things safely) inside a covered gas jar.
- The gas formed is **sulfur dioxide**. Shake it with a little water → **sulfurous acid** → **blue litmus turns red** → **acidic**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **metal + oxygen → metal oxide → BASIC**
> **non-metal + oxygen → non-metal oxide → ACIDIC**
>
> **Why this is useful:** it's a quick test for an unknown element — burn it, dissolve the product in water, and check with litmus.

## How metals and non-metals behave with air and water

| Substance | Behaviour | So it is stored… |
|---|---|---|
| **Sodium** (metal) | reacts **vigorously** with **oxygen and water**, giving off lots of heat — it can **catch fire** with water | in **kerosene** |
| **Phosphorus** (non-metal) | **catches fire in air** | in **water** |
| **Sulfur** (non-metal) | **no reaction** with water (Activity 4.8) | — |

> [!TIP]
> **Notice the neat reversal:** sodium hides **from** water (under kerosene), phosphorus hides **in** water. Each is kept away from the thing it reacts with.

**Sodium's oxide** — predict it from the rule: sodium is a metal, so its oxide is **basic**.

## So what is a non-metal?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Non-metals** are usually **soft and dull**, **neither malleable nor ductile**, **not sonorous**, **poor conductors** of heat and electricity, **do not react with water** in general, and form **acidic oxides**.
>
> Examples: **sulfur, phosphorus, carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen**, chlorine, iodine.

> [!WARNING]
> **Plastic, glass, wood, rubber and paper are neither metals nor non-metals.** "Metal" and "non-metal" sort only **elements**, and these materials aren't elements.
>
> An **element** is a substance that **cannot be broken down into simpler substances**. **118** are known; they're the **building blocks of all matter**. Some occur naturally; some are made only in laboratories.

## Non-metals matter just as much

- **Oxygen** — we **breathe** it; without it we couldn't survive.
- **Carbon** — the **building block of all living things**: proteins, fats and carbohydrates all contain it.
- **Nitrogen** — used to make **fertilisers**; an essential plant nutrient.
- **Chlorine** — used in **water purification**.
- **Iodine** solution — an **antiseptic** for wounds.

> [!NOTE]
> **Alloys** — mixtures of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal — are used for utensils, tools and almost every industry. Special metals serve special jobs: **zirconium** in atomic energy, **titanium** in aerospace. India **recycles** a lot of **iron and aluminium** to cut waste.

## Choosing a material — properties decide uses

| Job | Best choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Food packaging foil | **aluminium** | cheap, malleable — folds into any shape |
| Electrical wiring | **copper** | ductile + excellent conductor |
| Pan for boiling water | **copper** (or another metal) | good **heat** conductor (not because it conducts *electricity*!) |
| Pan handle | **wood / plastic** | poor heat conductor — stays cool |
| Jewellery | **gold, silver** | lustrous, malleable, ductile — and they **don't corrode easily**, so they keep their shine |
| Bells | metals | sonorous |

## Think-it-through questions

1. **Which metal catches fire with water — copper, aluminium, zinc or sodium?** → **Sodium.**
2. **True or false: "Copper vessels are used for boiling water because they conduct electricity."** → **False** — it's because they conduct **heat** well.
3. **True or false: "Metal oxides turn blue litmus red."** → **False** — metal oxides are **basic**; they turn **red litmus blue**.
4. **Iron nails dipped in oil, water and vinegar — which won't rust?** → The one in **oil**: oil keeps **air and water** off the iron. (Water supplies one ingredient with air above it; vinegar, an acid, makes corrosion even worse.)
5. **Sulfur doesn't react with water. Could it coat iron to stop rust, like zinc?** → **No.** Sulfur is **brittle** — it can't be spread into a thin, firm, lasting layer the way zinc can, so it won't seal the iron off.
6. **Which material for a boiling pan: copper, sulfur, coal, plastic, wood, cardboard?** → **Copper** — strong, malleable to shape, and a good heat conductor; the others are poor conductors (and plastic, wood, cardboard can melt or burn).

## In a nutshell

- Metals are generally **lustrous, hard, malleable, ductile, sonorous**, and **good conductors** of heat and electricity; non-metals generally aren't.
- **Exceptions:** sodium/potassium are soft; mercury is liquid; graphite conducts.
- **Rusting needs both air and water.** Corrosion turns copper green and silver black. Prevent rust by painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising.
- **Metal oxides are basic; non-metal oxides are acidic.**
- Non-metals generally don't react with water. **Sodium is kept in kerosene; phosphorus in water.**
- Only **elements** are metals or non-metals — plastic, glass, wood aren't.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** not malleable ≠ brittle (wood) · rust needs air AND water · copper pans are for **heat** conduction · metal oxide → red litmus blue · plastic/glass are not non-metals.
