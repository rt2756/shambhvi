# 4. Metals & non-metals

<!-- NCERT Class 7 "Curiosity" Chapter 4 — The World of Metals and Non-metals. -->

## The five tests that spot a metal

| Property | What it means | Metals | Non-metals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lustre** | shines when polished (**metallic lustre**) | shiny | dull |
| **Malleability** | can be beaten into **thin sheets** | ✅ | ✗ break — **brittle** |
| **Ductility** | can be drawn into **wires** | ✅ | ✗ |
| **Sonority** | makes a **ringing sound** when struck | ✅ | ✗ dull thud |
| **Conduction** | lets **heat and electricity** pass | good conductors | poor conductors |

- **Malleable:** silver foil on sweets, aluminium foil. **Gold and silver are the most malleable.**
- **Ductile:** copper wires, sitar and guitar strings. **Gold is so ductile that 1 gram makes a 2 km wire.**
- **Sonorous:** the school bell, ghungroos, a dropped coin.

> [!WARNING]
> **Brittle ≠ "not malleable" for everything.** Coal and sulfur **break** (brittle); wood does **neither** — it won't flatten *or* shatter. So "not malleable" doesn't automatically mean "brittle".

## Exceptions worth remembering

- **Not all metals are hard:** **sodium and potassium** are so soft they can be cut with a knife.
- **Not all metals are solid:** **mercury** is a **liquid** at room temperature (thermometers).
- **Carbon is a non-metal, but graphite (pencil lead) conducts electricity** — the odd one out.

> [!NOTE]
> **Plastic, glass, wood, rubber, paper are neither metals nor non-metals** — those two words only sort **elements**, and these materials aren't elements. An **element** can't be broken into simpler substances; **118** are known.

## Why the kitchen and the electrician's kit look like they do

- **Metal vessel + wooden handle:** the metal conducts heat *into the food*, the wood is a poor conductor so the handle stays cool enough to hold.
- **Plastic-covered screwdriver, rubber gloves and shoes:** rubber and plastic are poor conductors of electricity, so the current can't pass into the electrician.

## Rusting — needs air AND water, both

Three-bottle experiment with iron nails:

| Bottle | Set-up | Air? | Water? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | nail + **silica gel** (dries the air) | dry air only | ✗ | **no rust** |
| **B** | nail in **boiled, cooled water** under an **oil layer** | ✗ | water only | **no rust** |
| **C** | nail **partly** dipped, bottle open | ✅ | ✅ | **rust** ✅ |

- **Rust** = the brown deposit on iron; the process is **rusting**.
- **Why the fancy set-up:** silica gel removes moisture from the air, boiling removes dissolved gases from water, and the oil layer stops air redissolving. Each bottle removes **one** ingredient — so whichever bottle rusts tells you what's truly needed. Only C had both, and only C rusted → **moist air causes rusting**.
- **Corrosion** is the general word: the gradual eating away of a metal surface by air, water or other substances — **green** coating on copper, **black** on silver.
- **Preventing rust:** painting, oiling, greasing, **galvanisation** (coating iron with zinc) — all work by keeping air and water off the iron.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Iron Pillar of Delhi** — made over 1600 years ago (time of Chandragupta II), 8 m tall, 6000+ kg — has barely rusted after centuries in the open. Evidence of remarkably advanced ancient Indian metallurgy.

## Metal oxides are basic, non-metal oxides are acidic

This links straight back to Chapter 2's indicators.

- **Magnesium ribbon** burns with a **dazzling white flame** → white powder = **magnesium oxide**. Dissolved in water it turns **red litmus blue** → **basic**.
  - **metal + oxygen → metal oxide → basic**
- **Sulfur** burns → **sulfur dioxide** gas. Dissolved in water → **sulfurous acid**, turns **blue litmus red** → **acidic**.
  - **non-metal + oxygen → non-metal oxide → acidic**

> [!TIP]
> One line to carry: **metal oxides = basic · non-metal oxides = acidic.** It's the fastest way to classify an unknown element — burn it and test the oxide with litmus.

## Storage tells you about reactivity

- **Sodium is stored in kerosene** — it reacts vigorously with air and water, releasing lots of heat. Kerosene keeps both away.
- **Phosphorus is stored in water** — it catches fire in ordinary air. (Note the neat reversal: one metal hides *from* water, one non-metal hides *in* it.)
- **Sulfur does not react with water** at all.
