## A winter evening in Gangtok

**Pema and Palden** sit by a fireplace in **Gangtok**. Palden visited **Kerala** in the winter holidays and found it **warm and humid**. Why so different? Their grandfather explains: **Kerala is closer to the equator and has a long coastline**, which makes it warmer and more humid. And for us on Earth, **the Sun is the main source of heat and light.**

Grandmother is cooking **thukpa** (a Sikkimese dish) in a **metal pan**. *Why are cooking pots metal?* Because metals are **good conductors of heat** (Chapter 4). But **how** does heat actually travel? This chapter shows there are **three ways**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Heat always moves from a HOTTER place to a COLDER place.** It travels in three ways:
>
> | | How heat moves | Do the particles move? | Needs a medium? | Mainly in |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **Conduction** | each particle passes heat to its **neighbour** | **No** — they stay in place | **Yes** | **solids** |
> | **Convection** | the heated **particles themselves travel** | **Yes** | **Yes** | **liquids and gases** |
> | **Radiation** | heat travels **directly** from the hot object | — | **No** | everywhere, even empty space |

## 1. Conduction — heat passed along

**Activity 7.1 — the pin experiment:** a **15 cm** aluminium or iron strip on a stand, with **four pins stuck on with wax**, about **2 cm apart** (labelled I, II, III, IV from the free end). Heat the **free end** with a candle.

- **Pin I** (nearest the flame) **falls first**, then **II**, then **III**, then **IV**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the pins fall one by one:** heat **travels along** the strip from the hot end to the cold end. As it reaches each pin, the wax there melts and the pin drops. If heat appeared everywhere at once, all the pins would fall together — they don't.
>
> **CONDUCTION** = heat transfer from the **hotter part** of an object to the **colder part**. The heated particle passes heat to its **neighbour**, which passes it on — **but the particles do not leave their positions**.
>
> **In solids, heat moves mainly by conduction.**

> [!TIP]
> **Think of a bucket line.** People stand still and pass buckets hand to hand — the bucket travels, the people don't. In conduction the particles are the people and heat is the bucket.

### Good and poor conductors

- **Good conductors** let heat pass easily — **metals** (steel, iron, aluminium, copper). That's why **cooking pots are metal**.
- **Poor conductors (insulators)** don't — **wood, glass, clay, porcelain, plastic, air**. Repeat the pin test with a **wood or glass strip** and **the pins don't fall**.
- **Why tea stays hot longer in a clay (kulhad) or porcelain cup:** those are poor conductors, so heat leaks out slowly.

> [!NOTE]
> A **saucepan** is made of **two materials**: a **metal body** (good conductor — to heat the food) and a **plastic or wooden handle** (poor conductor — so you can hold it).

### Air is a poor conductor — and that's useful

- **Woollen clothes** trap **air** in their pores. Air is a poor conductor, so it **slows the heat leaving your body** — you feel warm. (The wool doesn't *make* heat; it **keeps yours in**.)
- **Layers of clothing** trap air between them — same effect.
- **Two thin blankets are warmer than one thick one** — the **layer of air between them** is an extra insulator.
- **Hollow bricks** trap air inside, so houses built with them stay **warm in winter and cool in summer** — heat can't easily get in *or* out.

> [!NOTE]
> In the **Mori block of Uttarkashi (Uttarakhand)**, high in the Himalayas with heavy winter snow, houses have walls of **two wooden layers with cow dung and mud packed between**. Wood and mud are poor conductors, so the house **holds its heat**.

> [!TIP]
> **The leaky lassi tumbler:** a shopkeeper puts your leaky tumbler inside a second tumbler. Besides catching drips, the **air trapped between the two tumblers** is a poor conductor, so the lassi **stays cold longer**.

## 2. Convection — the hot stuff moves

Pema notices **smoke from the firewood rising**. Why up?

**Activity 7.2:** hang two identical paper cups **upside down** from the ends of a stick, balanced. Put a **candle under one** → **that cup rises**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why hot air rises:** air near the flame heats up, **expands** (takes up more space), becomes **lighter**, and **rises**. Cooler, heavier air sinks to take its place.

- A **partly inflated balloon left in the Sun gets bigger** — the air inside expands as it warms.
- **Smoke from an incense stick (agarbatti) rises** — smoke is **hot gases and tiny solid particles**, **warmer than the air around it**. Even if you point the incense stick **downwards**, the smoke **still curls upwards**.

**Activity 7.3 — convection in water:** a **grain of potassium permanganate** placed (with a straw) at the **centre of the bottom** of a beaker of water, a candle right underneath.

- A **coloured streak rises up the middle**, spreads at the top, and **comes down the sides** — a loop.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why the loop:** water at the bottom-centre is heated → **expands, gets lighter, rises**. Cooler, heavier water at the sides **sinks** to replace it, gets heated, rises in turn… until all the water is hot.
>
> **CONVECTION** = heat transfer by the **actual movement of the particles** of a liquid or gas. **Liquids and gases are heated mainly by convection.**

> [!TIP]
> **Smoke detectors go on the CEILING.** Hot smoke rises, so the ceiling is where it arrives first.

> [!TIP]
> **Heating a test tube at the top vs the bottom.** Heat a test tube of water **near the bottom** and the hot water rises and spreads — the **whole tube warms**, and a thermometer in it reads higher. Heat it **near the top** and the warm water just **stays at the top** (warm water doesn't sink), so the bottom stays cool.

### Land breeze and sea breeze

**Activity 7.4:** two identical bowls in the sun — one with **soil**, one with **water** — each with a thermometer (bulb **in** the soil/water, **not touching** the bowl). Read them every **5 minutes**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Soil heats up FASTER than water — and also COOLS DOWN faster.** (Bring both indoors for 20 minutes and the soil cools first.)

| | **Day** | **Night** |
|---|---|---|
| Which is warmer? | **land** (heats faster) | **sea** (land has cooled faster) |
| Warm air rises over… | the **land** | the **sea** |
| Cooler air flows… | **from sea to land** | **from land to sea** |
| Name | **SEA BREEZE** | **LAND BREEZE** |

- **Why coastal houses have windows facing the sea:** to catch the cool **sea breeze** that brings relief on hot days.
- People at the seashore feel the **wind direction reverse between day and night**.

> [!TIP]
> **A breeze is named after where it comes FROM.** Sea breeze blows *from* the sea; land breeze blows *from* the land.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why large water bodies stop extreme temperatures:** water heats up and cools down **slowly**, so the air near a sea or big lake never gets as hot by day or as cold by night as places far inland — and the breezes carry that mild air over the land.

## 3. Radiation — no medium needed

Sitting near the fireplace, Pema and Palden feel warm on their faces — though nothing hot is touching them.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **RADIATION** = heat travelling **directly from the hot object to us**, with **no material medium needed**.
>
> - **The Sun's heat reaches the Earth by radiation** — across empty space, where there are no particles to conduct or convect.
> - **All objects radiate heat.** A hot utensil taken off the flame cools down by **radiating heat** to its surroundings.

> [!TIP]
> **Light clothes in summer, dark clothes in winter.** **Light-coloured** surfaces **reflect** most of the heat falling on them; **dark** surfaces **absorb** more.

## All three at once

**Heating water in a pan on a flame:**

- **Flame → pan:** **conduction** (through the metal).
- **Through the water:** **convection** (hot water rises, cool water sinks).
- **The warmth you feel standing nearby:** **radiation**.

> [!NOTE]
> The Himalayan **bukhari** is an **iron stove** that burns wood or charcoal, with a **long chimney pipe** for the smoke and a **flat top for cooking**. It uses **all three** processes to cook and warm a room together.

> [!NOTE]
> **Ice cubes melting in a dish** get their heat from the **surrounding air** (by convection and conduction through the dish) and by **radiation** from everything around them.

## The water cycle — powered by the Sun's heat

**Wet clothes dry faster on a sunny day** because the Sun's heat **speeds up evaporation**. The same heat drives water around the whole planet.

Water exists in **three states** in nature: **liquid** (oceans, rivers, lakes), **solid** (snow, ice sheets, glaciers in mountains and polar regions) and **gas** (water vapour in the air).

1. **Melting** — in summer, the Sun melts some **snow and ice**; it flows down as **rivers** to the oceans. Fresh **snow in winter** replaces it.
2. **Evaporation** — the Sun heats **oceans, rivers and lakes**; water becomes **water vapour**.
3. **Transpiration** — water also evaporates from **trees and plants**.
4. **Condensation** — rising vapour **cools** and **condenses** into **clouds**.
5. **Precipitation** — clouds bring **rain, snow and hail**.
6. Water flows into **ponds, lakes, rivers and oceans**, or **seeps into the ground**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The WATER CYCLE** = the continuous movement of water — **up as vapour**, **down as precipitation**, through **soil, rocks and plants**, and back to water bodies.
>
> **Why it matters:** it **redistributes and replenishes** water in rivers, lakes and oceans, and **conserves the total amount of water on Earth** — water is moved around, never used up.

> [!NOTE]
> **Varahamihira** — astronomer and mathematician of the **sixth century CE** in **Ujjaini (modern Ujjain), Madhya Pradesh** — gave methods for **predicting seasonal rainfall** in his ***Brihatsamhita***, using **cloud formation, wind patterns, and the positions of stars and the Moon**.

## How rain becomes groundwater

**Activity 7.5:** three cut, upside-down bottles (holes in the caps) filled with **clay**, **sand** and **gravel**. Pour **200 mL** of water into each and collect what comes out in **10 minutes**.

| Bottle | Water comes through… |
|---|---|
| **Gravel** | **fastest** |
| **Sand** | slower |
| **Clay** | **slowest** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why:** the **spaces between gravel pieces are the widest**, so water slips through easily. Sand's spaces are smaller; clay's are tiniest. Water moves fastest where the spaces are **wide, open and connected**.
>
> - **INFILTRATION** = surface water **seeping** through soil and rock.
> - **GROUNDWATER** = water stored in the **pore spaces** of sediments and the **openings in rocks** underground.
> - **AQUIFERS** = underground **layers of sediment and rock that store water** in their pores. **Wells and bore wells** are dug into aquifers — a few metres to hundreds of metres down.

> [!WARNING]
> **Groundwater is NOT unlimited.**
> - A **growing population** pumps out **too much**.
> - **Fewer plants** and **more concrete** in cities let **less rain soak in**.
>
> So groundwater is **getting depleted**. **Rainwater harvesting** and **recharge pits** help refill it.

> [!NOTE]
> **Ice stupas of Ladakh.** In **spring**, streams dry up because the Sun isn't yet strong enough to melt mountain snow. So in **winter**, stream water is piped down and **sprayed into the freezing air**; it **freezes as it falls**, building a tall **cone of ice — an ice stupa**. In spring it **melts slowly**, giving water for farming through the summer.

## Think-it-through questions

1. **Saucepan of materials A (body) and B (handle).** → A is a **good** conductor, B a **poor** conductor.
2. **Candle under the middle of a pin strip (pins I, II on one side, III, IV further).** → Pins **nearest the flame fall first**; pins at equal distances fall **at about the same time**.
3. **True or false: heat moves through solids by convection.** → **False** — solids conduct; their particles can't flow.
4. **True or false: clay allows more seepage than sand.** → **False** — clay's spaces are tiny; sand lets water through faster.
5. **True or false: cooler air moving from land to sea is a land breeze.** → **True.**
6. **Paper wrapped tightly round a metal rod, held in a candle flame while turning — does it burn?** → **No (or very slowly).** The metal **conducts the heat away** from the paper, so the paper doesn't reach its **ignition temperature**.
7. **A paper spiral hung above a candle.** → It **spins** — rising hot air (convection) pushes on it.

## In a nutshell

- Heat moves **hot → cold** by **conduction, convection, radiation**.
- **Conduction:** particles pass heat along, staying in place — mainly **solids**. Metals are good conductors; wood, glass, clay, air are poor.
- **Convection:** heated particles **move** — **liquids and gases**. Land and sea breezes are convection.
- **Radiation:** **no medium needed** — how the Sun heats Earth. All objects radiate.
- Conduction and convection **need a medium**; radiation doesn't.
- Heat-transfer ideas are used in **houses and clothing**.
- **Water cycle:** evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation; **infiltration** fills **aquifers** with **groundwater**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** conduction particles don't move · wool keeps heat **in**, doesn't make it · soil heats AND cools faster than water · breezes are named by where they come **from** · smoke rises even from a downward-pointing stick · groundwater can run out.
