# 7. Heat transfer in nature

<!-- NCERT Class 7 "Curiosity" Chapter 7 — Heat Transfer in Nature. -->

## Three ways heat moves

| | How it moves | Do particles move? | Medium needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conduction** | particle passes heat to its neighbour | **No** — they stay put | **Yes** (mainly solids) |
| **Convection** | the hot particles themselves travel | **Yes** | **Yes** (liquids & gases) |
| **Radiation** | heat travels directly, on its own | — | **No** |

Heat always moves from the **hotter part to the colder part**.

## Conduction — the relay race

**The pin experiment:** four pins are stuck along a metal strip with wax, and one end is heated.

- The pin **nearest the flame falls first**, then the next, then the next.
- **Why in order:** heat travels *along* the strip. As it reaches each pin, the wax there melts and the pin drops. Falling one by one is the proof that heat **moves gradually through the material**, rather than appearing everywhere at once.
- **How it works:** the particle that gets heated passes the heat to its **neighbour**, which passes it on — but **the particles do not move from their positions**. Only the heat travels.
- Do the same with **wood or glass** and the pins **don't fall** — poor conductors.

**Good conductors:** metals (so cooking utensils are metal).
**Poor conductors (insulators):** wood, glass, **clay and porcelain** — which is why tea in a clay or porcelain cup stays hot longer.

### Air is a poor conductor — and that's useful

- **Woollen clothes** trap air in their pores; trapped air blocks heat escaping from the body, so we feel warm.
- **Two thin blankets beat one thick blanket** — the **layer of air between them** is an extra insulator.
- **Hollow bricks** keep houses warm in winter and cool in summer, because of the **air trapped inside them**.

> [!NOTE]
> In the Mori block of Uttarkashi (Uttarakhand), houses have walls of **two wooden layers filled with cow dung and mud** — wood and mud are poor conductors, so the heat can't escape.

## Convection — the hot stuff rises

**Why hot air rises:** when air is heated it **expands**, taking up more space, which makes it **lighter** — so it rises. Cooler, heavier air sinks to take its place.

- **Paper-cup experiment:** a candle under one inverted cup makes that cup **rise** — the heated air below it is going up.
- Same reason a **partially inflated balloon grows in the Sun**, and why **smoke from an incense stick rises** (smoke is hot gas + tiny particles, warmer than the air around it).

**In liquids — the potassium permanganate experiment:** a coloured grain at the bottom-centre of a beaker of water, heated from below.

- The colour **rises up the middle and comes down the sides** — a visible loop.
- **Why:** water at the bottom heats, expands, becomes lighter and rises; cooler heavier water at the sides sinks to replace it, gets heated in turn, and rises. The cycle repeats until all the water is hot.
- Heat moves by the **actual movement of the particles** — that's convection.

### Land breeze & sea breeze

**The key fact:** **soil heats faster than water — and cools faster too.** (Shown by putting thermometers in a bowl of soil and a bowl of water in the sun.)

- **Day → sea breeze:** land heats faster, warm air above the land rises, and **cooler air moves in from the sea to the land**. This is why coastal houses have windows facing the sea.
- **Night → land breeze:** land cools faster, so now the air above the **sea** is warmer and rises, and **cooler air moves from the land out to sea**.

> [!TIP]
> Name the breeze by **where it comes FROM**: sea breeze blows *from the sea*, land breeze *from the land*. The wind direction reverses between day and night.

## Radiation — no medium needed

- Heat travels **directly from the hot object to us**, with **no material medium** required.
- That's how **the Sun's heat reaches the Earth** across empty space, and how you feel warmth sitting near a fire.
- **All objects radiate heat** — a hot utensil left off the flame cools down by radiating heat to its surroundings.

> [!TIP]
> **Light clothes in summer, dark clothes in winter.** Light colours **reflect** most of the heat falling on them; dark surfaces **absorb** more.

## All three together

Heating a pan of water on a flame uses every process at once:

- **Flame → pan: conduction.**
- **Through the water: convection.**
- **The warmth you feel standing near it: radiation.**

> [!NOTE]
> The Himalayan **bukhari** — an iron stove burning wood or charcoal, with a chimney pipe and a flat top for cooking — uses all three processes to warm a room and cook at the same time.

## The water cycle

The Sun is the Earth's main source of heat, and it drives the whole cycle:

1. **Evaporation** — the Sun heats oceans, rivers and lakes; water becomes **water vapour**. (Wet clothes dry faster on a sunny day for the same reason.)
2. **Transpiration** — water also evaporates from **trees and plants**.
3. **Condensation** — rising vapour **cools** higher up and condenses into **clouds**.
4. **Precipitation** — clouds bring **rain, snow and hail** back down.
5. The water flows into ponds, lakes, rivers and oceans, or **seeps into the ground** (which is what wells and handpumps draw on).

**Why it matters:** the cycle **redistributes and replenishes** water — and **conserves the total amount of water on Earth**. Snow and ice melted by the Sun in summer flow down as rivers, and are replenished by fresh snow in winter.

> [!NOTE]
> **Varahamihira**, a sixth-century CE astronomer and mathematician of Ujjaini (modern Ujjain), gave methods for **predicting seasonal rainfall** in his *Brihatsamhita*, using cloud formation, wind patterns and the positions of stars and the Moon.
