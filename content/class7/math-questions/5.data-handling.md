<!-- ===== Tally & frequency ===== -->

Show the number **7** in tally marks, and explain the grouping.

> [!ANSWER]
> **卌 ‖** — one full group of 5 (four lines with the fifth crossing them) plus 2 singles.
>
> **Why group in 5s:** you count finished bundles (5, 10, 15…) instead of every stroke — much faster and harder to miscount than 7 loose lines.

---

The marks scored by 10 students in a 5-mark quiz are: **3, 4, 3, 5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 3, 4**. Make a frequency table.

> [!ANSWER]
> Count how often each mark occurs — that count is its **frequency**:
>
> | Marks | Tally | Frequency |
> |---|---|---|
> | 2 | I | 1 |
> | 3 | IIII | 4 |
> | 4 | III | 3 |
> | 5 | II | 2 |
>
> **Check:** frequencies must add back to the number of students — `1 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 10` ✓

<!-- ===== Pictograph ===== -->

In a pictograph, **1 apple symbol = 10 apples**. A row shows **3½ symbols**. How many apples is that?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each full symbol is worth 10 (that's what the **key** says), and the half symbol is worth half of 10 = 5:
>
> `3 × 10 + 5 = ` **35 apples**.
>
> Without reading the key first, the symbols mean nothing — the key is what turns pictures into numbers.

---

A shop sold **45 fans**. With a key of **1 symbol = 10 fans**, how would you draw this row?

> [!ANSWER]
> `45 = 40 + 5` → **4 full symbols + 1 half symbol**.
>
> The half symbol exists exactly for this: 45 isn't a whole number of tens, and 4 or 5 full symbols would both lie (40 or 50).

<!-- ===== Bar graph ===== -->

On a bar graph the scale says **1 division = 5 books**. The "Friday" bar is **7 divisions** tall. How many books is that?

> [!ANSWER]
> Match the top of the bar to the scale: `7 × 5 = ` **35 books**.
>
> The **height carries the value** — that's why all bars must have **equal width** and equal gaps: only height should differ, so your eye compares fairly.

---

When would you use a **double bar graph** instead of an ordinary one?

> [!ANSWER]
> When comparing **two sets of data side by side** for the same items — e.g. marks in **two different years**, or boys vs girls per class.
>
> Two bars stand together per item, so each pair can be compared at a glance — two separate graphs would make your eye jump back and forth.

<!-- ===== Averages — mean, median, mode, range ===== -->

Find the **mean** of **4, 6, 8, 10, 12**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Mean = **sum ÷ how many** = `(4 + 6 + 8 + 10 + 12) ÷ 5 = 40 ÷ 5 = ` **8**.
>
> **Why it works:** the mean is the "fair share" — if the 5 values pooled everything and split equally, each would get 8.

---

Find the **mean** of the first five **odd numbers**.

> [!ANSWER]
> First five odd numbers: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. Sum = 25 → mean = `25 ÷ 5 = ` **5**.
>
> Notice: 5 is also the **middle** value — for an evenly spaced list, mean = median.

---

Find the **median** of **12, 5, 9, 3, 7**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Arrange in order first** — median means the middle *of the ordered line-up*:
>
> `3, 5, 7, 9, 12` → 5 values → the 3rd is the middle → median = **7**.
>
> Skipping the sorting step is *the* classic error: the middle of the jumbled list (9) would be wrong.

---

Find the **median** of **2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12** (six values).

> [!ANSWER]
> Even count → **no single middle** — two values (6 and 8) share the centre.
>
> Take their mean: `(6 + 8) ÷ 2 = ` **7**.
>
> 7 is fair: exactly 3 values sit below it and 3 above it — which is the whole job of a median.

---

The shoe sizes sold in a day: **5, 6, 6, 7, 6, 8, 5, 6**. Find the **mode**. Why does the shopkeeper care?

> [!ANSWER]
> Count: size 5 twice, **size 6 four times**, 7 once, 8 once → mode = **6**.
>
> The mode is the **most frequent** value — the shopkeeper should stock the most of size 6, because it's what sells most often. (Mean or median "size 6.1" wouldn't help — nobody wears size 6.1.)

---

Find the **range** of the temperatures **21°, 27°, 19°, 24°, 30°**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Range = largest − smallest = `30 − 19 = ` **11°**.
>
> Range measures the **spread** — how far apart the extremes are — not the centre.

---

Five friends score **10, 12, 14, 12, 12** in a game. A sixth friend scores **100**. What happens to the **mean** and the **median**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Before:** mean = `60 ÷ 5 = 12`, median (ordered `10, 12, 12, 12, 14`) = 12.
>
> **After adding 100:** mean = `160 ÷ 6 ≈ 26.7` — it **more than doubles**. Median (ordered `10, 12, 12, 12, 14, 100`) = `(12 + 12) ÷ 2 = ` **12 — unchanged**.
>
> **Why:** the mean adds every value into its sum, so one giant value drags it hard. The median only cares about the middle *position* — 100 just stands at the end of the line. That's the notes' tip: one extreme value pulls the **mean** a lot; the **median** stays a steadier "middle".

<!-- ===== Chance & probability ===== -->

A fair coin is tossed. What is **P(head)**, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> 2 outcomes (head, tail), both **equally likely**, 1 of them is favourable:
>
> P(head) = favourable ÷ total = **1/2**.
>
> The "equally likely" part matters — the formula only counts outcomes fairly if each has the same chance.

---

A die is rolled once. Find **P(3)** and **P(even number)**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Total outcomes: 6 (the faces 1–6), all equally likely.
>
> P(3) = `1/6` (one favourable face).
>
> P(even) = favourable are 2, 4, 6 → `3/6 = ` **1/2**.

---

A die is rolled. What is **P(a number less than 7)**? And **P(7)**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Every face (1–6) is less than 7 → `6/6 = ` **1** — a **certain** event.
>
> No face shows 7 → `0/6 = ` **0** — an **impossible** event.
>
> This is the 0-to-1 scale in action: probability can never leave it, because favourable outcomes can't be fewer than none or more than all.

---

A bag holds **3 red and 2 blue** marbles. One is drawn without looking. Find **P(red)**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Total = `3 + 2 = 5` marbles, each equally likely to be picked.
>
> P(red) = `3/5`.
>
> Check with the sum rule: P(blue) = `2/5`, and `3/5 + 2/5 = 1` ✓ — one of the two colours *must* come out.

---

A spinner is divided into **4 equal sectors**: red, blue, green, yellow. What is **P(green)**? Would the answer change if the sectors were unequal?

> [!ANSWER]
> Equal sectors → 4 equally likely outcomes → P(green) = **1/4**.
>
> **Yes, it would change.** With unequal sectors the outcomes are no longer equally likely, so "favourable ÷ total" can't be used on the *count* of sectors — a bigger green sector means green comes up more often than 1 in 4.

---

The weather report says rain is *possible* tomorrow. Place these on the probability scale: **the sun rising**, **rolling a 7 on a die**, **getting a head on a coin toss**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sun rising → 1** (certain) · **rolling 7 → 0** (impossible — a die has no 7) · **head → 1/2** (equally likely either way).
>
> Everything else in life sits somewhere between 0 and 1 — that in-between zone is what "chance" means.
