<!-- ===== Meaning ===== -->

Write **3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3** as a power and evaluate it. Name the base and the exponent.

> [!ANSWER]
> Five copies of 3 multiplied → **3⁵** — base **3** (the number repeated), exponent **5** (how many copies).
>
> `3⁵ = 9 × 9 × 3 = 81 × 3 = ` **243**.

---

Evaluate **2⁴**. Why is it *not* 8?

> [!ANSWER]
> `2⁴ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = ` **16**.
>
> ⚠️ `2 × 4 = 8` is the trap — the exponent **counts copies to multiply**, it isn't a number to multiply *by*.

---

What is **10⁵**? What does the exponent tell you about the zeros?

> [!ANSWER]
> `10⁵ = ` **100,000** — the power **counts the zeros** (5 of them).
>
> Why: each extra ×10 tacks one more zero on; five tens = five zeros.

---

Evaluate **100⁰** and **7¹**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `100⁰ = ` **1** and `7¹ = ` **7**.
>
> **Why a⁰ = 1:** walk the powers downwards — `10³ = 1000 → 10² = 100 → 10¹ = 10`, each step **÷10**. One more step: `10⁰ = 10 ÷ 10 = 1`. The pattern forces it (and the same walk works for any base).

---

Evaluate **(−2)⁴** and **(−2)³**. Why do the signs differ?

> [!ANSWER]
> `(−2)⁴ = ` **+16** · `(−2)³ = ` **−8**.
>
> **Why:** minuses cancel **in pairs** (each − × − = +). An **even** count of minuses pairs up completely → positive; an **odd** count leaves one unpaired minus → negative.

---

What is **(−1)⁹⁷**?

> [!ANSWER]
> 97 is **odd** → one minus left unpaired → **−1**.
>
> (No multiplying needed — with base −1 the size is always 1; only the sign question remains, and odd/even answers it.)

---

Which is greater: **2³ or 3²**?

> [!ANSWER]
> `2³ = 8` and `3² = 9` → **3² is greater**.
>
> Swapping base and exponent usually changes the value — always compute both, never assume symmetry.

---

<!-- ===== Laws of exponents ===== -->

Simplify **2³ × 2⁴** using a law, and explain why the law works.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Same base, multiplying → add the powers:** `2³ × 2⁴ = 2³⁺⁴ = 2⁷ = ` **128**.
>
> **Why:** `(2 × 2 × 2) × (2 × 2 × 2 × 2)` is just **7 copies of 2** in a row — 3 copies then 4 more. Adding the exponents counts the total copies.
>
> ⚠️ The base stays 2 — it does **not** become 4.

---

Simplify **5⁶ ÷ 5⁴**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Same base, dividing → subtract the powers:** `5⁶⁻⁴ = 5² = ` **25**.
>
> **Why:** six 5s on top, four 5s below — four pairs cancel, leaving `6 − 4 = 2` copies on top.

---

Simplify **(3²)⁴**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Power of a power → multiply:** `3²ˣ⁴ = 3⁸`.
>
> **Why:** (3²)⁴ means four copies of 3² — that's `2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 2 × 4 = 8` threes in all.
>
> `3⁸ = 6561` (= 81 × 81).

---

Simplify **2³ × 5³**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Same power, different bases → multiply the bases:** `2³ × 5³ = (2 × 5)³ = 10³ = ` **1000**.
>
> **Why:** `(2×2×2) × (5×5×5)` can be re-paired as `(2×5) × (2×5) × (2×5)` — three 10s. Multiplication lets you shuffle the order freely.

---

Simplify **6⁴ ÷ 3⁴**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Same power → divide the bases:** `(6 ÷ 3)⁴ = 2⁴ = ` **16**.
>
> Each 6 on top pairs with a 3 below, and every `6/3` pair equals 2 — four pairs → 2⁴.

---

Simplify **(2³ × 2⁵) ÷ 2⁶**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Top first: `2³ × 2⁵ = 2⁸` (add). Then divide: `2⁸ ÷ 2⁶ = 2² = ` **4** (subtract).
>
> Chaining the laws: add for ×, subtract for ÷ — always tracking the count of 2s.

---

Express **72** as a product of powers of primes.

> [!ANSWER]
> Factor tree: `72 = 8 × 9 = 2³ × 3²`.
>
> **72 = 2³ × 3²**. Check: `8 × 9 = 72` ✓

---

Express **360** as a product of powers of primes.

> [!ANSWER]
> `360 = 36 × 10 = (2² × 3²) × (2 × 5) = ` **2³ × 3² × 5**.
>
> Check: `8 × 9 × 5 = 360` ✓

---

Which is greater: **2¹⁰ or 10³**?

> [!ANSWER]
> `2¹⁰ = 1024` and `10³ = 1000` → **2¹⁰ wins** (just!).
>
> Small base, big exponent can beat big base, small exponent — repeated doubling grows shockingly fast. (This 1024 ≈ 1000 near-miss is why computer "kilo" is 1024.)

---

<!-- ===== Expanded form with powers of 10 ===== -->

Write **47,561** in expanded form using powers of 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each place is the next power of 10, ones place = 10⁰:
>
> `47561 = 4 × 10⁴ + 7 × 10³ + 5 × 10² + 6 × 10¹ + 1 × 10⁰`
>
> The exponent just names the place: 10⁴ = ten-thousands, … 10⁰ = ones.

---

Write **30,208** in expanded form. What happens to the empty places?

> [!ANSWER]
> `30208 = 3 × 10⁴ + 2 × 10² + 8 × 10⁰`
>
> The thousands and tens places hold **0**, and `0 × 10³` and `0 × 10¹` add nothing — so those terms simply drop out. The zeros in the numeral are exactly the missing terms.

---

<!-- ===== Standard form ===== -->

Write **8500** in standard form.

> [!ANSWER]
> Put the decimal point just after the **first digit**: 8.5. The point moved **3 places** → power = 10³:
>
> `8500 = ` **8.5 × 10³**
>
> Rule check: 8.5 is between 1 and 10 ✓ — that's what makes it *standard*, so every number has exactly one such form.

---

Write **597,000,000** in standard form.

> [!ANSWER]
> Point after the first digit: 5.97 (drop the trailing zeros). It moved **8 places**:
>
> **5.97 × 10⁸**

---

The distance from the Earth to the Sun is about **150,000,000 km**. Standard form?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.5 × 10⁸ km** — point after the 1, moved 8 places.
>
> This is why standard form exists: 1.5 × 10⁸ is instantly readable and comparable; counting zeros in 150000000 is not.

---

Write **3.02 × 10⁶** as an ordinary number.

> [!ANSWER]
> ×10⁶ slides the point **6 places right**, filling with zeros:
>
> `3.02 × 10⁶ = ` **3,020,000**.

---

Which is larger: **9.9 × 10⁵ or 1.1 × 10⁶**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Compare the powers first:** 10⁶ beats 10⁵, so **1.1 × 10⁶** (= 1,100,000) is larger than 9.9 × 10⁵ (= 990,000).
>
> **Why the power wins:** the front number is always between 1 and 10, so it can never make up a whole missing power of 10 — a higher exponent means a longer number, full stop.
