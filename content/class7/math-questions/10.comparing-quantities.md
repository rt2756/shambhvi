<!-- ===== Conversions ===== -->

Convert **3/4** to a percentage.

> [!ANSWER]
> Fraction → %: multiply by 100 → `3/4 × 100% = ` **75%**.
>
> **Why ×100:** % means "out of 100", so the question is really "3/4 of 100 is how many?" — 75.

---

Convert **0.35** to a percentage, and **8%** to a decimal.

> [!ANSWER]
> Decimal → %: ×100 → `0.35 = ` **35%**.
>
> % → decimal: ÷100 → `8% = 8/100 = ` **0.08** (⚠️ not 0.8 — the point slides **two** places, one per zero of 100).

---

Convert **20%** to a fraction in simplest form. And **45%**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Put it over 100, then simplify:
>
> `20% = 20/100 = ` **1/5** (÷20 top and bottom).
>
> `45% = 45/100 = ` **9/20** (÷5).

---

Two friends split a prize in the ratio **3 : 1**. What percentage does each get?

> [!ANSWER]
> Total parts = `3 + 1 = 4`. Each share ÷ total parts × 100:
>
> `3/4 × 100% = ` **75%** and `1/4 × 100% = ` **25%**.
>
> Check: `75 + 25 = 100%` ✓ — all the parts of a whole always rebuild 100%.

---

Convert **1/8** to a percentage.

> [!ANSWER]
> `1/8 × 100% = 100/8 % = ` **12.5%**.
>
> Handy anchor: 1/8 is half of 1/4 — and 12.5 is half of 25. Halving known percentages extends the memorised list.

---

Which is bigger: **3/5 or 65%**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Put both in the same language: `3/5 = 60%`.
>
> `60% < 65%` → **65% is bigger**. (Converting to a common form is the only fair comparison — same trick as common denominators.)

---

<!-- ===== Using percentages ===== -->

Find **15% of 200**.

> [!ANSWER]
> "of" means ×: `15/100 × 200 = 15 × 2 = ` **30**.
>
> Why it collapses so nicely: 200 is 2 hundreds, and 15% takes 15 *per hundred* — 15 twice.

---

Find **24% of 350**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `24/100 × 350 = 24 × 3.5 = ` **84**.
>
> (Or split it: 10% = 35, so 20% = 70; 1% = 3.5, so 4% = 14; `70 + 14 = 84` — the 10%-and-1% blocks method.)

---

Shambhvi scored **34 out of 40**. What percentage is that?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A is what % of B** → `A/B × 100%`:
>
> `34/40 × 100% = 85%` → **85%**.
>
> The ÷40 rescales the marks to "out of 100" — that's all a percentage is.

---

A price rises from **₹40 to ₹50**. Find the percentage increase.

> [!ANSWER]
> **% change = change ÷ original × 100%**. Change = `50 − 40 = 10`, original = **40**:
>
> `10/40 × 100% = ` **25% increase**.
>
> ⚠️ Dividing by the new price 50 (giving 20%) is the classic trap — the **base is always the original** value, the one the change happened *to*.

---

A price falls from **₹60 to ₹45**. Find the percentage decrease.

> [!ANSWER]
> Change = `60 − 45 = 15`, original = 60:
>
> `15/60 × 100% = ` **25% decrease**.

---

A town's population grows from **8000 to 8400**. What is the percentage growth?

> [!ANSWER]
> Change = 400, original = 8000: `400/8000 × 100% = ` **5%**.

---

<!-- ===== Profit & loss ===== -->

A trader buys a bag for **₹250** and sells it for **₹325**. Find the profit and the profit %.

> [!ANSWER]
> SP > CP → profit = `325 − 250 = ₹75`.
>
> **Profit% = profit/CP × 100** = `75/250 × 100 = ` **30%**.
>
> ⚠️ Always over the **CP** — profit is measured against what the trader *put in*, not what came back.

---

A cycle bought for **₹600** is sold for **₹510**. Find the loss %.

> [!ANSWER]
> SP < CP → loss = `600 − 510 = ₹90`.
>
> Loss% = `90/600 × 100 = ` **15%**.

---

A shopkeeper buys a lamp for **₹800** and wants a **15% profit**. At what price should she sell it?

> [!ANSWER]
> Profit = 15% of CP = `15/100 × 800 = ₹120`.
>
> SP = CP + profit = `800 + 120 = ` **₹920**.

---

A toy is sold for **₹540** at a **loss of 10%**. Find the cost price.

> [!ANSWER]
> Loss of 10% means SP is `100 − 10 = 90%` **of the CP**:
>
> `90/100 × CP = 540` → `CP = 540 × 100/90 = ` **₹600**.
>
> Check: 10% of 600 = 60; `600 − 60 = 540` ✓
>
> ⚠️ Taking 10% of the *SP* (`540 + 54 = 594`) is the trap — the loss was 10% of the **CP**, which we didn't know yet; that's why we solve backwards.

---

Bought at **₹450**, sold at **₹450**. Profit, loss, or neither?

> [!ANSWER]
> SP = CP → **neither** — profit = `450 − 450 = ₹0`, so profit% = 0%.
>
> This break-even point is the boundary the profit/loss definitions are built around: above it profit, below it loss.

---

<!-- ===== Simple interest ===== -->

Find the simple interest on **₹5000** at **8% per year** for **2 years**, and the total amount.

> [!ANSWER]
> **SI = P × R × T ÷ 100** = `5000 × 8 × 2 ÷ 100 = ` **₹800**.
>
> Amount = P + SI = `5000 + 800 = ` **₹5800**.
>
> **Why the formula:** 8% of 5000 is the interest for *one* year (`400`); T years just collects it T times — `400 × 2 = 800`.

---

Find the SI on **₹2400** at **5% per year** for **3 years**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `SI = 2400 × 5 × 3 ÷ 100 = 2400 × 15 ÷ 100 = ` **₹360**.

---

Find the SI on **₹6000** at **10% per year** for **6 months**.

> [!ANSWER]
> ⚠️ **T must be in years:** 6 months = `1/2` year.
>
> `SI = 6000 × 10 × 1/2 ÷ 100 = 600 × 1/2 = ` **₹300**.
>
> Using T = 6 would charge for six *years* — ₹3600, twelve times too much. The formula's R is *per year*, so T must speak the same language.

---

₹4000 grows by **₹480 interest in 2 years**. What is the rate per year?

> [!ANSWER]
> Fill the formula and solve for R: `480 = 4000 × R × 2 ÷ 100` → `480 = 80 × R` → `R = ` **6% per year**.
>
> Check: `4000 × 6 × 2 ÷ 100 = 480` ✓

---

In how many years does **₹3000** at **8% per year** earn **₹720** simple interest?

> [!ANSWER]
> `720 = 3000 × 8 × T ÷ 100` → `720 = 240 × T` → `T = ` **3 years**.
>
> Sense-check: one year earns `3000 × 8 ÷ 100 = ₹240`, and `720 ÷ 240 = 3` ✓ — SI just stacks the same yearly interest.

---

Which earns more: ₹2000 at **6% for 3 years**, or ₹2000 at **9% for 2 years**?

> [!ANSWER]
> First: `2000 × 6 × 3 ÷ 100 = ₹360`. Second: `2000 × 9 × 2 ÷ 100 = ₹360`.
>
> **They're equal!** SI depends on the **product** R × T (here `6 × 3 = 9 × 2 = 18`) — swap rate for time and the interest doesn't notice.
