<!-- ===== Angle sum property ===== -->

Two angles of a triangle are **65° and 45°**. Find the third.

> [!ANSWER]
> The three angles of any triangle add to **180°**:
>
> `180° − (65° + 45°) = 180° − 110° = ` **70°**.

---

A right triangle has one angle of **30°**. Find the third angle.

> [!ANSWER]
> "Right triangle" already gives one angle: 90°.
>
> `180° − (90° + 30°) = ` **60°**.
>
> Quick rule hiding here: the two non-right angles of a right triangle always add to `180° − 90° = 90°` — they are complementary.

---

The angles of a triangle are in the ratio **1 : 2 : 3**. Find them. What type of triangle is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> Split 180° into `1 + 2 + 3 = 6` parts → 1 part = `180° ÷ 6 = 30°`.
>
> Angles = **30°, 60°, 90°**. Check: sum = 180° ✓
>
> One angle is 90° → a **right triangle** (and scalene, since all angles differ, all sides differ too).

---

Can a triangle have **two right angles**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Two right angles already use up `90° + 90° = 180°` — the entire budget — leaving **0°** for the third angle, which is impossible.
>
> Same reasoning shows a triangle can have at most one obtuse angle.

---

Can **80°, 60° and 50°** be the angles of a triangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> `80 + 60 + 50 = 190° ≠ 180°` → **no**.
>
> The 180° total isn't a suggestion — every triangle hits it exactly, so a 190° set can't close up into a triangle.

---

Each angle of an **equilateral triangle** is 60°. Why exactly 60°?

> [!ANSWER]
> Equilateral → all sides equal → all angles equal (equal sides face equal angles).
>
> Three equal angles sharing 180° → each = `180° ÷ 3 = ` **60°**. No other value is possible.

---

An **isosceles** triangle has vertex angle **40°**. Find the base angles.

> [!ANSWER]
> The 2 base angles (opposite the equal sides) are **equal** — call each x:
>
> `40° + x + x = 180°` → `2x = 140°` → x = **70° each**. Check: `40 + 70 + 70 = 180` ✓

---

An isosceles triangle has a **base angle** of **65°**. Find the vertex angle.

> [!ANSWER]
> Base angles are equal → both are 65°:
>
> `180° − (65° + 65°) = 180° − 130° = ` **50°**.

---

<!-- ===== Exterior angle property ===== -->

The interior opposite angles of a triangle are **60° and 70°**. Find the exterior angle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Exterior angle = sum of the two interior opposite angles** = `60° + 70° = ` **130°**.
>
> **Why it works:** the exterior angle and the third interior angle make a straight line (180°), and the three interior angles also total 180°. Both equal `180° − third angle` — so the exterior angle must equal the other two combined.

---

An exterior angle of a triangle is **130°**, and one interior opposite angle is **60°**. Find the other.

> [!ANSWER]
> `130° − 60° = ` **70°** — just the exterior-angle rule run backwards.

---

An exterior angle measures **110°**, and the two interior opposite angles are **equal**. Find them.

> [!ANSWER]
> Two equal angles summing to 110° → each = `110° ÷ 2 = ` **55°**.

---

An exterior angle of a triangle is **100°**. Can one of its interior opposite angles be **100°**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The two interior opposite angles must **add** to 100°, so each must be **less than 100°** (the other would need to be 0°).
>
> The exterior angle is always bigger than either interior opposite angle alone — a useful sanity check.

---

<!-- ===== Median & altitude ===== -->

What is the difference between a **median** and an **altitude** of a triangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Median:** vertex → **midpoint** of the opposite side; it cuts that side in half. (**M**edian → **M**idpoint.)
>
> **Altitude:** vertex → opposite side at **90°**; it's the height. (**A**ltitude → right **A**ngle.)
>
> A triangle has **3 of each**. They're different jobs: the median balances *length*, the altitude measures *height* — only in special triangles (like equilateral) do they coincide.

---

In which type of triangle can an **altitude fall outside** the triangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> An **obtuse triangle**. From a vertex next to the obtuse angle, the perpendicular to the opposite side lands beyond the side's end — the side has to be *extended* to meet it, so the altitude hangs outside.
>
> (Medians never do this — a midpoint is always on the side itself, so all 3 medians stay inside every triangle.)

<!-- ===== Triangle inequality ===== -->

Can sides **4 cm, 5 cm, 6 cm** form a triangle? Can **2 cm, 3 cm, 6 cm**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Quick check: add the **two smallest** — the sum must beat the longest side.
>
> **4, 5, 6:** `4 + 5 = 9 > 6` → ✓ **yes**.
>
> **2, 3, 6:** `2 + 3 = 5 < 6` → ✗ **no** — the two short sides can't reach across the long one; the triangle can't close.
>
> **Why:** the two short sides must stretch from the ends of the longest side and *meet* — if together they're shorter than it, they can't touch.

---

Can sides **5 cm, 7 cm, 12 cm** form a triangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> `5 + 7 = 12` — equal, **not greater** → **no**.
>
> With the sum exactly equal, the two sides lie down flat along the third — a straight line, not a triangle. The inequality is strict: sum must be **greater**.

---

Two sides of a triangle are **8 cm and 5 cm**. Between what lengths must the third side lie?

> [!ANSWER]
> The third side must be **less than the sum** and **more than the difference** of the other two:
>
> `8 − 5 < third < 8 + 5` → **between 3 cm and 13 cm** (not including 3 or 13).
>
> Check the ends: at 13 the triangle flattens (`8 + 5 = 13`); at 3 it flattens the other way (`3 + 5 = 8`). Strictly inside the range, it closes properly.

---

<!-- ===== Pythagoras property ===== -->

In a right triangle, which side is the **hypotenuse**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The side **opposite the right angle** — it never touches the 90° corner — and it is always the **longest** side.
>
> Why longest: the biggest side always faces the biggest angle, and 90° is the biggest angle a right triangle has.

---

The legs of a right triangle are **3 cm and 4 cm**. Find the hypotenuse.

> [!ANSWER]
> `hypotenuse² = leg² + leg² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25` → hypotenuse = **5 cm** (since `5² = 25`).
>
> This is the **3-4-5 triplet** — worth knowing on sight.

---

The legs are **6 cm and 8 cm**. Find the hypotenuse without squaring big numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> 6-8-? is just 3-4-5 **doubled** → hypotenuse = `5 × 2 = ` **10 cm**.
>
> Check: `36 + 64 = 100 = 10²` ✓
>
> **Why scaling works:** doubling all sides doubles every length but keeps the shape (and its right angle) identical.

---

A right triangle has hypotenuse **13 cm** and one leg **5 cm**. Find the other leg.

> [!ANSWER]
> Rearrange: `leg² = 13² − 5² = 169 − 25 = 144` → leg = **12 cm**.
>
> The **5-12-13** triplet again. ⚠️ Subtract when the hypotenuse is known — adding (`169 + 25`) is the classic error; the hypotenuse is always the biggest, so the legs come from taking away.

---

Is a triangle with sides **8, 15, 17** right-angled?

> [!ANSWER]
> Test the reverse of Pythagoras with the **longest side as candidate hypotenuse**:
>
> `8² + 15² = 64 + 225 = 289` and `17² = 289` — equal → **yes, right-angled**, with the right angle facing the 17 side.

---

Is a triangle with sides **4, 5, 6** right-angled?

> [!ANSWER]
> `4² + 5² = 16 + 25 = 41`, but `6² = 36`. Not equal → **no**.
>
> (Since `41 > 36`, the "hypotenuse" is too short — the angle facing 6 is actually *less* than 90°: an acute triangle.)

---

A **5 m ladder** leans against a wall with its foot **3 m** from the base of the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?

> [!ANSWER]
> Wall ⊥ ground → right triangle with the ladder as **hypotenuse** (5) and the ground distance as one leg (3):
>
> `height² = 5² − 3² = 25 − 9 = 16` → height = **4 m** — the 3-4-5 triplet in real life.

---

A rectangular field is **12 m by 9 m**. How long is the walk **diagonally** across it?

> [!ANSWER]
> The diagonal splits the rectangle into two right triangles with legs 12 and 9:
>
> `diagonal² = 12² + 9² = 144 + 81 = 225` → diagonal = **15 m** (`15² = 225`).
>
> That's the 3-4-5 triplet ×3 (9-12-15). Walking the two sides would be `12 + 9 = 21 m` — the diagonal saves 6 m, and Pythagoras says exactly how much.
