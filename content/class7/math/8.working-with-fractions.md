## What this chapter is about

You already know how to add and subtract fractions. This chapter is about **multiplying** and **dividing** them, and, as always, about **why** the rules work.

- **Whole number × fraction:** "3 lots of 1/4".
- **Fraction × anything:** "2/5 of 3", "3/4 of 2/5", pictured as an **area** inside a unit square.
- **Brahmagupta's rules:** multiply tops and bottoms; to divide, multiply by the **reciprocal**.
- When does multiplying make a number **bigger**, and when **smaller**?
- Word problems, some of them more than a thousand years old.

> [!NOTE]
> **Writing fractions.** Here a/b means the fraction with numerator a and denominator b, and 2 1/4 means the mixed fraction "two and a quarter" (= 9/4).

## 8.1 Multiplying a whole number and a fraction

**Worked example.** Aaron walks 3 km in 1 hour. In 5 hours he walks 5 × 3 = 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = **15 km**.

**Worked example.** His pet tortoise walks only 1/4 km in an hour. How far does it walk in 3 hours?

The question is the same kind, so the operation is the same: multiply. 3 × 1/4 = 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = **3/4 km**.

![Number line from 0 to 1 km in quarters: three hours of 1/4 km reach 3/4 km](assets/img/class7/math/8/tortoise.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Whole number × fraction:** n × a/b means n lots of a/b, which is **(n × a)/b**.
>
> **Why:** a/b is "a pieces of size 1/b". n lots of it is n × a pieces of the **same** size 1/b. The piece size (the denominator) does not change; only the number of pieces does.

**Now the time is a fraction.** Aaron walks 3 km in an hour. How far does he walk in **1/5 hour**?

In 1/5 of an hour he covers 1/5 of the distance: split 3 km into 5 equal parts. Each part is **3/5 km**. So **1/5 × 3 = 3/5**.

**And in 2/5 hour?** That is twice as long as 1/5 hour, so twice as far: 2 × 3/5 = **6/5 km**.

![A 3 km bar split into 5 parts of 3/5 km; two parts make 6/5 km](assets/img/class7/math/8/aaron_fifths.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Fraction × number: "a/b of" something.** To find 2/5 × 3:
> 1. **Divide** the number (the multiplicand, 3) by the **denominator** of the multiplier (5): 3 ÷ 5 = 3/5. That is 1/5 of it.
> 2. **Multiply** the result by the **numerator** (2): 2 × 3/5 = 6/5.
>
> **Why:** 2/5 means "2 of the 5 equal parts". First make the 5 equal parts, then take 2 of them.

**Worked example.** A farmer gives 2/3 acre of land to each of her 5 grandchildren. How much land does she give in all?

5 × 2/3 = 2/3 + 2/3 + 2/3 + 2/3 + 2/3 = **10/3 acres** (= 3 1/3 acres).

**Worked example.** One hour of internet time costs ₹8. What do 1 1/4 hours cost?
1. 1 1/4 hours = 5/4 hours.
2. Cost = 5/4 × 8. One quarter of 8 is 8 ÷ 4 = 2; five quarters is 5 × 2 = **₹10**.

> [!TIP]
> **Divide first when you can.** 5/4 × 8: doing 8 ÷ 4 = 2 first keeps the numbers small (5 × 2 = 10) instead of 40/4.

## 8.2 Multiplying two fractions

The tortoise walks 1/4 km in an hour. How far in **half an hour**? Distance = 1/2 × 1/4: half of 1/4.

To see "half of a quarter", draw a **unit square** to stand for 1 whole. Shade 1/4 of it (one row of four), then split that row into 2 equal parts.

![A unit square in 4 rows and 2 columns: half of one quarter row is 1/8 of the square](assets/img/class7/math/8/quarter_halved.svg)

The square is now cut into 4 × 2 = 8 equal parts, and the green part is one of them: **1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8**. The tortoise walks 1/8 km.

**Worked example.** A faster tortoise walks 2/5 km in an hour. How far in 3/4 hour?

Distance = 3/4 × 2/5. Use the same two steps as before.
1. **Divide 2/5 into 4 equal parts.** Draw the unit square in 5 rows; 2/5 is 2 rows (blue). Cut it into 4 columns too. The square now has 5 × 4 = 20 equal cells, and 1/4 of the blue part is 2 cells: **2/20**.
2. **Multiply by 3:** 3 × 2/20 = **6/20 = 3/10**.

![Unit square with 5 rows and 4 columns: 2/5 is two rows; three of the four columns of those rows are 6/20](assets/img/class7/math/8/three_quarters_of_two_fifths.svg)

**Worked example.** Find 5/4 × 3/2.
1. 3/2 is one whole and a half: draw two unit squares, each in 2 rows; 3 rows are shaded.
2. Divide 3/2 into 4 equal parts: cut every square into 4 columns. Each square now has 2 × 4 = 8 cells, and one column of the shaded part is 3 cells: **3/8**.
3. Multiply by 5: 5 × 3/8 = **15/8**.

![3/2 shown as one and a half unit squares, each cut into 2 rows and 4 columns; one quarter of the shaded part is 3/8](assets/img/class7/math/8/five_quarters_of_three_halves.svg)

## 8.3 Multiplying is finding an area

The shaded rectangle below has sides 1/2 and 1/4 (it sits inside a unit square of side 1).

![A rectangle of sides 1/2 and 1/4 inside a unit square; eight of them fill the square](assets/img/class7/math/8/rectangle_area.svg)

8 such rectangles fill the square of area 1, so each has area **1/8**. And 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The area of a rectangle with fractional sides is still length × breadth.** So **a/b × c/d is the area of a rectangle with sides a/b and c/d** inside a unit square.

## 8.4 Brahmagupta's rule

Look at 1/12 × 1/18. Cut the unit square into 18 rows and 12 columns: 18 × 12 = 216 equal cells, and the product is **one** cell: 1/216. In general:

1/b × 1/d = 1/(b × d).

Now 5/12 × 7/18: 7/18 is 7 rows; 1/12 of it is 7 cells out of 216; five times that is 5 × 7 = 35 cells. So 5/12 × 7/18 = (5 × 7)/(12 × 18) = **35/216**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To multiply fractions, multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators:**
> a/b × c/d = (a × c)/(b × d).
>
> **Why:** cutting the unit square into b columns and d rows makes **b × d equal cells**, the denominator. The rectangle a/b by c/d covers **a columns of c rows**, which is **a × c cells**, the numerator.
>
> This rule was first written in this general form by **Brahmagupta** in his book *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE).

**Whole numbers fit the rule too:** write 3 as 3/1. Then 3 × 3/4 = (3 × 3)/(1 × 4) = 9/4, and 3/5 × 4 = 12/5.

## 8.5 Cancel first, then multiply

**Worked example.** 12/7 × 5/24 in lowest form.
- Write it as (12 × 5)/(7 × 24). The 12 on top and the 24 below share the factor 12.
- Divide both by 12: (1 × 5)/(7 × 2) = **5/14**.

**Worked example.** 14/15 × 25/42.
- 14 (top) and 42 (bottom) share 14: they become 1 and 3.
- 25 (top) and 15 (bottom) share 5: they become 5 and 3.
- (1 × 5)/(3 × 3) = **5/9**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why cancelling is allowed:** dividing the numerator and the denominator by the same number does not change a fraction (6/8 = 3/4). Cancelling before multiplying is the same thing, done earlier, while the numbers are small. In ancient India this reducing to lowest terms was called **apavartana**; it was so familiar that the Jain scholar Umāsvāti (around 150 CE) used it as a comparison in a book about philosophy.

**Try this.** Take a square sheet of paper as "1". Fold it in half one way and shade one half; then fold it in thirds the other way and shade one third of your half with a different colour. Unfold. **What you will see:** the double-shaded piece is 1 of 6 equal pieces, 1/6 = 1/2 × 1/3. **Why:** the folds cut the sheet into 2 × 3 equal pieces, and the double-shaded part is one of them.

**1.** A tap fills 7/10 of a tank in 1 hour. How much of the tank fills in (a) 1/3 hour, (b) 2/3 hour, (c) 3/4 hour, (d) 7/10 hour? (e) How long does it take to fill the whole tank?

> [!ANSWER]
> In t hours the tap fills t × 7/10 of the tank.
> - (a) 1/3 × 7/10 = **7/30**
> - (b) 2/3 × 7/10 = 14/30 = **7/15**
> - (c) 3/4 × 7/10 = **21/40**
> - (d) 7/10 × 7/10 = **49/100**
> - (e) We need t × 7/10 = 1. Since 10/7 × 7/10 = 1, **t = 10/7 hours = 1 3/7 hours**.

**2.** The government takes 1/6 of Somu's land for a road. What part of the land is left with her?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1 − 1/6 = **5/6** of the land.

**3.** Somu gives half of the remaining land to her daughter Krishna and 1/3 of it to her son Bora, and keeps the rest. What part of the **original** land did (a) Krishna, (b) Bora, (c) Somu get?

> [!ANSWER]
> "Of the remaining" means "of 5/6".
> - (a) Krishna: 1/2 × 5/6 = **5/12**.
> - (b) Bora: 1/3 × 5/6 = **5/18**.
> - (c) Somu: 5/6 − 5/12 − 5/18. With denominator 36: 30/36 − 15/36 − 10/36 = **5/36**.
>
> Check: 1/6 + 5/12 + 5/18 + 5/36 = 6/36 + 15/36 + 10/36 + 5/36 = 36/36 = 1 ✓.

> [!ANSWER]

> 2. Area = 15/4 × 48/5. Cancel 15 with 5 (→ 3 and 1) and 48 with 4 (→ 12 and 1).

**6.** Which is heavier: 12/15 of 500 g or 3/20 of 4 kg?

> [!ANSWER]
> - 12/15 × 500 g: 500 ÷ 15 × 12, or simplify 12/15 = 4/5 first: 4/5 × 500 = **400 g**.
> - 3/20 × 4 kg = 3/20 × 4000 g = 3 × 200 = **600 g**.
>
> So **3/20 of 4 kg** is heavier (by 200 g). Change both to the same unit before comparing.

## 8.6 Does multiplying always make bigger?

- 3 × 5 = 15: bigger than both.
- 1/4 × 8 = 2: bigger than 1/4 but **smaller** than 8.
- 3/4 × 2/5 = 6/20. Compare with 3/4 = 15/20 and 2/5 = 8/20: **smaller than both**.

| Situation | Example | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Both numbers greater than 1 | 4/3 × 4 = 16/3 | greater than both |
| Both between 0 and 1 | 3/4 × 2/5 = 3/10 | less than both |
| One between 0 and 1, one greater than 1 | 3/4 × 5 = 15/4 | between them |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - Multiplying by a number **between 0 and 1** makes the other number **smaller**.
> - Multiplying by a number **greater than 1** makes the other number **bigger**.
>
> **Why:** 3/4 × 8 means "3/4 **of** 8", a part of 8, so less than 8. And 4/3 × 8 is "8, and then a third more", so more than 8. Multiplying by exactly 1 leaves a number unchanged; less than 1 shrinks it, more than 1 stretches it.

## 8.7 Order does not matter

![A 1/2 by 1/4 rectangle and a 1/4 by 1/2 rectangle have the same area 1/8](assets/img/class7/math/8/order_swap.svg)

1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8 and 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8: turning a rectangle on its side does not change its area. So **a/b × c/d = c/d × a/b**. Brahmagupta's rule shows the same: a × c = c × a on top and b × d = d × b below.

**Try this.** Draw a 12 cm line and call it "1". Mark 1/2 × 1/3 of it (half of a third) and 1/3 × 1/2 of it (a third of a half). **What you will see:** both marks are at 2 cm, which is 1/6 of the line. **Why:** order does not matter in multiplication: both are 1 part when the line is cut into 2 × 3 = 6 equal parts.

## 8.8 Dividing fractions

Every division can be turned into a multiplication question. 12 ÷ 4 asks: **what times 4 makes 12?** (4 × 3 = 12, so the answer is 3.) We use the same idea for fractions.

**Worked example.** 1 ÷ 2/3. Ask: 2/3 × ? = 1.

If we multiply 2/3 by 3/2, the 2s and 3s cancel: 2/3 × 3/2 = 6/6 = **1**. So **1 ÷ 2/3 = 3/2**.

**Worked example.** 3 ÷ 2/3. Ask: 2/3 × ? = 3. We know 2/3 × 3/2 = 1, so multiply that by 3: 2/3 × (3/2 × 3) = 3. So **3 ÷ 2/3 = 3/2 × 3 = 9/2**.

**Worked example.** 1/5 ÷ 1/2. Ask: 1/2 × ? = 1/5. 1/2 × 2 = 1, and then × 1/5 gives 1/5. So **1/5 ÷ 1/2 = 2 × 1/5 = 2/5**.

**Worked example.** 2/3 ÷ 3/5. Ask: 3/5 × ? = 2/3. 3/5 × 5/3 = 1; then × 2/3. So **2/3 ÷ 3/5 = 5/3 × 2/3 = 10/9**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The **reciprocal** of a/b is **b/a** (turn it upside down). A number times its reciprocal is **1**: a/b × b/a = (a × b)/(b × a) = 1.
>
> **To divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal:**
> a/b ÷ c/d = a/b × d/c = (a × d)/(b × c).
>
> **Why:** a division asks "what times c/d gives a/b?" Multiplying c/d by its reciprocal d/c gives 1, and multiplying that by a/b gives a/b. So the answer is d/c × a/b.
>
> Brahmagupta stated this rule in 628 CE: swap the numerator and denominator of the divisor, then multiply. Bhāskara II (1150 CE) put it as "dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal".

**Check by multiplying back.** 2/3 ÷ 3/5 = 10/9. Check: 10/9 × 3/5 = 30/45 = 2/3 ✓. Always check a division this way.

## 8.9 When is the quotient bigger than the dividend?

- 6 ÷ 3 = 2: the answer is **smaller** than 6.
- 6 ÷ 1/4 = 6 × 4 = 24: the answer is **bigger** than 6!
- 1/8 ÷ 1/4 = 1/8 × 4 = 1/2: again **bigger** than 1/8.

**Why dividing by 1/4 makes things bigger:** 6 ÷ 1/4 asks "how many quarters fit into 6?" Each whole holds 4 quarters, so 6 wholes hold 24. Small pieces fit in many times.

**Try this.** Take a measuring cup and a 1/4-cup scoop (or any small cup that fills the big one in 4 goes). How many scoops of water fill 2 big cups? **What you will see:** 8 scoops, so 2 ÷ 1/4 = 8. **Why:** "divide by 1/4" means "count how many quarters fit", and every whole holds 4 of them.

> [!TIP]
> **Divide by a number less than 1 → the answer grows. Divide by a number more than 1 → the answer shrinks.** It is the mirror image of the rule for multiplying, because dividing by c/d is multiplying by d/c, and d/c is more than 1 exactly when c/d is less than 1.

### Check yourself

**1.** When is the quotient less than the dividend, and when is it greater?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Divisor **greater than 1** → quotient **less** than the dividend (6 ÷ 3 = 2).
> - Divisor **between 0 and 1** → quotient **greater** than the dividend (6 ÷ 1/4 = 24).
> - Divisor exactly 1 → the same number.
>
> **Why:** dividing by c/d is multiplying by d/c; if c/d < 1 then d/c > 1, which stretches the number.

**2.** Is there a similar rule comparing the **divisor** and the quotient?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No simple rule using the divisor alone.** 6 ÷ 3 = 2 is less than the divisor 3, but 12 ÷ 3 = 4 is more than 3. Whether the quotient beats the divisor depends on the **dividend** too, not only on the divisor.

## 8.10 Problems with fractions

**Worked example (tea).** Leena makes 5 cups of tea with 1/4 litre of milk. How much milk is in each cup?
1. Milk per cup = 1/4 ÷ 5. As a multiplication: 5 × (milk per cup) = 1/4.
2. Reciprocal of 5 is 1/5: 1/4 × 1/5 = **1/20 litre** per cup.

**Worked example (bricks, from the Śulbasūtras).** The Śulbasūtras, India's oldest geometry texts (Baudhāyana's is from about 800 BCE), describe building fire altars. One problem: cover an area of 7 1/2 square units with square bricks of side 1/5 unit. How many bricks?
1. One brick: 1/5 × 1/5 = **1/25** square unit.
2. Total area: 7 1/2 = 15/2 square units.
3. Number of bricks = 15/2 ÷ 1/25 = 15/2 × 25 = 375/2 = **187 1/2** bricks (187 whole bricks and one half brick).

**Worked example (four fountains).** This puzzle comes from Pṛthūdakasvāmī's commentary (about 860 CE). Four fountains fill a tank. The first fills it in 1 day, the second in 1/2 day, the third in 1/4 day, the fourth in 1/5 day. Together, how long do they take?
1. In one day the first fills the tank 1 ÷ 1 = 1 time.
2. The second: 1 ÷ 1/2 = 2 times. The third: 1 ÷ 1/4 = 4 times. The fourth: 1 ÷ 1/5 = 5 times.
3. Together, in one day: 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 = **12 tanks**.
4. So one tank takes **1/12 day** (2 hours).

> [!NOTE]
> **Turn time into "jobs per day".** You cannot add the times (1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/5 days would say they get **slower** together!). Instead add how many tanks each fills in a day, because those **do** add up when all flow together.

## 8.11 Fractional relations in a square

**Worked example.** What fraction of the whole square is shaded?

![A square with lines inside; the shaded part lies in the top-right quarter](assets/img/class7/math/8/relations_example.svg)

1. Let the whole square have area 1. The top-right square (outlined in red) is **1/4** of it.
2. The diagonal of the red square cuts it in half. The triangle below the diagonal is 1/2 × 1/4 = **1/8**.
3. The lines through the middle of the red square cut that triangle into 4 equal-area pieces: a small square (worth 2 small triangles) and 2 small triangles. The shaded part is the small square and the upper triangle, **3/4** of the big triangle.
4. Shaded area = 3/4 × 1/8 = **3/32** of the whole square.

> [!TIP]
> **Peel the layers.** "Fraction of a fraction of a fraction" is a product: 3/4 of 1/2 of 1/4 = 3/4 × 1/2 × 1/4. Find one layer at a time, from big to small.

## 8.12 A dramma-tic donation

From Bhāskara II's book *Līlāvatī* (1150 CE): "O wise one! A miser gave a beggar 1/5 of 1/16 of 1/4 of 1/2 of 2/3 of 3/4 of a dramma. If you know fractions well, tell me, child, how many cowrie shells did he give?" A **dramma** was a silver coin, worth 1280 cowrie shells in this story.

1/2 × 2/3 × 3/4 × 1/5 × 1/16 × 1/4 = 6/7680 = **1/1280** of a dramma, which is exactly **one cowrie shell**. That is the joke: the miser gave the smallest coin there was!

Around the 12th century many coins were in use: gold (dinars, hunas), silver (drammas), copper (panas, mashakas) and cowrie shells, with rates that changed from place to place. Suppose 1 gold dinar = 12 silver drammas, 1 dramma = 4 copper panas, 1 pana = 6 mashakas, and 1 pana = 30 cowrie shells. Then 1 pana = 1/4 dramma = 1/4 × 1/12 = **1/48 dinar**.

**A little history.** Fractions with any numerator, together with all four operations on them, developed largely in India. The Śulbasūtras (from about 800 BCE) already divided such fractions. Brahmagupta (628 CE) wrote down the general rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions; Bhāskara I (629 CE) explained multiplication as cutting a square into rectangles, the picture used in this chapter. Later Indian mathematicians such as Śrīdharācārya, Mahāvīrācārya, Pṛthūdakasvāmī and Bhāskara II developed the subject further. The ideas travelled to Arab and African mathematicians such as al-Ḥaṣṣār of Morocco (about 1192 CE), and then to Europe, where they came into general use only around the 17th century.

**Try this.** Cut a square of paper into 4 equal squares, cut one of them along a diagonal, and so on, writing on every piece the fraction of the original square it is. **What you will see:** each cut in half halves the fraction: 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, … **Why:** each piece is "1/2 of" the piece before, so you multiply by 1/2 each time.

### Check yourself

**1.** In the fountain problem, fill in: the second, third and fourth fountains fill the tank how many times a day? How many times together?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Second: 1 ÷ 1/2 = **2**. Third: 1 ÷ 1/4 = **4**. Fourth: 1 ÷ 1/5 = **5**.
> - Together with the first: 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 = **12** times a day, so one fill takes 1/12 day.

What fraction of the big square is shaded?

**(a)**

![A square with a shaded diagonal band between two parallel slanting lines](assets/img/class7/math/8/relations_a.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The long slanting line joins two opposite corners, so the part below it is **1/2** of the square.
> 2. The short slanting line joins the midpoints of the left and bottom sides. The corner triangle below it has legs 1/2 and 1/2, so its area is 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = **1/8**.
> 3. The shaded band is the half minus the corner triangle: 1/2 − 1/8 = **3/8**.

**(b)**

![A square whose top-left quarter holds a tilted square; two corner triangles on its right are shaded](assets/img/class7/math/8/relations_b.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The top-left square is **1/4** of the big square.
> 2. The tilted square joins the midpoints of its sides. That cuts off 4 equal corner triangles; together they are half of the small square, so each is 1/4 × 1/2 × 1/4 = **1/32** of the big square. (The tilted square is the other half.)
> 3. Two corner triangles are shaded: 2 × 1/32 = **1/16**.

**2.** Using 1 gold dinar = 12 drammas, 1 dramma = 4 panas and 1 pana = 30 cowries: what is 1 cowrie in panas? In gold dinars?

> [!ANSWER]
> - 30 cowries make 1 pana, so 1 cowrie = **1/30 pana**.
> - 1 pana = 1/48 dinar, so 1 cowrie = 1/30 × 1/48 = **1/1440 dinar**.
>
> (With these rates a dramma is 4 × 30 = 120 cowries, not 1280 as in Bhāskara's story: rates really did differ from region to region.)

### 2. Choose the expression that describes each situation, then simplify it.

**(a)** Maria has 8 m of lace and uses 1/4 m for each bag until the lace is finished. How many bags? (i) 8 × 1/4 (ii) 1/8 × 1/4 (iii) 8 ÷ 1/4 (iv) 1/4 ÷ 8

> [!ANSWER]
> "How many 1/4 m pieces fit into 8 m?" is a division: **(iii) 8 ÷ 1/4** = 8 × 4 = **32 bags**.

**(b)** 1/2 m of ribbon makes 8 badges. How much ribbon per badge? (i) 8 × 1/2 (ii) 1/2 ÷ 1/8 (iii) 8 ÷ 1/2 (iv) 1/2 ÷ 8

> [!ANSWER]
> Share 1/2 m equally among 8: **(iv) 1/2 ÷ 8** = 1/2 × 1/8 = **1/16 m**.

**(c)** A loaf needs 1/6 kg of flour. How many loaves from 5 kg? (i) 5 × 1/6 (ii) 1/6 ÷ 5 (iii) 5 ÷ 1/6 (iv) 5 × 6

> [!ANSWER]
> How many 1/6 kg portions fit in 5 kg: **(iii) 5 ÷ 1/6** = 5 × 6 = **30 loaves**. (Option (iv) gives the same number, but (iii) is the expression that describes the situation.)

### 3. 1/4 kg of flour makes 12 rotis. How much flour do 6 rotis need?

> [!ANSWER]
> 6 rotis are half of 12, so they need half the flour: 1/2 × 1/4 = **1/8 kg**.

### 4. From the *Pāṭīgaṇita* of Śrīdharācārya (9th century): "Friend, after thinking, what is the sum of 1 ÷ 1/6, 1 ÷ 1/10, 1 ÷ 1/13, 1 ÷ 1/9 and 1 ÷ 1/2?"

> [!ANSWER]
> 1 ÷ 1/n is the reciprocal of 1/n, which is n. So the sum is 6 + 10 + 13 + 9 + 2 = **40**.

### 5. Mira's novel has 400 pages. She read 1/5 of the pages yesterday and 3/10 today. How many pages are left?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Fraction read: 1/5 + 3/10 = 2/10 + 3/10 = 5/10 = 1/2.
> 2. Fraction left: 1 − 1/2 = 1/2.
> 3. Pages left: 1/2 × 400 = **200 pages**.

### 6. A car runs 16 km on 1 litre of petrol. How far does it go on 2 3/4 litres?

> [!ANSWER]
> 2 3/4 × 16 = 11/4 × 16 = 11 × 4 = **44 km**.

### 7. By train Amritpal's trip takes 5 1/6 hours; by plane, 1/2 hour. How much time does the plane save?

> [!ANSWER]
> 5 1/6 − 1/2 = 31/6 − 3/6 = 28/6 = 14/3 = **4 2/3 hours** (4 hours 40 minutes).

### 8. Mariam and her cousins ate 4/5 of a cake. The rest was shared equally by her three friends. How much did each friend get?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Left: 1 − 4/5 = 1/5 of the cake.
> 2. Shared by 3: 1/5 ÷ 3 = 1/5 × 1/3 = **1/15 of the cake** each.

### 9. Which describe the product 565/465 × 707/676? (a) > 565/465 (b) < 565/465 (c) > 707/676 (d) < 707/676 (e) > 1 (f) < 1

> [!ANSWER]
> - 565/465 is **greater than 1** (top bigger than bottom), and so is 707/676.
> - Multiplying by a number greater than 1 makes the other number bigger, so the product is greater than each: **(a) and (c)**.
> - Both are more than 1, so their product is more than 1 × 1 = 1: **(e)**.
>
> Answer: **(a), (c), (e)**. No calculation needed.

### 10. What fraction of the whole square is shaded?

![A square in four quarters; in the bottom-right quarter a Y shape cuts off a shaded piece](assets/img/class7/math/8/relations_q10.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The bottom-right quarter is **1/4** of the square.
> 2. Inside it, the vertical line splits the quarter into two halves. The left half is a rectangle of 1/2 of the quarter.
> 3. The slanting line from the quarter's top-left corner to the middle cuts off a triangle from the top of that rectangle. That triangle is 1/4 of the rectangle (half of its top half), so the shaded piece is 3/4 of the rectangle.
> 4. Shaded = 3/4 × 1/2 × 1/4 = **3/32** of the whole square.

### 11. A colony of ants splits equally at every branching point and reaches two food sources. What fraction of the colony reaches each one?

![Ant paths branching at four points, ending at a mango tree and a sugarcane field](assets/img/class7/math/8/ants.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Follow the paths from the bottom. At each point the ants arriving there split equally among the paths leaving it.
> 1. **Point 1** splits in 2: 1/2 goes to the mango tree, 1/2 goes on.
> 2. **Point 2** splits that 1/2 in 2: 1/4 to the mango tree, 1/4 goes on.
> 3. **Point 3** splits that 1/4 in 4: two paths of 1/16 to the mango tree, 1/16 to sugarcane, 1/16 goes on.
> 4. **Point 4** splits that 1/16 in 2: 1/32 to the mango tree, 1/32 to sugarcane.
>
> - Mango tree: 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/16 + 1/16 + 1/32 = 16/32 + 8/32 + 2/32 + 2/32 + 1/32 = **29/32**.
> - Sugarcane: 1/16 + 1/32 = 2/32 + 1/32 = **3/32**.
> - Check: 29/32 + 3/32 = 1 ✓ (every ant ends up somewhere).

### 12. Find 1 − 1/2; then (1 − 1/2) × (1 − 1/3); then (1 − 1/2) × (1 − 1/3) × (1 − 1/4) × (1 − 1/5); then the product all the way to (1 − 1/10). Make a general statement and explain it.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each bracket is 1 − 1/k = (k − 1)/k.
> - 1 − 1/2 = **1/2**.
> - 1/2 × 2/3 = **1/3**.
> - 1/2 × 2/3 × 3/4 × 4/5 = **1/5**.
> - 1/2 × 2/3 × 3/4 × … × 9/10 = **1/10**.
>
> **General statement:** (1 − 1/2) × (1 − 1/3) × … × (1 − 1/n) = **1/n**.
>
> **Why:** written as 1/2 × 2/3 × 3/4 × … × (n − 1)/n, every numerator cancels the denominator just before it (the 2s cancel, the 3s cancel, …). Only the first numerator 1 and the last denominator n are left.

## In a nutshell

- n × a/b = (n × a)/b: n lots of a/b.
- "a/b of" a number: divide by b, then multiply by a.
- **Brahmagupta's rule:** a/b × c/d = (a × c)/(b × d). Picture: the area of an a/b by c/d rectangle in a unit square.
- **Cancel common factors first**, then multiply.
- Order does not matter: a/b × c/d = c/d × a/b.
- Multiplying by a number **less than 1** shrinks; **more than 1** stretches.
- **Reciprocal** of a/b is b/a; their product is 1.
- **Dividing by a fraction = multiplying by its reciprocal:** a/b ÷ c/d = (a × d)/(b × c). Check by multiplying back.
- Dividing by a number **less than 1** makes the answer **bigger**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** multiplying by a fraction can make a number **smaller** · dividing by 1/4 makes it 4 times **bigger** · flip the **divisor**, not the dividend · change mixed fractions to fractions before multiplying or dividing · 4 saplings have 3 gaps · compare weights in the same unit · fountains: add the tanks per day, not the times · 5 ÷ 1/6 (how many portions) is not 1/6 ÷ 5.
