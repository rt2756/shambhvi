## When two lines cross

Fold a square sheet of paper in different ways and draw along the creases. Some pairs of lines **meet at a point** — we say they **intersect**. Others never seem to meet.

> [!NOTE]
> **Two different straight lines can meet at most once.** If they shared two points, they would be the same line — through two points there is only one straight line.

When line l crosses line m, they make **four angles** around the crossing point. Call them a, b, c, d going round:

```
          \   m
       a   \   b
   ---------X--------- l
       d     \   c
              \
```

**If ∠a = 120°, what are the others — without measuring?**
- ∠a and ∠b together make a straight line, which is **180°**. So ∠b = 180° − 120° = **60°**.
- ∠b and ∠c also make a straight line → ∠c = 180° − 60° = **120°**.
- ∠c and ∠d → ∠d = **60°**.

So ∠a = ∠c and ∠b = ∠d.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Linear pair:** two adjacent angles made by intersecting lines, sitting on one straight line → they **add up to 180°**.
> **Vertically opposite angles:** the angles facing each other across the crossing (∠a & ∠c, ∠b & ∠d) → they are **equal**.
>
> **Why they're always equal (a proof, not a measurement):** ∠a + ∠b = 180° and ∠b + ∠c = 180°. Both sums equal 180° and both contain ∠b, so whatever is left — ∠a and ∠c — must be the same. This works for **any** ∠a, not just 120°.

**Book Figure it Out:** in Fig. 5.3 the linear pairs are ∠a & ∠b, ∠b & ∠c, ∠c & ∠d, ∠d & ∠a; the vertically opposite pairs are ∠a & ∠c and ∠b & ∠d.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why your protractor sometimes disagrees:** real pencil lines have **thickness** and protractors are placed slightly off. Geometry studies *ideal* lines with no thickness, and gets its answers by **reasoning**. Measurements come out very close, which is why geometry is so useful in engineering, art and architecture.

## Perpendicular lines

Can two crossing lines make **four equal angles**? The four angles add up to 360° (a full turn), so each would be 360° ÷ 4 = **90°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Perpendicular lines intersect at right angles (90°).** We mark the right angle with a small square.
> If ONE angle at a crossing is 90°, all four are: its linear-pair partner is 180° − 90° = 90°, and vertically opposite angles are equal.

## Parallel lines

Railway tracks, the lines on a ruled notebook, the opposite edges of a door — they never look likely to meet.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Parallel lines lie in the same plane and never meet, however far they are extended at both ends.** We mark parallel lines with matching arrowheads (>, then >> for a second set).

> [!WARNING]
> **"Same plane" matters.** A line drawn on your desk and a line on the blackboard may never meet, but they aren't parallel — they are in different planes (flat surfaces).

### Paper folding (book Activity 2)

- Opposite edges of a square sheet are **parallel**; adjacent edges are **perpendicular**.
- Fold in half horizontally → the crease is parallel to the top and bottom edges: **3** parallel lines, and the crease is perpendicular to the vertical sides.
- Fold again → **5** parallel lines; again → **9**. Each fold puts a new crease in the middle of every strip, doubling the strips: with 1, 2, 3 folds there are 2, 4, 8 strips, and lines = strips + 1.
- A vertical fold is **perpendicular** to all the horizontal creases.

> [!TIP]
> **How to spot parallel lines on dot paper:** count the steps. A segment that goes "2 right, 1 up" is parallel to every other segment that goes "2 right, 1 up" (or 4 right, 2 up). Same slope = same direction = parallel.

## Transversals — one line crossing two lines

Being "sure" that two lines are parallel just by looking is hard. So we draw a third line across both.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A transversal is a line that crosses two (or more) other lines.** It makes **8 angles** — 4 at each crossing.

```
            t
             \
          1   \  2
     ----------X----------- l
          4     \  3
                 \
              5   \  6
     --------------X------- m
              8     \  7
                     \
```

**Can all 8 angles be different?** **No.** At each crossing, vertically opposite angles are equal (∠1 = ∠3, ∠2 = ∠4, ∠5 = ∠7, ∠6 = ∠8). So there are **at most 4 different measures**. Even 5 different ones (say ∠2, ∠3, ∠4, ∠5, ∠6) is impossible, because ∠2 = ∠4.

### Corresponding angles

Angles in the **same position** at the two crossings are **corresponding angles**: ∠1 & ∠5, ∠2 & ∠6, ∠3 & ∠7, ∠4 & ∠8. (Both "top-left", both "top-right", and so on.)

**Book Activity 3:** draw line l and transversal t making 60° at X. At another point Y on t, draw line m making the **same** 60° in the same position. Lines l and m come out **parallel**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two rules that go both ways:**
> 1. **If the lines are parallel, corresponding angles are equal.**
> 2. **If corresponding angles are equal, the lines are parallel.**
>
> **Why it makes sense:** corresponding angles measure how much each line is "tilted" against the same transversal. Equal tilt = same direction = they never close in on each other. If one line is tilted even a little more, the two lines lean towards each other and eventually meet — and then the corresponding angles can't be equal.

**Drawing parallel lines with a set square:** draw line l, place the set square on it and draw two perpendiculars at different places. Both make 90° with l, and those 90° angles are corresponding angles → the two lines are parallel. Sliding a set square along a ruler works the same way: every line you draw makes the same angle with the ruler.

**A parallel through point A by paper folding (book):** fold a crease t through A perpendicular to l; then fold a crease m through A perpendicular to t. Both l and m make 90° with t → corresponding angles equal → **l ∥ m**.

### Alternate angles

In the figure above, **∠4 and ∠6** are **alternate angles** (inside the two lines, on opposite sides of t), and so are **∠3 and ∠5**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When the lines are parallel, alternate angles are equal.**
> **Why:** ∠6 = ∠2 (corresponding angles, lines parallel) and ∠2 = ∠4 (vertically opposite). So ∠6 = ∠4 — for **any** measure, no protractor needed.

### Interior angles on the same side

∠3 and ∠6 (both between the lines, both on the right of t) are **interior angles on the same side** of the transversal.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When the lines are parallel, interior angles on the same side add up to 180°.**
> **Why:** ∠6 = ∠2 (corresponding). And ∠2 + ∠3 = 180° (linear pair). Swap ∠2 for ∠6: **∠3 + ∠6 = 180°.**

| Pair | Where | If lines are parallel… |
|---|---|---|
| Corresponding | same position at each crossing | **equal** |
| Alternate | between the lines, opposite sides of t | **equal** |
| Interior, same side | between the lines, same side of t | **add to 180°** |
| Vertically opposite | across one crossing | **equal (always, parallel or not)** |
| Linear pair | side by side on one line | **add to 180° (always)** |

## Worked examples (book)

**Example 1 — one angle gives all eight.** Parallel lines l, m with transversal t; one angle is 135°.
Every angle at a crossing is either that angle or its linear-pair partner. Corresponding angles copy them to the other crossing. So the **eight angles are four 135°s and four 45°s** (180° − 135° = 45°).

> [!TIP]
> **Big shortcut:** with two parallel lines and a transversal, there are only **two** different angle sizes, and they add up to 180°. The "sharp-looking" ones are all equal and the "wide-looking" ones are all equal.

**Example 2 — testing for parallel.** ∠a = 120° at l, and the angle corresponding to its neighbour at m is 70°. ∠a's linear-pair partner is 60°. Its corresponding angle at m is 70°, not 60° → corresponding angles unequal → **l and m are NOT parallel.**

**Example 3 — interior angles.** l ∥ m and ∠3 = 50°. Then its linear-pair partner ∠2 = 130°, and ∠6 corresponds to ∠2 → ∠6 = **130°**. Check: ∠3 + ∠6 = 50° + 130° = 180° ✔ (interior angles on the same side).

**Example 4 — a parallelogram ABCD** (AB ∥ CD, AD ∥ BC) with diagonal AC; ∠DAC = 65°, ∠ADC = 60°.
- AD is a transversal of AB ∥ DC → interior angles: ∠DAB + ∠ADC = 180° → ∠DAB = **120°**.
- ∠DAB = ∠DAC + ∠CAB → 120° = 65° + ∠CAB → **∠CAB = 55°**.
- DC is a transversal of AD ∥ BC → ∠ADC + ∠BCD = 180° → **∠BCD = 120°**.
- Similarly **∠ABC = 60°**.

> [!NOTE]
> This shows a general fact about parallelograms: **neighbouring angles add to 180° and opposite angles are equal** (60° and 60°, 120° and 120°).

## Angle-chasing strategy

The end-of-chapter figures (Fig. 5.30–5.35) ask you to find marked angles. The book's answers include a = 48°, b = 52°, c = 81°, d = 99°, e = 69°, j = 97°, and ∠NOP = 108°. Whatever the figure, the method is the same:

1. **Spot the parallel lines** (arrow marks) and the **transversal** joining them.
2. **Name the relationship** between the angle you know and the one you want: vertically opposite? linear pair? corresponding? alternate? interior?
3. **Write one line of reasoning** for each step: "∠x = 65° (alternate angles, AB ∥ CD)".
4. If no single line crosses both parallel lines where you need it, **draw an extra parallel line** through the corner (the book's hint for ∠NOP).

**A worked "bent" problem (the ∠NOP type):** two parallel lines, and a path that bends at point O between them, making 40° with the top line and 68° with the bottom line. Draw a third line through O parallel to both. The bend splits into two parts: the top part is 40° (alternate angles with the top line), the bottom part is 68° (alternate angles with the bottom line). So the bend angle = 40° + 68° = **108°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **"Zig-zag" rule:** in a Z or N shape between two parallel lines, the two angles inside the Z are **equal** (they are alternate angles). In a "bent arrow" between parallel lines, the bend angle **equals the sum** of the two angles it makes with the lines.

**Book exercise 5 (in words):** AB ∥ CD ∥ EF, EA ⟂ AB, and ∠BEF = 55°. Since AB ∥ EF, the angle EB makes with AB on one side and its partner make interior angles: 55° + y = 180° → **y = 125°**, and x corresponds to y → **x = 125°**.

## Parallel illusions

Some pictures have lines that really **are** parallel but **look** bent, because of the patterns drawn across them. Check with a ruler! Our eyes compare a line with what's around it, so slanting stripes or tiles nearby fool us. That's another reason mathematicians trust **reasoning** over appearance.

## In a nutshell

- Two crossing lines make 4 angles: **linear pairs add to 180°**, **vertically opposite angles are equal**.
- **Perpendicular** = crossing at 90°. **Parallel** = same plane, never meet.
- A **transversal** across two lines makes 8 angles, at most 4 different sizes.
- Lines parallel ⇔ **corresponding angles equal**.
- With parallel lines: **alternate angles equal**, **same-side interior angles add to 180°**.
- With parallel lines + transversal, there are only **two angle sizes**, adding to 180°.
- Stuck? Draw an **extra parallel line** through a corner.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** corresponding/alternate angles are equal **only if the lines are parallel** (vertically opposite angles are equal always) · interior angles on the same side **add to 180°**, they are not equal · lines in different planes are not "parallel" · 8 angles but never more than 4 different sizes · don't trust how a figure *looks* — check the arrow marks.
