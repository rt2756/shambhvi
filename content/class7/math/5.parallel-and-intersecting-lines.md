## What this chapter is about

Look around you: the two rails of a railway track, the lines in your notebook, the edges of a door. Some lines **cross** each other, and some **never meet**, however long you make them. This chapter is about those two situations:

- What happens to the **angles** when two lines **cross**?
- What does it mean for two lines to be **parallel**, and how can we be **sure** they are?
- When a third line cuts across two lines, which angles must be **equal**, and why?

By the end you will be able to find unknown angles in a figure **by reasoning alone**, without a protractor.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** A **line** goes on forever in both directions. A **line segment** is a piece of a line with two **endpoints**. A **plane** is a flat surface, like a table top, a sheet of paper or a blackboard. Every line in this chapter lies on one flat surface.

## 5.1 When two lines cross

**Try this.** Take a square sheet of paper and fold it in different ways. Open it and draw along each crease with a pencil and ruler. Pick any two of the lines. Do they meet? If they don't meet on the paper, would they meet if you made them longer?

When two lines meet at a point, we say they **intersect**, and the point is their **point of intersection**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Two different straight lines can cross at most once.** Why? Through two points there is only **one** straight line. If two lines shared two points, they would have to be the **same** line.

### Four angles at a crossing

When line l crosses line m, they make **four angles** around the crossing point. Name them a, b, c and d, going round:

![Two lines l and m crossing, making four angles a, b, c, d](assets/img/class7/math/5/crossing.svg)

**Try this.** Draw two crossing lines and measure all four angles with a protractor. Do it for four different pairs of crossing lines. What patterns do you notice?

**Worked example.** In the figure, ∠a = 120°. Find ∠b, ∠c and ∠d **without measuring**.

![∠a is 120°; find b, c and d](assets/img/class7/math/5/crossing_120.svg)

1. ∠a and ∠b sit side by side and together make the straight line l. A straight line is a **straight angle**, which measures **180°**. So ∠b = 180° − 120° = **60°**.
2. ∠b and ∠c together make the straight line m. So ∠c = 180° − 60° = **120°**.
3. ∠c and ∠d together make line l again. So ∠d = 180° − 120° = **60°**.

So ∠a = ∠c = 120° and ∠b = ∠d = 60°. The angles **across** from each other came out equal. Is that just luck with 120°?

### Why opposite angles are always equal

Let's reason without using any number at all.

- ∠a + ∠b = 180° (they make the straight line l).
- ∠a + ∠d = 180° (they make the straight line m).

Both sums are 180° and both contain ∠a. Take ∠a away from each, and what is left must be equal: **∠b = ∠d**. In the same way, ∠b + ∠a = 180° and ∠b + ∠c = 180°, so **∠a = ∠c**.

This works for **any** size of ∠a, not just 120°. A reason like this, which shows something is true in every case, is called a **proof**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Linear pair:** two angles side by side at a crossing, which together make a straight line. **A linear pair always adds up to 180°.**
> **Vertically opposite angles:** the two angles facing each other across a crossing (∠a and ∠c, ∠b and ∠d). **Vertically opposite angles are always equal.**
> **Why:** each angle and its neighbour make 180°, and two angles that make 180° with the same neighbour must be equal.

### Check yourself

In this figure, lines l and m cross and make angles p, q, r and s. List every linear pair and every pair of vertically opposite angles.

![Lines l and m crossing with angles p, q, r, s](assets/img/class7/math/5/crossing_check.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Linear pairs** (neighbours, adding to 180°): **p and q, q and r, r and s, s and p.** Going round the crossing, every angle has two neighbours, so there are 4 linear pairs.
>
> **Vertically opposite pairs** (facing each other, equal): **p and r, q and s.**
>
> For example, if p = 85°, then q = 180° − 85° = 95°, r = p = 85° and s = q = 95°.

### Measuring versus reasoning

When you measure, a linear pair may come to 179° or 181°, or two opposite angles may differ by a degree. Why?

- A protractor may be placed slightly off, or read slightly wrong.
- A pencil line has **thickness**. The "ideal" line of geometry has **no thickness at all**, but nobody can draw one.

Geometry works with these ideal lines and finds answers by **reasoning**: a straight angle is 180°, so its two parts must add to 180°, exactly. Measurements come out very close to what reasoning predicts, and that is why geometry is so useful in physics, art, engineering and architecture.

## 5.2 Perpendicular lines

Can two crossing lines make **four equal angles**? The four angles all the way round add up to a full turn, **360°**. If they are equal, each is 360° ÷ 4 = **90°**, a **right angle**.

![Perpendicular lines l and m with right-angle marks](assets/img/class7/math/5/perpendicular.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Perpendicular lines** cross at **right angles (90°)**. We show a right angle with a small square in the corner.
> **If one angle at a crossing is 90°, all four are.** Why: its linear-pair neighbour is 180° − 90° = 90°, and vertically opposite angles are equal.

## 5.3 How segments can meet

Line segments can meet in several ways. Mathematicians describe them with careful words: **a point**, **an endpoint**, **the midpoint** (the point exactly halfway along), **meet**, **intersect**.

![Three ways segments meet: FG and FH at endpoint F; AB and CD crossing at X; IJ meeting KL at I](assets/img/class7/math/5/segments.svg)

- **FG and FH meet at their common endpoint F**, making an angle of 115°.
- **AB and CD intersect at X**, which is the **midpoint of both** (AX = XB and CX = XD). The angle between them is 110° on one side, so 70° on the other (a linear pair).
- **IJ meets KL at I**, which is an endpoint of IJ and the **midpoint of KL**. The angle is 90°, so IJ is perpendicular to KL.

Now look at two segments that point in very slightly different directions. If you extend them far enough, they will meet somewhere. But some segments look as if they would **never** meet, however far you extend them. That brings us to parallel lines.

## 5.4 Parallel lines

The two rails of a railway track, the ruled lines in a notebook, the top and bottom edges of a blackboard: none of these look as if they would ever meet.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Parallel lines** lie on the **same plane** and **never meet**, however far we extend them at both ends.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Same plane" matters.** A line drawn on your desk and a line drawn on the blackboard may never meet, but they are **not** called parallel, because they are not on the same flat surface.

**Try this.** Name five pairs of parallel lines you can see in your classroom (window bars, edges of the door, edges of a book, floor tiles…).

### How to show parallel and perpendicular lines in a figure

We put an **arrow mark (>)** on lines that are parallel. If a figure has a second set of parallel lines, that set gets **two** arrow marks (>>), and so on. Perpendicular lines get the small **square** at the corner.

![Notation: one set of parallel lines with single arrows, a second set with double arrows, and a right angle marked with a square](assets/img/class7/math/5/notation.svg)

> [!TIP]
> In every exercise, **look for the arrow marks first**. Lines that *look* parallel but have no arrows may not be parallel at all, and the rules for parallel lines will not work on them.

### Check yourself

Which of these segments look parallel to each other? (Some have no partner.)

![Eight segments a to h at different slants](assets/img/class7/math/5/which_parallel.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **a ∥ e** (both slope gently upwards to the right), **b ∥ g** (both slope steeply down to the right), **c ∥ h** (both nearly upright, leaning the same way). **d** and **f** have no partner: d is almost flat, and f slopes up more steeply than a and e.
>
> **How to decide:** two segments are parallel when they point in the **same direction**. Slide one, without turning it, until it lies over the other: if they line up, they are parallel. (The sign ∥ means "is parallel to".)

## 5.5 Parallel and perpendicular lines by paper folding

**Try this (with a square sheet, a newspaper page works well).**

1. Look at the **opposite edges** of the sheet. They are **parallel** to each other.
2. Look at two **adjacent edges** (edges that meet at a corner). They meet at a right angle, so they are **perpendicular** to each other.
3. Fold the sheet **in half horizontally** (top edge onto bottom edge) and open it. The crease is a new line. Now there are **3** parallel lines: top edge, crease, bottom edge. The crease is **perpendicular** to the two vertical sides.
4. Fold it in half again (fold the folded sheet once more) and open it. Now there are **5** parallel lines.
5. One more time: **9** parallel lines.

![Square sheets after 1, 2 and 3 horizontal folds, showing 3, 5 and 9 parallel lines](assets/img/class7/math/5/folds.svg)

**Why 3, 5, 9?** Each new fold puts a crease in the **middle of every strip**, so the number of strips **doubles**: 2, then 4, then 8 strips. There is always **one more line than strips** (a line at each edge and between every two strips). So with n folds there are 2ⁿ strips and 2ⁿ + 1 lines. The next fold would give 16 strips and **17** lines.

6. Now make a **vertical** fold. The new crease is **perpendicular** to all the horizontal lines, because it runs straight down, like the sides of the sheet.
7. Fold the sheet along a **diagonal** (corner to opposite corner). Can you make a fold **parallel** to the diagonal? Yes: fold a corner (one of the two that are not on the diagonal) inward so that its crease runs in the same direction as the diagonal.

### Check yourself

Take a square sheet. Fold it in half down the middle and unfold. Then fold the left and right edges in to meet that middle crease and unfold. You now have three vertical creases. Next, fold the top-right corner down so its edge lies along the right-hand crease, and fold the bottom-left corner up so its edge lies along the left-hand crease. Each folded corner makes a small triangle, as shaded here. Is **a ∥ p**, **b ∥ q** and **c ∥ r**? Why?

![Folded square with corner triangles; sides a, b, c at top right and p, q, r at bottom left](assets/img/class7/math/5/corner_folds.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, all three pairs are parallel.**
> - **a ∥ p:** a lies along the top edge and p along the bottom edge of the square, and opposite edges of a square are parallel.
> - **b ∥ q:** both lie along vertical creases, and every vertical crease is perpendicular to the top edge. Two lines that make the same angle (90°) with the top edge point the same way, so they are parallel. (Section 5.6 explains exactly why this test works.)
> - **c ∥ r:** each triangle has a right angle and two equal sides, so its slanted side makes **45°** with the edge. Both slanted sides lean the same way at 45°, so they point in the same direction: parallel.

## 5.6 Parallel lines on dot paper

Dot paper makes parallel and perpendicular lines easy to check, because you can **count steps**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Step rule for parallel:** describe each segment by its steps, like "2 right, 1 up". Segments with the **same steps** (or the same steps multiplied, like "4 right, 2 up") point in the same direction, so they are **parallel**.
> **Step rule for perpendicular:** turn the steps a quarter turn: **swap the two numbers and change one direction**. "2 right, 1 up" becomes "1 left, 2 up" (or "1 right, 2 down").
>
> **Why the perpendicular rule works:** turning a segment through 90° turns its "right" movement into "up" movement and its "up" movement into "left" movement. So the numbers swap and one direction flips, and the new segment makes a right angle with the old one.

Judging by eye only works so well. To be **sure** two lines are parallel, we need a better tool: a third line drawn across them.

## 5.7 Transversals

A **transversal** is a line that crosses two (or more) other lines. Here line t crosses lines l and m. It makes **8 angles**, 4 at each crossing, numbered like this:

![Transversal t crossing lines l and m, making angles 1 to 8](assets/img/class7/math/5/transversal_8.svg)

**Can all 8 angles be different?** **No.** At each crossing, vertically opposite angles are equal: ∠1 = ∠3, ∠2 = ∠4, ∠5 = ∠7 and ∠6 = ∠8. So the 8 angles have **at most 4 different sizes**.

**Could ∠2, ∠3, ∠4, ∠5 and ∠6 be five different sizes?** **No**, because ∠2 = ∠4 (vertically opposite).

## 5.8 Corresponding angles

Each angle at the first crossing has a partner in the **same position** at the second crossing. These pairs are **corresponding angles**: ∠1 and ∠5 (both top-left), ∠2 and ∠6 (both top-right), ∠3 and ∠7 (both bottom-right), ∠4 and ∠8 (both bottom-left).

### Making a parallel line with equal corresponding angles

**Try this, step by step.**

1. Draw a line l and a transversal t crossing it at a point X.
2. Measure the angle between l and t at X, on the top-right. Say it is **60°**. Its linear-pair neighbour is then 120°, so there are already two different angle sizes: 60° and 120°.
3. Mark a point Y lower down on t.
4. Through Y, draw a line m that makes **60°** with t, **in the same position** (top-right). Copy the angle with tracing paper, or use a protractor.

![Line l and transversal t make 60° at X; line m through Y also makes 60° with t](assets/img/class7/math/5/make_parallel.svg)

Look at l and m: **they come out parallel.** The two 60° angles are corresponding angles, and they are equal.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **If the corresponding angles are equal, the lines are parallel.**
> **If the lines are parallel, the corresponding angles are equal.**
>
> **Why it makes sense:** a corresponding angle tells you how much each line is **tilted** compared with the same transversal. Equal angles mean equal tilt: both lines point in **exactly the same direction**, so they can never get closer and meet. If one line were tilted even slightly more, the two lines would slowly lean towards each other and meet somewhere, and then the corresponding angles could not be equal.

**Try this.** In this figure, l ∥ m (see the arrows) and t is a transversal. Trace ∠a on tracing paper and place it over ∠b. They match exactly. Check the other three pairs of corresponding angles with a protractor: each pair is equal.

![Parallel lines l and m with transversal t; corresponding angles a and b](assets/img/class7/math/5/corresponding_par.svg)

**Try this.** These two lines are **not** parallel. Try to draw a transversal so that a pair of corresponding angles is equal.

![Two lines l and m that are not parallel](assets/img/class7/math/5/not_parallel.svg)

You cannot do it. **When two lines are not parallel, their corresponding angles are never equal**, because the lines have different tilts, so every transversal meets them at different angles.

## 5.9 Drawing parallel lines

**With a ruler and a set square.** Draw a line l with a ruler. Place a set square on l and draw a line along its upright edge. Slide the set square along l and draw another line. Both new lines make **90°** with l. Seen with l as the transversal, these 90° angles are **corresponding angles**, and they are equal, so the two new lines are **parallel**.

You can also use the long slanted edge of the set square: hold a ruler firmly, slide the set square along the ruler, and draw along the slanted edge in two places. Every line you draw makes the **same angle** with the ruler, so the lines are parallel.

### Check yourself

**1.** Draw a line through point A parallel to line l, using tools from your geometry box. Describe your method.

![Line l and a point A above it](assets/img/class7/math/5/parallel_through_A.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Tools:** ruler, set square, pencil.
> 1. Place one edge of the set square exactly along line l.
> 2. Hold a ruler firmly against another edge of the set square, so the ruler cannot move.
> 3. Slide the set square along the ruler until its first edge reaches point A.

>

**2.** Line l is a crease on a sheet of paper, and A is a point not on it. Fold a crease t through A that is **perpendicular** to l. Then fold a crease m through A that is perpendicular to t. Why is m parallel to l?

> [!ANSWER]
> ![Answer: t perpendicular to l through A, and m perpendicular to t through A](assets/img/class7/math/5/parallel_through_A_ans.svg)
>
> 1. l is perpendicular to t, so the angle between l and t is **90°**.
> 2. m is perpendicular to t, so the angle between m and t is also **90°**.
> 3. With t as the transversal, these two 90° angles are **corresponding angles**, and they are equal.
> 4. Equal corresponding angles mean the lines are parallel, so **m ∥ l**.

## 5.10 Alternate angles

Here are parallel lines l and m with transversal t. The angles are lettered a to h.

![Parallel lines l and m with transversal t; angles a, b, c, d at l and e, f, g, h at m](assets/img/class7/math/5/alternate.svg)

**∠d and ∠f** are called **alternate angles**: both are **between** the two lines, on **opposite sides** of the transversal. **∠c and ∠e** are also alternate angles.

**Worked example.** If ∠f = 120°, find its alternate angle ∠d.

1. ∠b and ∠f are corresponding angles, and l ∥ m, so ∠b = ∠f = **120°**.
2. ∠d and ∠b are vertically opposite, so ∠d = ∠b = **120°**.

So ∠d = ∠f = 120°.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When the lines are parallel, alternate angles are equal.**
> **Why (for any size of ∠f):** ∠f = ∠b (corresponding, lines parallel) and ∠b = ∠d (vertically opposite). So ∠f = ∠d, always.

## 5.11 Interior angles on the same side

∠c and ∠f are both between the lines and both on the **same side** (the right) of the transversal. They are called **interior angles on the same side**. So are ∠d and ∠e.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When the lines are parallel, interior angles on the same side add up to 180°.**
> **Why:** ∠b + ∠c = 180° (a linear pair at line l). And ∠b = ∠f (corresponding, lines parallel). Put ∠f in place of ∠b: **∠f + ∠c = 180°.**

### All the angle facts in one table

| Pair of angles | Where they are | When is the rule true? | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear pair | side by side on one line | **always** | add to 180° |
| Vertically opposite | facing each other at one crossing | **always** | equal |
| Corresponding | same position at the two crossings | only when the lines are **parallel** | equal |
| Alternate | between the lines, opposite sides of t | only when the lines are **parallel** | equal |
| Interior, same side | between the lines, same side of t | only when the lines are **parallel** | add to 180° |

## 5.12 Worked examples

**Example 1.** Lines l and m are parallel, and t is a transversal. ∠6 = 135°. Find all the other angles.

![Parallel lines l and m, transversal t, angles 1 to 8 with angle 6 = 135°](assets/img/class7/math/5/ex1.svg)

1. ∠2 corresponds to ∠6, and l ∥ m, so **∠2 = 135°**.
2. ∠8 is vertically opposite ∠6, so **∠8 = 135°**.
3. ∠4 corresponds to ∠8, so **∠4 = 135°**.
4. ∠5 and ∠6 are a linear pair, so ∠5 = 180° − 135° = **45°**.
5. In the same way, ∠7 (vertically opposite ∠5) = 45°, and ∠1 and ∠3 (corresponding to ∠5 and ∠7) = 45°.

So **∠2, ∠4, ∠6, ∠8 = 135°** and **∠1, ∠3, ∠5, ∠7 = 45°**.

> [!TIP]
> **Shortcut:** two parallel lines and a transversal make only **two** angle sizes, and the two sizes add up to 180°. All the "wide" angles are equal, and all the "narrow" angles are equal.

**Example 2.** Lines l and m are cut by transversal t. ∠a = 120° and ∠f = 70°. Are l and m parallel?

![Lines l and m with transversal t; a = 120° at l, f = 70° at m](assets/img/class7/math/5/ex2.svg)

1. ∠a and ∠b are a linear pair, so ∠b = 180° − 120° = **60°**.
2. ∠b and ∠f are corresponding angles (both top-right).
3. If l and m were parallel, ∠b would equal ∠f. But 60° ≠ 70°.
4. So **l and m are not parallel.**

**Example 3.** Lines l and m are parallel. ∠3 = 50°. Find ∠6.

![Parallel lines l and m; angle 3 = 50° at l, find angle 6 at m](assets/img/class7/math/5/ex3.svg)

1. ∠2 and ∠3 are a linear pair, so ∠2 = 180° − 50° = **130°**.
2. ∠2 and ∠6 are corresponding angles, and l ∥ m, so **∠6 = 130°**.
3. Check: ∠3 and ∠6 are interior angles on the same side, and 50° + 130° = 180°, just as the rule says.

**Example 4.** In ABCD, AB ∥ DC and AD ∥ BC. The diagonal AC is drawn. ∠DAC = 65° and ∠ADC = 60°. Find ∠CAB, ∠ABC and ∠BCD.

![Four-sided shape ABCD with AB ∥ DC and AD ∥ BC, diagonal AC, ∠DAC = 65°, ∠ADC = 60°](assets/img/class7/math/5/ex4.svg)

1. Look at AB ∥ DC with AD as the transversal. ∠DAB and ∠ADC are interior angles on the same side, so ∠DAB + 60° = 180°, giving **∠DAB = 120°**.
2. The diagonal splits ∠DAB into two parts: ∠DAB = ∠DAC + ∠CAB, so 120° = 65° + ∠CAB, giving **∠CAB = 55°**.
3. Look at AD ∥ BC with DC as the transversal. ∠ADC + ∠BCD = 180° (interior, same side), so **∠BCD = 120°**.
4. Look at AD ∥ BC with AB as the transversal. ∠DAB + ∠ABC = 180°, so ∠ABC = 180° − 120° = **60°**.

> [!NOTE]
> A four-sided shape with both pairs of opposite sides parallel is a **parallelogram**. This example shows a general fact about it: **neighbouring angles add up to 180°** and **opposite angles are equal** (60° and 60°, 120° and 120°).

## 5.13 Angle chasing: a method for any figure

1. **Find the parallel lines** (arrow marks) and a **transversal** that crosses both.
2. **Name the relationship** between the angle you know and the one you want: vertically opposite, linear pair, corresponding, alternate, or interior on the same side.
3. **Write one line of reason for each step**, for example "∠x = 65° (alternate angles, l ∥ m)".
4. **Stuck because no single line crosses both parallels where you need it?** Draw an **extra line through the corner, parallel to the given lines**. Then use alternate angles on each side.

**Worked example (a bend between parallel lines).** Two parallel lines are joined by a path that bends at O. The path makes 35° with the top line and 50° with the bottom line. Find the angle at the bend.

![Two parallel lines; a path bends at O making 35° with the top line and 50° with the bottom; a dashed parallel line through O splits the bend into 35° and 50°](assets/img/class7/math/5/bend.svg)

1. Draw a dashed line through O parallel to both lines.
2. The upper part of the path is a transversal of the top line and the dashed line. The angle it makes at O equals the 35° at the top line (alternate angles). So the upper part of the bend is **35°**.
3. In the same way, the lower part of the bend equals the 50° at the bottom line (alternate angles): **50°**.
4. The bend = 35° + 50° = **85°**.

> [!TIP]
> **Bend rule:** when a path bends once between two parallel lines, the angle at the bend equals the **sum** of the angles the path makes with the two lines. You can always re-derive it with the extra parallel line, as above.

## 5.14 Parallel illusions

In this picture the two red lines are **exactly parallel**. Check with a ruler! But most people see them bowing outwards in the middle.

![Two parallel red lines over a fan of grey lines from the centre; the red lines look curved](assets/img/class7/math/5/illusion.svg)

**What causes it?** Our eyes judge a line by comparing it with the lines around it. The slanting lines cross the red lines at angles that change along their length, and the brain mistakes those changing angles for a bend. This is one more reason mathematicians trust **reasoning and measuring** over how a figure **looks**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **∠NOP = 108°.** No single line joins the parallel lines where we need one, so draw **extra lines through N and O, parallel to LM and PQ** (dashed below).
>

## In a nutshell

- Two crossing lines make four angles. **Linear pairs add up to 180°**, and **vertically opposite angles are equal**. Both are true for **every** crossing.
- **Perpendicular** lines cross at 90°; if one angle is 90°, all four are.
- **Parallel** lines lie on the same plane and never meet. Show them with matching **arrow marks**.
- On dot paper, **same steps = parallel**; **swap the steps and flip one direction = perpendicular**.
- A **transversal** across two lines makes 8 angles, with at most 4 different sizes.
- **Corresponding angles are equal ⇔ the lines are parallel.** This is how we can be sure lines are parallel.
- When the lines are parallel: **alternate angles are equal**, and **interior angles on the same side add up to 180°**.
- Two parallel lines and a transversal make only **two angle sizes**, and they add up to 180°.
- Stuck? Draw an **extra line parallel to the given lines** through a corner, then use alternate angles.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** corresponding and alternate angles are equal **only if the lines are parallel** (vertically opposite angles are equal always) · interior angles on the same side **add to 180°**, they are not equal · lines on different planes are not "parallel" · 8 angles, but never more than 4 different sizes · ignore angles on a **different** transversal · don't trust how a figure **looks**: check the arrow marks.
