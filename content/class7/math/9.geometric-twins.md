## What this chapter is about

Two copies of a photo, two coins of the same kind, the two halves of a cut sandwich: they are **exact copies** of each other, like twins. In geometry such twins are called **congruent** figures. In this chapter we ask:

- What does it mean for two figures to be congruent?
- What is the **least** we need to measure to make an exact copy of a triangle?
- How can we **prove** two triangles are congruent without placing one on the other?
- What does congruence tell us about the angles of **isosceles** and **equilateral** triangles?

The big idea: if a few measurements **fix** a triangle completely, then any two triangles with those measurements must be twins. You already learnt to *construct* triangles from measurements in Chapter 7; now that skill tells us when two triangles must be the same.

## 9.1 Exact copies: congruent figures

A signboard has a symbol shaped like a bent arm, with corner points A, B and C. We want to paint the same symbol on a new board. Tracing is hard when the symbol is big. What should we **measure** instead?

**Are the arm lengths enough?** Say AB = 4 cm and BC = 8 cm. Here are three symbols, all with these arm lengths:

![Three bent-arm symbols, each with arms 4 cm and 8 cm, but opened to 40°, 80° and 130°](assets/img/class7/math/9/symbol_arms.svg)

They are clearly **different**: the arms are opened to different angles. So the lengths alone don't fix the shape. But once we also know the angle, say **∠ABC = 80°**, there is only one way to draw it: draw BC = 8 cm, turn 80° at B, and draw BA = 4 cm. **Two arm lengths and the angle between them fix the symbol.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Congruent figures** have exactly the **same shape and the same size**. One can be placed on the other so that they **fit exactly**. We say one can be **superimposed** on the other.
> While checking, you may **slide** a figure, **turn** it, or **flip** it over. None of these changes its shape or size.
> The sign **≅** means "is congruent to".

![An L-shape and three congruent copies: slid, turned and flipped](assets/img/class7/math/9/moves.svg)

**Why flipping is allowed:** flipping a paper cut-out over doesn't stretch or bend it. Every length and every angle stays the same, so it is still an exact copy, only facing the other way. Your left hand and right hand are congruent in this sense.

> [!TIP]
> To decide what to measure, ask: **"If I keep these measurements the same, can the figure still wiggle into a different shape?"** If it can, you need another measurement.

**Try this.** Trace one of two shapes that look the same onto tracing paper, then slide, turn or flip the tracing over the other shape. If it fits exactly, the shapes are congruent.

**Question 3.** What would you measure to make a figure congruent to a given:

**(a)** circle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Its radius** (or its diameter). Every circle with that radius is the same size, and all circles have the same shape. **To check two circles:** they are congruent exactly when their **radii are equal**.

**(b)** rectangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Its length and its breadth.** Every corner of a rectangle is 90°, so once you know the two side lengths there is nothing left that can change. **To check two rectangles:** they are congruent when their **lengths are equal and their breadths are equal**.

**4.** A figure is made of a segment PQ with a branch RS sticking out of it at R. How would you check whether two such figures are congruent? Use your method on pairs (a) and (b).

![A branch figure with PR 2 cm, RQ 3 cm, RS 2.5 cm and 60° between RQ and RS; pairs (a) and (b) below](assets/img/class7/math/9/cy_branch.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **What to measure:** the three lengths **PR, RQ and RS**, and the **angle between RS and RQ**. Together they fix the figure: the lengths say where R is on PQ and how long the branch is, and the angle says which way the branch points.
>
> - **(a) Congruent.** Measuring both figures gives the same three lengths and the same angle; the second is turned and flipped.
> - **(b) Not congruent.** The lengths match, but the branch makes a **different angle** in the second figure (it stands almost straight up), so the shapes differ.

## 9.2 Congruent triangles: the SSS condition

Meera and Rabia must make a cardboard copy of a big triangular frame at school, too big to trace. They measure its sides: **40 cm, 60 cm and 80 cm**. Rabia reaches for a protractor to measure the angles too. Meera stops her: "We don't need the angles. The sides are enough." Is Meera right?

Think of a smaller version that fits on a page: sides **4 cm, 6 cm and 8 cm**. Rabia constructs it: draw AB = 6 cm, then an arc of radius 4 cm from A and an arc of radius 8 cm from B. But the arcs cross at **two** points, E above AB and F below it!

![AB = 6 cm with arcs of 4 cm from A and 8 cm from B crossing at E and F, giving triangles ABE and ABF](assets/img/class7/math/9/sss_two.svg)

Are ∆ABE and ∆ABF different triangles? **No.** The construction above AB is exactly the same as below it, so AB works like a **mirror line**: ∆ABF is ∆ABE **flipped over AB**. Cut them out and you'll see one fits exactly on the other.

So **every** triangle with sides 4, 6 and 8 cm is the same triangle, perhaps moved, turned or flipped. Meera was right.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SSS (Side–Side–Side) condition: if the three sides of one triangle are equal to the three sides of another, the triangles are congruent.**
> **Why:** three side lengths fix the compass construction completely. The only choice left is "above or below the base", and that is just a flip.

### Writing congruence the right way

These two triangles are congruent. To make them fit, we must place the **equal sides** on each other.

![Congruent triangles ABC and XYZ; equal sides carry the same number of tick marks](assets/img/class7/math/9/correspond.svg)

The tick marks show AB = XY, BC = YZ and CA = ZX. So A must go on X, B on Y and C on Z. Then the angles that land on each other are equal too: ∠A = ∠X, ∠B = ∠Y, ∠C = ∠Z.

The matching pieces are called **corresponding** parts:
- **Corresponding vertices:** A and X, B and Y, C and Z.
- **Corresponding sides:** AB and XY, BC and YZ, AC and XZ.
- **Corresponding angles:** ∠A and ∠X, ∠B and ∠Y, ∠C and ∠Z.

We write **∆ABC ≅ ∆XYZ**, and the order of the letters carries the matching:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In **∆ABC ≅ ∆XYZ**, the **1st** letters correspond (A ↔ X), the **2nd** letters correspond (B ↔ Y), and the **3rd** letters correspond (C ↔ Z).
> So **∆ACB ≅ ∆XZY** is also correct (both names reordered the same way), but **∆ACB ≅ ∆XYZ is wrong**: it would pair C with Y.
> **Why it matters:** once you write the congruence correctly, you can read off every pair of equal sides and equal angles straight from the letters.

**Worked example.** ABCD is a rectangle and BD is a diagonal. Are ∆ABD and ∆CDB congruent?

![Rectangle ABCD with diagonal BD](assets/img/class7/math/9/rectangle_bd.svg)

1. Opposite sides of a rectangle are equal: **AB = CD** and **AD = CB**.
2. The diagonal **BD** is a side of both triangles: it is equal to itself.
3. Three pairs of equal sides: by **SSS** the triangles are congruent.

But **which** vertices match? Suppose we tried A ↔ C, B ↔ B, D ↔ D. That would place side AB on side CB. In a rectangle AB and CB need not be equal (one is the length, the other the breadth), so this matching is wrong. We find the right one in the next Check yourself.

### Check yourself

**1.** Is there any other way to place ∆ABC on ∆XYZ (in the picture above) so that they fit exactly?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** All three sides of ∆ABC have **different** lengths (one, two and three tick marks). Each side can only go on the side of ∆XYZ with the same length, so AB must go on XY, BC on YZ and CA on ZX. That fixes A ↔ X, B ↔ Y, C ↔ Z: only **one** way. (A triangle with two or three equal sides could fit in more ways; see Exercise 4.)

**2.** In the rectangle, find the correct matching of vertices for ∆ABD and ∆CDB, and write the congruence.

> [!ANSWER]
> Match the equal sides:
> 1. AB = CD, so the side AB goes on CD.
> 2. AD = CB, so AD goes on CB.
> 3. A is the vertex **between** AB and AD; the vertex between CD and CB is **C**. So **A ↔ C**.
> 4. B is at the other end of AB; the other end of CD is **D**. So **B ↔ D**, and then **D ↔ B**.
>
> **∆ABD ≅ ∆CDB.** (Read it letter by letter: A↔C, B↔D, D↔B. Then AB ↔ CD, BD ↔ DB, AD ↔ CB: all equal, as they must be.)
>
> **Try this:** cut a rectangle along its diagonal. Turn one half half a turn and it fits exactly on the other.

**3.** ∆HEN ≅ ∆BIG. Write all the other correct ways of expressing this congruence.

> [!ANSWER]
> The matching is H ↔ B, E ↔ I, N ↔ G. Any order of H, E, N works, as long as B, I, G follow in the **same** order:
> - ∆HNE ≅ ∆BGI
> - ∆EHN ≅ ∆IBG
> - ∆ENH ≅ ∆IGB
> - ∆NHE ≅ ∆GBI
> - ∆NEH ≅ ∆GIB
>
> That makes **5 other ways** (6 in all, because three letters can be ordered in 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 ways).

**4.** Are these triangles congruent? If yes, write the congruence.

![Triangle RED with RE 3.5 cm, ED 5 cm, RD 6 cm, and triangle JAM with JA 3.5 cm, AM 5 cm, JM 6 cm](assets/img/class7/math/9/red_jam.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, by SSS:** RE = JA = 3.5 cm, ED = AM = 5 cm, RD = JM = 6 cm.
>
> **Matching the vertices:**
> - E is between the 3.5 cm and 5 cm sides; so is A. **E ↔ A.**
> - R is between the 3.5 cm and 6 cm sides; so is J. **R ↔ J.**
> - D is between the 5 cm and 6 cm sides; so is M. **D ↔ M.**
>
> **∆RED ≅ ∆JAM.**

**Question 5.** In this figure AB = AD and CB = CD.

![A kite ABCD with AB = AD (one tick) and CB = CD (two ticks), and the diagonal AC dashed](assets/img/class7/math/9/kite.svg)

**(a)** Is there a pair of congruent triangles? Explain why.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes: ∆ABC ≅ ∆ADC.**
> 1. AB = AD (given).
> 2. CB = CD (given).
> 3. AC is a side of both triangles.
>
> Three pairs of equal sides, so they are congruent by **SSS**. Matching: A ↔ A, B ↔ D, C ↔ C.

**(b)** Does AC divide ∠BAD and ∠BCD into two equal parts? Give reasons.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, both.** From ∆ABC ≅ ∆ADC (part a), the corresponding angles are equal:
> - ∠BAC ↔ ∠DAC, so **∠BAC = ∠DAC**: AC cuts ∠BAD into two equal halves.
> - ∠BCA ↔ ∠DCA, so **∠BCA = ∠DCA**: AC cuts ∠BCD into two equal halves.
>
> ![The kite with equal half-angles marked at A and at C](assets/img/class7/math/9/kite_ans.svg)
>
> **Why we can say this:** corresponding parts of congruent triangles are always equal, because they lie exactly on top of each other when the triangles are superimposed.

**6.** In this figure DF = DG and FE = GE. Are ∆DFE and ∆GED congruent?

![Triangle-like figure with D at the top, E inside, F and G at the bottom; DF = DG and FE = GE](assets/img/class7/math/9/dfe_ged.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **The two triangles are congruent, but the name ∆DFE ≅ ∆GED is written wrongly.**
> 1. DF = DG (given), FE = GE (given), and DE is a side of both triangles.
> 2. By **SSS** the triangles DFE and DGE are congruent.
> 3. Matching: D ↔ D (between the single-tick side and DE), F ↔ G, E ↔ E.
>
> So the correct statement is **∆DFE ≅ ∆DGE**. Writing "∆DFE ≅ ∆GED" would pair D with G and F with E, which are not corresponding vertices.

## 9.3 Do three angles fix a triangle?

What if Meera and Rabia measured the three **angles** instead, say 30°, 70° and 80°?

![Three triangles of different sizes, each with angles 30°, 70° and 80°](assets/img/class7/math/9/aaa.svg)

All three triangles have angles 30°, 70° and 80°, but they are of **different sizes**: same shape, different size. So they are **not** congruent.

> [!WARNING]
> **Equal angles alone (AAA) do NOT make triangles congruent.** A photo and its enlargement have the same angles but are not the same size.

## 9.4 Two sides and an angle

### The angle between the two sides: SAS

**Worked example.** ∆ABC and ∆XYZ have **AB = XY = 6 cm, AC = XZ = 5 cm and ∠A = ∠X = 30°**. Are they congruent?

These are two sides and the **included** angle (the angle between them). Construct it: draw AB = 6 cm, turn 30° at A, and go 5 cm along to C, then join BC. At no step was there a choice, so **every** student's triangle comes out the same.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **SAS (Side–Angle–Side) condition: if two sides and the angle between them are equal in two triangles, the triangles are congruent.**
> **Why:** the angle fixes the direction of the second side and the lengths fix where both sides end, so the third side has only one possible position.

### An angle that is NOT between the sides: SSA fails

Now: **AB = XY = 6 cm, AC = XZ = 4 cm and ∠B = ∠Y = 30°.** The 30° angle is at B, which is **not** between the sides AB and AC. Let's try to construct it (calling the triangle PQR for now).

1. Draw **PQ = 6 cm**.
2. At P draw a line l making **30°** with PQ.
3. With centre Q, draw an arc of radius **4 cm**.

![PQ = 6 cm, a 30° line from P, and a 4 cm arc from Q cutting the line at two points R and S](assets/img/class7/math/9/ssa.svg)

The arc cuts line l at **two** points, R and S. Both ∆PQR and ∆PQS have PQ = 6 cm, a 4 cm side from Q, and 30° at P. But ∆PQR is short and wide and ∆PQS is tall: **not congruent**.

> [!WARNING]
> **SSA (Side–Side–Angle) does NOT guarantee congruence.** The side opposite the given angle can swing into two different positions.

### Two angles and the side between them: ASA

∆ABC and ∆XYZ have **BC = YZ = 5 cm, ∠B = ∠Y = 50° and ∠C = ∠Z = 30°**. Construct: draw BC = 5 cm and rays at 50° from B and 30° from C. Two rays can cross in only one point, so A has just one possible place.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ASA (Angle–Side–Angle) condition: if two angles and the side between them are equal in two triangles, the triangles are congruent.**
> **Why:** the side fixes where the two rays start, the angles fix their directions, and two rays meet in exactly one point.

**Worked example.** O is the midpoint of both AD and BC. What can we say about AB and CD?

![Segments AD and BC cross at O, their common midpoint](assets/img/class7/math/9/midpoint_o.svg)

1. **AO = OD**, because O is the midpoint of AD.
2. **BO = OC**, because O is the midpoint of BC.
3. **∠AOB = ∠DOC**, because they are vertically opposite angles.
4. The angle at O lies **between** the two pairs of sides, so **SAS** holds: the triangles are congruent.
5. **Matching:** AO must lie on DO and BO on CO, so A ↔ D, O ↔ O, B ↔ C. **∆AOB ≅ ∆DOC.**
6. AB and DC are corresponding sides, so **AB = DC**.

### Check yourself

**1.** Explain why **all** triangles with AB = 6 cm, AC = 5 cm and ∠A = 30° are congruent.

> [!ANSWER]
> Follow the construction: draw AB = 6 cm (no choice), draw the 30° ray at A (no choice, apart from drawing it above or below AB, which is only a flip), mark C at 5 cm along the ray (no choice), join BC (no choice). Every step has **only one** possible result, so everyone draws the same triangle, perhaps flipped. Hence all such triangles are congruent.

**2.** Are these triangles congruent? Which condition did you use? Write the congruence.

![Triangle ABC with BA 7 cm, BC 5 cm and 47° at B; triangle XYZ with ZX 7 cm, ZY 5 cm and 47° at Z](assets/img/class7/math/9/sas_47.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, by SAS.**
> 1. BA = ZX = 7 cm.
> 2. BC = ZY = 5 cm.
> 3. ∠B = ∠Z = 47°, and in each triangle this angle is **between** the two given sides.
>
> Matching: B ↔ Z (the 47° corners), A ↔ X (the ends of the 7 cm sides), C ↔ Y (the ends of the 5 cm sides). **∆ABC ≅ ∆XZY.**

**3.** CD and AB are parallel, and AB = CD. Segments AC and BD cross at O. What other parts are equal? Are the two triangles congruent?

![CD parallel to AB with AB = CD; AC and BD cross at O](assets/img/class7/math/9/par_cd_ab.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. AC is a transversal of the parallel lines AB and CD, so the **alternate angles** are equal: **∠OAB = ∠OCD**.
> 2. BD is also a transversal, so **∠OBA = ∠ODC** (alternate angles).
> 3. **AB = CD** (given), and AB lies between the angles at A and B, just as CD lies between the angles at C and D.
> 4. So **ASA** holds: **∆AOB ≅ ∆COD** (A ↔ C, O ↔ O, B ↔ D).
>
> ![The equal alternate angles marked](assets/img/class7/math/9/par_cd_ab_ans.svg)
>
> **Other equal parts** (corresponding parts): **AO = CO**, **BO = DO** and **∠AOB = ∠COD**. So O is the midpoint of both AC and BD.

**4.** ∠ABC = ∠DBC and ∠ACB = ∠DCB. Show that ∠BAC = ∠BDC. Are the two triangles congruent?

![A above and D below the line BC; equal angles marked at B (one arc) and at C (two arcs)](assets/img/class7/math/9/two_angle_pairs.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Showing ∠BAC = ∠BDC (angle sum):**
> 1. In ∆ABC: ∠BAC = 180° − ∠ABC − ∠ACB.
> 2. In ∆DBC: ∠BDC = 180° − ∠DBC − ∠DCB.
> 3. The right-hand sides are equal, because ∠ABC = ∠DBC and ∠ACB = ∠DCB. So **∠BAC = ∠BDC**.
>
> **Congruent?** **Yes, by ASA:** ∠ABC = ∠DBC, the side **BC** is common and lies between these angles, and ∠ACB = ∠DCB. So **∆ABC ≅ ∆DBC** (A ↔ D, B ↔ B, C ↔ C). This also gives AB = DB and AC = DC.

**5.** In this figure ∠ABD = ∠DCA and ∠ACB = ∠DBC. Identify the equal parts.

![Points A and D above BC; AB, DC, AC and BD drawn; equal angles marked at B and C](assets/img/class7/math/9/cross_abdc.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Whole angles at B and C:** ∠ABC = ∠ABD + ∠DBC and ∠DCB = ∠DCA + ∠ACB. The pieces are equal pair by pair (given), so **∠ABC = ∠DCB**.
> 2. Now look at ∆ABC and ∆DCB: ∠ABC = ∠DCB (step 1), side **BC is common** and lies between these angles, and ∠ACB = ∠DBC (given). By **ASA**, **∆ABC ≅ ∆DCB** (A ↔ D, B ↔ C, C ↔ B).
> 3. **Equal parts from the congruence:** **AB = DC**, **AC = DB**, and **∠BAC = ∠CDB**.

## 9.5 Two angles and a side not between them: AAS

**Worked example.** ∆ABC and ∆XYZ have **∠A = ∠X = 35°, ∠C = ∠Z = 75° and BC = YZ = 4 cm**. Here BC is **not** between ∠A and ∠C. Are they congruent?

**Use the angle sum to find the missing angle.**
1. ∠B + 35° + 75° = 180°, so ∠B + 110° = 180°, and **∠B = 70°**.
2. In the same way, **∠Y = 70°**.

![Two triangles with 35°, 75° and the found 70°, each with a 4 cm side between the 70° and 75° angles](assets/img/class7/math/9/aas.svg)

Now ∠B = ∠Y, BC = YZ and ∠C = ∠Z, with BC **between** ∠B and ∠C. That is **ASA**, so **∆ABC ≅ ∆XYZ**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **AAS (Angle–Angle–Side) condition: if two angles and a side not between them are equal in two triangles, the triangles are congruent.**
> **Why:** two equal angles force the third angles to be equal too (each is 180° minus the other two), and then the side is between two known angles, so ASA applies.

## 9.6 Right triangles: the RHS condition

SSA usually fails. But there is one important case where it works: when the angle is a **right angle**.

**Worked example.** ∆ABC and ∆XYZ have **∠B = ∠Y = 90°, BC = YZ = 4 cm and AC = XZ = 5 cm**. Are they congruent? Construct (calling it PQR):

1. Draw **QR = 4 cm**.
2. At Q draw a line l **perpendicular** to QR.
3. With centre R, draw an arc of radius **5 cm** cutting l.
4. The arc cuts l at P. Join PR.

![QR = 4 cm, a perpendicular line l at Q, and a 5 cm arc from R cutting l at P above and P′ below](assets/img/class7/math/9/rhs.svg)

The arc also cuts l at a point **P′ below** QR. Does that give a different triangle? **No:** l is perpendicular to QR, so the picture below QR is exactly the **mirror image** of the picture above it. ∆P′QR is just ∆PQR flipped over QR. So all triangles with these measurements are congruent, and ∆ABC ≅ ∆XYZ.

The side **opposite the right angle** is called the **hypotenuse**. It is always the longest side of a right triangle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **RHS (Right angle–Hypotenuse–Side) condition: if two right triangles have equal hypotenuses and one other pair of equal sides, they are congruent.**
> **Why this SSA case works:** with a right angle, the two places where the arc meets the line are mirror images of each other, so they give the **same** triangle flipped, not a fat one and a thin one.

### All the conditions in one table

| Condition | What is equal | Congruent? |
|---|---|---|
| **SSS** | three sides | ✔ always |
| **SAS** | two sides and the angle **between** them | ✔ always |
| **ASA** | two angles and the side **between** them | ✔ always |
| **AAS** | two angles and a side **not** between them | ✔ always |
| **RHS** | right angle, hypotenuse and one more side | ✔ always |
| AAA | three angles | ✘ sizes can differ |
| SSA | two sides and an angle **not** between them | ✘ two triangles possible |

## 9.7 Using congruence: isosceles and equilateral triangles

Congruence is a powerful tool for **proving** new facts. Here is an important one.

**Worked example.** ∆ABC is isosceles with **AB = AC** and **∠A = 80°**. What can we say about ∠B and ∠C?

Draw the altitude **AD** from A to BC.

![Isosceles triangle ABC with AB = AC, ∠A = 80° and the altitude AD](assets/img/class7/math/9/iso_altitude.svg)

1. In ∆ADB and ∆ADC: **AB = AC** (given). These are the hypotenuses, because they are opposite the right angles at D.
2. **∠ADB = ∠ADC = 90°**, because AD is an altitude.
3. **AD** is a side of both triangles.
4. So by **RHS**, **∆ADB ≅ ∆ADC**, and the corresponding angles **∠B and ∠C are equal**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In any triangle, the angles opposite equal sides are equal.** (In an isosceles triangle, the two "base angles" are equal.)
> **Why:** the altitude from the top vertex cuts the triangle into two right triangles that are congruent by RHS, so the base angles match.

### Angles of an equilateral triangle

In an equilateral triangle ABC, all sides are equal.
- AB = AC, so the angles opposite them are equal: **∠C = ∠B**.
- AB = BC, so **∠C = ∠A**.

So **all three angles are equal**. They add up to 180°, so 3 × (each angle) = 180°, and **each angle is 60°**. We have *proved* this using congruence, not just measured it.

**Try this.** Construct an equilateral triangle with your compass and measure its angles. Each should come out at 60°.

### Check yourself

**1.** In the RHS construction, why is the triangle P′QR below QR congruent to ∆PQR?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **QR is common** to both triangles.
> 2. **∠PQR = ∠P′QR = 90°**, because l is perpendicular to QR (it makes a right angle on both sides).
> 3. **PR = P′R = 5 cm**, because both points are on the same arc about R.
>
> So by **RHS**, ∆PQR ≅ ∆P′QR. You can also see it as a flip: folding along QR puts the lower half of l exactly on the upper half (both are at 90°), and the arc on itself, so P′ lands on P.

**2.** Use "angles opposite equal sides are equal" to find ∠B and ∠C when AB = AC and ∠A = 80°.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. ∠B = ∠C (opposite the equal sides AC and AB).
> 2. Angle sum: ∠B + ∠C = 180° − 80° = 100°.
> 3. They are equal, so **∠B = ∠C = 50°**.

## 9.8 Congruent triangles around us

**Try this.** Look for congruent triangles in buildings and designs: the glass pyramid in front of a famous museum in Paris (the Louvre), the Great Pyramid of Giza in Egypt, the steel frame of the Howrah Bridge (Rabindra Setu) in Kolkata, patterned domes, and rangoli designs. **What you will see:** the same triangle repeated many times, turned or flipped. **Why builders like this:** congruent triangles in a frame are cut to one pattern, fit together neatly, and share the load equally, and a triangle's shape can't be squashed without changing a side length (SSS!), which makes triangle frames very strong.

## Exercises — try first

Solve each question on paper first, then press **Show answer** to check.

### 1. Corresponding parts

∆AIR ≅ ∆FLY. Write the corresponding vertices, sides and angles.

> [!ANSWER]
> Read the letters in order:
> - **Vertices:** A ↔ F, I ↔ L, R ↔ Y.
> - **Sides:** AI ↔ FL, IR ↔ LY, AR ↔ FY (so AI = FL, IR = LY, AR = FY).
> - **Angles:** ∠A = ∠F, ∠I = ∠L, ∠R = ∠Y.

### 2. Which pairs are congruent?

Each part gives measurements from two triangles, ABC and DEF. Say whether the triangles must be congruent, give the reason, and write the congruence when they are.

**(a)** AB = DE, BC = EF, CA = DF

> [!ANSWER]
> **Congruent by SSS.** Three pairs of equal sides. Matching A ↔ D, B ↔ E, C ↔ F: **∆ABC ≅ ∆DEF**.

**(b)** AB = EF, ∠A = ∠E, AC = ED

> [!ANSWER]
> **Congruent by SAS.** ∠A is between AB and AC, and ∠E is between EF and ED, so the equal angle is the **included** angle in both. Matching A ↔ E, B ↔ F, C ↔ D: **∆ABC ≅ ∆EFD**.

**(c)** AB = DF, ∠B = ∠D = 90°, AC = FE

> [!ANSWER]
> **Congruent by RHS.** Both are right triangles. AC is opposite the right angle at B, and FE is opposite the right angle at D, so these are the **hypotenuses**, and they are equal. One more pair of sides, AB = DF, is equal. Matching B ↔ D (the right angles), A ↔ F, C ↔ E: **∆ABC ≅ ∆FDE**.

**(d)** ∠A = ∠D, ∠B = ∠E, AC = DF

> [!ANSWER]
> **Congruent by AAS.** Two pairs of equal angles, and the side AC is **not** between ∠A and ∠B (it is opposite ∠B); in the same way DF is opposite ∠E. The third angles are equal too (∠C = 180° − ∠A − ∠B = 180° − ∠D − ∠E = ∠F), so this becomes ASA. **∆ABC ≅ ∆DEF**.

**(e)** AB = DF, ∠B = ∠F, AC = DE

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not necessarily congruent.** ∠B is between AB and **BC**, not between AB and AC. So this is two sides and an angle **not between them: SSA**, which does not guarantee congruence (the side AC could swing into two positions, as in the 6 cm, 4 cm, 30° construction).

### 3. Proving lines parallel

OB = OC and OA = OD, where AD and BC cross at O. Show that AB is parallel to CD.

![AD and BC cross at O with OA = OD (one tick) and OB = OC (two ticks); segments AB and CD drawn](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex_ob_oc.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **OA = OD** and **OB = OC** (given).
> 2. **∠AOB = ∠DOC** (vertically opposite angles), and each lies between the given sides.
> 3. So **∆AOB ≅ ∆DOC** by **SAS** (A ↔ D, O ↔ O, B ↔ C).
> 4. Corresponding angles of congruent triangles are equal: **∠OAB = ∠ODC**.
> 5. AD is a transversal of the lines AB and CD, and ∠OAB and ∠ODC are on opposite sides of it, between the two lines: they are **alternate angles**.
> 6. Equal alternate angles mean the lines are parallel, so **AB ∥ CD**.
>
> ![The equal alternate angles at A and D marked, showing AB parallel to CD](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex_ob_oc_ans.svg)

### 4. A square and its diagonal

ABCD is a square and AC is a diagonal.

![Square ABCD with diagonal AC](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex_square.svg)

**(a)** Show that ∆ABC ≅ ∆ADC.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **AB = AD** (all sides of a square are equal).
> 2. **BC = DC** (all sides of a square are equal).
> 3. **AC** is common.
>
> By **SSS**, **∆ABC ≅ ∆ADC** (A ↔ A, B ↔ D, C ↔ C). (SAS also works: AB = AD, ∠B = ∠D = 90°, BC = DC.)

**(b)** Is ∆ABC also congruent to ∆CDA?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** ∆ABC ≅ ∆CDA pairs A ↔ C, B ↔ D, C ↔ A. Check the sides: AB ↔ CD (equal, sides of the square), BC ↔ DA (equal), CA ↔ AC (the same segment). By **SSS** it holds. So one triangle fits on the other in **two different ways**. **Why:** in a square, all four sides are equal, so the two short sides of each triangle can be matched either way round.

**(c)** Give more examples of two triangles where one is congruent to the other in two different ways.

> [!ANSWER]
> Any **isosceles** triangle (that is not equilateral) and its copy. If AB = AC, then ∆ABC ≅ ∆ACB as well as ∆ABC ≅ ∆ABC: flip the triangle over its middle line and B and C swap places. For instance a triangle made by the centre of a circle and two points on it (two sides are radii), or the 75°, 5 cm, 75° triangle from Chapter 7.

**(d)** Can you give two triangles where one is congruent to the other in **six** different ways?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two equilateral triangles of the same size.** All sides are equal, so **any** matching of vertices works. For ∆ABC and ∆XYZ: ∆ABC ≅ ∆XYZ, ∆XZY, ∆YXZ, ∆YZX, ∆ZXY and ∆ZYX. That is 3 × 2 × 1 = **6 ways** (three turns, each with or without a flip).

### 5. A triangle in a circle

A is the centre of the circle, and B and C are on the circle. ∠BAC = 120°. Find ∠B and ∠C.

![Circle with centre A; B and C on the circle; ∠BAC = 120°](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex_circle.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **AB = AC**, because both are **radii** of the circle. So ∆ABC is isosceles.
> 2. Angles opposite equal sides are equal: **∠B = ∠C**.
> 3. Angle sum: ∠B + ∠C = 180° − 120° = 60°.
> 4. So **∠B = ∠C = 30°**.

### 6. Find the missing angles

Sides with the same tick marks are equal. Find each angle marked **?**.

**(a)** An isosceles triangle with 68° between the equal sides.

![Isosceles triangle with apex angle 68°](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex6a.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The two angles at the base are opposite the equal sides, so they are **equal**.
> 2. Together they make 180° − 68° = 112°.
> 3. Each is 112° ÷ 2 = **56°**.

**(b)** An isosceles triangle with 34° between the equal sides.

![Isosceles triangle with apex angle 34°](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex6b.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The base angles are equal (opposite equal sides).
> 2. Together: 180° − 34° = 146°.
> 3. Each is **73°**.

**(c)** An isosceles triangle with a right angle between the equal sides.

![Right-angled isosceles triangle](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex6c.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The base angles are equal (opposite equal sides).
> 2. Together: 180° − 90° = 90°.
> 3. Each is **45°**.

**(d)** A triangle with all three sides equal.

![Equilateral triangle with all sides ticked](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex6d.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> All sides are equal, so all angles are equal (each angle is opposite one of the equal sides). Three equal angles making 180°: each is **60°**.

**(e)** An isosceles triangle with a base angle of 44°.

![Isosceles triangle with one base angle 44°](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex6e.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The other base angle is opposite the other equal side, so it is also **44°**.
> 2. The top angle is 180° − 44° − 44° = **92°**.

**(f)** AB = AC, AD is perpendicular to BC, and ∠BAD = 34°.

![Isosceles triangle ABC with altitude AD; ∠BAD = 34°](assets/img/class7/math/9/ex6f.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. In ∆ADB and ∆ADC: AB = AC (hypotenuses), ∠ADB = ∠ADC = 90°, AD common. By **RHS**, ∆ADB ≅ ∆ADC.
> 2. So **∠CAD = ∠BAD = 34°** (corresponding angles).
> 3. In ∆ADB: ∠B = 180° − 90° − 34° = **56°**.
> 4. In the same way (or because ∠C = ∠B), **∠C = 56°**.

## In a nutshell

- **Congruent** figures have the same shape and the same size; you may **slide, turn or flip** one to fit it on the other. Sign: **≅**.
- To copy a figure exactly, measure just enough to stop it "wiggling": a circle needs its radius, a rectangle its length and breadth, a bent arm two lengths and the angle between them.
- Triangles are congruent if **SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS** or **RHS** holds. Each works because those measurements allow **only one** triangle (up to a flip).
- **AAA** (same shape, any size) and **SSA** (two possible triangles) do **not** guarantee congruence.
- Write ∆ABC ≅ ∆XYZ with **matching letters in matching places**; then corresponding sides and angles can be read off the letters.
- **Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal**, which lets us prove new facts.
- **Angles opposite equal sides are equal**, and every angle of an equilateral triangle is **60°**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** equal angles alone don't make triangles congruent · SSA is not a rule (except RHS, with a right angle) · in SAS the angle must be **between** the two sides, in ASA the side must be **between** the two angles · ∆ACB ≅ ∆XYZ is wrong if A↔X, B↔Y, C↔Z · the hypotenuse is the side **opposite** the right angle · find the matching vertices from the **equal sides**, not from how the picture looks.
