## What this chapter is about

India once had about **one lakh** varieties of rice. A city can have **more than a crore** people. The Sun is about **15 crore kilometres** away. How big are these numbers, really?

In this chapter you will learn to:

- **read and write** big numbers (lakh, crore, arab) and their **American** names (million, billion);
- see **why** our number system is built the way it is, using calculators with only one kind of button;
- **round** big numbers sensibly and **estimate** sums and differences;
- multiply big numbers **quickly**, and say in advance **how many digits** a product will have;
- answer "could this really happen?" questions by **estimating in stages**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** A **digit** is one of the ten symbols 0, 1, 2, …, 9. A **number** is written with one or more digits. The **place** of a digit (ones, tens, hundreds, …) tells you how much it is worth: in 507, the 5 is worth 5 hundreds.

## 1.1 How big is one lakh?

Start with a number you know well and add 1:

| Largest number with this many digits | add 1 | Smallest number with one more digit |
|---|---|---|
| 999 (largest 3-digit) | + 1 | 1,000 (smallest 4-digit) |
| 9,999 (largest 4-digit) | + 1 | 10,000 (smallest 5-digit) |
| 99,999 (largest 5-digit) | + 1 | **1,00,000 (smallest 6-digit)** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **1,00,000 is read "one lakh".** It is 1 followed by **5 zeros**, the **smallest 6-digit number**.
>
> **Why it works:** the largest 5-digit number has a 9 in every place. Adding 1 to the ones makes 10 ones, which become 1 ten; that makes 10 tens, which become 1 hundred, and so on. Every place "overflows" one after another until a brand-new place appears on the left. So "largest n-digit number + 1" is always "smallest (n + 1)-digit number".

**Worked example.** If you tasted **one new variety of rice every day** for 100 years, would you taste one lakh varieties?

1. One year has 365 days, so 100 years have about 100 × 365 = **36,500 days**.
2. One variety a day gives 36,500 varieties.
3. 1,00,000 − 36,500 = 63,500. You would still be **63,500 varieties short**. Not even close!

### Check yourself

**1.** What if you ate **2** new varieties every day for 100 years? What about **3** a day?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. 100 years ≈ 36,500 days.
> 2. **2 a day:** 2 × 36,500 = **73,000** varieties. That is still 27,000 short of a lakh, so **no**.
> 3. **3 a day:** 3 × 36,500 = **1,09,500** varieties. That is more than 1,00,000, so **yes**, with 9,500 to spare. (You would reach one lakh after about 1,00,000 ÷ 3 ≈ 33,334 days, which is about 91 years.)

**2.** Pick a number of years y. How close to one lakh is the number of days in y years? Which y gets closest?

> [!ANSWER]
> The number of days in y years is about **365 × y**.
> - y = 100 → 36,500 days (63,500 short).
> - y = 200 → 73,000 days (27,000 short).
> - y = 273 → 99,645 days (355 short).
> - y = 274 → **1,00,010 days** (only 10 more than a lakh).
>
> So **one lakh days is about 274 years**. Why 274? Because 1,00,000 ÷ 365 ≈ 273.97, which is just under 274.

Some facts about the town of Chintamani in Karnataka help us practise.

**3.** In 2011 the population of Chintamani was about 75,000. How much less than one lakh is that?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1,00,000 − 75,000 = **25,000**. So 75,000 is **25,000 less** than one lakh.

**4.** The population in 2024 was estimated at 1,06,000. How much more than one lakh is that?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1,06,000 − 1,00,000 = **6,000**. So it is **6,000 more** than one lakh.

**5.** By how much did the population grow from 2011 to 2024?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1,06,000 − 75,000 = **31,000**. The town grew by about **31,000 people**.
>
> **Check:** 25,000 to reach one lakh, then 6,000 more: 25,000 + 6,000 = 31,000. ✓

### Getting a feel for large numbers

The Statue of Unity in Gujarat is about **180 m** tall. Kunchikal waterfall in Karnataka drops about **450 m**. Numbers like these are hard to picture. The trick is to **compare them with something familiar**.

Somu is **1 m** tall. He lives in a building with **10 floors**, and each floor is about **4 times** his height.

![Bar chart: Somu 1 m, his 10-floor building 40 m, Statue of Unity 180 m, Kunchikal falls 450 m](assets/img/class7/math/1/heights.svg)

**Worked example.** How tall is Somu's building?

1. One floor is 4 times Somu's height: 4 × 1 m = **4 m**.
2. Ten floors: 10 × 4 m = **40 m**.

### Check yourself

**1.** Which is taller, the Statue of Unity or Somu's building? By how much? About how many of Somu's buildings would you stack to reach the top of the statue?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **statue** is taller, by 180 − 40 = **140 m**.
>
> 180 ÷ 40 = 4.5, so the statue is about **4½ buildings** tall, close to 4 times the building.

**2.** How much taller than Somu's building is Kunchikal waterfall?

> [!ANSWER]
> 450 − 40 = **410 m** taller.

**3.** How many floors would Somu's building need to be as high as the waterfall?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each floor is 4 m, so we need 450 ÷ 4 = 112.5 floors. Floors come in whole numbers, so about **113 floors** (112 floors would be only 448 m).

### Is a lakh big or small?

It depends on **what you compare it with**.

- **Big:** living one lakh days would take about 274 years. One lakh people standing shoulder to shoulder would make a line about 38 km long.
- **Small:** the cricket stadium in Ahmedabad seats **more than** one lakh people. Most people have between 80,000 and 1,20,000 hairs on their head, all packed onto one small scalp. Some fish lay about a lakh eggs at once.

**Try this.** Measure the width of your shoulders with a ruler (for a child it is about 30 to 40 cm). Suppose one lakh people stand shoulder to shoulder, each taking about 38 cm. How long is the line? **What you will find:** 1,00,000 × 38 cm = 38,00,000 cm. There are 100 cm in a metre, so that is 38,000 m, and 1,000 m make a kilometre, so the line is about **38 km** long, longer than many cities! **Why it helps:** turning "one lakh" into a distance you could walk makes the number feel real.

> [!TIP]
> **To feel a big number, compare it with something you know.** "180 m" means little; "four and a half of Somu's buildings" you can picture.

## 1.2 Reading and writing large numbers

We use **commas** to split a long number into groups, so the eye can read it in pieces. In the **Indian system** the commas go, **from the right**: after the first **3** digits, then after every **2** digits.

| Ten lakh | Lakh | Ten thousand | Thousand | Hundred | Ten | One |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 7 | 8 | 8 | 3 | 0 |

**Worked example.** Read 1278830. Put the commas in from the right: 3 digits, then 2, then 2 → 12,78,830. Then read each group with its name.

So 1278830 is written **12,78,830** and read "**twelve lakh** seventy-eight **thousand** eight hundred thirty". Similarly 15,75,000 is "fifteen lakh seventy-five thousand".

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To read a big number:** put the commas in first (3 digits from the right, then every 2), then read each group followed by its name: … lakh, … thousand, and the last three digits.
>
> **Why commas help:** each comma marks the place where a new **name** starts (thousand, lakh, crore). Instead of counting seven digits, you see "12" in the lakh group and read "12 lakh" straight away.

> [!WARNING]
> **An empty place must be filled with 0.** "Four lakh seven thousand" is 4,07,000, not 4,7,000. Every group after the first one must have its full 2 digits (or 3 for the last group).

## 1.3 The Land of Tens: calculators with one kind of button

Imagine calculators that start at 0 and can only **add** a fixed amount each time you press their button.

- **Thoughtful Thousands** has only a **+1000** button.
- **Tedious Tens** has only a **+10** button.
- **Handy Hundreds** has only a **+100** button.

**Worked example.** How many presses does Thoughtful Thousands need to show three thousand? Each press adds 1000, so 3 presses give 3000. To show a number, **divide it by the button's value**: 3000 ÷ 1000 = 3 presses.

### Check yourself

**1.** Thoughtful Thousands (+1000). How many presses for: (a) three thousand, (b) 10,000, (c) fifty-three thousand, (d) 90,000, (e) one lakh? (f) Which number needs 153 presses? (g) How many thousands make one lakh?

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide by 1000 (or multiply by 1000 to go backwards):
> - (a) **3** · (b) 10,000 ÷ 1000 = **10** · (c) **53** · (d) **90** · (e) 1,00,000 ÷ 1000 = **100**
> - (f) 153 × 1000 = **1,53,000**
> - (g) **100 thousands make one lakh**, which is why we say "a lakh is a hundred thousand".

**2.** Tedious Tens (+10). How many presses for: (a) five hundred, (b) 780, (c) 1000, (d) 3700, (e) 10,000, (f) one lakh? (g) Which number needs 435 presses?

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide by 10 (remove one zero):
> - (a) **50** · (b) **78** · (c) **100** · (d) **370** · (e) **1,000** · (f) 1,00,000 ÷ 10 = **10,000**
> - (g) 435 × 10 = **4,350**

**3.** Handy Hundreds (+100). How many presses for: (a) four hundred, (b) 3,700, (c) 10,000, (d) fifty-three thousand, (e) 90,000, (f) 97,600, (g) 1,00,000? (h) Which number needs 582 presses? (i) How many hundreds make ten thousand? (j) How many hundreds make one lakh? (k) Handy Hundreds boasts: "Some numbers can't be shown by Tedious Tens **or** Thoughtful Thousands, but I can show them." True?

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide by 100 (remove two zeros):
> - (a) **4** · (b) **37** · (c) **100** · (d) **530** · (e) **900** · (f) **976** · (g) **1,000**
> - (h) 582 × 100 = **58,200**
> - (i) **100** hundreds make ten thousand · (j) **1,000** hundreds make one lakh
> - (k) **False.** Handy Hundreds can only show multiples of 100. Every multiple of 100 is also a multiple of 10 (100 = 10 × 10), so Tedious Tens can show it too, just with ten times as many presses. (It is true that Thoughtful Thousands cannot show 3,700, but the boast needs **both** to fail.)

**Try this (a game for two).** One player names a number below 1,000. The other player, acting as Chitti, must make it with **exactly** a number of presses chosen by the first player. Is it always possible? Play a few rounds, then read on to find the rule that decides it.

### The +9 rule

Here is a useful fact about Chitti's presses. Take **one** press of +100 and replace it with **ten** presses of +10. The number stays the same (100 = 10 × 10), but you now press **9 more times** (10 presses instead of 1). The same happens with any button and the next smaller one.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Breaking one press into ten smaller presses keeps the number the same and adds exactly 9 presses.**
> So if the fewest presses for a number is s, the other possible press-counts are s + 9, s + 18, s + 27, …

### Check yourself

**(a)** Using **exactly 30** button presses, what is the largest 3-digit number you can make? The smallest?

> [!ANSWER]
> For a 3-digit number we use +100, +10 and +1. Pressing each button "digit" times uses (sum of the digits) presses, and by the +9 rule every other way uses 9, 18, 27, … more. So 30 presses work only if the digit sum is **30, 21, 12 or 3**.
>
> **Largest:** try 999 downwards. 999 has digit sum 27 (counts 27, 36, … never 30). 998 → 26, 997 → 25, 996 → 24, 995 → 23, 994 → 22: none of these plus a multiple of 9 gives 30. **993** has digit sum 21, and 21 + 9 = 30. ✓
> One way: 8 × 100 + 19 × 10 + 3 × 1 = 800 + 190 + 3 = 993, with 8 + 19 + 3 = **30** presses.
>
> **Smallest:** 100 has digit sum 1 (counts 1, 10, 19, 28, 37: no 30). 101 → 2 (2, 11, 20, 29, 38: no). **102** has digit sum 3, and 3 + 27 = 30. ✓
> One way: 8 × 10 + 22 × 1 = 80 + 22 = 102, with 8 + 22 = **30** presses.

**(b)** 997 can be made with 25 presses. Can it be made with a different number of presses?

> [!ANSWER]
> Yes. 25 is the digit sum (9 + 9 + 7), so by the +9 rule we can also use 34, 43, 52, … presses.
> For example, break one +100 into ten +10s: **8 × 100 + 19 × 10 + 7 × 1** = 800 + 190 + 7 = 997, with 8 + 19 + 7 = **34** presses.

### The fewest presses

**Systematic Sippy** has the buttons +1, +10, +100, +1000, +10000 and +100000, and wants to press **as few times as possible**.

**Worked example.** One way to make 5072 is 5 presses of +1000, 6 presses of +10 and 12 presses of +1: 5000 + 60 + 12 = 5072, using 5 + 6 + 12 = **23 presses**.

### Check yourself

**(a)** Make 5072 with fewer than 23 presses. What is the fewest possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> Look at the 12 presses of +1. Ten of them add 10, the same as **one** press of +10. Swap them: now we have 5 × 1000 + 7 × 10 + 2 × 1 = 5072, using 5 + 7 + 2 = **14 presses** (9 fewer).
>
> No button is now pressed 10 or more times, so there is nothing left to swap: **14 is the fewest**. It is the sum of the digits 5 + 0 + 7 + 2.

**(b)** What is the fewest number of presses for 8300?

> [!ANSWER]
> 8300 = (8 × 1000) + (3 × 100), so **8 + 3 + 0 + 0 = 11 presses**.

### Why the fewest presses is the digit sum

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Fewest presses = the sum of the digits**, and the fewest-press recipe is exactly the place-value expansion: 5072 = 5 thousands + 0 hundreds + 7 tens + 2 ones.
>
> **Why it works:** if a recipe presses any button **10 or more** times, swap ten of those presses for **one** press of the next bigger button. The number stays the same and you save 9 presses. Keep doing this until no button is pressed more than 9 times. Now each button is pressed 0 to 9 times, and that is exactly what the **digits** of the number say.

### Check yourself

**1.** Find the fewest presses, with the expression, for 8300, 40629, 56354, 66666 and 367813.

> [!ANSWER]
> - 8300 = (8 × 1000) + (3 × 100) → **11**
> - 40629 = (4 × 10000) + (6 × 100) + (2 × 10) + (9 × 1) → 4 + 6 + 2 + 9 = **21**
> - 56354 = (5 × 10000) + (6 × 1000) + (3 × 100) + (5 × 10) + (4 × 1) → 5 + 6 + 3 + 5 + 4 = **23**
> - 66666 = (6 × 10000) + (6 × 1000) + (6 × 100) + (6 × 10) + (6 × 1) → **30**
> - 367813 = (3 × 100000) + (6 × 10000) + (7 × 1000) + (8 × 100) + (1 × 10) + (3 × 1) → 3 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 1 + 3 = **28**

**2.** What is the connection between each number and its fewest presses?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The fewest presses is the sum of the number's digits.** For instance, 40629 → 4 + 0 + 6 + 2 + 9 = 21.

**3.** Why do the fewest-press expressions also give the place-value form of the number?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because in the fewest-press recipe **no button is pressed 10 or more times** (ten presses could always be replaced by one press of the next button, saving 9). So each button is pressed between 0 and 9 times. The number of presses of +1000 is then the thousands digit, of +100 the hundreds digit, and so on. That is exactly the place-value form: our number system is built so that **each place holds at most 9**, and **10 of one place make 1 of the next**.

## 1.4 Crores and arabs, millions and billions

If Chitti presses +10,00,000 (ten lakh) **ten times**, it shows 100 lakh. We call this **one crore**, written **1,00,00,000**: 1 followed by **7 zeros**.

Different countries name big numbers differently. Look at both systems side by side:

| Indian system | Indian name | American (International) system | American name |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | one thousand | 1,000 | one thousand |
| 10,000 | ten thousand | 10,000 | ten thousand |
| 1,00,000 | one lakh | 100,000 | one hundred thousand |
| 10,00,000 | ten lakh | 1,000,000 | **one million** |
| 1,00,00,000 | one crore | 10,000,000 | ten million |
| 10,00,00,000 | ten crore | 100,000,000 | one hundred million |
| 1,00,00,00,000 | one arab (or a hundred crore) | 1,000,000,000 | **one billion** |

- **Indian commas** group digits **3-2-2-2…** from the right: thousands, lakhs, crores, arabs.
- **American commas** group digits **3-3-3…** from the right: thousands, millions, billions.

![The number 9876501234 in boxes: Indian groups arab, crore, lakh, thousand, hundreds above; American groups billion, million, thousand, hundreds below](assets/img/class7/math/1/comma_groups.svg)

**Worked example.** Read 9876501234 in both systems. Put in the commas each way (see the picture), then read each group with its name.

So **9876501234** reads:
- Indian: 9,87,65,01,234 → **9 arab 87 crore 65 lakh 1 thousand 234** (or 987 crore 65 lakh 1 thousand 234);
- American: 9,876,501,234 → **9 billion 876 million 501 thousand 234**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **100 thousand = 1 lakh** (5 zeros) · **100 lakh = 1 crore** (7 zeros) · **100 crore = 1 arab** (9 zeros)
> - **1 million = 10 lakh** (6 zeros) · **1 billion = 100 crore = 1 arab** (9 zeros)
>
> **Why the two systems agree up to a thousand and then split:** the Indian names step up every **2** digits (each new name is 100 times the last), while the American names step up every **3** digits (each new name is 1000 times the last). Both start the same way with hundreds and thousands.

The words **lakh** and **crore** come from the Sanskrit *lakṣha* and *koṭi*. The Indian naming is also used in Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Pakistan and a few other countries.

> [!TIP]
> **Counting zeros is the safest way to convert.** Multiplying adds zeros: a thousand (3 zeros) × a lakh (5 zeros) has 3 + 5 = 8 zeros.

## 1.5 Exact and approximate values

A newspaper says "1 lakh people visited a book fair". Was it **exactly** 1,00,000? Almost surely not. If one person had stayed home, nobody would print "99,999 people visited". The number is an **approximation**, and that is fine: it tells us **how big** the crowd was.

In 2011 Chintamani had **76,068** people. Saying "about **75,000**" gives the right idea with an easier number.

- **Round up** (choose a bigger number) when running short would be bad. A school with 732 people orders **750** sweets, not 700.
- **Round down** (choose a smaller number) when that suits the situation. A shopkeeper with an item costing ₹470 may say "around ₹450" rather than "around ₹500".
- **Use exact numbers** when every unit matters: a phone number, a bank balance, a train's departure time.

### Nearest neighbours

With big numbers it often helps to know the **nearest** thousand, lakh or crore. Take 6,72,85,183. Its two neighbouring lakhs are 6,72,00,000 and 6,73,00,000. Which is nearer?

![Number line from 6,72,00,000 to 6,73,00,000 with the halfway mark; 6,72,85,183 is 14,817 from 6,73,00,000 but 85,183 from 6,72,00,000](assets/img/class7/math/1/rounding_line.svg)

It is only 14,817 away from 6,73,00,000 but 85,183 away from 6,72,00,000, so its **nearest lakh is 6,73,00,000**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To round to a place, look at everything to the right of that place.** If it is **at least half** of that place's value, round **up**; if it is less than half, round **down**.
>
> **Why "half":** the number lies between two neighbours. Exactly halfway between them is the point where both are equally far. Past halfway, the upper neighbour is closer; before halfway, the lower one is.

| ten thousand | 5,183 | 5,000 | 5,183 ≥ 5,000 → up → **6,72,90,000** |

| crore | 72,85,183 | 50,00,000 | up → **7,00,00,000** |

## 1.6 Patterns in products

### A multiplication shortcut

**Worked example.** Roxie works out 116 × 5 like this: **116 × 5 = 116 × 10 ÷ 2 = 1160 ÷ 2 = 580.**

**Worked example.** Estu works out 824 × 25 like this: **824 × 25 = 824 × 100 ÷ 4 = 82,400 ÷ 4 = 20,600.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **× 5 is the same as × 10 then ÷ 2. × 25 is the same as × 100 then ÷ 4. × 125 is the same as × 1000 then ÷ 8.**
>
> **Why it works:** 5 = 10 ÷ 2, 25 = 100 ÷ 4 and 125 = 1000 ÷ 8. Multiplying by 10, 100 or 1000 is easy (write zeros), and halving or quartering is easy, so one hard step becomes two easy ones.

> [!TIP]
> **Look for pairs that make 10, 100 or 1000:** 2 × 5 = 10, 2 × 50 = 100, 4 × 25 = 100, 8 × 125 = 1000. You may multiply numbers **in any order** and group them any way you like, so put these pairs together first.

### Roxie's claim: checking the extremes

Roxie says: "**The product of two 2-digit numbers always has 3 or 4 digits.**" There are 90 × 90 = 8,100 such products. Must we check them all? No. Roxie checks the **extremes**:

- The **smallest** product is 10 × 10 = **100**, which has 3 digits. Every other product is bigger.
- Every 2-digit number is **less than 100**, so every product is less than 100 × 100 = **10,000**, the smallest 5-digit number. So every product has at most 4 digits.

So the product always has **3 or 4 digits**. She is right, and she proved it without trying 8,100 cases.

**Try this.** Use a calculator to multiply five pairs of 3-digit numbers of your own choice, including the largest, 999 × 999. Count the digits of each answer. **What you will see:** every answer has 5 or 6 digits; even 999 × 999 = 9,98,001 has only 6. **Why:** the next box explains it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An a-digit number times a b-digit number has either (a + b − 1) or (a + b) digits.**
>
> **Why it works:** the smallest a-digit number is 1 followed by (a − 1) zeros, and the smallest b-digit number is 1 followed by (b − 1) zeros. Their product is 1 followed by (a + b − 2) zeros, which has **a + b − 1 digits**. Every a-digit number is less than 1 followed by a zeros (and the same for b), so every product is less than 1 followed by (a + b) zeros, which is the smallest number with a + b + 1 digits. So the product has **at most a + b digits**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1,42,019**, **4,12,019**, **4,20,119** (putting the 1 either just before or just after the existing 1 gives the same number) and **4,20,191**.

**5.** Start with 63,890. **Move exactly four sticks** (take four off and put them back somewhere else) to make a bigger number. One answer is 88,078. Find others. What is the biggest?

> [!ANSWER]
> 63,890 uses 6 + 5 + 7 + 6 + 6 = 30 sticks, and any answer must still use 30. **How to check an answer:** for each digit, count the sticks it **gains** (sticks in new places). The gains must add up to exactly 4; then 4 sticks are also lost, so the total stays 30.
>
> **88,078** (given): 6 → 8 gains 1, 3 → 8 gains 2, 8 → 0 gains 0, 9 → 7 gains 0, 0 → 8 gains 1. Gains: 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 ✓.
>
> The **biggest** is **99,985**:
> - 6 → 9: the bottom-left stick moves to the top-right (gains 1)
> - 3 → 9: gains the top-left stick (gains 1)
> - 8 → 9: loses the bottom-left stick (gains 0)
> - 9 → 8: gains the bottom-left stick (gains 1)
> - 0 → 5: loses two sticks and gains the middle one (gains 1)
>
> Gains: 1 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 1 = 4 ✓. (A computer count finds 704 bigger numbers that can be made this way, so there are lots to discover.)

**6.** What is the **biggest** number you can make with **exactly 24 sticks**?

> [!ANSWER]
> A number with **more digits** is always bigger, so use as many digits as possible. The cheapest digit is **1** (2 sticks), so 24 sticks make at most 24 ÷ 2 = **12 digits**, and a 12-digit number can only be made if every digit is a 1.
>
> So the biggest is twelve 1s: **111111111111**, which with Indian commas is **1,11,11,11,11,111**.

**7.** What is the **smallest** number you can make with exactly 24 sticks?

> [!ANSWER]
> Use **as few digits as possible**. The most sticks a digit can take is 7 (the 8), so 3 digits use at most 21 sticks: not enough. So we need **4 digits**.
>
> Now make the first digit as small as possible. If it is 1 (2 sticks), the other three digits need 22 sticks, but they can hold at most 21. So the first digit can't be 1. Try **2** (5 sticks): 19 sticks are left for 3 digits. Next, the smallest second digit is **0** (6 sticks), leaving 13 for 2 digits. The smallest third digit is **0** (6), leaving **7** for the last digit: that is an **8**.
>
> Smallest: **2008** (5 + 6 + 6 + 7 = 24 ✓).

## Exercises — try first

### 1. Using each digit 0–9 exactly once (the first digit cannot be 0), make a 10-digit number that is:

**(a)** the largest multiple of 5.

> [!ANSWER]
> A multiple of 5 ends in **0 or 5**. To make the number as large as possible, put the biggest digits first: 9876543210. It ends in 0, so it is a multiple of 5. **9876543210.**
>
> (If it had to end in 5, the best would be 9876432105, which is smaller.)

**(b)** the smallest even number.

> [!ANSWER]
> Smallest first digit (not 0): **1**. Then 0, then the rest in increasing order: 1023456789. But that ends in 9, which is odd. An even number must end in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8. Keep the start as small as possible and only change the end: swap the last two digits so it ends in **8**: **1023456798**.

### 2. The number 10,30,285 is "ten lakhs thirty thousand two hundred eighty-five", which has 42 letters. Give a 7-digit number whose name has the most letters.

> [!ANSWER]
> A 7-digit number is read as: (2-digit part) lakhs, (2-digit part) thousand, (digit) hundred, (2-digit part).
> - The longest 2-digit name: "seventy" (7 letters) is the longest tens word, and "three", "seven", "eight" (5 letters) are the longest ones words, so **seventy-three, seventy-seven, seventy-eight** have 12 letters. (No teen word is that long: "seventeen" has 9.)
> - The longest hundreds digit: three, seven or eight (5 letters).
>
> So take **77,77,777**: "seventy-seven lakhs seventy-seven thousand seven hundred seventy-seven". Letters: 12 + 5 + 12 + 8 + 5 + 7 + 12 = **61** (60 if you say "lakh" instead of "lakhs"). Numbers like 73,78,377 tie with it.

### 3. Write a 9-digit number where swapping any two digits gives a bigger number. How many such numbers are there?

> [!ANSWER]
> If two digits were **equal**, swapping them would give the **same** number, not a bigger one. So all 9 digits are different.
> If a bigger digit stood to the **left** of a smaller one, swapping those two would make the number **smaller**. So the digits must **increase** from left to right.
> The digit 0 can't be used: in an increasing order it would have to come first, which is not allowed.
> So the digits are 1 to 9 in increasing order: **123456789**, and it is the **only one**.

### 4. Strike out 10 digits from 12345123451234512345 so that the number left is as large as possible.

> [!ANSWER]
> The number has 20 digits; we keep 10. Choose the kept digits **one at a time from the left**, each time as big as possible, while leaving enough digits to finish:
> 1. The 1st kept digit must leave 9 more after it, so it comes from the first 11 digits: the biggest is the **5** in position 5.
> 2. The 2nd comes from positions 6–12 (leaving 8 after): the biggest is the **5** in position 10.
> 3. The 3rd comes from positions 11–13 (1, 2, 3): the biggest is **3** (position 13).
> 4. Now 7 digits remain (positions 14–20: 4, 5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) and we need 7, so keep them all.
>
> Answer: **5534512345**. **Why choose greedily:** a bigger digit further left beats anything that happens further right.

### 5. "Zero" and "one" share the letters e and o; "one" and "two" share o; "two" and "three" share t. How far must you count to find two consecutive numbers whose English names share no letter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You never find one.** Check the places where it could happen:
> - From zero to twelve, every neighbouring pair shares a letter: zero/one (e, o), one/two (o), two/three (t), three/four (r), four/five (f), five/six (i), six/seven (s), seven/eight (e), eight/nine (e, i, n), nine/ten (e, n), ten/eleven (e, n), eleven/twelve (e, l, v), twelve/thirteen (e, t).
> - Inside the teens, all names end in "-teen". Inside any decade (twenty-one, twenty-two, …) both names start with the same tens word.
> - At each change of decade they still share a letter: nineteen/twenty (n, t, e), twenty-nine/thirty (t), thirty-nine/forty (t, r), forty-nine/fifty (f, t, y), and so on up to ninety-nine/one hundred (n, e).
> - From 100 on, neighbouring names share "hundred", "thousand" or "million" (or a part like "one hundred ninety-nine / two hundred", which share "hundred").
>
> (A computer check of every pair up to two million finds no exception.)

### 6. Write the counting numbers one after another: 123456789101112… The 10th digit you write is the 1 of 10, and the 11th is its 0.

**(a)** What is the 1000th digit, and in which number does it occur?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The numbers 1–9 use **9 digits**.
> 2. The 90 two-digit numbers 10–99 use 90 × 2 = **180 digits**. Total so far: 189.
> 3. We need 1000 − 189 = **811** more digits from three-digit numbers. 811 = 270 × 3 + 1, so we write **270** full three-digit numbers and then **1** more digit.
> 4. The 270 numbers are 100 to 369 (100 + 270 − 1 = 369). The next number is **370**, and we need its first digit.
>
> The 1000th digit is **3**, in the number **370**.

**(b)** Which number contains the millionth digit?

> [!ANSWER]
> Count the digits used by each length:
> - 1-digit: 9 · 2-digit: 180 · 3-digit: 900 × 3 = 2,700 · 4-digit: 9,000 × 4 = 36,000 · 5-digit: 90,000 × 5 = 4,50,000
> - Total up to 99,999: 9 + 180 + 2,700 + 36,000 + 4,50,000 = **4,88,889 digits**.
>
> We need 10,00,000 − 4,88,889 = **5,11,111** more digits from 6-digit numbers. 5,11,111 = 85,185 × 6 + 1, so we write 85,185 full six-digit numbers (1,00,000 to 1,85,184) and then **1** more digit. The millionth digit is the first digit of **1,85,185** (it is a 1).

**(c)** When do you write the digit 5 for the 5000th time?

> [!ANSWER]
> Count the 5s place by place.
> 1. From 0 to 9,999 (think of every number as four digits, 0000 to 9999), each of the 4 places shows 5 in one-tenth of the 10,000 numbers: 4 × 1,000 = **4,000 fives**.
> 2. From 10,000 to 12,999 the first two digits are 10, 11 or 12 (no 5), and the last three digits run through 000–999 three times. Each run has 3 × 100 = 300 fives, so **900 more**. Total: 4,900.
> 3. From 13,000 to 13,499: the hundreds digit is 0–4 (no 5). The tens digit is 5 in 13,050–13,059, 13,150–13,159, …, 13,450–13,459: 5 × 10 = 50 fives. The ones digit is 5 once in every ten numbers: 50 fives. That is **100 more**, reaching exactly **5,000**.
> 4. The last of these is the 5 at the end of **13,495** (nothing between 13,496 and 13,499 has a 5).
>
> The 5000th five is written in **13,495**.

### 7. A calculator has only **+10,000** and **+100** buttons. Write an expression for the button presses (using as few presses as possible) for each number.

### 8. How many lakhs make a billion?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1 billion = 1,000,000,000 = 1 followed by 9 zeros. 1 lakh = 1 followed by 5 zeros. Dividing removes 5 zeros: 1 followed by 4 zeros = **10,000 lakh**. (Check: 1 billion = 100 crore and 1 crore = 100 lakh, so 100 × 100 = 10,000 lakh.)

### 11. How many coins, each 1 mm thick, must be stacked to match the height of the Statue of Unity (180 m)?

> [!ANSWER]
> Change the height to millimetres: 1 m = 100 cm = 1,000 mm, so 180 m = 180 × 1,000 = **1,80,000 mm**. Each coin is 1 mm, so we need **1,80,000 coins** (one lakh eighty thousand).

### 12. Grey-headed albatrosses can fly about 900–1000 km in a day. One recorded trip was about 12,000 km. About how many days would such a trip take?

> [!ANSWER]
> - At 1,000 km a day: 12,000 ÷ 1,000 = **12 days**.
> - At 900 km a day: 12,000 ÷ 900 ≈ **13.3 days**.
>
> So about **12 to 14 days**. (A faster bird needs fewer days, so the two speeds give the two ends of the range.)

### 13. A bar-tailed godwit flew 13,560 km non-stop from Alaska to Australia in about 11 days. About how far did it fly each day? Each hour?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Each day: 13,560 ÷ 11 ≈ **1,233 km**. (Check: 11 × 1,233 = 13,563, very close.)
> - Each hour: 1,233 ÷ 24 ≈ **51 km**. (Check: 24 × 50 = 1,200, so a little over 50.)

### 14. Bald eagles fly as high as 4,500–6,000 m; Mount Everest is about 8,850 m high; aeroplanes fly at 10,000–12,800 m. How many times the height of Somu's 40 m building is each?

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide each height by 40 m.
> - Eagles: 4,500 ÷ 40 = 112.5 and 6,000 ÷ 40 = 150 → about **112 to 150 times**.
> - Everest: 8,850 ÷ 40 ≈ 221 → about **221 times**.
> - Aeroplanes: 10,000 ÷ 40 = 250 and 12,800 ÷ 40 = 320 → about **250 to 320 times**.

## In a nutshell

- **1 lakh = 1,00,000** (5 zeros) · **1 crore = 1,00,00,000** (7 zeros) · **1 arab = 100 crore** (9 zeros).
- **1 million = 10 lakh** (6 zeros) · **1 billion = 100 crore** (9 zeros).
- Indian commas go **3-2-2-2…** from the right; American commas go **3-3-3…**.
- Our place-value system means **each place holds 0 to 9**, and **10 of one place make 1 of the next**; that is why the fewest button presses equals the **sum of the digits**.
- To round: look at **everything to the right** of the place; **half or more → up**, less → down.
- **× 5 = × 10 ÷ 2**, **× 25 = × 100 ÷ 4**, **× 125 = × 1000 ÷ 8**; pair numbers to make 10, 100, 1000.
- An a-digit × b-digit product has **a + b − 1 or a + b** digits.
- To test a "could it happen?" question: **assume**, work **in stages**, then **compare**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** forgetting the 0 in an empty place (4,07,704, not 4,7,704) · a million is **10 lakh**, not 1 lakh · a billion is **100 crore**, not 1 crore · rounding 6,72,85,183 to the nearest ten lakh gives 6,70,00,000 (look at 2,85,183, not just the next digit) · a 3-digit × 3-digit product can never have 4 digits · count zeros carefully when dividing big numbers.
