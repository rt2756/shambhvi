## How big is one lakh?

Start with something you already know and keep adding 1:

| Largest number with this many digits | + 1 | Smallest number with one more digit |
|---|---|---|
| 999 (largest 3-digit) | → | 1,000 (smallest 4-digit) |
| 9,999 (largest 4-digit) | → | 10,000 (smallest 5-digit) |
| 99,999 (largest 5-digit) | → | **1,00,000 (smallest 6-digit)** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **1,00,000 is read "one lakh".** It is **1 followed by 5 zeros** — the **smallest 6-digit number**.
>
> **Why the +1 trick works:** the largest 5-digit number has 9 in every place. Adding 1 makes every place "overflow" (10 ones → 1 ten, 10 tens → 1 hundred, …) until a brand-new place appears on the left. So "largest n-digit + 1" is always "smallest (n+1)-digit".

**Is a lakh big?** The book's farmer's children, Roxie and Estu, wonder whether they could taste one lakh varieties of rice in a 100-year life.

- One variety a day: 100 years × 365 days = **36,500 days** → only 36,500 varieties. Nowhere near 1,00,000.
- Two a day: 2 × 36,500 = **73,000**. Still short.
- Three a day: 3 × 36,500 = **1,09,500** → yes, just over a lakh!
- **Living 1 lakh days** would take 1,00,000 ÷ 365 ≈ **274 years.**

But a lakh is also *small* in other ways: a cricket stadium in Ahmedabad seats **more than 1 lakh** people, and most people have **80,000 to 1,20,000 hairs** on their head. So "big" depends on **what you compare it with**.

> [!TIP]
> **To feel a big number, compare it with something familiar.** Somu is 1 m tall; each floor of his building is about 4 times his height (4 m), and the building has 10 floors → about **40 m** tall.
> - Statue of Unity ≈ 180 m → 180 − 40 = **140 m taller** than the building, and 180 ÷ 40 = 4.5, so **about 4½ buildings** stacked up.
> - Kunchikal waterfall ≈ 450 m → 450 − 40 = **410 m taller**; to match it the building needs 450 ÷ 4 ≈ **113 floors**.

**Book's Figure it Out (Chintamani's population):**
1. 1,00,000 − 75,000 = **25,000** less than a lakh.
2. 1,06,000 − 1,00,000 = **6,000** more than a lakh.
3. Increase 2011 → 2024 = 1,06,000 − 75,000 = **31,000**.

## Reading and writing big numbers (Indian system)

In the Indian system we put commas to split the number into named groups, **from the right**: first **3** digits (hundreds-tens-ones), then groups of **2** (thousands, lakhs, crores…).

| Place | Ten crore | Crore | Ten lakh | Lakh | Ten thousand | Thousand | Hundred | Ten | One |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12,78,830 | | | 1 | 2 | 7 | 8 | 8 | 3 | 0 |

12,78,830 → "**twelve lakh** seventy eight **thousand** eight hundred thirty".

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To read a number: put commas first (3 from the right, then every 2), then read each group with its name.**
> **Why commas help:** your eye can take in only 2–3 digits at a glance. Each comma marks where a new *name* (thousand, lakh, crore) starts, so you read "12 lakh" instead of counting seven digits.

**Book's examples, worked:**
- 3,00,600 → three lakh six hundred
- 5,04,085 → five lakh four thousand eighty five
- 27,30,000 → twenty seven lakh thirty thousand
- 70,53,138 → seventy lakh fifty three thousand one hundred thirty eight
- One lakh twenty three thousand four hundred fifty six → **1,23,456**
- Four lakh seven thousand seven hundred four → **4,07,704** (the ten-thousands place is empty, so write **0** there)
- Fifty lakh five thousand fifty → **50,05,050**
- Ten lakh two hundred thirty five → **10,00,235**

> [!WARNING]
> **Empty places must be filled with 0.** "Four lakh seven thousand" is 4,07,000 — not 4,7,000. Every group after the first must have its full 2 (or 3) digits.

## The Land of Tens — counting button presses

The book imagines calculators with a single button.

- **Thoughtful Thousands** (+1000 only): 53,000 needs **53** presses; 1 lakh needs **100** presses → **100 thousands make 1 lakh.**
- **Tedious Tens** (+10 only): 780 needs **78**; 10,000 needs **1,000**; 1 lakh needs **10,000** presses.
- **Handy Hundreds** (+100 only): 97,600 needs **976**; 1 lakh needs **1,000** presses → **100 hundreds = 10,000**, **1,000 hundreds = 1 lakh**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Handy Hundreds' boast** — "some numbers that Tens and Thousands can't show, I can" — is **false**. Any multiple of 100 is also a multiple of 10, so Tedious Tens can show it too (just with more presses).

**Creative Chitti** has buttons +1, +10, +100, +1000, … and makes a number in many ways:
- 5072 = (50 × 100) + (7 × 10) + (2 × 1) = 50 + 7 + 2 = **59 clicks**
- 5072 = (3 × 1000) + (20 × 100) + (72 × 1) = **95 clicks**

**Systematic Sippy** wants the **fewest clicks**. For 5072 it clicks +1000 five times, +10 seven times, +1 twice → **5 + 7 + 2 = 14 clicks**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Fewest clicks = the sum of the digits.** 40,629 → 4 + 0 + 6 + 2 + 9 = **21 clicks**; 66,666 → **30 clicks**.
>
> **Why:** if you press any button 10 or more times, you could replace those 10 presses with **one** press of the next bigger button and save 9 clicks. So the fewest-click recipe never uses a button more than 9 times — and "each button 0–9 times" is exactly what the digits of the number say. That's why the least-clicks expression *is* the place-value expansion.

**Chitti's puzzles — exactly 30 presses.** Key fact: replacing **one** press of a button by **ten** presses of the next smaller button keeps the number the same but adds **9** presses. So the possible press-counts for a number are its digit sum, digit sum + 9, digit sum + 18, … For 30 presses we need a digit sum of 30, 21, 12 or 3.
- **Largest:** 999 has digit sum 27 → counts 27, 36, … never 30. The largest 3-digit number with digit sum 21 is **993**: e.g. 9 × 100 + 8 × 10 + 13 × 1 = 9 + 8 + 13 = 30 presses.
- **Smallest:** 100 (sum 1) and 101 (sum 2) fail; **102** has sum 3 → 3 + 9 + 9 + 9 = 30: e.g. 8 × 10 + 22 × 1 = 8 + 22 = 30 presses.
- **997 in 25 clicks** (its digit sum) can also be made in 34 clicks: 8 × 100 + 19 × 10 + 7 × 1.

## Crores, arabs — and the American system

Press +10,00,000 ten times: you get **100 lakh = 1 crore = 1,00,00,000** (1 followed by **7** zeros).

| Indian | Name | American | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,00,000 | one lakh | 100,000 | hundred thousand |
| 10,00,000 | ten lakh | 1,000,000 | **one million** |
| 1,00,00,000 | one crore | 10,000,000 | ten million |
| 10,00,00,000 | ten crore | 100,000,000 | hundred million |
| 1,00,00,00,000 | one arab (= 100 crore) | 1,000,000,000 | **one billion** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Indian commas: 3-2-2-2…** from the right (thousand, lakh, crore, arab).
> **American (International) commas: 3-3-3…** (thousand, million, billion).
> - **100 thousand = 1 lakh**, **100 lakh = 1 crore**, **100 crore = 1 arab**.
> - **1 million = 10 lakh**, **1 billion = 100 crore = 1 arab**.
>
> **Why the two systems disagree after a thousand:** Indian names step up every **2** digits (×100), American names step up every **3** digits (×1000). Both agree up to 1,000 — after that they group differently.

**Counting zeros is the safest way to convert.** A thousand lakh = 1,000 × 1,00,000 → 3 + 5 = **8 zeros** (= 10 crore). A hundred thousand → 2 + 3 = **5 zeros** (= 1 lakh).

**Reading 9876501234 both ways:**
- Indian: 9,87,65,01,234 → 9 arab 87 crore 65 lakh 1 thousand 234 (or 987 crore 65 lakh…)
- American: 9,876,501,234 → 9 billion 876 million 501 thousand 234

**Book exercise — selected answers:**
- 4050678 → 40,50,678 = forty lakh fifty thousand six hundred seventy eight = 4,050,678 = four million fifty thousand six hundred seventy eight.
- 1020304050 → 1,02,03,04,050 = one arab two crore three lakh four thousand fifty = one billion twenty million three hundred four thousand fifty.
- One crore one lakh one thousand ten → **1,01,01,010**
- One billion one million one thousand one → **1,00,10,01,001**
- Nine billion eighty million seven hundred thousand six hundred → **9,08,07,00,600**
- 30 thousand **<** 3 lakh · 500 lakh (= 5 crore) **>** 5 million (= 50 lakh) · 800 thousand **<** 8 million · 640 crore (= 6.4 billion) **<** 60 billion.

## Exact or approximate? Rounding

A headline says "1 lakh people visited the book fair". It was surely not *exactly* 1,00,000 — it's an **approximation**. Chintamani's population was 76,068; saying "**about 75,000**" is enough to give the idea.

- **Round up** (choose a bigger value) when running short would be bad: 732 people at school → order **750** sweets.
- **Round down** (choose a smaller value) when you want to sound cheaper or safer: an item costing ₹470 → "around ₹450".
- **Exact numbers needed:** money in a bank, phone numbers, medicine doses.

### Nearest neighbours

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To round to a place, look at the part of the number to the right of that place. If it is at least half of that place's value, go up; otherwise go down.**
> **Why "half":** the number sits between two neighbours (e.g. 6,72,00,000 and 6,73,00,000). Whichever one is closer is the nearest neighbour, and the halfway point decides which is closer.

The book's example 6,72,85,183:

| Nearest | Answer | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| thousand | 6,72,85,000 | 183 is less than 500 → down |
| ten thousand | 6,72,90,000 | 5,183 ≥ 5,000 → up |
| lakh | 6,73,00,000 | 85,183 ≥ 50,000 → up |
| ten lakh | 6,70,00,000 | 2,85,183 < 5,00,000 → down |
| crore | 7,00,00,000 | 72,85,183 ≥ 50,00,000 → up |

**Worked (book):** 3,87,69,957 → 3,87,70,000 · 3,87,70,000 · 3,88,00,000 · 3,90,00,000 · 4,00,00,000.
29,05,32,481 → 29,05,32,000 · 29,05,30,000 · 29,05,00,000 · 29,10,00,000 · 29,00,00,000.

> [!TIP]
> **Math Talk:** a number whose five nearest neighbours are *all* 5,00,00,000 must round to 5,00,00,000 even at the thousand level, so it lies from **4,99,99,500 up to 5,00,00,499** — that's **1,000 numbers**.

### Estimating sums and differences

**4,63,128 + 4,19,682.** Roxie: "near 8,00,000 and more". Estu: "near 9,00,000 and less". Both are right — but whose is closer?
- Exact: **8,82,810**. That's 82,810 above 8 lakh but only 17,190 below 9 lakh → **Estu is closer.**
- Is it more than 8,50,000? Yes: 4,63,128 is already over 4,50,000 and 4,19,682 is over 4,00,000, so the sum is over 8,50,000.
- More than 8,83,128? **No:** 8,83,128 = 4,63,128 + 4,20,000, and 4,19,682 is *less* than 4,20,000.

**14,63,128 − 4,90,020** = **9,73,108**. Estu ("near 9 lakh, more") and Roxie ("near 10 lakh, less") are both right; 9,73,108 is 26,892 from 10 lakh but 73,108 from 9 lakh → **Roxie is closer.** It is more than 9,63,128 because we subtracted *less* than 5,00,000.

## Patterns in products — quick multiplication

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **× 5 = × 10 ÷ 2.  × 25 = × 100 ÷ 4.  × 125 = × 1000 ÷ 8.**
> **Why:** 5 = 10/2, 25 = 100/4, 125 = 1000/8. Multiplying by 10, 100, 1000 is easy (add zeros), and halving or quartering is easy, so swap a hard multiply for two easy steps.

- 116 × 5 = 116 × 10 ÷ 2 = 1160 ÷ 2 = **580**
- 824 × 25 = 82,400 ÷ 4 = **20,600**
- 72 × 125 = 72 × 1000 ÷ 8 = 9 × 1000 = **9,000**
- 2 × 1768 × 50 = (2 × 50) × 1768 = 100 × 1768 = **1,76,800** (regroup so a 100 appears)
- 125 × 40 × 8 × 25 = (125 × 8) × (40 × 25) = 1000 × 1000 = **10,00,000**
- 25 × 12 = 25 × 4 × 3 = 100 × 3 = **300**; 25 × 240 = 100 × 60 = **6,000**; 250 × 120 = 1000 × 30 = **30,000**; 2500 × 12 = 10,000 × 3 = **30,000**

> [!TIP]
> **Hunt for pairs that make 10, 100, 1000:** 2 × 5, 4 × 25, 8 × 125, 2 × 50. Multiplication can be done **in any order** (it's commutative and associative), so you are free to pair them up.

### How many digits will the product have?

Roxie says two 2-digit numbers always multiply to a 3- or 4-digit number. Instead of trying all 8,100 pairs, she checks the **extremes**:
- Smallest: 10 × 10 = **100** (3 digits). Every other product is bigger.
- Every 2-digit number is less than 100, so every product is less than 100 × 100 = **10,000** (5 digits) → at most 4 digits.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An a-digit number × a b-digit number has either (a + b − 1) or (a + b) digits.**
> **Why:** an a-digit number is at least 1 followed by (a−1) zeros and less than 1 followed by a zeros. Multiply the smallest cases: (a−1) + (b−1) zeros → a + b − 1 digits. The largest stays below 1 followed by a + b zeros → at most a + b digits.

- 3-digit × 3-digit → 5 or 6 digits (so **never 4**).
- 4-digit × 2-digit → 5 or 6 digits (1000 × 10 = 10,000 → yes, 5 is possible).
- 5 × 5 → 9 or 10 · 8 × 3 → 10 or 11 · 12 × 13 → 24 or 25.

**Book patterns:** 11 × 11 = 121, 111 × 111 = 12,321, 1111 × 1111 = 12,34,321 (a palindrome climbing up and down). 3 × 5 = 15, 33 × 35 = 1155, 333 × 335 = 1,11,555. 101 × 101 = 10,201, 102 × 102 = 10,404, 103 × 103 = 10,609.

## Did you ever wonder…? (thought experiments)

These questions are about **estimating in stages** and making **sensible assumptions**.

- **Could all of Mumbai fit in 1 lakh buses?** Assume 50 per bus: 1 lakh × 50 = 50 lakh. Mumbai has over 1 crore 24 lakh → **no.**
- **5,000 Titanics (2,500 each)?** 5,000 × 2,500 = 1,25,00,000 = 1 crore 25 lakh → **just about yes**, barely more than Mumbai's 1,24,42,373.
- **100 km a day to the Moon (3,84,400 km)?** One year: 36,500 km. Ten years: **3,65,000 km** → just short.
- **1000 km a day to the Sun (≈ 14,70,00,000 km)?** 3,65,000 km per year → 14,70,00,000 ÷ 3,65,000 ≈ **403 years** → not in a lifetime.
- **Lift 1 lakh sheets at 5 g each?** 5,00,000 g = **500 kg** → no.
- **250 babies a minute — a million a day?** 250 × 60 × 24 = **3,60,000** → no, a million is 10 lakh.
- **Count a million coins in a day, one per second?** A day has 24 × 60 × 60 = **86,400** seconds → no.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why stages help:** "per day → per year → per 10 years" keeps each step small, so mistakes are easy to spot.

## Figure it Out — end of chapter (worked)

1. **Using 0–9 once each:** largest multiple of 5 = **9876543210** (ends in 0, everything else in descending order). Smallest even number = **1023456798** (start with the smallest non-zero 1, then 0, ascend, but the last digit must be even — swap 9 and 8 so it ends in 8).
2. **A 7-digit number name with the most letters:** the book offers **77,77,777** ("seventy seven lakh seventy seven thousand seven hundred seventy seven", 60 letters).
3. **A 9-digit number where swapping any two digits makes it bigger:** digits must be in increasing order with no zero, e.g. **123456789**. Any swap moves a bigger digit to the left → bigger number. (Only one such number with digits 1–9 all different.)
4. **Strike 10 digits from 12345123451234512345 to leave the largest:** greedily keep the biggest available leading digits → **5534512345**.
5. **Clicks with only +10,000 and +100:** 20,800 = 2 + 8 = **10**; 92,100 = 9 + 21 = **30**; 1,20,500 = 12 + 5 = **17**; 65,30,000 = **653**; 70,25,700 = 702 + 57 = **759**.
6. **Lakhs in a billion:** 1 billion = 100 crore = 100 × 100 lakh = **10,000 lakh**.
7. **1000th digit when writing 1, 2, 3, …:** 1–9 use 9 digits; 10–99 use 90 × 2 = 180 → 189 so far. 1000 − 189 = 811 = 270 × 3 + 1, so after 270 three-digit numbers (100 to 369) we need 1 more digit → the first digit of **370**, which is **3**.
8. **Coins to match the Statue of Unity (1 mm each):** 180 m = 1,80,000 mm → **1,80,000 coins**.
9. **Albatross's 12,000 km trip at 900–1000 km/day:** 12,000 ÷ 1000 = 12, 12,000 ÷ 900 ≈ 13.3 → **about 12–14 days**.
10. **Godwit: 13,560 km in 11 days:** ≈ **1,233 km a day** → ÷ 24 ≈ **51 km an hour**.
11. **Heights vs Somu's 40 m building:** eagles 4500–6000 m → **112–150 times**; Everest 8850 m → **about 221 times**; planes 10,000–12,800 m → **250–320 times**.

## In a nutshell

- **1 lakh = 1,00,000 (5 zeros)**, **1 crore = 1,00,00,000 (7 zeros)**, **1 arab = 100 crore (9 zeros)**.
- **1 million = 10 lakh**, **1 billion = 100 crore**.
- Indian commas **3-2-2-2**, American **3-3-3**.
- **Fewest button clicks = digit sum** → the place-value expansion.
- Rounding: look right of the place; **half or more → up**.
- × 5, × 25, × 125 → × 10, 100, 1000 then ÷ 2, 4, 8.
- a-digit × b-digit has **a+b−1 or a+b** digits.
- Big questions: **assume, then estimate in stages**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** forgetting the 0 in an empty place (4,07,704 not 4,7,704) · a million is **10 lakh**, not 1 lakh · a billion is **100 crore**, not 1 crore · rounding 6,72,85,183 to the nearest ten lakh is 6,70,00,000 (look at 2,85,183, not just the next digit) · 3-digit × 3-digit can never be a 4-digit number.
