## What is an arithmetic expression?

Phrases like **13 + 2**, **20 − 4**, **12 × 5**, **18 ÷ 3** are **arithmetic expressions**. Every expression has a **value** — the single number it works out to. We join an expression and its value with "=": 13 + 2 = 15.

**Example (book):** Mallika spends ₹25 on lunch each school day, Monday to Friday. Total = **5 × 25** ("5 times 25") = ₹125.

> [!NOTE]
> **Many expressions can have the same value.** 12 = 10 + 2 = 15 − 3 = 3 × 4 = 24 ÷ 2. An expression is like a *recipe*; the value is the *dish*. Different recipes can make the same dish.

### Comparing expressions

We compare expressions by their values: 10 + 2 > 7 + 1 (12 > 8), and 13 − 2 < 4 × 3 (11 < 12).

**Book Figure it Out:**
1. 13 + 4 = **11** + 6 · 22 + **8** = 6 × 5 · 8 × **4** = 64 ÷ 2 · 34 − **9** = 25
2. Ascending: 120 ÷ 3 (40) < 67 − 20 (47) < 67 − 19 (48) < 5 × 11 (55) < 35 + 25 (60).

## Comparing without calculating — the marbles trick

**Which is greater: 1023 + 125 or 1022 + 128?** Picture it. Raja had 1023 marbles and got 125. Joy had 1022 and got 128. Raja started **1 ahead**, but Joy got **3 more** today. So Joy ends **2 ahead**: 1023 + 125 **<** 1022 + 128.

**113 − 25 or 112 − 24?** Raja had 1 more marble but also **lost 1 more**. So they end level: 113 − 25 **=** 112 − 24.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In a sum, compare each part: whatever extra one side has in one number, check what it loses in the other.**
> **In a difference, increasing BOTH numbers by the same amount leaves the difference unchanged.**
> **Why:** a difference is the gap between two numbers. Slide both along the number line by the same amount and the gap stays the same width.

**Book exercise, reasoned:**
- 245 + 289 **>** 246 + 285 → left gains 1 less in the first number but 4 more in the second → left is 3 bigger.
- 273 − 145 **=** 272 − 144 → both numbers 1 smaller → same gap.
- 364 + 587 **<** 363 + 589 → right loses 1, gains 2 → right is 1 bigger.
- 124 + 245 **<** 129 + 245 → same second number, smaller first.
- 213 − 77 **<** 214 − 76 → right starts 1 higher *and* takes away 1 less → right is 2 bigger.

## Why we need brackets

"Shalini sat next to a friend with toys" — who has the toys? A comma changes the meaning. Maths has the same problem.

**Example (book):** Mallesh brought 30 marbles. Arun brought 5 bags of 4 marbles. He writes **30 + 5 × 4**.
- Purna adds first: (30 + 5) × 4 = **140** — wrong for this story.
- Mallesh multiplies first: 30 + (5 × 4) = 30 + 20 = **50** — right.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Brackets say "do me first".** Work out everything inside a bracket before using it in the rest of the expression.

**Example (book):** Irfan buys biscuits ₹15 and toor dal ₹56 and pays with ₹100. Change = **100 − (15 + 56)** = 100 − 71 = **₹29**. Without brackets, 100 − 15 + 56 = 141 — more than he paid! The brackets show "subtract the *total* cost".

## Terms — the parts joined by +

When there are no brackets, we use **terms** to decide the order.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Terms are the parts of an expression separated by + signs** (after turning every subtraction into "+ the inverse").
> - 83 − 14 = 83 + (−14) → terms **83** and **−14**.
> - 2 − 10 + 4 × 6 = 2 + (−10) + 4 × 6 → terms **2, −10, 4 × 6**.
> - A product like **6 × 5** is **one** term: there is no + inside it.

**Why "subtracting 14 = adding −14":** in the token model from Class 6, a positive token and a negative token cancel. Taking away 14 positive tokens has the same effect as dropping in 14 negative tokens — each negative one cancels one positive one.

| Expression | As a sum of terms | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| 13 − 2 + 6 | 13 + (−2) + 6 | 13, −2, 6 |
| 5 + 6 × 3 | 5 + 6 × 3 | 5, 6 × 3 |
| 4 + 15 − 9 | 4 + 15 + (−9) | 4, 15, −9 |
| 23 − 2 × 4 + 16 | 23 + (−2 × 4) + 16 | 23, −2 × 4, 16 |
| 28 + 19 − 8 | 28 + 19 + (−8) | 28, 19, −8 |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How to evaluate: first find the value of each term (do the × and ÷ inside it, and any brackets), then add all the terms.**
> 30 + 5 × 4 → terms 30 and 20 → **50**.
> 5 × (3 + 2) + 7 × 8 + 3 → terms 25, 56, 3 → **84**.
>
> **Why this rule (and not "left to right"):** a term like 5 × 4 is a *bundle* — "5 bags of 4". It is a single quantity, so it must be counted as one number before you add it to anything else.

## Swapping and grouping terms

**Drone (book):** a drone goes 6 m up, then 4 m down: 6 + (−4) = 2. Swap: (−4) + 6 = 2. Same.

**Three terms:** (−7) + 10 + (−11). Add the first two first: 3 + (−11) = −8. Add the last two first: (−7) + (−1) = −8. Add −7 and −11 first: −18 + 10 = −8. Always **−8**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When an expression has only additions, you can add the terms in ANY order and group them ANY way — the value doesn't change.**
> - Swapping = **commutative property** of addition.
> - Grouping = **associative property** of addition.
>
> **Why (token model):** a sum is just "put all these tokens in one pile and cancel pairs". It doesn't matter in which order the tokens are dropped in — the pile at the end is the same.

**Manasa's problem (book):** she added a long list and got 11,749, then noticed she forgot 9,055. Does she start again? **No** — by grouping, she just adds 9,055 to her total: 11,749 + 9,055 = **20,804**.

> [!TIP]
> **Everyday version:** "hat and shoes" — order doesn't matter. "Socks and shoes" — order matters! Addition is like hat-and-shoes. Subtraction is socks-and-shoes: 10 − 3 ≠ 3 − 10. That's exactly why we turn subtractions into "+ negative" first — then everything is an addition and the order is free.

## Writing expressions for stories

- **Dosas:** 4 friends, dosa ₹23 each, tip ₹5 → **4 × 23 + 5** = 92 + 5 = **₹97**. With 7 friends: 7 × 23 + 5 = **₹166** (terms 7 × 23 and 5).
- **Fire in the mountain:** 33 children, teacher calls 5 → 6 groups of 5 and 3 left over: **6 × 5 + 3**. Calls 4 → **8 × 4 + 1**. Calls 7 → **4 × 7 + 5**.
- **Rice packets:** had 4 packets, packs 100 kg into 2 kg packets → **4 + 100 ÷ 2** = 4 + 50 = **54 packets**.
- **Paying ₹432:** 4 × 100 + 1 × 20 + 1 × 10 + 2 × 1, or 8 × 50 + 1 × 10 + 4 × 5 + 2 × 1 — every product is one term.
- **5 × 2 + 3** means "3 more than 5 × 2" = 13. But "2 rows of (5 + 3)" needs brackets: **2 × (5 + 3)** = 16 = 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 5 × 2 + 3 × 2.

**Book Figure it Out — values by terms:**
- 28 − 7 + 8 → 28, −7, 8 → **29**
- 39 − 2 × 6 + 11 → 39, −12, 11 → **38**
- 40 − 10 + 10 + 10 → **50**
- 48 − 10 × 2 + 16 ÷ 2 → 48, −20, 8 → **36**
- 6 × 3 − 4 × 8 × 5 → 18, −160 → **−142**
- **Queen Alia:** Elsa doubled her 100 coins, Anna has half of hers: **2 × 100 + 100 ÷ 2** = 200 + 50 = **250 coins**.
- **Metro:** adult ₹40, child ₹20. Four adults and three children: 4 × 40 + 3 × 20 = **₹220**. Two groups of three adults: 2 × (3 × 40) = **₹240**.

## Removing brackets — I (a minus in front)

**200 − (40 + 3).** You could do 200 − 43 = 157. Or: take away 40 (→ 160), then take away 3 (→ 157). That's **200 − 40 − 3**.

**500 − (250 − 100).** We must take away only 150. If we take away 250, we've taken away 100 too many — so **add 100 back**: **500 − 250 + 100** = 350.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When you remove a bracket that has a − in front, every term inside changes sign.**
> 100 − (15 + 56) = 100 − 15 − 56 · 500 − (250 − 100) = 500 − 250 + 100
> **When the bracket has a + in front, nothing changes:** 28 + (35 − 10) = 28 + 35 − 10 = 53.
>
> **Why:** "− (bracket)" means "take away the whole bracket's value". Taking away a sum = take away each part. Taking away a difference (250 − 100) means you're taking away *less* than 250 — so after subtracting 250 you must give back the 100.

> [!TIP]
> The book says it well: rather than memorising when to change signs, **think about what the expression means** — are you taking away more than you should, or less?

### Tinker the terms — I

If one term grows by 1, the value grows by 1. So from 53 + (−16) = 37:
- 54 + (−16) = **38** · 52 + (−16) = **36**
- 53 + (−15) = **38** (−15 is one *more* than −16) · 53 + (−17) = **36**

From −87 + (−16) = **−103**: −88 + (−15) = **−103** (one term down 1, the other up 1), −86 + (−18) = **−104**, −97 + (−26) = **−123**.

**Book Figure it Out (removing brackets):**
1. 24 + (6 − 4) = 24 + 6 **− 4** · 38 + (9 − 4) = 38 + 9 − 4 · 24 − (6 + 4) = 24 **−** 6 − 4 · 27 − (8 + 3) = 27 − 8 − 3 · 27 − (8 − 3) = 27 − 8 + 3.
2. 14 + (12 + 10) = 14 + 12 + 10 · 14 − (12 + 10) = 14 − 12 − 10 · 14 + (12 − 10) = 14 + 12 − 10 · 14 − (12 − 10) = 14 − 12 + 10 · −14 + (12 − 10) = −14 + 12 − 10 · 14 − (−12 − 10) = 14 + 12 + 10.
3. (6 + 10) − 2 = 6 + (10 − 2) = **14** ✔ · 16 − (8 − 3) = **11** but (16 − 8) − 3 = **5** ✘ · 27 − (18 + 4) = 27 + (−18 − 4) = **5** ✔. They're equal exactly when the terms come out the same after removing brackets.
4. 319 − 537 and −537 + 319 have the same terms (319, −537) → equal. 537 − 319 has the opposite terms.
5. Brackets for target values: **34 − (9 + 12) = 13** · **(56 − 14) − 8 = 34** · **−22 − (12 + 10) + 22 = −22**.
6. 207 − 68 = 210 − **71** (both numbers up by 3 → same gap).
7. **Jasoda subtracts 9 by "−10 then +1"** — always correct because 9 = 10 − 1, so −9 = −(10 − 1) = −10 + 1. Similarly −8 = −10 + 2, and −99 = −100 + 1.
8. 73 − 14 + 1 equals **73 − (14 − 1)** and **73 + (−14 + 1)**; 73 − 14 − 1 equals **73 − (14 + 1)** and **73 + (−14 − 1)**.

## Removing brackets — II (the distributive property)

**Lhamo and Norbu (book):** each has a cutlet (₹43) and a rasgulla (₹24). Total = **2 × (43 + 24)** — twice one person's bill. That equals 2 cutlets + 2 rasgullas = **2 × 43 + 2 × 24** = 86 + 48 = **₹134**. (Writing 2 × 43 + 24 would mean only one rasgulla!) If Sangmu joins: **3 × (43 + 24)** = ₹201.

**Parade (book):** scouts in 4 rows of 5, guides in 3 rows of 5 → 4 × 5 + 3 × 5 = 20 + 15 = 35 = **(4 + 3) × 5**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Distributive property: a multiple of a sum (or difference) = the sum (or difference) of the multiples.**
> (10 + 3) × 98 = 10 × 98 + 3 × 98 · (14 − 6) × 10 = 14 × 10 − 6 × 10
>
> **Why:** 10 × 98 + 3 × 98 is "ten 98s and three more 98s" — that's simply thirteen 98s. And fourteen 10s with six 10s removed leaves eight 10s.

> [!WARNING]
> **5 × 4 + 3 ≠ 5 × (4 + 3).** The first is 20 + 3 = 23; the second is five 7s = 35. The bracket makes the 5 multiply *both* the 4 and the 3.

### Tinker the terms — II (smart multiplication)

- Given 53 × 18 = 954, find 63 × 18: (53 + 10) × 18 = 954 + 180 = **1,134**.
- 97 × 25 = (100 − 3) × 25 = 2500 − 75 = **2,425**.
- 95 × 8 = 800 − 40 = **760** · 104 × 15 = 1500 + 60 = **1,560** · 49 × 50 = 2500 − 50 = **2,450**.

> [!TIP]
> **Works best when one number is close to a round number** (98, 103, 49, 999…). Split it into "round number ± small bit", then use the distributive property.

**Book Figure it Out (distributive):**
- 3 × (5 + 8) = 3 × 5 **+** 3 × **8** · (9 + 2) × 4 = 9 × 4 **+** 2 × **4** · (13 + 6) × 4 = 13 × 4 + **6 × 4** · (2 + 3) × 4 = 2 × 4 + 3 × 4 · 5 × (8 − 3) = 5 × 8 **−** 5 × **3** · (17 − 9) × 7 = 17 × 7 − 9 × 7.
- **Compare by reasoning:** (8 − 3) × 29 **>** (3 − 8) × 29 (5 vs −5 lots of 29) · 15 + 9 × 18 **<** (15 + 9) × 18 (the right also multiplies the 15) · 23 × (17 − 9) **<** 23 × 17 + 23 × 9 · (34 − 28) × 42 **=** 34 × 42 − 28 × 42.
- **Ways to make 14:** 2 × (1 + 6), 2 × (3 + 4), 2 × (5 + 2), 7 × (1 + 1).

## Story problems (end of chapter)

1. **Mangoes:** Rahim 9 kg, Shyam 11 kg each day, 7 days → **7 × (9 + 11) = 140 kg**.
2. **Binu's savings:** earns ₹20,000, spends 5,000 + 5,000 + 2,000 a month → **12 × (20,000 − 12,000) = ₹96,000** a year.
3. **The snail:** climbs 3 cm by day, slips 2 cm at night on a 10 cm post. Net 1 cm a day → after 7 days it's at 7 cm; on day 8 it climbs 3 cm to **10 cm** and gets the treat **on day 8** (it doesn't slip once it's at the top). Trap: "10 days" forgets the last climb.
4. **Melvin** reads a 2-page story every day except Tuesday and Saturday, for 8 weeks → stories = **(7 − 2) × 8 = 7 × 8 − 2 × 8 = 40**. (5 × 2 × 8 counts *pages*, not stories.)
5. **1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + … + 9 − 10:** pair them, (1 − 2) + (3 − 4) + … = five (−1)s = **−5**; or odds 25 + evens (−30) = −5. **1 − 1 + 1 − 1 … (ten terms) = 0.**
6. **Compare:** 83 × 42 − 18 **>** 83 × 40 − 18 · 145 − 17 × 8 **<** 145 − 17 × 6 · 23 × 48 − 35 **>** 23 × (48 − 35) · (16 − 11) × 12 **=** −11 × 12 + 16 × 12 · (76 − 53) × 88 **>** 88 × (53 − 76) · 25 × (42 + 16) **=** 25 × (43 + 15) · 36 × (28 − 16) **>** 35 × (27 − 15).
7. **Which equal 83 − 37 − 12?** 84 − 38 − 12 ✔ (both up by 1) and −37 + 83 − 12 ✔ (same terms). **Which equal 93 + 37 × 44 + 76?** Only 37 × 44 + 93 + 76 (same terms, reordered).

> [!TIP]
> **Expression Engineer (book puzzle):** with three 3s: (3 + 3) ÷ 3 = 2, 3 + 3 − 3 = 3, 3 × 3 + 3 = 12. Try four 4s for every value 1 to 20, e.g. 4 ÷ 4 + 4 ÷ 4 = 2.

## In a nutshell

- An **expression** has a **value**; different expressions can share a value.
- Compare cleverly: in a **difference**, shift both numbers the same way → same value.
- **Brackets first.** Without brackets, split into **terms** (parts joined by +), work out each term, then add.
- Subtraction = **adding the inverse** → 83 − 14 has terms 83 and −14.
- Terms can be **added in any order, grouped any way**.
- **− ( … )** flips every sign inside; **+ ( … )** changes nothing.
- **Distributive:** a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c; a × (b − c) = a × b − a × c.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 30 + 5 × 4 is 50, not 140 · 100 − (15 + 56) is 100 − 15 − **56**, not + 56 · 5 × 4 + 3 ≠ 5 × (4 + 3) · 6 × 5 is ONE term · the snail reaches the top on day 8, not day 10 · 16 − (8 − 3) ≠ (16 − 8) − 3.
