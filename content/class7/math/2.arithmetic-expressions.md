## What this chapter is about

"30 + 5 × 4": is that 140 or 50? Two careful people can get different answers unless we agree on **rules for reading** an expression. This chapter is about those rules and the reasons behind them:

- what an **expression** and its **value** are, and how to **compare** expressions without long sums;
- **brackets** and **terms**, which tell us what to work out first;
- why we may **swap and group** terms freely;
- how to **remove brackets** correctly, including the tricky minus sign in front;
- the **distributive property**, which turns hard multiplications into easy ones.

## 2.1 Simple expressions

Phrases like **13 + 2**, **20 − 4**, **12 × 5** and **18 ÷ 3** are **arithmetic expressions**. Every expression has a **value**: the single number it works out to. We join an expression and its value with "=":

13 + 2 = 15 (read "13 plus 2", or "the sum of 13 and 2").

**Worked example.** Mallika spends ₹25 on lunch every school day, Monday to Friday. Write an expression for what she spends in a week.

Five days of ₹25 each: **5 × 25** ("5 times 25", or "the product of 5 and 25"). Its value is ₹125.

> [!NOTE]
> **Different expressions can have the same value.** 12 = 10 + 2 = 15 − 3 = 3 × 4 = 24 ÷ 2. Think of an expression as a **recipe** and its value as the **dish**: different recipes can make the same dish.

We compare expressions **by their values**, using =, < and >:
- 10 + 2 > 7 + 1, because 12 is greater than 8;
- 13 − 2 < 4 × 3, because 11 is less than 12.

## 2.2 Comparing without calculating

**Worked example.** Which is greater: 1023 + 125 or 1022 + 128?

Imagine a story. Raja had 1023 marbles and got 125 more. Joy had 1022 marbles and got 128 more.
- Raja started **1 ahead** (1023 is 1 more than 1022).
- But Joy got **3 more** today (128 is 3 more than 125).
- So Joy ends **3 − 1 = 2 ahead**.

![Bar models: Raja has 1022, an extra 1, and 125; Joy has 1022, 125 and three extra 1s, so Joy has 2 more](assets/img/class7/math/2/marbles_sum.svg)

So **1023 + 125 < 1022 + 128**, and we never had to add.

**Worked example.** Which is greater: 113 − 25 or 112 − 24?

Raja had 113 marbles and lost 25. Joy had 112 and lost 24. Raja started 1 ahead, but also **lost 1 more**. So they end **level**: **113 − 25 = 112 − 24**.

**Try this.** Draw a number line from 0 to 20. Mark 12 and 5 and look at the gap between them (7). Now slide both marks 3 steps to the right, to 15 and 8. Measure the gap again. **What you will see:** it is still 7. **Why:** each mark moved the same distance, so the space between them could not change. This is the secret behind comparing differences.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In a sum:** compare the parts one by one. What one side gains in one part, check whether it loses it in another.
> **In a difference:** if both numbers go up (or down) by the **same** amount, the difference stays the **same**.
>
> **Why it works:** a difference is the **gap** between two numbers. If you slide both numbers along the number line by the same amount, the gap keeps the same width. For instance, 113 − 25 and 112 − 24: both numbers moved down by 1, so the gap (88) did not change.

### Check yourself

Write <, > or = without long calculations. Explain your thinking.

**(a)** 245 + 289 ___ 246 + 285

> [!ANSWER]
> First parts: 245 is **1 less** than 246. Second parts: 289 is **4 more** than 285. Overall the left side is 4 − 1 = **3 more**. So **245 + 289 > 246 + 285**.

**(b)** 273 − 145 ___ 272 − 144

> [!ANSWER]
> Both numbers on the right are 1 less than on the left. Sliding both by the same amount keeps the gap the same. So **273 − 145 = 272 − 144**.

**(c)** 364 + 587 ___ 363 + 589

> [!ANSWER]
> The left has 1 more in the first part (364 vs 363) but 2 less in the second (587 vs 589). Overall the left is 1 **less**. So **364 + 587 < 363 + 589**.

**(d)** 124 + 245 ___ 129 + 245

> [!ANSWER]
> Same second part (245); the first part 124 is less than 129. So **124 + 245 < 129 + 245**.

**(e)** 213 − 77 ___ 214 − 76

> [!ANSWER]
> The right side **starts** with 1 more (214 vs 213) **and takes away** 1 less (76 vs 77). Both changes make it bigger, so the right is 2 more. **213 − 77 < 214 − 76**.

## 2.3 Reading complex expressions

Look at two sentences:
- "Shalini sat next to a friend with toys." (The **friend** has the toys.)
- "Shalini sat next to a friend, with toys." (**Shalini** has the toys.)

One comma changes the meaning. Expressions can be unclear in the same way.

**Worked example.** Mallesh brought 30 marbles to the playground. Arun brought 5 bags with 4 marbles in each bag. Mallesh writes the total as **30 + 5 × 4**.

- Purna adds first: 30 + 5 = 35, then 35 × 4 = **140**.
- Mallesh multiplies first: 5 × 4 = 20, then 30 + 20 = **50**.

The story says Arun brought 5 bags of 4, which is 20 marbles, so **50** is right. But just looking at "30 + 5 × 4", how would anyone know? Maths uses two tools to remove the confusion: **brackets** and **terms**.

### Brackets

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Brackets mean "work me out first".** Find the value of everything inside a bracket before using it in the rest of the expression.
> 30 + (5 × 4) = 30 + 20 = 50.

**Worked example.** Irfan buys biscuits for ₹15 and toor dal for ₹56. He pays with a ₹100 note. Write an expression for his change.

The total cost is 15 + 56. His change is 100 **minus the total**: **100 − (15 + 56)** = 100 − 71 = **₹29**.

Without brackets, 100 − 15 + 56 would mean "take away 15, then add 56", giving 141: more than he paid! The brackets show that the **whole** cost is taken away.

## 2.4 Terms

What if there are no brackets, as in 30 + 5 × 4? Then we use **terms**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Terms are the parts of an expression joined by + signs**, after every subtraction has been turned into "adding the inverse".
> - 12 + 7 → terms **12** and **7**
> - 83 − 14 = 83 + (−14) → terms **83** and **−14**
> - −18 − 3 = −18 + (−3) → terms **−18** and **−3**
> - 6 × 5 + 3 → terms **6 × 5** and **3** (a product with no + inside is **one** term)
> - 2 − 10 + 4 × 6 = 2 + (−10) + 4 × 6 → terms **2, −10, 4 × 6**

The **inverse** of a number is the number with the opposite sign: the inverse of 14 is −14, and the inverse of −14 is 14.

**Why subtracting a number is the same as adding its inverse.** Use the **token model** from Class 6: a positive token (+1) and a negative token (−1) cancel each other out. Start with 83 positive tokens.
- **Subtract 14:** take away 14 positive tokens. 69 are left.
- **Add −14:** drop in 14 negative tokens. Each one cancels one positive token, so 14 positive tokens disappear. 69 are left.

Both ways leave the same 69, so 83 − 14 = 83 + (−14).

**Try this.** Use two kinds of buttons or coins as tokens (say, white = +1 and black = −1). Put down 7 white ones. In one pile, take away 3 white ones. In another pile of 7 white ones, add 3 black ones and remove each white–black pair. **What you will see:** both piles end with 4 white tokens. **Why:** a white and a black together make zero, so adding a black token wipes out a white one, exactly as taking a white one away does.

## 2.5 Swapping and grouping terms

**Worked example.** A drone goes 6 m up and then 4 m down. Its final height above the terrace is 6 − 4 = 6 + (−4) = **2 m**. Swap the terms: (−4) + 6 = 2. Same answer.

**Worked example.** Add (−7) + 10 + (−11) in three different orders.
- First two first: (−7) + 10 = 3; then 3 + (−11) = **−8**.
- Last two first: 10 + (−11) = −1; then (−7) + (−1) = **−8**.
- The two negatives first: (−7) + (−11) = −18; then −18 + 10 = **−8**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When an expression is only additions of terms, you may add the terms in any order and group them any way. The value does not change.**
> - "Swapping does not change the sum" is the **commutative property** of addition.
> - "Grouping does not change the sum" is the **associative property** of addition.
>
> **Why it works (token model):** adding terms means putting all their tokens into **one pile**, then cancelling positive–negative pairs. The final pile is the same whichever handful of tokens you drop in first.

**Try this.** Write −7, 10 and −11 on three slips of paper, and act each one out with tokens: put all the tokens on the table, in whatever order you like, then remove white–black pairs. Shuffle the slips and do it again. **What you will see:** the same 8 black tokens are left every time, so the sum is always −8. **Why:** the order you put tokens down cannot change what is on the table at the end.

> [!TIP]
> **Hat and shoes, or socks and shoes?** Putting on a hat and shoes: the order doesn't matter. Socks and shoes: it does! Addition is like hat and shoes. Subtraction is like socks and shoes: 10 − 3 is not 3 − 10. That is exactly why we turn each subtraction into "+ a negative" first: then everything is addition, and the order is free.

### Check yourself

**1.** Does swapping two terms still give the same sum when some terms are negative? Check examples, and explain with tokens.

> [!ANSWER]
> Examples: (−4) + (−2) = −6 and (−2) + (−4) = −6 ✓; 9 + (−5) = 4 and (−5) + 9 = 4 ✓.
>
> **Why:** a + b means "put a's tokens and b's tokens in one pile". The pile is the same whether a's tokens or b's go in first, so the number left after cancelling is the same.

**2.** Does grouping terms in different ways still give the same sum when some are negative? Check, and explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> (−6) + (−7) + (−13): grouping the first two first: −13 + (−13) = −26. Grouping the last two first: −6 + (−20) = −26 ✓.
>
> **Why:** grouping only changes **which tokens you combine first**. All the tokens still end up in the same pile, so the result is the same.

**3.** Does adding the terms in **any** order give the same value, even with more than 3 terms?

> [!ANSWER]
> Yes. For instance, 15 + (−8) + 5 + 12:
> - In order: 15 + (−8) = 7; 7 + 5 = 12; 12 + 12 = **24**.
> - Positives first: 15 + 5 + 12 = 32; then 32 + (−8) = **24**.
>
> Any order is just a series of swaps of neighbours and regroupings, and each of those keeps the value.

**4.** Manasa added a long list of numbers and got 11,749. Then she noticed she had left out the fourth number, 9,055. Must she start again?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The terms can be added in any order, so she can add the forgotten term **last**: 11,749 + 9,055 = **20,804**. Grouping lets her keep all her earlier work.

## 2.6 Evaluating expressions with terms

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How to find the value of an expression without brackets:** split it into **terms**, find the value of **each term** (do the × and ÷ inside it, and any brackets), then **add** all the terms.
> - 30 + 5 × 4 → terms 30 and 5 × 4 = 20 → 30 + 20 = **50**
> - 5 × (3 + 2) + 7 × 8 + 3 → terms 5 × 5 = 25, 7 × 8 = 56, and 3 → 25 + 56 + 3 = **84**
>
> **Why multiply inside a term first:** a term like 5 × 4 is a **bundle** ("5 bags of 4 marbles"). It is a single amount, so we must count it as one number before adding it to anything else.

**Worked example.** Four friends order 4 dosas at ₹23 each and give the waiter a ₹5 tip. The total is **4 × 23 + 5**: terms 4 × 23 = 92 and 5, so ₹97.

**Worked example.** In the game "Fire in the mountain, run, run, run!", children form groups of the size the teacher calls; anyone left over is out. 33 children play and the teacher calls 5. They make 6 groups of 5, with 3 left over, so Ruby writes **6 × 5 + 3** (terms 6 × 5 and 3). Check: 30 + 3 = 33 ✓.

**Worked example.** Raghu packs 100 kg of rice into 2 kg packets. He already had four 2 kg packets. Number of packets now: **4 + 100 ÷ 2** (terms 4 and 100 ÷ 2) = 4 + 50 = **54**.

**Worked example.** Which arrangement shows 5 × 2 + 3?

![Left: five green columns of 2 and a red column of 3, showing 5 × 2 + 3 = 13. Right: two columns of 3 blue and 5 yellow squares, showing 2 × (5 + 3) = 16](assets/img/class7/math/2/arrangements.svg)

**Try this.** Type 30 + 5 × 4 into a simple pocket calculator, pressing = only at the end, then into a phone calculator. **What you may see:** a simple calculator works each step as you press it and shows 140, while most phone and scientific calculators show 50. **Why:** the second kind follows the agreed rule (find each term, then add); the simple kind just works left to right. Brackets remove all doubt: 30 + (5 × 4).

5 × 2 + 3 means "**3 more than** 5 × 2" = 10 + 3 = 13: that is the **left** picture (five 2s, then 3 more). The right picture is **two** columns of (5 yellow + 3 blue), which needs brackets: **2 × (5 + 3)** = 16. It can also be written 5 + 3 + 5 + 3, or 5 × 2 + 3 × 2.

## 2.7 Removing brackets — I

**Worked example.** Find 200 − (40 + 3).

We could work out the bracket first: 200 − 43 = 157. But it is often easier to take away 40 (leaving 160), then take away 3 more (leaving 157). That is **200 − 40 − 3**. (Not 200 − 40 + 3: we must take away the 3 too.)

So 200 − (40 + 3) = 200 − 40 − 3. Irfan's change works the same way: 100 − (15 + 56) = 100 − 15 − 56 = 85 − 56 = 29. First take away the biscuits, then the dal.

**Worked example.** Write 500 − (250 − 100) without brackets.

The bracket is 150, so we should take away only 150. If we take away all 250, we have taken away **100 too much**, so we must **add 100 back**: 500 − 250 + 100 = 350. ✓ (500 − 150 = 350.)

**Worked example.** Hira has 28 coins in one bag and 35 in another, and gives away 10 from the second bag: 28 + (35 − 10). The bracket is 35 + (−10), and terms can be added in any order, so this is just 28 + 35 − 10 = **53**. Nothing changed sign.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Removing a bracket with a minus in front: every term inside changes sign.**
> 200 − (40 + 3) = 200 − 40 − 3 · 500 − (250 − 100) = 500 − 250 + 100
> **Removing a bracket with a plus in front: nothing changes.**
> 28 + (35 − 10) = 28 + 35 − 10
>
> **Why it works:** "− (bracket)" means "take away the whole bracket". Taking away a **sum** means taking away **each** part. Taking away a **difference** like (250 − 100) means taking away **less** than 250, so after taking away 250 you must give back the 100. With a plus in front, the terms inside are simply more terms to add, in any order.

> [!TIP]
> Don't just memorise "change the signs". Ask what the expression **means**: am I taking away more than I should, or less? Then fix it.

### Check yourself

**1.** Check that 500 − (250 − 100) is **not** equal to 500 − 250 − 100.

> [!ANSWER]
> 500 − (250 − 100) = 500 − 150 = **350**. But 500 − 250 − 100 = 250 − 100 = **150**. They differ by 200: the second takes away 100 when it should give 100 back, so it is wrong twice over (100 + 100 = 200).

## 2.8 Tinker the terms — I

What happens to a sum if we change one of its terms a little? **If one term goes up by 1, the sum goes up by 1; if a term goes down by 1, the sum goes down by 1.**

**Worked example.** 53 + (−16) = 37. Then 54 + (−16) = **38** (54 is one more than 53), and 52 + (−16) = **36**.

## 2.9 Removing brackets — II: the distributive property

**Worked example.** Lhamo and Norbu each order a vegetable cutlet (₹43) and a rasgulla (₹24). What do they pay together?

One person's bill is 43 + 24. Could the total be 2 × 43 + 24? No: that means "24 more than 2 × 43", which pays for only **one** rasgulla. We want **double the whole bill**, so we need brackets: **2 × (43 + 24)** = 2 × 67 = ₹134.

It is also two cutlets and two rasgullas: **2 × 43 + 2 × 24** = 86 + 48 = ₹134. So 2 × (43 + 24) = 2 × 43 + 2 × 24.

**Worked example.** In a parade, scouts march in 4 rows of 5 and guides in 3 rows of 5. How many in all?

![Dots: 4 blue rows of 5 scouts above 3 red rows of 5 guides; (4 + 3) × 5 = 4 × 5 + 3 × 5 = 35](assets/img/class7/math/2/parade.svg)

**Try this.** Arrange 35 counters (beans or bottle caps) in 7 rows of 5, then push a ruler between the 4th and 5th rows. **What you will see:** one picture shows both 7 × 5 and 4 × 5 + 3 × 5. **Why:** splitting the rows does not add or remove any counter, so the two expressions must have the same value.

Count each group: 4 × 5 + 3 × 5 = 20 + 15 = 35. Or count the rows first: (4 + 3) rows of 5 = 7 × 5 = 35. So **(4 + 3) × 5 = 4 × 5 + 3 × 5**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The distributive property: a multiple of a sum (or difference) equals the sum (or difference) of the multiples.**
> (10 + 3) × 98 = 10 × 98 + 3 × 98 · 98 × (10 + 3) = 98 × 10 + 98 × 3 · (14 − 6) × 10 = 14 × 10 − 6 × 10
>
> **Why it works:** 10 × 98 + 3 × 98 is "ten 98s, and three more 98s". Put them together and you have thirteen 98s, which is (10 + 3) × 98. In the same way, fourteen 10s with six 10s taken away leaves eight 10s: (14 − 6) × 10.

> [!WARNING]
> **5 × 4 + 3 is not 5 × (4 + 3).** The first is 20 + 3 = 23. The second is five 7s = 35. The bracket makes the 5 multiply **both** the 4 and the 3.

> [!ANSWER]

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes**, all are 35. 4 + 3 = 3 + 4 (swapping terms), and 5 × 7 = 7 × 5 (swapping the numbers in a product does not change it).

## In a nutshell

- An **expression** has a **value**. Different expressions can have the same value.
- Compare cleverly: in a **sum**, weigh what each part gains or loses; in a **difference**, shifting both numbers the same way keeps the value.
- **Brackets first.** With no brackets, split the expression into **terms** (parts joined by +), work out each term, then add.
- **Subtracting = adding the inverse**: 83 − 14 has terms 83 and −14.
- Terms may be **added in any order and grouped any way** (commutative and associative properties).
- **− ( … ) changes the sign of every term inside**; + ( … ) changes nothing.
- **Distributive property:** a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c, and a × (b − c) = a × b − a × c. Use it to multiply near round numbers.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 30 + 5 × 4 is 50, not 140 · 100 − (15 + 56) is 100 − 15 − 56, not 100 − 15 + 56 · 16 − (8 − 3) is not (16 − 8) − 3 · 5 × 4 + 3 is not 5 × (4 + 3) · 6 × 5 is **one** term · the snail reaches the top on day 8, not day 10 · 2 × 43 + 24 pays for only one rasgulla.
