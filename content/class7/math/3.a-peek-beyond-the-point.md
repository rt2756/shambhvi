## Why we need smaller units

Sonu's mother couldn't fix a toy because the screw was the wrong size — two screws looked the same but were **slightly different lengths**. A ruler marked only in whole centimetres can say "between 2 cm and 3 cm", but not *exactly* how long. So we **split each unit into 10 equal parts**.

A screw that reaches 2 whole centimetres and then 7 of those small parts is **2 and 7/10 cm**, read "two and seven-tenths centimetres".

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When a whole unit is too coarse to measure something exactly, split the unit into equal smaller parts and count those.** That is the whole idea of this chapter.

## A tenth part

A pencil of length **3 and 4/10 units** = 3 units + 4 one-tenths.

**Ten one-tenths make one unit:** 1/10 + 1/10 + … (10 times) = 10/10 = 1.

So the same pencil is also **34 one-tenths** (34/10): 30 tenths make 3 units, plus 4 more tenths.

| Written | Read as |
|---|---|
| 4 1/10 | four and one-tenth |
| 4/10 | four-tenths |
| 41/10 | forty-one tenths (= 4 1/10) |
| 41 1/10 | forty-one and one-tenth |

> [!WARNING]
> **4 1/10 and 41/10 are the SAME** (41 tenths = 4 units + 1 tenth), but **41 1/10 is ten times bigger**. Read slowly!

**Book: arrange in increasing order** 9/10, 1 7/10, 130/10, 13 1/10, 10 5/10, 7 6/10, 6 7/10, 4/10.
Turn each into "units and tenths": 0.9, 1.7, 13, 13.1, 10.5, 7.6, 6.7, 0.4 →
**4/10 < 9/10 < 1 7/10 < 6 7/10 < 7 6/10 < 10 5/10 < 130/10 < 13 1/10**.

### Adding and subtracting tenths

**Sonu's arm (book):** lower arm 2 7/10, upper arm 3 6/10.
Units: 2 + 3 = 5. Tenths: 7 + 6 = 13 tenths = **1 unit and 3 tenths**. Total **6 3/10** units.
Or in tenths: 27 tenths + 36 tenths = 63 tenths = **6 3/10**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Add units with units and tenths with tenths. If you get 10 or more tenths, trade 10 tenths for 1 unit.**
> **Why:** it's exactly the "carry" of ordinary addition — 10 of any place make 1 of the next place up.

**Shylaja's finger (book):** hand 12 4/10, palm 6 7/10. Finger = 12 4/10 − 6 7/10.
You can't take 7 tenths from 4 tenths, so **break one unit**: 12 4/10 = 11 14/10. Now 14 − 7 = 7 tenths, 11 − 6 = 5 units → **5 7/10**. (This is "borrowing".)

**Sequences (book):** find the step, then continue.
- 4, 4 3/10, 4 6/10, … step +3/10 → 4 9/10, 5 2/10, 5 5/10, 5 8/10.
- 13 5/10, 13, 12 5/10, … step −5/10 → 12, 11 5/10, 11, 10 5/10.

## A hundredth part

A sheet 8 9/10 units long is folded in half. It ends between 4 4/10 and 4 5/10 — the tenths markings aren't fine enough. So **split each tenth into 10 again**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **10 hundredths = 1 tenth, and 100 hundredths = 1 unit.**
> **Why:** each unit has 10 tenths and each tenth has 10 small parts → 10 × 10 = 100 small parts in a unit, so one small part is 1/100.

The folded sheet is **4 + 4/10 + 5/100** = "4 units, 4 tenths, 5 hundredths". Since 4 tenths = 40 hundredths, that's also **4 and 45 hundredths**. (Check: half of 8.9 is 4.45 ✔.)

A wire can be written three ways for the same length: 1 + 1/10 + 4/100 = 1 14/100 = 114/100.

**Adding 15 3/10 4/100 + 2 6/10 8/100 (book):**
units 17, tenths 9, hundredths 12. 12 hundredths = 1 tenth + 2 hundredths → tenths become 10 = 1 unit → **18 0/10 2/100 = 18.02**.
Compare this with 483 + 268: (400 + 200) + (80 + 60) + (3 + 8) = 600 + 140 + 11 = 751 — **the same carrying**, just in smaller places.

**Subtracting 25 9/10 − 6 4/10 7/100:** borrow 1 tenth from 9 tenths → 25 8/10 10/100. Then 10 − 7 = 3 hundredths, 8 − 4 = 4 tenths, 25 − 6 = 19 → **19 4/10 3/100 = 19.43**.

## Decimal place value — why always 10?

We *could* split a unit into 4 parts (quarters) or 16 parts. So why tens? Because our **place value system** works in tens:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every place is 10 times the place on its right** (and 1/10 of the place on its left):
> … 1000 · 100 · 10 · 1 · 1/10 · 1/100 · 1/1000 …
> Splitting into 10 continues the same pattern to the right of the ones place, so all the old rules (carrying, borrowing, comparing) keep working. That's why it's called the **decimal** system — Latin *decem* (Sanskrit *daśha*) = ten.

**How big? (book)**
- Thousandths in one unit: **1000** · in one tenth: **100** · in one hundredth: **10**.
- Tenths in one ten: **100** · hundredths in one ten: **1000**.

### The decimal point

Without a marker, "705" could mean 705, or 70 and 5 tenths, or 7 and 5 hundredths. The **decimal point** shows where whole units end:

| Quantity | Decimal |
|---|---|
| 7 hundreds + 5 ones | **705** |
| 7 tens + 5 tenths | **70.5** |
| 7 ones + 5 hundredths | **7.05** |

> [!NOTE]
> **Reading:** 70.5 = "seventy point five"; 7.05 = "seven point zero five"; 0.274 = "zero point two seven four" — **never** "point two hundred seventy four", because the digits are 2 tenths, 7 hundredths, 4 thousandths, not one big number.

**Book: write as decimals.**
- 2 ones, 3 tenths, 5 hundredths → **2.35** · 1 ten and 5 tenths → **10.5** · 4 ones and 6 hundredths → **4.06** · 1 hundred, 1 one, 1 hundredth → **101.01**
- 8/100 and 9/10 → **0.98**
- **234 tenths** = 200/10 + 30/10 + 4/10 = 20 + 3 + 0.4 = **23.4** · 234 hundredths = **2.34** · 105 tenths = **10.5**

> [!TIP]
> **Shortcut that's really place value:** "234 tenths" means the last digit (4) sits in the tenths place, so put the point just before it: 23.4. "234 hundredths": the last digit sits in hundredths → 2.34.

## Units of measurement

All metric units are built on 10s, so converting is just moving between places.

| Fact | So… |
|---|---|
| 1 cm = 10 mm | 1 mm = 1/10 cm = **0.1 cm** |
| 1 m = 100 cm | 1 cm = 1/100 m = **0.01 m** |
| 1 m = 1000 mm | 1 mm = **0.001 m** |
| 1 kg = 1000 g | 1 g = **0.001 kg** |
| 1 g = 1000 mg | 1 mg = **0.001 g** |
| ₹1 = 100 paise | 1 paisa = **₹0.01** |

**Worked conversions (book):**
- mm ↔ cm: 12 mm = 1.2 cm · 56 mm = 5.6 cm · 70 mm = **7 cm** · **9 mm** = 0.9 cm · 134 mm = **13.4 cm** · **2036 mm** = 203.6 cm
- cm ↔ m: 15 cm = 10/100 + 5/100 = **0.15 m** · 36 cm = **0.36 m** · 50 cm = **0.5 m** · **89 cm** = 0.89 m · 4 cm = **0.04 m** · 325 cm = **3.25 m** · **207 cm** = 2.07 m
- g ↔ kg: 5 g = **0.005 kg** · 254 g = **0.254 kg** · 465 g = **0.465 kg** · 68 g = **0.068 kg** · 1560 g = **1.56 kg** · 704 g = **0.704 kg** · **560 g** = 0.56 kg · **2500 g** = 2.5 kg
- paise ↔ ₹: 75 p = **₹0.75** · 10 p = **₹0.10** · **5 p** = ₹0.05 · **36 p** = ₹0.36 · **50 p** = ₹0.50 · 99 p = **₹0.99** · 250 p = **₹2.50**

> [!WARNING]
> **68 g is 0.068 kg, not 0.68 kg.** A gram is a *thousandth* of a kg, so 68 g needs three decimal places: 0.068. Count the zeros in the conversion (1000 → 3 places).

**Rice heaps (book):** 1 g + 10 g + 100 g + 1 kg + 10 kg = 0.001 + 0.01 + 0.1 + 1 + 10 = **11.111 kg** — each heap 10 times the last, so each one fills the next place.

## Locating and comparing decimals

**1.4 on the number line:** split the gap from 1 to 2 into 10 parts and go 4 parts along. To find **1.04**, split the gap from 1 to 1.1 into 10 more parts and go 4 along. To find **4.185**: 4 → 4.1 → 4.18 → 4.185, zooming in by 10 each time.

**When the line isn't split into ones (book):** from 5 to 10 is 5 units split into 10 divisions → each division is **0.5**. So the 5th division is 5 + 2.5 = **7.5**.

### The zero dilemma

Sonu says 0.2 = 0.20 = 0.200. Zara worries extra zeros change it. Place value settles it:

| Number | Units | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 0 | 2 | | |
| 0.20 | 0 | 2 | 0 | |
| 0.200 | 0 | 2 | 0 | 0 |
| 0.02 | 0 | 0 | 2 | |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Zeros at the END of the decimal part don't change the value** (0.2 = 0.20 = 0.200, all "2 tenths"). **Zeros right after the point DO** (0.02 is 2 hundredths — ten times smaller than 0.2).

From 4.5, 4.05, 0.405, 4.050, 4.50, 4.005, 04.50: **4.5 = 4.50 = 04.50** and **4.05 = 4.050**. Smallest 0.405, largest 4.5.

### Comparing digit by digit

**6.456 or 6.465?** Same units (6), same tenths (4). Hundredths: 5 vs 6 → **6.465 is larger.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Compare from the biggest place. At the first place where the digits differ, the bigger digit wins — stop there.**
> **Why you can stop:** everything to the right is worth less than one unit of the place you just compared. In 6.45**6** vs 6.46**5**, all the thousandths together can add at most 9/1000 — never enough to make up 1/100.

- 1.23 vs **1.32** (tenths 2 < 3) · 3.81 vs **13.800** (13 units beat 3) · 1.009 vs **1.090** (hundredths 0 < 9)

> [!WARNING]
> **More digits ≠ bigger.** 0.999 has more digits than 1.2 but is smaller. Always line up the decimal points first.

### Closest decimals

**Closest to 1 from 0.9, 1.1, 1.01, 1.11:** distances 0.1, 0.1, **0.01**, 0.11 → **1.01**. Closest to 1.09 → **1.1** (only 0.01 away).
- Closest to 4 from 3.56, 3.65, 3.099 → **3.65** (0.35 away).
- Closest to 1 from 0.8, 0.69, 1.08 → **1.08** (0.08 away).

## Adding and subtracting decimals

**Cloth (book):** Priya 2.7 m + Shylaja 3.5 m = **6.2 m** (7 + 5 = 12 tenths → write 2, carry 1). Difference 3.5 − 2.7 = **0.8 m** (borrow a unit → 15 − 7 = 8 tenths).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Line up the decimal points, fill empty places with 0, then add or subtract exactly like whole numbers — carrying or borrowing between places.**
> **Why lining up matters:** you must add tenths to tenths and hundredths to hundredths. Lining up the points lines up the places.

75.345 + 86.691 = **162.036**: thousandths 5 + 1 = 6, hundredths 4 + 9 = 13 (write 3, carry), tenths 3 + 6 + 1 = 10 (write 0, carry), ones 5 + 6 + 1 = 12, tens 7 + 8 + 1 = 16.

### Estimating sums

Sonu claims: the sum of two decimals is **more than** the sum of their whole parts and **less than** that + 2. For 25.936 + 8.202 = 34.138: between 33 and 35 ✔.
**Why it works:** each decimal part is less than 1, so together they add less than 2 to the whole parts. (If both decimal parts are 0, the sum *equals* the whole-part sum — the only exception.)

## Decimal disasters and fake decimals

- **Amsterdam, 2013:** €188 million paid instead of €1.8 million — a program read cents as euros.
- **Air Canada, 1983:** fuel loaded in pounds instead of kg — about half what was needed. The plane glided to an emergency landing.
- **Medicine:** reading 0.05 mg as 0.5 mg gives **10 times** the dose.

**Things that look like decimals but aren't:**
- "Bus arrives **4.5 hours** after noon" → 0.5 hour = 5 tenths of 60 min = **30 min** → **4:30 p.m.** (not 4:05 or 4:50).
- A door "**2.5 ft**" wide is 2 ft **6 inches** (1 ft = 12 in, half = 6 in) — not 2 ft 5 in.
- Cricket "**5.5 overs**" = 5 overs and **5 balls** (an over has 6 balls, so this isn't a true decimal).

> [!NOTE]
> **History:** Indian mathematicians like Śrīdharāchārya (8th century) used decimal fractions. Al-Uqlīdisī (~950 CE) described decimal notation; Napier and Clavius (16th century) used the point, Viète used a comma — some countries still write 1 000,5 for 1,000.5.

## In a nutshell

- Split a unit into **10 tenths**, a tenth into **10 hundredths**, a hundredth into **10 thousandths**.
- The **decimal point** separates whole units from parts: 70.5 = 7 tens + 5 tenths.
- Trailing zeros don't matter (0.2 = 0.20); zeros right after the point do (0.02 ≠ 0.2).
- Compare **place by place from the left**; more digits doesn't mean bigger.
- Add/subtract with **points lined up**, carry and borrow as usual.
- Metric conversions: mm→cm ÷10, cm→m ÷100, g→kg ÷1000, paise→₹ ÷100.
- Watch out for **fake decimals** — hours, feet, overs.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 68 g = 0.068 kg, not 0.68 · 0.274 is "point two seven four" · 1.090 > 1.009 · 4.5 hours = 4 h 30 min · 2.5 ft = 2 ft 6 in · line up the points when adding (0.934 + 0.6 = 1.534, not 0.940).
