## What this chapter is about

Two screws can look the same and still be different lengths, and only one of them fits. A ruler marked in whole centimetres can't tell them apart. To measure **more exactly**, we split each unit into **smaller equal parts**.

In this chapter you will:

- split a unit into **tenths**, then **hundredths**, then **thousandths**;
- see **why** we always split into **10** parts: it extends the place-value system you already know;
- write these numbers with a **decimal point** (like 2.7 or 0.405) and read them correctly;
- convert units (mm, cm, m, g, kg, paise, rupees);
- place decimals on a **number line**, **compare** them, **add** and **subtract** them;
- spot **"fake decimals"** that only look like decimals (cricket overs, feet and inches).

## 3.1 The need for smaller units

Sonu's mother could not fix a toy with one screw, but another screw that looked the same worked. The two screws were of **slightly different lengths**.

Measured on a ruler with only whole centimetres, a screw is "between 2 cm and 3 cm". With half-centimetre marks, it is "more than 2½ cm but less than 3 cm". But on a ruler where **each centimetre is split into 10 equal parts**, we can say exactly: it reaches 2 cm and then **7 of the small parts**. We write this **2 7⁄10 cm** and read it "**two and seven-tenths centimetres**".

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When a unit is too big to measure something exactly, split the unit into equal smaller parts and count those parts.** On a centimetre ruler each small part is **one-tenth** of a centimetre (a millimetre).

**Try this.** Measure a pen, a sharpener and one more object of your choice with a ruler. Write each length as "whole centimetres and tenths", like 13 4⁄10 cm. **What you will see:** almost nothing is an exact number of centimetres; the small marks let you say how much more there is. **Why:** objects are not made to fit our units, so we need smaller units to describe them exactly.

### Check yourself

**1.** Which ruler measures the screws most accurately: one with whole-centimetre marks, half-centimetre marks, or tenth-centimetre marks? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> The ruler with **tenth-centimetre marks**. Its smallest part is the shortest (1⁄10 cm, compared with ½ cm or 1 cm), so it can tell apart lengths that differ by only a tiny amount. **The smaller the smallest mark, the more exactly you can measure.**

**2.** What does a length of 2 7⁄10 cm mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> Go from 0 to **2 whole centimetres**, then take **7 of the 10 equal parts** of the next centimetre. So it is 2 cm and 7⁄10 cm, which is more than 2 cm but less than 3 cm.

**3.** Why did we split the unit into smaller parts to measure the screws?

> [!ANSWER]
> Both screws were between 2 cm and 3 cm, so whole centimetres could not show the difference. Splitting each centimetre into 10 parts gives **smaller units**, and the screws differ by a few of these small parts (for instance, 2 7⁄10 cm against 3 2⁄10 cm).

**4.** Read the lengths of the eraser, the pencil and the piece of chalk.

![An eraser, a pencil and a piece of chalk above a centimetre ruler with tenth marks](assets/img/class7/math/3/ruler_objects.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Count whole centimetres, then the small tenth marks after the last whole number:
> - **Eraser:** 2 cm and 4 tenths = **2 4⁄10 cm**.
> - **Pencil:** 4 cm and 5 tenths = **4 5⁄10 cm**, which is the same as 4½ cm (5 tenths is half of 10 tenths).
> - **Chalk:** 1 cm and 4 tenths = **1 4⁄10 cm**.

## 3.2 A tenth part

This pencil is **3 4⁄10 units** long: 3 units and four one-tenths, that is (3 × 1) + (4 × 1⁄10) units.

![A pencil 3 and 4 tenths units long above a ruler marked in tenths: 3 units + 4 tenths = 34 tenths](assets/img/class7/math/3/tenths_pencil.svg)

**Ten one-tenths make one unit:** 1⁄10 + 1⁄10 + … (ten times) = 10 × 1⁄10 = 1. So the same pencil is also **34 one-tenths** long:

34 × 1⁄10 = 34⁄10 = 10⁄10 + 10⁄10 + 10⁄10 + 4⁄10 = 1 + 1 + 1 + 4⁄10 = 3 4⁄10.

| Written | Read as | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 4 1⁄10 | four and one-tenth | 4 units and 1 tenth |
| 4⁄10 | four-tenths | less than 1 unit |
| 41⁄10 | forty-one tenths | the same as 4 1⁄10 |
| 41 1⁄10 | forty-one and one-tenth | ten times as big as 4 1⁄10 |

> [!WARNING]
> **4 1⁄10 and 41⁄10 are equal** (41 tenths = 40 tenths + 1 tenth = 4 units + 1 tenth), but **41 1⁄10 is about ten times bigger**. Read the whole number part carefully.

**Try this.** Any object can be measured "in two ways": as units and tenths, or as tenths only. A USB cable that reaches 4 units and 8 small parts is **4 8⁄10 units**, or **48⁄10 units**. Measure three things on your desk with a ruler and write each length both ways. **Why both are the same:** every whole unit is 10 tenths, so 4 units are 40 tenths, and 40 + 8 = 48 tenths.

### Check yourself

**1.** Put these lengths in increasing order: 9⁄10, 1 7⁄10, 130⁄10, 13 1⁄10, 10 5⁄10, 7 6⁄10, 6 7⁄10, 4⁄10.

> [!ANSWER]
> First write each as "units and tenths": 9⁄10 = 0 and 9 tenths; 130⁄10 = 13 units (130 tenths ÷ 10). Then compare the whole units first, and the tenths only if the units are equal:
>
> **4⁄10 < 9⁄10 < 1 7⁄10 < 6 7⁄10 < 7 6⁄10 < 10 5⁄10 < 130⁄10 < 13 1⁄10**
>
> (6 7⁄10 comes before 7 6⁄10 because 6 units is less than 7 units, however many tenths there are.)

**2.** Put these in increasing order: 4 1⁄10, 4⁄10, 41⁄10, 41 1⁄10.

> [!ANSWER]
> 4⁄10 is less than 1; 4 1⁄10 and 41⁄10 are both 4 units and 1 tenth; 41 1⁄10 is 41 units and 1 tenth.
>
> **4⁄10 < 4 1⁄10 = 41⁄10 < 41 1⁄10**

### Adding and subtracting tenths

**Worked example.** Sonu's lower arm is 2 7⁄10 units and his upper arm is 3 6⁄10 units. How long is his whole arm?

1. Add the units: 2 + 3 = **5 units**.
2. Add the tenths: 7 + 6 = **13 tenths**.
3. 13 tenths = 10 tenths + 3 tenths = **1 unit and 3 tenths**.
4. Total: 5 + 1 = 6 units and 3 tenths = **6 3⁄10 units**.

Or convert both to tenths: 27 tenths + 36 tenths = 63 tenths = 60 tenths + 3 tenths = **6 3⁄10**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Add units to units and tenths to tenths. If the tenths come to 10 or more, change 10 tenths into 1 unit.**
>
> **Why it works:** 10 tenths make exactly 1 unit, just as 10 ones make 1 ten. It is the same "carry" you use when adding whole numbers.

**Worked example.** Shylaja's hand is 12 4⁄10 units long and her palm 6 7⁄10 units. How long is her middle finger?

The finger is hand minus palm: 12 4⁄10 − 6 7⁄10.
1. Start with the tenths: we can't take 7 tenths from 4 tenths.
2. So **break one unit** of the 12 into 10 tenths: 12 4⁄10 = 11 units and 14 tenths.
3. Tenths: 14 − 7 = **7 tenths**. Units: 11 − 6 = **5 units**.
4. The finger is **5 7⁄10 units**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To subtract, start with the tenths. If there are not enough tenths, break 1 unit into 10 tenths** (this is "borrowing", exactly as with whole numbers).

> 2. Tenths: 3 + 4 + 5 = **12 tenths** = 1 unit and 2 tenths.
> 3. Total: 14 + 1 = 15 units and 2 tenths = **15 2⁄10 units**.

> [!ANSWER]

> [!ANSWER]

> Both fish are tiny: an adult's finger is about 7 or 8 cm long, so the Danio is about a third of a finger and the Goby is shorter than your fingernail.

## 3.3 A hundredth part

A sheet of paper is 8 9⁄10 units long. It is folded in half along its length. How long is it now?

Half of 8 is 4, and half of 9 tenths is 4½ tenths. So the fold ends between 4 4⁄10 and 4 5⁄10, and the tenth marks are not fine enough to say exactly where. We do what we did before: **split each tenth into 10 equal parts**.

- Each tenth now has 10 smaller parts, and a unit has 10 tenths, so a unit has **10 × 10 = 100** of these small parts.
- So each small part is **1⁄100** of a unit: **one-hundredth**.

![The number line from 4 to 5 in tenths, with the piece from 4.4 to 4.5 magnified into hundredths and 4.45 marked](assets/img/class7/math/3/hundredths_zoom.svg)

The folded paper ends at **4 4⁄10 5⁄100**: 4 units, 4 tenths and 5 hundredths.

**Try this.** Cut a strip of paper exactly 8 9⁄10 cm long (8 cm and 9 mm). Fold it in half and measure the folded strip. **What you will see:** it ends halfway between the 4 cm 4 mm mark and the 4 cm 5 mm mark, so millimetre marks are not fine enough. **Why:** half of 9 tenths is 4½ tenths, and only a ruler split into hundredths of a centimetre could show that half-tenth exactly.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **10 hundredths make 1 tenth, and 100 hundredths make 1 unit.**
> So 4 units, 4 tenths and 5 hundredths can also be said as **4 units and 45 hundredths** (4 tenths = 40 hundredths, plus 5).
>
> **Why:** a tenth was split into 10 equal parts, so 10 of those parts rebuild exactly one tenth.

### Adding and subtracting hundredths

**Worked example.** Find 15 3⁄10 4⁄100 + 2 6⁄10 8⁄100.

1. Units: 15 + 2 = 17. Tenths: 3 + 6 = 9. Hundredths: 4 + 8 = 12.
2. 12 hundredths = 1 tenth and 2 hundredths, so the tenths become 9 + 1 = 10.
3. 10 tenths = 1 unit, so the units become 18, with 0 tenths.
4. Answer: **18 2⁄100**.

Or change everything to hundredths: 1534⁄100 + 268⁄100 = 1802⁄100 = **18 2⁄100**.

**Worked example.** Find 25 9⁄10 − 6 4⁄10 7⁄100.
1. Hundredths: 0 − 7 is impossible, so break 1 tenth of the 9 into 10 hundredths: 25 8⁄10 10⁄100.
2. Hundredths: 10 − 7 = 3. Tenths: 8 − 4 = 4. Units: 25 − 6 = 19.
3. Answer: **19 4⁄10 3⁄100**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Add or subtract place by place: hundredths with hundredths, tenths with tenths, units with units.** Carry when a place reaches 10; borrow when a place doesn't have enough.
>
> **Why it works:** 10 hundredths = 1 tenth and 10 tenths = 1 unit, the same "10 of one place make 1 of the next" rule as for whole numbers. So the methods you already know for whole numbers work unchanged.

## 3.4 Decimal place value

Why do we always split a unit into **10** parts? We could split it into 4 parts (quarters), 5, 8 or any number. For example, the same length can be "1 and 5 tenths" or "1 and 2 quarters":

![The same length shown on a line split into tenths (1 and 5 tenths) and on a line split into quarters (1 and 2 quarters)](assets/img/class7/math/3/quarters.svg)

For a finer measure, each quarter could be split into 4 again, giving sixteenths (16 make a unit). So why tenths?

**Because of place value.** In 281, the 2 means 2 hundreds, the 8 means 8 tens and the 1 means 1 one. **Each place is 10 times the place on its right**: 10 ones make 1 ten, 10 tens make 1 hundred, 10 hundreds make 1 thousand. Going the other way, **each place is one-tenth of the place on its left**.

If we keep dividing by 10 past the ones place, we get the **tenths**, then the **hundredths**, then the **thousandths**, and so on:

| … | 1000 | 100 | 10 | 1 | 1⁄10 | 1⁄100 | 1⁄1000 | … |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | thousands | hundreds | tens | ones | tenths | hundredths | thousandths | |

Moving one place to the left always multiplies by 10; moving one place to the right always divides by 10. Splitting into 10 parts makes the new small parts fit **exactly** into this pattern, so all our methods for whole numbers keep working.

This is called the **decimal system**, from the Latin *decem* (ten), which is related to the Sanskrit *daśha*, meaning ten.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **1 unit = 10 tenths = 100 hundredths = 1000 thousandths.**
> **Why:** each new place is made by splitting the one before it into 10 equal parts, so each step multiplies the count by 10.

> [!ANSWER]

> [!ANSWER]
> 1 tenth = 10 hundredths = **100 thousandths**.

### The decimal point

We write 456, not "4 hundreds + 5 tens + 6 ones": the **place** of a digit shows its value. Could we write 4 2⁄10 as just "42"? Then how would anyone know whether 42 means 4 tens and 2 ones, or 4 ones and 2 tenths? Similarly 705 could mean 705, or 70 and 5 tenths, or 7 and 5 hundredths.

So we put a **decimal point** (.) right after the **ones** digit, to show where the whole number ends and the parts smaller than one begin:

| Quantity | Hundreds | Tens | Ones | Tenths | Hundredths | Written |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 hundreds and 5 ones | 7 | 0 | 5 | | | **705** |
| 7 tens and 5 tenths | | 7 | 0 | 5 | | **70.5** |
| 7 ones and 5 hundredths | | | 7 | 0 | 5 | **7.05** |

So 70.5 means 7 × 10 + 5 × 1⁄10, and 7.05 means 7 × 1 + 5 × 1⁄100.

**How to read them:** 70.5 is "seventy **point** five" (short for seventy and five-tenths). 7.05 is "seven point **zero five**" (seven and five-hundredths). 0.274 is "zero point two seven four", **not** "zero point two hundred seventy-four", because it means 2 tenths, 7 hundredths and 4 thousandths.

> [!WARNING]
> **After the point, read the digits one by one.** 0.274 is "point two seven four". The digits after the point are tenths, hundredths, thousandths: they are **not** a whole number.

**Worked example.** Write 234 tenths as a decimal.
234 tenths = 200 tenths + 30 tenths + 4 tenths = 20 + 3 + 4⁄10 = **23.4**. (Just as 23 tens = 230 shifts the digits one place left, 234 tenths shifts them one place right.)

## 3.5 Units of measurement

Our units are related by 10, 100 and 1000, which is why decimals fit them so well.

### Length

**1 cm = 10 mm**, so **1 mm = 1⁄10 cm = 0.1 cm**.
- 5 mm = 5⁄10 cm = **0.5 cm**.
- 12 mm = 10 mm + 2 mm = 1 cm + 2⁄10 cm = **1.2 cm**.
- 5.6 cm = 5 cm + 0.6 cm = 50 mm + 6 mm = **56 mm**.

**1 m = 100 cm**, so **1 cm = 1⁄100 m = 0.01 m**.
- 10 cm = 10⁄100 m = 1⁄10 m = **0.1 m**.
- 15 cm = 15⁄100 m = 1⁄10 + 5⁄100 m = **0.15 m**.

Some small things: a human hair is about **0.1 mm** thick; a newspaper sheet is 0.05 to 0.08 mm thick; a mustard seed is 1 to 2 mm across; a hummingbird's egg is about 1.3 cm long and 0.9 cm wide.

### Weight

**1 kg = 1000 g**, so **1 g = 1⁄1000 kg = 0.001 kg**.
- 5 g = 5⁄1000 kg = **0.005 kg**.
- 254 g = 254⁄1000 kg = 2⁄10 + 5⁄100 + 4⁄1000 kg = **0.254 kg**.

Also **1 g = 1000 mg** (milligrams), so 1 mg = 0.001 g.

### Money

**₹1 = 100 paise**, so **1 paisa = ₹0.01**.
- 75 paise = 75⁄100 rupee = 7⁄10 + 5⁄100 = **₹0.75**.

> [!TIP]
> **To convert a small unit into a big unit, divide by how many small ones fit in the big one** (10, 100 or 1000). Dividing by 10 moves every digit one place to the right; by 100, two places; by 1000, three places. To go from big to small, multiply and move the digits left.

### There is zero dilemma!

Sonu says 0.2 can also be written 0.20 or 0.200. Zara thinks extra zeros on the right change the value. Who is right? Put them in a place-value table:

| Number | Ones | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 0 | 2 | | |
| 0.20 | 0 | 2 | 0 | |
| 0.200 | 0 | 2 | 0 | 0 |
| 0.02 | 0 | 0 | 2 | |
| 0.002 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 2 |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Zeros at the right end of the decimal part do not change the value** (0.2 = 0.20 = 0.200), and neither do zeros at the left end of the whole part (04.5 = 4.5).
> **Zeros in between do matter:** 4.05 ≠ 4.5.
> **Why:** a zero at the very end adds "0 hundredths" or "0 thousandths", which is nothing. A zero in the middle **pushes the other digits** into smaller places.

### Comparing decimals

Which is larger: **6.456 or 6.465**? Compare place by place, starting from the **biggest** place:
1. Ones: both 6. Same.
2. Tenths: both 4. Same.
3. Hundredths: 5 against **6**. Different, and 6 is bigger.

So **6.465 > 6.456**. We stop at the first place where the digits differ.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To compare decimals, compare digits place by place from the left (after lining up the decimal points). The first place where they differ decides.**
>
> **Why the later digits can't change the result:** 6.456 is less than 6.46, however many more digits follow the 5 (6.4559999… is still less than 6.46). Everything after a place is always worth **less than one unit of that place**, so it can never make up a difference of 1 in that place.

## 3.7 Adding and subtracting decimals

**Worked example.** Priya needs 2.7 m of cloth for a skirt and Shylaja 3.5 m for a kurti. How much in total? How much more does Shylaja need?

Line up the decimal points (so tenths are under tenths) and add or subtract as with whole numbers:

| | Sum | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| | &nbsp; 2.7 | &nbsp; 3.5 |
| | + 3.5 | − 2.7 |
| | **6.2** | **0.8** |

- Sum: 7 + 5 = 12 tenths → write 2, carry 1 unit. 2 + 3 + 1 = 6. Total **6.2 m**.
- Difference: 5 − 7 tenths is impossible; break a unit: 15 − 7 = 8 tenths, 2 − 2 = 0. Shylaja needs **0.8 m** more.

This is exactly the 2 7⁄10 + 3 5⁄10 calculation from before, written more compactly.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Line up the decimal points, then add or subtract as with whole numbers**, carrying and borrowing between places. Fill empty places with zeros if it helps: 18 − 8.8 becomes 18.0 − 8.8.
>
> **Why lining up the points matters:** it puts ones under ones, tenths under tenths, hundredths under hundredths. You can only add things of the same kind (tenths to tenths), just as you add tens to tens.

**Worked example.** 75.345 + 86.691, place by place: hundreds 0, tens 7 + 8 = 15, ones 5 + 6 = 11, tenths 3 + 6 = 9, hundredths 4 + 9 = 13, thousandths 5 + 1 = 6. Now carry from the right: 13 hundredths = 1 tenth + 3 hundredths (tenths become 10); 10 tenths = 1 one + 0 tenths (ones become 12); 12 ones = 1 ten + 2 ones (tens become 16); 16 tens = 1 hundred + 6 tens. Answer: **162.036**.

### Estimating sums and differences

Sonu claims: "When you add two decimals, the sum is **more than** the sum of their whole number parts, and **less than** that plus 2."

For 25.936 + 8.202 this says the sum is more than 25 + 8 = 33 and less than 26 + 9 = 35.

### Check yourself

**1.** Is Sonu's claim true for 25.936 + 8.202? Will it work for any two decimals, for instance, 25.93603259 + 8.202?

> [!ANSWER]
> 25.936 + 8.202 = **34.138**, which is between 33 and 35 ✓.
>
> **It always works** (as long as the decimal parts are not both zero). **Why:** each number is its whole part plus a decimal part that is at least 0 and **less than 1**. So the two decimal parts together add something from 0 up to (but not reaching) 2. That puts the sum at or above (whole + whole) and below (whole + whole + 2). With 25.93603259 + 8.202 = 34.13803259 the same is true: more digits don't matter, because the decimal part is still less than 1.

**2.** Find a similar rule for the **difference** of two decimals.

> [!ANSWER]
> For a bigger number with whole part A and a smaller one with whole part B, the difference is **more than (A − B) − 1 and less than (A − B) + 1**.
> **Why:** the first number's decimal part adds something less than 1, and taking away the second number's decimal part removes something less than 1. So the answer can move less than 1 either way from A − B.
> Example: 25.936 − 8.202 = 17.734, which is between 16 and 18 ✓.

**Try this.** Take a shop bill with prices in rupees and paise. Before adding, estimate the total using only the whole rupees (and the "less than 1 more per item" rule). > [!TIP]
> **Estimate before you calculate.** If you get 341.38 for 25.936 + 8.202, the estimate (between 33 and 35) tells you immediately that the decimal point is in the wrong place.

## 3.8 More on the decimal system

### Decimal disasters

Small slips with decimals or units can cause big trouble:
- A city office once paid out about **€188 million** in housing benefits instead of **€1.8 million**, because the computer read amounts in euro **cents** as euros.
- In 1983 an aeroplane ran out of fuel in mid-air because the fuel was worked out in **pounds** instead of **kilograms** (1 pound is only about 0.453 kg), so it carried about half the fuel it needed. The pilots glided to an emergency landing on an old airfield, and everyone survived.
- Reading a medicine dose of **0.05 mg** as **0.5 mg** gives **10 times** the dose.

### Numbers that only look like decimals

Some notations **look** like decimals but are not, because the parts are not tenths.

- **"The bus arrives 4.5 hours after noon."** 0.5 hour is 5 tenths of an hour. One tenth of 60 minutes is 6 minutes, so 0.5 hour = 30 minutes: the bus arrives at **4:30 p.m.**, not 4:05 or 4:50.
- **The door that would not close.** A girl measured a gap as **2 ft 5 inches** and told the carpenter "**2.5 ft**". But 1 ft = 12 inches, so 0.5 ft = 6 inches: he made the door 2 ft **6** inches, one inch too wide!
- **Cricket overs.** "Overs left: 5.5" means 5 overs and **5 balls**. An over has 6 balls, so this is really 5 5⁄6 overs, not 5 and 5 tenths.

> [!WARNING]
> **A decimal point means tenths, hundredths, thousandths.** When the smaller unit is not a tenth of the bigger one (60 minutes, 12 inches, 6 balls), "x.y" is **not** a decimal. Always check the units.

### A little history

Fractions with denominators 10, 100, 1000 were used by ancient Indian astronomers and mathematicians, including Śhrīdharāchārya in the 8th century. Around 950 CE the Arab mathematician al-Uqlīdisī described decimal notation much like ours in a book on Indian arithmetic. In the 16th century the point was used by John Napier and Christopher Clavius, while François Viète used a comma. Many countries still use a comma: there, 1,000.5 is written 1 000,5.

### Check yourself

**1.** Where else do you see "decimal-looking" numbers that are not really decimals?

> [!ANSWER]
> Examples:
> - **Time:** 4.30 on a timetable means 4 hours 30 **minutes** (sixtieths of an hour), not 4.3 hours.
> - **Heights in feet:** "5.6 ft" often means 5 ft 6 **inches** (twelfths of a foot).
> - **Cricket:** 5.5 overs = 5 overs 5 **balls** (sixths of an over).
> - **Dates:** 12.05.2025 is just day, month and year separated by dots.
> - **Version numbers** like 2.10 (which comes after 2.9, unlike the decimal 2.10).
>
> In each case the "point" separates units that are not related by 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> Between 6.4 and 6.5 there are 4 divisions, so each is 0.1 ÷ 4 = **0.025**.
> - **a** = 6.4 + 2 × 0.025 = **6.45**
> - **c** = 6.5 + 0.025 = **6.525**
> - **b** = 6.5 + 2 × 0.025 = **6.55**

### 4. Arrange in descending (decreasing) order.

**(a)** 11.01, 1.011, 1.101, 11.10, 1.01

> [!ANSWER]
> Two have 11 ones; compare their tenths: 11.10 > 11.01. The rest have 1 one; compare tenths (1.101 has 1, the others 0), then hundredths and thousandths: 1.011 > 1.010.
>
> **11.10 > 11.01 > 1.101 > 1.011 > 1.01**

**(b)** 2.567, 2.675, 2.768, 2.499, 2.698

> [!ANSWER]
> All have 2 ones. Tenths: 7, then two 6s, then 5, then 4. For the two 6s, hundredths 9 > 7.
>
> **2.768 > 2.698 > 2.675 > 2.567 > 2.499**

**(c)** 4.678 g, 4.595 g, 4.600 g, 4.656 g, 4.666 g

> [!ANSWER]
> Tenths: four have 6, one has 5 (smallest). Among the 6s, hundredths: 7, 6, 5, 0.
>
> **4.678 g > 4.666 g > 4.656 g > 4.600 g > 4.595 g**

**(d)** 33.13 m, 33.31 m, 33.133 m, 33.331 m, 33.313 m

> [!ANSWER]
> Tenths 3 for three of them, 1 for two. Among tenths 3: hundredths 3 (33.331) beats 1 (33.313, 33.31); then 33.313 > 33.310. Among tenths 1: 33.133 > 33.130.
>
> **33.331 m > 33.313 m > 33.31 m > 33.133 m > 33.13 m**

### 5. Using the digits 1, 4, 0, 8 and 6 (each once), make:

**(a)** the decimal number closest to 30.

> [!ANSWER]
> The whole part should be near 30, but there is no 2 or 3. The choices near 30 are in the teens or the forties: the best are 18.640 (11.36 below) and **40.168** (10.168 above, using the smallest decimal part from 1, 6, 8). **40.168** is closer.

**(b)** the smallest decimal number between 100 and 1000.

> [!ANSWER]
> It needs a 3-digit whole part, as small as possible: first digit 1 (not 0), then 0, then 4 → 104. Then the smallest decimal part from 6 and 8: .68. **104.68**.

### 6. Is a decimal number with more digits always greater than one with fewer digits?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 2.05 has more digits than 2.5, but 2.5 is greater (5 tenths beat 0 tenths). And 0.999 has more digits than 1.1 but is smaller. **What matters is the value of each place, starting from the biggest place**, not how many digits there are.

### 7. Mahi buys 0.25 kg beans, 0.3 kg carrots, 0.5 kg potatoes, 0.2 kg capsicum and 0.05 kg ginger. What is the total weight?

> [!ANSWER]
> Write all in hundredths of a kg: 0.25 + 0.30 + 0.50 + 0.20 + 0.05.
> Hundredths: 5 + 5 = 10 → 1 tenth. Tenths: 2 + 3 + 5 + 2 + 1 = 13 → 1 one and 3 tenths.
> Total: **1.30 kg** (= 1.3 kg).

### 8. Pinto supplies 3.79 L, 4.2 L and 4.25 L of milk in the first three days. In 6 days he supplies 25 L. How much in the last three days?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. First three days: 3.79 + 4.20 + 4.25 = **12.24 L**.
> 2. Last three days: 25.00 − 12.24 = **12.76 L**.
>
> Check: 12.24 + 12.76 = 25.00 ✓.

### 9. Tinku weighed 35.75 kg in January and 34.50 kg in February. Did he gain or lose weight? By how much?

> [!ANSWER]
> 34.50 is less than 35.75, so he **lost** weight. Change: 35.75 − 34.50 = **1.25 kg**.

### 10. Continue the pattern: 5.5, 6.4, 6.39, 7.29, 7.28, 8.18, 8.17, ___, ___

> [!ANSWER]
> The steps alternate: **+0.9**, then **−0.01**. (5.5 + 0.9 = 6.4; 6.4 − 0.01 = 6.39; 6.39 + 0.9 = 7.29; …)
> 8.17 + 0.9 = **9.07**, then 9.07 − 0.01 = **9.06**.

### 11. How many millimetres make 1 kilometre?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1 km = 1000 m, and 1 m = 1000 mm. So 1 km = 1000 × 1000 = **10,00,000 mm** (ten lakh, or one million).

### 12. Railway travel insurance costs 45 paise per passenger. If 1 lakh people buy it in a day, what is the total fee?

> [!ANSWER]
> 45 p × 1,00,000 = 45,00,000 paise. Since 100 paise = ₹1, divide by 100: **₹45,000**. (Or: 45 paise = ₹0.45, and 0.45 × 1,00,000 = 45,000.)

### 15. Using each digit 0–9 at most once, fill the boxes so that the sum is as close as possible to 10.5.

### 16. Write each fraction as a decimal.

**(a)** 1⁄2

> [!ANSWER]

**(b)** 3⁄2

> [!ANSWER]

**(c)** 1⁄4

> [!ANSWER]

**(d)** 3⁄4

> [!ANSWER]

**(e)** 1⁄5

> [!ANSWER]
> 1⁄5 = 2⁄10 = **0.2**.

**(f)** 4⁄5

## In a nutshell

- To measure more exactly, split a unit into **10 tenths**, a tenth into **10 hundredths**, a hundredth into **10 thousandths**.
- **1 unit = 10 tenths = 100 hundredths = 1000 thousandths.** Each place is 10 times the place on its right.
- The **decimal point** sits right after the ones digit: 70.5 = 7 tens + 5 tenths; 7.05 = 7 ones + 5 hundredths.
- Read the digits after the point one by one: 0.274 is "zero point two seven four".
- **Zeros at the end of the decimal part don't change the value** (0.2 = 0.20); zeros in the middle do (4.05 ≠ 4.5).
- **Compare** place by place from the left; the first different digit decides.
- **Add and subtract** with the decimal points lined up, carrying and borrowing as usual.
- Unit facts: 1 cm = 10 mm, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 kg = 1000 g, ₹1 = 100 paise.
- Cricket overs, clock times and feet-and-inches only **look** like decimals.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 4.05 is not 4.5 · 0.274 is not "point two hundred seventy-four" · more digits does not mean bigger (2.05 < 2.5) · line up the **points**, not the last digits, when adding · 4 cm = 0.04 m, not 0.4 m · 68 g = 0.068 kg · 0.5 hour is 30 minutes, not 50 · 5.5 overs is 5 overs and 5 balls.
