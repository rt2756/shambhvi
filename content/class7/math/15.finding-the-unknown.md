## What this chapter is about

You already know how to write **expressions** with letter-numbers, like 2n + 1 or 25p + 50. In this chapter the letter stands for **one particular number we do not know yet**, and we are given a fact about it, such as "2n + 1 is 99". Our job is to **find** that number.

You will learn to:

- solve **balance puzzles**, where some weights are hidden;
- write a puzzle as an **equation**, like 2n + 1 = 99;
- solve equations **step by step**, by doing the same thing to both sides, and **check** the answer;
- turn word problems (parties, savings, sequences, ages of coins…) into equations and solve them.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** An **unknown** is a letter-number whose value we must find. An **equation** says two expressions are equal; the part left of "=" is the **Left Hand Side (LHS)** and the part right of it is the **Right Hand Side (RHS)**. A **solution** is a value of the unknown that makes LHS = RHS.

## 15.1 Unknown weights

### Hanging mobiles

A **mobile** is a bar hung from its middle, with things hanging from each end. The number in the circle at the top is the **total weight** of everything hanging below it.

![Two mobiles: total 4 with 2 and 2, hanging level; total 7 with 4 and 3, tilting](assets/img/class7/math/15/mobile_rule.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two facts about a mobile:**
> 1. The total at the top = the sum of **all** the weights below it.
> 2. If the bar hangs **level**, the left side and the right side weigh **the same**, so **each side is half the total**.
> **Why (2):** a bar hung from its middle tips towards the heavier end. If it stays level, neither end is heavier.

**Worked example.** A level mobile has total 16. On the left hang a triangle, a circle and another triangle; on the right hangs a star. Each triangle weighs 3. Find the circle and the star.

![Mobile with total 16: left triangle, circle, triangle; right star; triangle = 3](assets/img/class7/math/15/mobile_1.svg)

1. The bar is level, so each side is half of 16: **8**.
2. Left side: 3 + circle + 3 = 8, so circle = 8 − 6 = **2**.
3. Right side: the star alone is **8**.
4. Check: 3 + 2 + 3 + 8 = 16. ✓

### Pan balances

A pan balance is level when both pans hold the same weight. All sacks in one picture weigh the same.

**Worked example.** One sack and a 2 kg weight balance a 10 kg weight and a 2 kg weight.

![Balance: sack and 2 kg on the left; 10 kg and 2 kg on the right](assets/img/class7/math/15/scale_1.svg)

Both pans have a 2 kg weight. **Take the 2 kg off both pans**: the scale stays level (we removed the same weight from each side). Now one sack balances 10 kg, so the **sack weighs 10 kg**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A level balance stays level if you add the same weight to both pans, or remove the same weight from both pans** (or double both, or halve both…).
> **Why:** the two pans were equal; changing both by exactly the same amount keeps them equal.
> This one idea is how we will solve every equation in this chapter.

## 15.2 Equations

**Worked example: a matchstick pattern.** Triangles are made from matchsticks:

![Matchstick triangles: positions 1 to 4 use 3, 5, 7 and 9 sticks](assets/img/class7/math/15/match_triangles.svg)

Position 1 uses 3 sticks, position 2 uses 5, position 3 uses 7. **Why 2n + 1?** The first triangle needs 3 sticks, and each new triangle shares one side with the one before, so it adds only **2** sticks. Position n has 1 + 2 + 2 + … = **2n + 1** sticks (one stick, then 2 for each of the n steps).

Jasmine wants to use **exactly 99 sticks**. Which position is that? We need the number n for which

**2n + 1 = 99.**

This is an **equation**: it says the expression 2n + 1 has the value 99.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> An **equation** is a statement that two expressions are equal: **LHS = RHS**. Examples: 3x + 4 = 7; 20 = y − 3; a ÷ 3 = 50; 2z + 4 = 5z − 14.
> **Solving** an equation means finding the value(s) of the letter-number that make LHS equal to RHS.

The balance puzzles were equations too. For the egg mobile (three slices of bread of 2 each balance two eggs, e each): 2 + 2 + 2 = e + e, that is **2e = 6**.

>
> (Trying to solve: 2n + 1 = 200 gives 2n = 199, n = 99.5, which is not a position number.)

## 15.3 Solving equations step by step

### Trial and error

> [!TIP]
> **Trial and error done well:** keep a table, and use each try to aim the next one (too small → go bigger; too big → go smaller). It is a good way to **check** an answer or to get a feel for the size of the answer, even when you solve by steps.

One way to solve 2n + 1 = 99 is to **try values** of n and see how close LHS gets to 99:

| n | 5 | 10 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 49 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2n + 1 | 11 | 21 | 61 | 81 | 101 | **99** ✓ |

So **n = 49**. This is the **trial and error** method. It works, but it can be slow, and if the answer is not a whole number we may never hit it.

### Check yourself

**14.** Can 2n + 1 = 99 have any other solution?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Every time n goes up by 1, 2n + 1 goes up by 2. So as n grows, 2n + 1 keeps **increasing** and passes each value only once: 97 at n = 48, **99 at n = 49**, 101 at n = 50. Below 49 it is less than 99, above 49 more. So n = 49 is the only solution.

**15.** Try solving 5x − 4 = 7 by trial and error.

> [!ANSWER]
> | x | 1 | 2 | 3 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 5x − 4 | 1 | 6 | 11 |
>
> x = 2 gives 6 (too small) and x = 3 gives 11 (too big), so x is **between 2 and 3**: no whole number works. Trying 2.2: 5 × 2.2 − 4 = 11 − 4 = 7 ✓. So **x = 2.2 = 11/5**. But we only found it by lucky guessing between 2 and 3. We need a better method.

### Doing the same to both sides

The balance gave us the idea: **change both sides in exactly the same way, and they stay equal.**

### Check yourself: same on both sides

**16.** We know 15 + 8 = 23. If we add, subtract, multiply or divide both sides by the same number, do the two sides stay equal? Check with 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes**, in every case.
> - Add 10: 15 + 8 + 10 = 33 and 23 + 10 = 33. Equal. ✓
> - Subtract 10: 13 and 13. ✓
> - Multiply by 10: 230 and 230. ✓
> - Divide by 10: 2.3 and 2.3. ✓
>
> **Why:** LHS and RHS are **the same number** written in two ways. Doing the same thing to the same number must give the same result. (The only thing we may **not** do is divide by 0, which has no meaning.)

**Example 1 (adding and subtracting undo each other).** We know 14593 − 1459 + 145 − 14 + 88 = 13353. What is 14593 − 1459 + 145 − 14?

We don't need to work it out from scratch. The LHS is the wanted expression **plus 88**. To remove "+ 88", **subtract 88 from both sides**:

14593 − 1459 + 145 − 14 + 88 − 88 = 13353 − 88, so 14593 − 1459 + 145 − 14 = **13265**.

**Example 2 (multiplying and dividing undo each other).** We know 23 × 41 × 11 × 8 × 7 = 5,80,888. What is 23 × 41 × 11 × 8?

The LHS is the wanted product **times 7**. **Divide both sides by 7**: 23 × 41 × 11 × 8 = 5,80,888 ÷ 7 = **82,984**.

**17.** Is "divide 5,80,888 by 7" the same as "divide both sides by 7"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Dividing both sides by 7 gives (23 × 41 × 11 × 8 × 7) ÷ 7 = 5,80,888 ÷ 7. On the left, "× 7" followed by "÷ 7" cancel (they are inverse operations), leaving just 23 × 41 × 11 × 8. On the right we are left with 5,80,888 ÷ 7. So "divide both sides by 7" **is** "remove the × 7 from the left and divide the right by 7". Check: 82,984 × 7 = 5,80,888. ✓

**Example 3 (a negative term).** We know 12345 − 5432 + 135 − 24 − (−67) = 7091. What is 12345 − 5432 + 135 − 24?

We need to remove the term "− (−67)". Subtracting −67 is the same as adding 67, so the term is really **+ 67**; remove it by **adding (−67) to both sides**:

12345 − 5432 + 135 − 24 − (−67) + (−67) = 7091 + (−67), so 12345 − 5432 + 135 − 24 = **7024**.

Check by working it out directly: 12345 − 5432 = 6913; 6913 + 135 = 7048; 7048 − 24 = 7024. ✓

**Example 4 (a fraction factor).** We know (35/113) × 24 × 14 × (8/9) = 94080/1017. Find (35/113) × 24 × 14.

The LHS has an extra factor 8/9. Remove it by **dividing both sides by 8/9**, which is the same as **multiplying both sides by 9/8**:

(35/113) × 24 × 14 = (94080/1017) × (9/8) = 846720/8136 = **11760/113** (divide top and bottom by 72; check: 1017 = 113 × 9 and 94080 = 11760 × 8, so (11760 × 8)/(113 × 9) × 9/8 = 11760/113).

### Now solve 5x − 4 = 7 properly

1. We want 5x alone on the LHS, so remove "− 4" by **adding 4 to both sides**: 5x − 4 + 4 = 7 + 4, so **5x = 11**.
2. We want x alone, so remove the factor 5 by **dividing both sides by 5**: 5x ÷ 5 = 11 ÷ 5, so **x = 11/5**.
3. **Check:** LHS = 5 × (11/5) − 4 = 11 − 4 = 7 = RHS. ✓

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To solve an equation, undo what has been done to the unknown, in reverse order, doing the same thing to both sides.**
> - A term is **added** → **subtract** it from both sides. A term is **subtracted** → **add** it to both sides.
> - The unknown is **multiplied** by a number → **divide** both sides by it. The unknown is **divided** by a number → **multiply** both sides by it.
> **Why reverse order:** 5x − 4 was made by "multiply by 5, then subtract 4". To get back to x, undo the last step first (add 4), then the first (divide by 5), like taking off socks and shoes: shoes come off first.
> **Always check** by putting your answer back into the **original** equation.

**Example 5.** Solve 11y + (−5) = 61.

1. Remove the term −5 by **subtracting (−5)** from both sides: 11y + (−5) − (−5) = 61 − (−5), so **11y = 66**.
2. 11 × 6 = 66, so **y = 6**. (Or divide both sides by 11: y = 66 ÷ 11 = 6.)

### Check yourself: checking

**18.** Check that y = 6 really solves 11y + (−5) = 61.

> [!ANSWER]
> LHS = 11 × 6 + (−5) = 66 − 5 = **61** = RHS. ✓ So y = 6 is correct.

### Unknowns on both sides

**Example 6.** Solve 6y + 7 = 4y + 21.

Now there are y's on **both** sides. On a balance, we took sacks off both pans until sacks were on one side only. Do the same:

1. **Subtract 4y from both sides**: 6y + 7 − 4y = 4y + 21 − 4y, so **2y + 7 = 21**.
2. **Subtract 7 from both sides**: 2y = 21 − 7 = **14**.
3. **Divide both sides by 2**: **y = 7**.
4. Check: LHS = 6 × 7 + 7 = 49; RHS = 4 × 7 + 21 = 49. ✓

> [!TIP]
> Subtract the **smaller** number of unknowns (here 4y) from both sides. Then the unknowns stay positive on the other side, and there are fewer minus signs to trip over.

**20.** Make up an equation that has **no** solution.

> [!ANSWER]
> **x + 4 = x + 5.** "4 more than a number" can never equal "5 more than the same number". If we subtract x from both sides we get 4 = 5, which is false whatever x is. So **no value** of x works.
>
> Other examples: 2y = 2y + 1; 3(z + 1) = 3z + 7 (it becomes 3z + 3 = 3z + 7, so 3 = 7, impossible).

### A shortcut: moving a term to the other side

Look at what "doing the same to both sides" does each time:

| Doing the same to both sides | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 11y + (−5) − (−5) = 61 − (−5) | 11y = 61 − (−5) |
| 11y ÷ 11 = 66 ÷ 11 | y = 66 ÷ 11 |
| 2y + 7 − 7 = 21 − 7 | 2y = 21 − 7 |
| u ÷ 15 × 15 = 6 × 15 | u = 6 × 15 |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The shortcut ("transposing"):**
> 1. A term **added** on one side can be removed from that side if its **opposite** (additive inverse) appears on the other side: 2y + 7 = 21 becomes **2y = 21 − 7**.
> 2. A **factor** multiplying one side can be removed if the other side is **divided** by it: 2y = 14 becomes **y = 14 ÷ 2**.
> 3. A **divisor** on one side can be removed if the other side is **multiplied** by it: u ÷ 15 = 6 becomes **u = 6 × 15**.
> **Why it is allowed:** each shortcut is just "do the same to both sides" with the cancelling step skipped. It is not a new rule; if you are ever unsure, write out the full step.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Change the side, change the sign" is only for terms that are added or subtracted.** A factor does not become negative when it moves: 2y = 14 gives y = 14 ÷ 2, **not** y = 14 − 2 or y = 14 ÷ (−2).

## 15.4 Solving problems with equations

**Example 7: a tile pattern.** Ranjana makes T-shapes from square tiles. Can she make one with exactly **100** tiles? If so, which step?

![T-shapes of tiles: steps 1, 2, 3 use 4, 7, 10 tiles](assets/img/class7/math/15/t_tiles.svg)

**See the pattern.** Step k has a top row of **2k + 1** tiles and a stem of **k** tiles below it: k + (2k + 1) = **3k + 1** tiles. (Another way: the centre tile, plus three arms of k tiles each: k + k + k + 1 = 3k + 1.) Check: step 1: 4 ✓, step 2: 7 ✓, step 3: 10 ✓.

**Equation:** 3k + 1 = 100.

**Example 8: a party.** Each plate of snacks costs ₹25, and the shop charges ₹50 extra to deliver. Madhubanti's family has 5 people, including her, and she may spend ₹500. Everyone, family and friends, gets one plate. How many friends can she invite?

![Bar model: 18 plates of ₹25 and ₹50 delivery make ₹500](assets/img/class7/math/15/party.svg)

There are several ways, and they all agree:

- **Thinking it through:** take off the fixed ₹50 delivery: ₹450 is left for plates. 450 ÷ 25 = 18 plates. 18 − 5 family = **13 friends**.
- **Equation with p = number of people at the party:** cost = 25p + 50, so **25p + 50 = 500**. Subtract 50: 25p = 450. Divide by 25: p = 18. Friends = 18 − 5 = **13**.
- **Equation with f = number of friends:** people = f + 5, cost of plates = 25(f + 5) = 450 (after delivery), so **25(f + 5) = 450**. Divide by 25: f + 5 = 18. Subtract 5: f = **13**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Many ways, one answer.** When different methods give the same answer, that is a good sign the answer is right. Try more than one way when you can.

**Example 9: savings.** Jahnavi starts with ₹4000 and saves ₹650 a month. Sunita starts with ₹5050 and saves ₹500 a month. After how many months will they have the same amount?

1. Let **m** = the number of months. Jahnavi then has 4000 + 650m; Sunita has 5050 + 500m.
2. They are equal: **4000 + 650m = 5050 + 500m**.
3. Subtract 500m from both sides: 4000 + 150m = 5050.
4. Subtract 4000: 150m = 1050.
5. Divide by 150: **m = 7 months**.

**Example 10: a bracket.** Solve 28(x + 4) + 300 = 1000. Three good routes:

- **Peel off the outside first:** subtract 300: 28(x + 4) = 700. Divide by 28: x + 4 = 25. Subtract 4: **x = 21**.
- **Make the numbers smaller:** 28, 300 and 1000 are all divisible by 4. Divide both sides by 4: 7(x + 4) + 75 = 250. Subtract 75: 7(x + 4) = 175, so 7x + 28 = 175; subtract 28: 7x = 147; **x = 21**.
- **Open the bracket first:** 28x + 112 + 300 = 1000, so 28x + 412 = 1000; subtract 412: 28x = 588; divide by 28: **x = 21**.

Check: 28 × (21 + 4) + 300 = 28 × 25 + 300 = 700 + 300 = 1000. ✓

**Example 11: a number trick.** Riyaz asks Akash to think of a number, subtract 3, multiply by 4, add 8, and say the result. Akash says **24**. What was his number?

Let the number be x and follow the steps:

| Step | Expression |
|---|---|
| Think of a number | x |
| Subtract 3 | x − 3 |
| Multiply by 4 | 4(x − 3) = 4x − 12 |
| Add 8 | 4x − 12 + 8 = **4x − 4** |

So **4x − 4 = 24**. Add 4: 4x = 28. Divide by 4: **x = 7**. (Check: 7 − 3 = 4, × 4 = 16, + 8 = 24 ✓.)

**Example 12: two unknowns.** Ramesh and Suresh have 60 marbles between them, and Ramesh has 30 more than Suresh. How many does each have?

![Bar model: Suresh has y, Ramesh has y and 30 more; together 60](assets/img/class7/math/15/marbles.svg)

With two letters we would get two equations: x + y = 60 and x = y + 30. We only know how to solve **one** equation with **one** unknown, so describe **both** amounts using **one** letter: Suresh has **y**, so Ramesh has **y + 30**. Together: **y + (y + 30) = 60**, that is **2y + 30 = 60**.

**Try this.** Play "number machine" with a friend. One of you secretly decides a rule with two steps (say "× 3, then − 2"). The other says inputs; you say the outputs. Can your friend find the rule? Then give an **output** and ask for the input: that is solving an equation.

> [!TIP]

## 15.5 Building equations from a solution

> [!NOTE]
> **Why build equations backwards?** It shows that solving is just "walking back" along a chain of same-on-both-sides steps, and it is how teachers (and you) can make puzzles with nice answers.

So far we were given an equation and found the solution. Now go **backwards**: start with the solution and build equations.

Start from **y = 5** and do the same thing to both sides, again and again:

- y = 5 → add 1 to both sides → **y + 1 = 6**.
- y = 5 → multiply both sides by 3 → **3y = 15**.

**Why every equation in such a chain has the same solution:** y = 5 makes the first one true, and doing the same thing to both sides of a true statement keeps it true. So y = 5 solves every equation in the chain.

Two chains, each step doing the same operation to both sides:

| Chain 1 | Operation | Chain 2 | Operation |
|---|---|---|---|
| y + 1 = 6 | multiply by −1 | 3y = 15 | add 6 |
| −y − 1 = −6 | add y | 3y + 6 = 21 | divide by 3 |
| −1 = −6 + y | add 6 | y + 2 = 7 | subtract 2 |
| 5 = y | | y = 5 | |

> [!ANSWER]

> - y + 1 = 6 (add 1); 3y = 15 (multiply by 3); y − 8 = −3 (subtract 8); y ÷ 5 = 1 (divide by 5); 2y + 7 = 17 (multiply by 2, then add 7); 4y = 3y + 5 (add 3y to both sides).
>

**26.** Can you go up each chain, from the bottom equation to the top? Compare the operations going down and going up.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Going **up**, use the **inverse** of each operation, in **reverse order**:
> - Chain 1 upwards: 5 = y → **subtract 6** → −1 = −6 + y → **subtract y** → −y − 1 = −6 → **multiply by −1** → y + 1 = 6.
> - Chain 2 upwards: y = 5 → **add 2** → y + 2 = 7 → **multiply by 3** → 3y + 6 = 21 → **subtract 6** → 3y = 15.
>
> Adding and subtracting undo each other; multiplying and dividing undo each other; multiplying by −1 undoes itself. So the operations going up are the "undo" of the ones going down. Solving an equation is exactly this: walking **down** a chain to y = 5.

**27.** Without calculating, what is the value of the unknown in each equation of the two chains?

> [!ANSWER]

## 15.6 Mind the mistake, mend the mistake

Each equation below was solved with a mistake (or maybe not). Find the wrong step, say what went wrong, and solve it correctly.

### Check yourself: mind the mistake

**34.** 4x + 6 = 10 → 4x = 10 + 6 → 4x = 16 → x = 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake in step 1.** To remove "+ 6" from the left you must **subtract** 6 from both sides, so it should be 4x = 10 − 6.
> **Correct:** 4x = 4, so **x = 1**. Check: 4 × 1 + 6 = 10 ✓.

**35.** 7 − 8z = 5 → 8z = 7 − 5 → 8z = 2 → z = 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> Getting to 8z = 2 is actually fine: add 8z to both sides (7 = 5 + 8z), then subtract 5 (2 = 8z).
> **Mistake in the last step.** 8z = 2 means "8 times z is 2", so z = 2 ÷ 8 = **1/4**; the solver worked out 8 ÷ 2 instead (the wrong way round).
> Check: 7 − 8 × 1/4 = 7 − 2 = 5 ✓.

**36.** 2v − 4 = 6 → v − 4 = 6 − 2 → v − 4 = 4 → v = 8.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake in step 1.** The 2 **multiplies** v; it is not added. You cannot remove it by subtracting 2 from the other side, and dividing would have to divide **every** term.
> **Correct:** add 4: 2v = 10; divide by 2: **v = 5**. Check: 2 × 5 − 4 = 6 ✓. (With v = 8: 2 × 8 − 4 = 12 ≠ 6.)

**37.** 5z + 2 = 3z − 4 → 5z + 3z = −4 + 2 → 8z = −2 → z = −2/8.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake in step 1**, two sign errors. Moving 3z from the right to the left makes it **−3z**, and moving +2 from the left to the right makes it **−2**.
> **Correct:** 5z − 3z = −4 − 2, so 2z = −6 and **z = −3**. Check: 5(−3) + 2 = −13 and 3(−3) − 4 = −13 ✓.

**38.** 15w − 4w = 26 → 15w = 26 + 4w → 15w = 30 → w = 2.

> [!ANSWER]
> Step 1 is correct but unhelpful. **Mistake in step 2:** 26 + 4w is **not** 30; you cannot add a number and a term with w.
> **Correct:** 15w − 4w = 11w, so 11w = 26 and **w = 26/11**. Check: 15 × 26/11 − 4 × 26/11 = 11 × 26/11 = 26 ✓.

**39.** 3x + 1 = −12 → x + 1 = −12/3 → x + 1 = −4 → x = −5.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake in step 1.** Dividing both sides by 3 must divide **every** term on the left: (3x + 1) ÷ 3 = x + 1/3, not x + 1.
> **Correct:** subtract 1 first: 3x = −13; divide by 3: **x = −13/3**. Check: 3 × (−13/3) + 1 = −13 + 1 = −12 ✓.

**40.** 4(4q + 2) = 50 → 4(4q) = 50 − 2 → 16q = 48 → q = 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake in step 1.** The 2 is **inside** the bracket, so it is really 4 × 2 = 8 on the left. You cannot move it out without the 4.
> **Correct:** open the bracket: 16q + 8 = 50. Subtract 8: 16q = 42. Divide: **q = 42/16 = 21/8**. (Or divide both sides by 4 first: 4q + 2 = 12.5, 4q = 10.5, q = 2.625 = 21/8.) Check: 4 × (4 × 21/8 + 2) = 4 × (10.5 + 2) = 4 × 12.5 = 50 ✓.

**41.** −2(3 − 4x) = 14 → −6v − 8x = 14 → −8x = 14 + 6 → −8x = 20 → x = −20/8.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mistake in step 1** (and a stray letter v). −2 × 3 = −6 and **−2 × (−4x) = +8x** (negative × negative is positive).
> **Correct:** −6 + 8x = 14. Add 6: 8x = 20. Divide by 8: **x = 20/8 = 5/2**. Check: −2 × (3 − 4 × 5/2) = −2 × (3 − 10) = −2 × (−7) = 14 ✓.

**42.** 3(7y + 4) = 9 + 5y → 7y + 4 = 9/3 + 5y → 7y + 4 = 3 + 5y → 7y − 5y + 4 = 3 → 2y = 4 − 3 → y = 1/2.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two mistakes.** (1) Dividing both sides by 3 must divide **all** of the right side: (9 + 5y) ÷ 3 = 3 + 5y/3, not 3 + 5y. (2) From 2y + 4 = 3, subtracting 4 gives 2y = 3 − 4, not 4 − 3.
> **Correct:** open the bracket: 21y + 12 = 9 + 5y. Subtract 5y: 16y + 12 = 9. Subtract 12: 16y = −3. Divide: **y = −3/16**.
> Check: LHS = 3 × (7 × (−3/16) + 4) = 3 × (−21/16 + 64/16) = 3 × 43/16 = 129/16. RHS = 9 + 5 × (−3/16) = 144/16 − 15/16 = 129/16 ✓.

> [!TIP]
> **The commonest slips:** moving a term without changing its sign; dividing only **part** of a side; forgetting that the number in front of a bracket multiplies **every** term inside; and negative × negative. The cure is always the same: **check the answer in the original equation.**

## 15.7 A pinch of history

In India, solving problems with unknown numbers was called **bījagaṇita**. *Bīja* means **seed**: the answer is hidden inside the unknown number, the way a tree is hidden inside a seed, and solving the problem lets it grow, step by step.

- **Brahmagupta** (628 CE) explained, in Chapter 18 of his *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, how to add, subtract and multiply unknown quantities, one of the earliest known works on algebra. The mathematician David Mumford called him "the key person in the creation of algebra as we know it".
- In the 8th century Indian works were translated into Arabic. Around 825 CE **al-Khwarizmi**, in Baghdad, wrote *Hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala* ("calculation by restoring and balancing"). From *al-jabr* comes our word **algebra**.
- Indian mathematicians wrote unknowns as syllables: **yā** (short for *yāvat-tāvat*, "as much as"), **kā** (*kālaka*, black), **nī** (*nīlaka*, blue)… and known numbers with **rū** (*rūpa*, form). 2x + 1 was "yā 2 rū 1"; a **dot over a number** meant it was negative; and the two sides of an equation were written **one under the other**.

**Worked example (an old problem).** One man has ₹300 and 6 horses. Another has 10 horses and a debt of ₹100. They are equally rich, and all horses cost the same. What is the price of a horse?

1. Let a horse cost ₹x. The first man is worth 300 + 6x; the second is worth 10x − 100 (the debt takes ₹100 away).
2. **300 + 6x = 10x − 100.**
3. Add 100 to both sides: 400 + 6x = 10x.
4. Subtract 6x: 400 = 4x.
5. Divide by 4: **x = 100**. A horse costs **₹100**.

Check: 300 + 600 = 900 and 1000 − 100 = 900 ✓.

### Brahmagupta's formula

Equations like 5x + 4 = 3x + 8 all have the same shape: **Ax + B = Cx + D** (A, B, C, D are numbers). Solve the general one exactly as before:

1. Subtract Cx from both sides: (A − C)x + B = D.
2. Subtract B from both sides: (A − C)x = D − B.
3. Divide by (A − C): **x = (D − B) ÷ (A − C)**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Brahmagupta's rule:** the solution of Ax + B = Cx + D is **x = (D − B) ÷ (A − C)**, as long as A ≠ C.
> **Why:** it is just the three usual steps done once with letters, so it works for every equation of this shape.
> For the savings problem, 650m + 4000 = 500m + 5050: m = (5050 − 4000) ÷ (650 − 500) = 1050 ÷ 150 = 7. ✓

### Check yourself

**43.** Use the formula to solve 5x + 4 = 3x + 8 and 3x − 6 = 2x + 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> - 5x + 4 = 3x + 8: A = 5, B = 4, C = 3, D = 8. x = (8 − 4) ÷ (5 − 3) = 4 ÷ 2 = **2**. Check: 14 = 14 ✓.
> - 3x − 6 = 2x + 4: A = 3, B = −6, C = 2, D = 4. x = (4 − (−6)) ÷ (3 − 2) = 10 ÷ 1 = **10**. Check: 24 = 24 ✓.
>
> Careful with the sign of B: here B is **−6**, so D − B = 4 + 6.

**44.** Use the formula to solve 2x + 3 = 4x + 5.

> [!ANSWER]
> A = 2, B = 3, C = 4, D = 5. x = (5 − 3) ÷ (2 − 4) = 2 ÷ (−2) = **−1**.
> Check: 2(−1) + 3 = 1 and 4(−1) + 5 = 1 ✓.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No mistake.** Halving both sides divides **every** term by 2 (14y → 7y, 24 → 12, 36 → 18) correctly. Then subtract 12: 7y = 6, so **y = 6/7**. Check: 14 × 6/7 + 24 = 12 + 24 = 36 ✓.

**(c)** 4x − 5 = 9x + 8 → 4x = 9x + 8 − 5 → 4x = 9x + 3 → 4x − 9x = 3 → −5x = 3 → x = −5/3.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two mistakes.** (1) To remove "− 5" from the left you **add** 5: 4x = 9x + 8 + 5 = 9x + 13. (2) From −5x = (something), divide by −5; x = 3/(−5), not −5/3.
> **Correct:** 4x = 9x + 13 → −5x = 13 → **x = −13/5**. Check: 4(−13/5) − 5 = −52/5 − 25/5 = −77/5 and 9(−13/5) + 8 = −117/5 + 40/5 = −77/5 ✓.

**Question 12.** Find the angles of these triangles.

**(a)** An isosceles triangle with top angle y and two equal base angles, each y + 15.

![Isosceles triangle with top angle y and base angles y + 15](assets/img/class7/math/15/triangle_y.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> The angles of a triangle add up to 180°.
> 1. y + (y + 15) + (y + 15) = 180, so 3y + 30 = 180.
> 2. Subtract 30: 3y = 150. Divide by 3: **y = 50**.
>
> **Angles: 50°, 65°, 65°.** Check: 50 + 65 + 65 = 180 ✓.

**(b)** A triangle with angles x, x − 10 and x + 10.

![Triangle with angles x, x − 10 and x + 10](assets/img/class7/math/15/triangle_x.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. x + (x − 10) + (x + 10) = 180. The −10 and +10 cancel: 3x = 180.
> 2. **x = 60**.
>
> **Angles: 60°, 50°, 70°.** Check: 60 + 50 + 70 = 180 ✓.

**13.** Write 4 equations whose solution is u = 6.

> [!ANSWER]
> Start from u = 6 and do the same to both sides:
> 1. Add 4: **u + 4 = 10**.
> 2. Multiply by 5: **5u = 30**.
> 3. Multiply by 2, subtract 7: **2u − 7 = 5**.
> 4. Divide by 3, add 1: **u ÷ 3 + 1 = 3**.
>
> Check (3): 12 − 7 = 5 ✓.

**14.** An old problem: the first person is given an unknown amount. The second gets twice as much as the first, the third three times as much as the second, and the fourth four times as much as the third. In all, 132 is given out. How much did the first person get?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. First: **x**. Second: **2x**. Third: 3 × 2x = **6x**. Fourth: 4 × 6x = **24x**.
> 2. x + 2x + 6x + 24x = 132, so **33x = 132**.
> 3. Divide by 33: **x = 4**.
>
> The amounts are 4, 8, 24 and 96. Check: 4 + 8 + 24 + 96 = 132 ✓.

**15.** A giraffe's height is two and a half metres more than half its height. How tall is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Let the height be h metres: h = h ÷ 2 + 2.5.
> 2. Subtract h ÷ 2 from both sides: h ÷ 2 = 2.5.
> 3. Multiply by 2: **h = 5 m**.
>
> Check: half of 5 is 2.5, and 2.5 + 2.5 = 5 ✓. (Same idea as the brick: the "other half" is the 2.5 m.)

**Question 16.** Two stick patterns:

![Pattern A: houses made of 1, 2, 3 squares in a row with a triangle roof at the end](assets/img/class7/math/15/stick_houses.svg)

![Pattern B: staircases of squares; positions 1, 2, 3 have 4, 7, 10 squares](assets/img/class7/math/15/stick_stairs.svg)

**First, the rules.**
- **Pattern A:** position n has **n squares** in a row with a pointed end. A row of n squares needs 3n + 1 sticks (the first square 4, each next one 3 more, because it shares a side), and the point adds 2: **3n + 3 sticks**. (Check: 6, 9, 12.)
- **Pattern B:** position n has a staircase of **3n + 1 squares** (4, 7, 10). Counting the sticks in the drawings gives 13, 22, 31: each position adds **9** sticks (3 new squares that share 3 sides with the old ones: 3 × 4 − 3 = 9). So position n has **9n + 4 sticks**.

**(a)** How many squares are there in position 11?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **A:** n squares, so **11 squares**.
> - **B:** 3n + 1 = 3 × 11 + 1 = **34 squares**.

**(b)** How many sticks are needed for position 11?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **A:** 3 × 11 + 3 = **36 sticks**.
> - **B:** 9 × 11 + 4 = **103 sticks**.

**(c)** Can a position use exactly 85 sticks? Which one?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **A:** 3n + 3 = 85 → 3n = 82 → n = 27.33… **Not a whole number, so no.** (Also: 3n + 3 is always a multiple of 3, and 85 is not.)
> - **B:** 9n + 4 = 85 → 9n = 81 → **n = 9. Yes, position 9.** Check: 9 × 9 + 4 = 85 ✓.

**(d)** Can a position use exactly 150 sticks? Which one?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **A:** 3n + 3 = 150 → 3n = 147 → **n = 49. Yes, position 49.** Check: 147 + 3 = 150 ✓.
> - **B:** 9n + 4 = 150 → 9n = 146 → n = 16.2… **No.** (9n + 4 always leaves remainder 4 when divided by 9; 150 leaves remainder 6.)

**17.** A number increased by 36 equals ten times the number. Find it.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. x + 36 = 10x.
> 2. Subtract x: 36 = 9x.
> 3. Divide by 9: **x = 4**.
>
> Check: 4 + 36 = 40 = 10 × 4 ✓.

**Question 18.** Solve these equations.

**(a)** 5(r + 2) = 10

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide by 5: r + 2 = 2. Subtract 2: **r = 0**. Check: 5 × 2 = 10 ✓.

**(b)** −3(u + 2) = 2(u − 1)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Open brackets: −3u − 6 = 2u − 2.
> 2. Add 3u to both sides: −6 = 5u − 2.
> 3. Add 2: −4 = 5u. Divide by 5: **u = −4/5**.
>
> Check: −3(−4/5 + 2) = −3 × 6/5 = −18/5; 2(−4/5 − 1) = 2 × (−9/5) = −18/5 ✓.

**(c)** 2(7 − 2n) = −6

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Divide by 2: 7 − 2n = −3.
> 2. Add 2n: 7 = −3 + 2n. Add 3: 10 = 2n.
> 3. **n = 5**.
>
> Check: 2(7 − 10) = 2 × (−3) = −6 ✓.

**(d)** 2(x − 4) = −16

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide by 2: x − 4 = −8. Add 4: **x = −4**. Check: 2(−8) = −16 ✓.

**(e)** 6(x − 1) = 2(x − 1) − 4

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Open brackets: 6x − 6 = 2x − 2 − 4 = 2x − 6.
> 2. Subtract 2x: 4x − 6 = −6.
> 3. Add 6: 4x = 0, so **x = 0**.
>
> Check: 6(−1) = −6 and 2(−1) − 4 = −6 ✓.

**(f)** 3 − 7s = 7 − 3s

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Add 7s to both sides: 3 = 7 + 4s.
> 2. Subtract 7: −4 = 4s.
> 3. **s = −1**.
>
> Check: 3 + 7 = 10 and 7 + 3 = 10 ✓.

**(g)** 2x + 1 = 6 − (2x − 3)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Remove the bracket: the minus sign changes **both** signs inside: 6 − 2x + 3 = 9 − 2x. So 2x + 1 = 9 − 2x.
> 2. Add 2x: 4x + 1 = 9. Subtract 1: 4x = 8. **x = 2**.
>
> Check: 5 and 6 − (4 − 3) = 5 ✓.

**(h)** 10 − 5x = 3(x − 4) − 2(x − 7)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Right side: 3x − 12 − 2x + 14 = **x + 2** (−2 × −7 = +14).
> 2. So 10 − 5x = x + 2. Add 5x: 10 = 6x + 2. Subtract 2: 8 = 6x.
> 3. **x = 8/6 = 4/3**.
>
> Check: 10 − 20/3 = 10/3; 3(4/3 − 4) − 2(4/3 − 7) = 3(−8/3) − 2(−17/3) = −8 + 34/3 = 10/3 ✓.

**19.** A maze: start in the green box. Solve its equation; then go through the door (circle) whose number is your answer into the next box. Keep going until you reach END.

![Maze of 12 boxes with an equation in each and numbered doors between boxes](assets/img/class7/math/15/maze.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> | Box | Solve | Answer | Go through door |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 8x = 20 + 3x | 5x = 20 | x = 4 | **4** (down) |
> | 2x − 9 = −3 | 2x = 6 | x = 3 | **3** (right) |
> | −2x = −42 | divide by −2 | x = 21 | **21** (up-right) |
> | 15 = 19 − 4x | 4x = 4 | x = 1 | **1** (down) |
> | 2x + 3 = x + 5 | subtract x and 3 | x = 2 | **2** (down) |
> | 2x + 5 = 3(x − 1) | 2x + 5 = 3x − 3, so 8 = x | x = 8 | **8** (left) |
> | 2(x + 1) − 10 = 18 | 2(x + 1) = 28, x + 1 = 14 | x = 13 | **13** (left) |
> | 8m + 8 = −72 | 8m = −80 | m = −10 | **−10** (down) |
> | −4 = 16 − 5k | 5k = 20 | k = 4 | **+4** (right) |
> | 2x − 9 = 3 − x | 3x = 12 | x = 4 | **4** (right) |
> | 30 = 4 − 50n | 50n = −26 | n = −13/25 | **END** |
>

**20.** Some children and donkeys are on a beach. Together they have 28 heads and 80 feet. How many donkeys and how many children?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Let d = donkeys. Every child and donkey has one head, so children = 28 − d.
> 2. Feet: donkeys have 4, children 2: 4d + 2(28 − d) = 80.
> 3. 4d + 56 − 2d = 80, so 2d + 56 = 80, 2d = 24, **d = 12**.
>
> **12 donkeys and 16 children.** Check: 12 + 16 = 28 heads ✓; 48 + 32 = 80 feet ✓.
> (Another way to see it: if all 28 were children there would be 56 feet; each donkey adds 2 more feet; 80 − 56 = 24 extra feet means 12 donkeys.)

**21.** A magic trick: think of any number, multiply by 2, add 10, divide by 2, subtract the number you first thought of, and add 3. "You now have 8!" Why does this always work? Make up a trick of your own.

> [!ANSWER]
> Call the number x and follow it:
>
> | Step | Expression |
> |---|---|
> | Think of a number | x |
> | Multiply by 2 | 2x |
> | Add 10 | 2x + 10 |
> | Divide by 2 | x + 5 |
> | Subtract the first number | x + 5 − x = **5** |
> | Add 3 | **8** |
>
> The x **disappears** when you subtract the number you started with, so the answer is 8 **whatever** x was.
>
> **Your own trick:** do anything to x, then later remove the x. For example: think of a number, add 7, multiply by 3, subtract 6, divide by 3, subtract your number → always **5** (because 3(x + 7) − 6 = 3x + 15, ÷ 3 = x + 5, − x = 5).

## In a nutshell

- An **equation** says two expressions are equal: **LHS = RHS**. Solving means finding the value of the unknown that makes it true.
- **Doing the same thing to both sides** (add, subtract, multiply, divide by a non-zero number) keeps an equation true, just as a balance stays level.
- **Undo in reverse order:** remove added/subtracted terms first, then the multiplier or divisor.
- **Unknowns on both sides:** subtract the smaller number of unknowns from both sides first.
- **Shortcut:** a term moving across the "=" becomes its opposite (+ ↔ −); a factor moving across divides; a divisor moving across multiplies.
- **Brackets:** the number outside multiplies **every** term inside; a minus outside changes every sign inside.
- **Word problems:** say what the letter stands for, write every quantity using **one** letter, form the equation, solve, and answer in words.
- Brahmagupta: Ax + B = Cx + D gives **x = (D − B) ÷ (A − C)**.
- **Always check** the answer in the **original** equation.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** forgetting to change the sign when moving a term across · dividing only part of a side (6x + 9 = 66 does **not** give x + 9 = 11) · not multiplying every term in a bracket · negative × negative · moving a multiplier by subtracting instead of dividing · writing an equation without saying what the letter means · not checking the answer.
