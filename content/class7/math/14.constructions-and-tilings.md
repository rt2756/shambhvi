## Only a ruler and a compass

In this chapter we construct with just two tools:
- an **unmarked ruler** — draws straight lines, but **doesn't measure**;
- a **compass** — draws circles/arcs and **copies a length** from one place to another.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every construction comes with a reason (usually congruent triangles) that shows it is EXACT** — not just "looks right". A careful construction is often more accurate than measuring with a scale.

## The perpendicular bisector

**Drawing a symmetric "eye":** the upper and lower arcs must have the same radius, with centres A (above) and B (below) the line XY. We need **AX = AY = BX = BY**.
**How to find A and B:** with the same radius, draw arcs from X and from Y above the line; they meet at A. Do the same below; they meet at B.

Join AB. It cuts XY exactly at its **midpoint O**, at **90°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Bisect = cut into two identical halves.** The **perpendicular bisector** of XY is the line through its midpoint at right angles to it.

**Why AB is always the perpendicular bisector (proof by congruence):**
1. ∆ABX ≅ ∆ABY by **SSS** (AX = AY, BX = BY, AB common). So ∠XAO = ∠YAO.
2. Now ∆AOX ≅ ∆AOY by **SAS** (AX = AY, the angles at A just found, AO common).
3. So **OX = OY** (O is the midpoint) and ∠AOX = ∠AOY. These two angles make a straight line (180°), so each is **90°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Any point at equal distances from X and Y lies on the perpendicular bisector of XY.**
> **Why:** take two such points (one above, one below) — the proof above shows the line through them is the perpendicular bisector, and XY has only one perpendicular bisector. So eyes of different shapes just use different pairs of centres along the same line.

**Construction steps:**
1. With any radius more than half of XY, draw arcs from X and Y **above** XY → they meet at A.
2. Same radius, arcs **below** → they meet at B.
3. Join AB.

**Book Figure it Out:**
- **Must the radius above equal the radius below?** **No.** A is equidistant from X and Y, and so is B, whatever the two radii are — so both lie on the perpendicular bisector, and two points fix a line.
- **Must the arcs be on opposite sides?** **No** — two equidistant points on the *same* side also fix the line (but if they're close together, the line drawn is less accurate).
- **Must a pair of arcs (one from X, one from Y) have equal radii?** **Yes** — otherwise the meeting point is NOT equally far from X and Y, so it isn't on the bisector.

### A 90° angle at a point O on a line

Use the compass to mark X and Y on the line at **equal distances from O** — now O is the midpoint of XY. The perpendicular bisector of XY passes through O. You only need **one** extra point A (arcs from X and Y with equal radius); join OA → **90°** at O.

### The rope method from the Śulba-sūtras

The **Śulba-sūtras** — Vedic geometry texts for building fire altars — construct perpendiculars with a **rope** (it draws arcs and stretches into straight lines). Kātyāyana-Śulbasūtra 1.2: fix pegs at X and Y, tie the rope's ends to them, mark the rope's midpoint, pull it taut **above** XY (point A) and **below** (point B). **AB is the perpendicular bisector.**
**Why:** A splits the rope into two equal halves, so AX = AY; likewise BX = BY. Equidistant points lie on the perpendicular bisector.

## Bisecting an angle

An 8-petalled design needs 8 lines through a point with equal angles between them: 360° ÷ 8 = **45°** each. We can make 90°; halving it gives 45°.

**Steps to bisect ∠XOY:**
1. With centre O, draw an arc cutting the arms at **A** and **B** (so OA = OB).
2. With the same radius, draw arcs from A and from B; they meet at **C**.
3. Join OC — it **bisects** the angle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why it works:** ∆OAC ≅ ∆OBC by **SSS** (OA = OB, AC = BC, OC common). So ∠AOC = ∠BOC.

**Book Figure it Out:**
- **Arcs drawn on the far side** (so C lands on the other side of O)? OC still bisects the angle — the triangles are still congruent by SSS; the line just extends backwards through O.
- **Which angles can we get by bisecting?** From 90°: 45°, 22.5°, … From 60°: 30°, 15°, 7.5°, … and sums like 60° + 15° = 75°, 90° + 45° = 135°. A strange angle like **65.5°** can't be made this way.
- **With a rope:** mark equal lengths OA = OB along both arms, then find a point equally far from A and B using a rope folded in half (as in the Śulba method) — joining it to O bisects the angle.

## Copying an angle

To make exact copies of a shape we need equal angles without a protractor.

**Steps to copy ∠A onto a ray from X:**
1. With centre A, draw an arc cutting the arms at **B** and **C**.
2. Same radius, centre X: draw an arc cutting the ray at **Z**.
3. Set the compass to the length **BC**; from Z, cut the second arc at **Y**.
4. Join XY. Then **∠YXZ = ∠BAC**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why:** ∆ABC ≅ ∆XYZ by **SSS** (AB = XY, AC = XZ are the same radius; BC = YZ copied). Corresponding angles of congruent triangles are equal.

### A parallel line through a point

Given line m and a point B not on it: draw any line l through B cutting m at A. **Copy the angle** that l makes with m to the point B, in the **same (corresponding) position**. The new line n is parallel to m.
**Why:** equal corresponding angles mean parallel lines (Chapter 5).

## Arches

- **Trefoil arch** (Diwan-i-Aam, Red Fort): support lines AB and DC with **AB = DC** and **∠BAD = ∠CDA** — copy the angle at A to D and copy the length with a compass — then draw arcs between the supports.
- **Pointed arch:** two equal support segments; arcs centred at their midpoints meet in a point. Changing the radius changes the arch.

## The regular hexagon and the 60° angle

A **regular polygon** has all sides and all angles equal (equilateral triangle, square, …). A regular pentagon needs ideas from later classes, but a regular hexagon we can do!

**Six equilateral triangles fit around a point:** each has 60° at the centre, and 6 × 60° = **360°**, a full turn — no gap, no overlap. The outer edges form a hexagon with equal sides, and each corner is 60° + 60° = **120°**. So it's a **regular hexagon**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Angles fit around a point when they add up to 360°.** Book check: 40° + 60° + 50° + 30° + 40° + 90° = 310°, so the gap is 360 − 310 = **50°** — a 70° angle won't fit.
> In the hexagon, AOD is a straight line because ∠AOB + ∠BOC + ∠COD = 3 × 60° = 180°.

### Constructing 60° (and hence 120°, 30°, 15°)

1. On ray AX, draw an arc with centre A cutting AX at **B**.
2. Same radius, centre B: cut the first arc at **C**.
3. **∠CAX = 60°.**

**Why:** AB = AC (radius from A) and BC = AB (same radius from B) — ∆ABC is **equilateral**, so every angle is 60°.

- **120°** = 180° − 60° (the angle next to 60° on a straight line), or 60° + 60°.
- **30°** = bisect 60°; **15°** = bisect 30°; **45°** = bisect 90°.
- **Regular hexagon of side 5 cm:** draw a circle of radius 5 cm and step the compass (still 5 cm) round it — it fits exactly 6 times. Join the 6 points. (Each step makes an equilateral triangle with the centre.)

**6-pointed star:** extend the sides of a regular hexagon. Each point is a triangle whose base angles are 180° − 120° = 60°, so its third angle is 60° → all six points are **equilateral triangles**.

**Perpendicular from a point P outside line l:** with centre P draw an arc cutting l at X and Y. P is equally far from X and Y, so it's on their perpendicular bisector — construct that bisector; it passes through P.

## Tiling

**Tiling** means covering a region with shapes **without gaps or overlaps**. (The 7 **tangram** pieces from China can be rearranged to tile many shapes.)

### Dominoes (2 × 1 tiles) on grids

- **4 × 6 grid** → tileable (e.g. fill each column of 4 with 2 vertical tiles).
- **4 × 7** → tileable (28 squares; the 4 rows are even, so every column of 4 takes 2 vertical tiles).
- **5 × 7** → **impossible**: 35 squares, but every tile covers **2** → the total covered is always even.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An m × n grid can be tiled with 2 × 1 tiles exactly when at least one of m, n is even.**
> **Why:** if, say, m (the number of rows) is even, cover every column with vertical tiles. If both are odd, the number of squares m × n is odd (odd × odd = odd), and dominoes always cover an even number.

### The colouring trick

A 5 × 3 grid with one square removed has 14 squares (even) — but it still might not be tileable! **Colour it like a chessboard.** Every domino covers **one black and one white** square (neighbours always have different colours).

A 5 × 3 chessboard has 8 squares of the corner colour (say black) and 7 white. Remove a **white** square → 8 black, 6 white → **impossible** (tiles need equal numbers). Remove a **black** square → 7 and 7 → can be tiled.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **If a region has unequal numbers of black and white squares, dominoes can't tile it.**
> **Why:** each domino covers exactly one of each colour, so any tiled region must have equal numbers.
> Making the problem *more* complicated (adding colours) made it *easier* to solve!

### Tiling the whole plane

| Regular polygon | Each angle | Fits around a point? | Tiles the plane? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equilateral triangle | 60° | 360 ÷ 60 = 6 ✔ | **Yes** |
| Square | 90° | 360 ÷ 90 = 4 ✔ | **Yes** |
| Regular pentagon | 108° | 360 ÷ 108 = 3.33… ✘ | **No** |
| Regular hexagon | 120° | 360 ÷ 120 = 3 ✔ | **Yes** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A regular polygon tiles the plane only if its angle divides 360° exactly** — copies meeting at a corner must fill the full turn with no gap or overlap. Only the triangle, square and hexagon do.

> [!NOTE]
> **Tiling is still active research.** The artist M.C. Escher (1898–1972) tiled planes with animal shapes; a new single-shape tiling was found as recently as **2023**. In nature, **honeycombs and wasp nests** are tiled with hexagons — no space wasted.

## In a nutshell

- Tools: **unmarked ruler + compass**; every construction is justified by **congruence**.
- **Perpendicular bisector:** equal arcs from both ends meet at points equidistant from them; join those points.
- Any point **equidistant from X and Y** is on XY's perpendicular bisector.
- **90° at O:** make O a midpoint, then bisect.
- **Angle bisector** and **copying an angle** both rely on **SSS**.
- **Parallel line:** copy a corresponding angle.
- **60°:** an equilateral triangle; then 120°, 30°, 15°, 45° follow.
- **Regular hexagon** = 6 equilateral triangles around a point; its side equals its circumradius.
- **Dominoes:** m × n tileable iff one side even; **colouring** proves impossibility.
- Regular polygons tiling the plane: **triangle, square, hexagon**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** each pair of arcs (from X and from Y) must use the **same** radius · an even number of squares doesn't guarantee a tiling — check the colours · 360 ÷ 108 isn't whole, so regular pentagons can't tile · the hexagon's angle is 120°, not 60° · you can't construct just any angle (like 65.5°) by bisecting.
