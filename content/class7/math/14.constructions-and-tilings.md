## What this chapter is about

Long before protractors and marked rulers, people built temples, altars and arches with perfect right angles and perfect symmetry. Their tools were simple: a **straight edge** and something to draw circles with (a compass, or a rope tied to a peg). In this chapter you will:

- **construct** perpendicular lines, bisect angles, copy angles and draw parallel lines with only an **unmarked ruler** and a **compass**;
- prove, with **congruent triangles**, that each construction is **exactly** right, not just "looks right";
- build beautiful designs: petals, arches, hexagons and stars;
- learn when a region can be **tiled** (covered with no gaps or overlaps), and a clever colouring trick that shows when it **cannot**.

> [!NOTE]
> **The two tools.** An **unmarked ruler** draws straight lines but does **not** measure. A **compass** draws circles and arcs, and also **carries a length** from one place to another (open it to a length once, then use that opening elsewhere). We may use a marked scale only when a question asks for a length like "4 cm".

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why constructions can be trusted.** Every point we make with a compass is at a **known, equal distance** from something. Equal distances give **congruent triangles** (SSS, SAS), and congruent triangles give equal angles and equal lengths. That chain of reasons is what makes a construction **exact**.

## 14.1 The perpendicular bisector

### Drawing a symmetric eye

To draw an eye, we draw a lower arc and an upper arc that meet at the corners X and Y. For a **symmetric** eye, the two arcs need **equal radii**, with centre A (above, for the lower lid) and centre B (below, for the upper lid). Since each arc passes through both X and Y, we need

**AX = AY = BX = BY.**

![An eye: lower lid drawn with centre A, upper lid with centre B, both through X and Y](assets/img/class7/math/14/eye.svg)

**Worked example: finding A and B.** Open the compass to some radius (more than half of XY). From X and from Y draw arcs **above** XY: they meet at A. With the same radius, draw arcs **below**: they meet at B. Then AX = AY = BX = BY = the radius.

**Try this.** Draw a line segment XY about 8 cm long. Find A and B as above (radius about 6 cm), then draw the two lids and a small circle for the pupil.

### What line is AB?

Join A and B. It seems to cut XY **exactly in the middle**, at a **right angle**.

![Perpendicular bisector: arcs from X and Y meet at A and B; AB cuts XY at its midpoint O at 90°](assets/img/class7/math/14/perp_bisector.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Cutting something into **two identical halves** is called **bisecting** it. The line through the **midpoint** of XY at **right angles** to XY is its **perpendicular bisector**.

**Why AB is always the perpendicular bisector (whatever the length of XY and whatever radius we use).** Draw AX, AY, BX, BY and call O the point where AB crosses XY.

![Triangles AXB and AYB with AX = AY and BX = BY; O where AB meets XY](assets/img/class7/math/14/perp_proof.svg)

1. **∆ABX ≅ ∆ABY by SSS**: AX = AY, BX = BY, and AB is common. So the matching angles are equal: **∠XAO = ∠YAO**.
2. **∆AOX ≅ ∆AOY by SAS**: AX = AY, ∠XAO = ∠YAO (step 1), and AO is common.
3. So **OX = OY**: O is the midpoint of XY.
4. Also **∠AOX = ∠AOY**. These two angles together make the straight angle at O, 180°, so each is **90°**.

So AB passes through the midpoint at right angles: it **is** the perpendicular bisector.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Any point that is the same distance from X and from Y lies on the perpendicular bisector of XY.**
> **Why:** take any such point C. The proof above works for **any two** such points, one above and one below (it never used the actual radius). So the line through C and B is a perpendicular bisector of XY. But XY has only **one** perpendicular bisector (only one line goes through its midpoint at 90°), so C is on line AB.

This also explains eyes of **different shapes**: their centres C and D are other points equally far from X and Y, so they lie on the same line AB, just farther out (a bigger radius makes a flatter eye).

![A flatter eye with centres C and D farther out along the same line](assets/img/class7/math/14/eyes_cd.svg)

**Worked example: the steps to construct the perpendicular bisector of XY** (ruler and compass only):
1. With a radius more than half of XY, draw arcs from X and from Y **above** XY. Call their meeting point A.
2. With the same radius, draw arcs from X and from Y **below** XY. Call their meeting point B.
3. Join AB. It is the perpendicular bisector.

> [!TIP]
> This is also the **most accurate way to find the midpoint** of a segment, better than measuring with a scale and halving the reading.

### Check yourself

**1.** Use the facts proved above to justify: any point at the same distance from X and Y lies on the perpendicular bisector of XY.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Let C be any point with CX = CY, say above XY. Let B be a point below XY with BX = BY (from the construction).
> 2. The proof (SSS for ∆CBX ≅ ∆CBY, then SAS for the two small triangles) shows that line **CB** cuts XY at its midpoint at 90°, so CB is **a** perpendicular bisector of XY.
> 3. A segment has only **one** midpoint and only **one** line through it at 90°. So line CB is the same line as AB, and C lies on it.
>
> (If C were below XY, use A instead of B. If C is on XY itself, CX = CY means C **is** the midpoint, which is on the bisector too.)

**2.** When constructing the perpendicular bisector, must the arcs **above** XY have the same radius as the arcs **below**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Draw both arcs above with one radius (say 160) and both arcs below with another (say 115).
>
> ![Arcs above with radius 160 and below with radius 115; AB is still the bisector](assets/img/class7/math/14/bisector_unequal_radii.svg)
>
> - A is still the same distance from X and Y (the two arcs above have equal radius), so A is on the perpendicular bisector.
> - B is also the same distance from X and Y, so B is on it too.
> - **Two points fix a line**, so AB is the perpendicular bisector.

**3.** Must the two pairs of arcs be on **opposite sides** of XY, or can both pairs be on the **same side**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They can be on the same side.** Use two different radii, both above XY, giving points A and C.
>
> ![Two meeting points A and C, both above XY; the line through them hits XY at its midpoint](assets/img/class7/math/14/bisector_same_side.svg)
>
> Each of A and C is equally far from X and Y, so both lie on the perpendicular bisector, and the line AC (extended down) **is** the bisector. The radii must be **different**, though, or A and C would be the same point. **Tip:** if A and C are close together, a tiny slip in either point tilts the line a lot, so opposite sides (far apart) is usually more accurate.

**4.** For **one** pair of meeting arcs (one from X and one from Y), must the two radii be equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** If the arc from X has radius 170 and the arc from Y has radius 110, their meeting point A has AX = 170 and AY = 110. A is **not** equally far from X and Y, so it is **not** on the perpendicular bisector; it lies off to the side.
>
> ![Arcs of radius 170 from X and 110 from Y meet at a point A that is off the bisector](assets/img/class7/math/14/bisector_wrong.svg)

**5.** Recreate a design of four pointed petals meeting at a centre, using only a ruler and compass.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each petal is an **eye** shape drawn on a segment.
> 1. Draw two perpendicular lines through a point O (use the perpendicular bisector: draw a segment, bisect it, and use the midpoint as O).
> 2. With the compass, mark four points at the **same distance** from O along the four arms: the petal tips.
> 3. On each segment from O to a tip, draw an eye: find two centres equally far from both ends (arcs from O and from the tip, same radius, on both sides), then draw the two lids from those centres.
> 4. Rub out the support lines and colour the outline.
>
> ![Four petals drawn as eyes on the four arms of two perpendicular lines](assets/img/class7/math/14/four_petals.svg)
>
> All petals are equal because every petal uses the **same** arm length and the **same** radius.

## 14.2 A right angle at a point, and the rope method

**Worked example: constructing 90° at a point O on a line.** Use the perpendicular bisector idea:

1. Put the compass point on O and mark two points X and Y on the line, **equally far** from O. Now O is the midpoint of XY.
2. From X and from Y, with a larger radius, draw arcs on one side; they meet at A.
3. Join OA. **∠AOY = 90°.**

![90° at O: X and Y marked equally far from O, arcs from X and Y meet at A, OA is perpendicular](assets/img/class7/math/14/right_angle_at_O.svg)

**Why only one pair of arcs?** A line needs two points. We already know one point of the perpendicular bisector of XY: its midpoint **O**. So we only need **one more** point, A.

### The Śulba-sūtras and a rope

The **Śulba-sūtras** are geometry texts of the Vedic period, written for building fire altars of exact shapes. They contain these same constructions, done with a **rope**: a rope tied to a peg draws circles, and a stretched rope makes a straight line. One method (from the Kātyāyana-Śulbasūtra) for the perpendicular bisector of XY:

1. Drive pegs at X and Y. Tie the two ends of a rope to them. Fold the rope in half and mark its **middle**.
2. Hold the middle and pull it away from XY until both halves are tight: mark that point A.
3. Do the same on the other side: B.
4. AB is the perpendicular bisector.

![A rope tied at X and Y, its middle pulled out to A and to B](assets/img/class7/math/14/rope.svg)

### Check yourself

**6.** Justify why AB is the perpendicular bisector in the rope method.

> [!ANSWER]
> When the middle of the rope is at A and both halves are tight, the half from X to A and the half from A to Y are **straight** and each is **half the rope**. So **AX = AY**. In the same way **BX = BY**.
> So A and B are both equally far from X and Y, and both lie on the perpendicular bisector. Two points fix the line, so **AB is the perpendicular bisector**. (It is exactly the compass method, with the rope halves acting as equal radii.)

**7.** Can you think of other ways to make a 90° angle at a point on a line using a rope?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two good ways:
> - **Copy the compass method.** With the rope from O, mark X and Y on the line equally far from O. Then use the middle of a second rope tied at X and Y (pulled tight) to find A. OA is perpendicular to the line.
> - **A 3-4-5 triangle.** Tie knots to divide a rope into 3 + 4 + 5 = 12 equal parts. Hold the knots at the 3-part and 7-part marks to make a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5. The angle between the 3 and 4 sides is **90°**. (Builders have used this "12-knot rope" for thousands of years; you will learn why it works when you meet the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem in Class 8.)

## 14.3 Bisecting an angle

An 8-petalled design needs 8 lines through a point with **equal** angles between them. All the way round is 360°, so each angle is 360° ÷ 8 = **45°**. We can make 90°; if we can **halve** it, we get 45°.

**Worked example: how to bisect any angle XOY.** We want two **congruent** triangles OAC and OBC that share the line OC. Then ∠AOC = ∠BOC.

1. With centre O and any radius, draw an arc cutting the arms at **A** and **B**. So **OA = OB**.
2. With any one (large enough) radius, draw arcs from A and from B. They meet at **C**. So **AC = BC**.
3. Join OC. It **bisects** ∠XOY.

![Angle bisector: arc from O cuts the arms at A and B; arcs from A and B meet at C; OC halves the 70° angle into 35° and 35°](assets/img/class7/math/14/angle_bisector.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why it works:** ∆OAC ≅ ∆OBC by **SSS** (OA = OB, AC = BC, OC common). Matching angles of congruent triangles are equal, so **∠AOC = ∠BOC**.

So a **45°** angle is made by constructing 90° and then bisecting it.

### Check yourself

**8.** Construct at least 4 different angles and bisect each one. How can you check your bisectors?

> [!ANSWER]
> Draw any four angles (say a narrow one, about 30°; a right angle; a wide one, about 120°; and one near 170°) and follow the three steps for each.
> **Checks:** (1) fold the paper along OC: the two arms should lie exactly on each other (the bisector is a line of symmetry of the angle); (2) measure both halves with a protractor: they should be equal and add up to the whole angle. For a wide angle, use a **bigger** radius in step 2, or the arcs from A and B may not meet.

**9.** Construct the 8-petalled design.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw a line and construct the perpendicular at a point O on it: that gives 4 arms at 90°.
> 2. **Bisect** each of the four 90° angles: now there are 8 arms, 45° apart.
> 3. With the compass, mark the same length on each arm (the petal tips).
> 4. On each arm draw an eye-shaped petal from O to the tip (two equal arcs, centres equally far from both ends).
>
> ![Eight equal petals on eight arms 45° apart](assets/img/class7/math/14/eight_petals.svg)
>
> The petals are equal because every arm is the same length and every petal uses the same radius; they are evenly spaced because each angle is exactly half of 90°.

**10.** In step 2, what if the arcs from A and B are drawn on the **other side**, so that they meet at a point C behind O? Is OC still the bisector?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** C is still equally far from A and B (equal radii), and OA = OB. So ∆OAC ≅ ∆OBC by SSS, and ∠AOC = ∠BOC. The line through C and O, **extended beyond O**, splits ∠XOY into two equal parts.
>
> ![Arcs from A and B meeting at C behind O; the line CO, extended, bisects the angle](assets/img/class7/math/14/bisector_far_side.svg)
>
> (Another way to see it: both O and C are equally far from A and B, so both lie on the perpendicular bisector of AB, and that line is the bisector of the angle.) **Careful:** the radius must be big enough for the arcs to meet, and C should not land too close to O, or the line will be drawn inaccurately.

**11.** What other angles can you construct by bisecting? Can you construct 65.5°?

> [!ANSWER]
> Starting from angles we can make (180°, 90°, and later 60°), keep halving and add or subtract them:
> - from 90°: **45°, 22.5°, 11.25°**, …; from 180°: 90°, 45°, …;
> - from 60°: **30°, 15°, 7.5°**, …;
> - sums and differences: 45° + 30° = **75°**, 90° + 45° = **135°**, 60° + 45° = 105°, 90° − 15° = 75°, …
>
> **65.5° cannot be made this way.** Every angle we can get is built from 90° and 60° by halving, adding and subtracting. Now 90 = 6 × 15 and 60 = 4 × 15, so both are **multiples of 15**. Halving k times gives an angle that becomes a multiple of 15 again when it is doubled k times; adding or subtracting such angles keeps this true. So **every constructible angle, doubled enough times, is a multiple of 15**. But 65.5 doubled any number of times is 131, 262, 524, …: these are 131 × (a power of 2), and 131 is not divisible by 3 or by 5, so none of them is a multiple of 15. So 65.5° **cannot** be constructed by these methods (we can only get close to it).

**12.** Come up with a method to bisect an angle using a rope.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. With a rope tied to a peg at O, mark points **A** and **B** on the two arms at the **same** distance from O (the rope swings round like a compass).
> 2. Tie the two ends of a second rope to pegs at A and B. Hold its **middle** and pull it tight, away from O: mark that point **C**. Now CA = CB (each is half the rope).
> 3. Stretch a rope from O through C. It is the bisector.
>
> **Why:** OA = OB, CA = CB and OC is common, so ∆OAC ≅ ∆OBC (SSS) and ∠AOC = ∠BOC. It is the compass method again, with the rope's equal halves as the equal radii.

**13.** Construct four petals inside a square, each as large as possible, meeting at the centre.

> [!ANSWER]
> ![Four petals in a square, each made of two quarter circles; tips at the centre and at the corners](assets/img/class7/math/14/petals_square.svg)
>
> 1. Construct the square and find the **midpoints** of its sides (perpendicular bisectors). Join opposite midpoints: two lines crossing at the centre.
> 2. Each petal runs from the **centre** to a **corner**. Its two edges are quarter circles of radius **half the side**:
>    - one centred at the midpoint M of a side (it passes through the centre and the corner, both half a side from M);
>    - the other centred at the midpoint of the neighbouring side.
> 3. Repeat for all four corners.
>
> **Why this is the biggest possible:** each petal's arcs reach right to the **sides** of the square (an arc centred at M with radius half the side touches the side's corner and the square's middle), and neighbouring petals touch each other along the centre lines. Any bigger and a petal would cross the square or overlap its neighbour.

## 14.4 Copying an angle

**Try this.** Look at this design: one **unit** (a fan shape: two equal arms and an arc) repeats, sometimes upright and sometimes upside down.

![A row of fan-shaped units, alternately upright and upside down, sharing their straight edges](assets/img/class7/math/14/fig_units.svg)

To copy the unit exactly we need the **same arm length** (easy: the compass carries it) and the **same angle** between the arms. How can we copy an angle without a protractor?

**Worked example: copying ∠A onto a ray from X.**
1. With centre A, draw an arc cutting the arms at **B** and **C**. (∆ABC is isosceles: AB = AC.)
2. With the **same radius** and centre X, draw an arc cutting the ray at **Z**.
3. Open the compass to the length **BC**. With centre Z, cut the second arc at **Y**. So YZ = BC.
4. Join XY. **∠YXZ = ∠BAC.**

![Copying an angle: arc from A cuts B and C; same arc from X cuts Z; length BC carried to give Y; ∠X equals ∠A](assets/img/class7/math/14/copy_angle.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why it works:** AB = XY and AC = XZ (same radius), and BC = YZ (carried by the compass). So **∆ABC ≅ ∆XYZ by SSS**, and the matching angles at A and X are equal.

### Check yourself

**14.** Construct at least 4 angles in different directions without measuring, and copy each one.

> [!ANSWER]
> Draw four angles freehand with a ruler (any sizes, pointing different ways). For each, follow the four steps above to copy it onto a new ray.
> **Check:** cut out (or trace) the original angle and place it on the copy: the arms should line up. Or measure both with a protractor. If they differ, the usual cause is changing the compass opening between step 1 and step 2: the **same radius** must be used at A and at X.

**15.** Construct the repeating fan design.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw a base line. Construct the first fan: a vertex on the line, two arms of length r making the chosen angle, and an arc of radius r joining the arm ends.
> 2. **Copy the angle** at the next vertex (the end of the first fan's right arm becomes part of the next, upside-down fan). Use the compass to keep **every arm the same length r**.
> 3. For the upside-down fans, the vertex is at the top: copy the same angle pointing down, and draw the arc below.
> 4. Continue, alternating. Shade alternate fans.
>
> Every unit is congruent because each has the **same two arm lengths** and the **same angle** (copied by SSS); the arcs all have the same radius r.

## 14.5 Drawing a parallel line

Two lines are parallel when a transversal makes **equal corresponding angles** with them. So to draw a line through a point B parallel to a line m, we just **copy the angle** that a transversal makes with m.

**Worked example: the steps.**

1. Draw any line **l** through B, crossing m at **A**. It is the transversal.
2. With centre A, draw an arc cutting m at **C** and l at **D**.
3. With the same radius and centre B, draw an arc cutting l at **F** (beyond B, on the same side as D is from A).
4. Open the compass to **CD**. With centre F, cut the arc from B at **E**.
5. Draw the line **n** through B and E. Then **n ∥ m**.

![Parallel line: arcs of equal radius at A and B, length CD carried to FE, line BE parallel to m](assets/img/class7/math/14/parallel_line.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why it works:** ∆ACD ≅ ∆BEF by SSS (AC = BE, AD = BF, CD = EF). So ∠EBF = ∠CAD: the angles that n and m make with the transversal l, **in the same position**, are equal. Equal corresponding angles mean the lines are **parallel**.

### Check yourself

**16.** Construct 4 pairs of parallel lines in different directions.

> [!ANSWER]
> For each pair: draw a line m in some direction, choose a point B off it, and follow the 5 steps.
> **Check:** measure the gap between the two lines at two places far apart: it should be the same. Or slide a set square along one line: the other edge should stay touching the second line.

**17.** Construct this 8-pointed star of rhombuses (each "point" is a rhombus split into a grey and a white triangle).

![An eight-pointed star: eight rhombuses around the centre, each split by its long diagonal into a grey and a white triangle](assets/img/class7/math/14/star8.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Find the angles first.** There are 8 rhombuses round the centre O, so each has a 360° ÷ 8 = **45°** corner at O.
> 1. Construct 8 lines through O at 45° (perpendicular lines, then bisect the four right angles).
> 2. With the compass, mark A, B, C, …, H on the 8 lines, all the **same distance** from O.
> 3. For each neighbouring pair, say H and A: through A draw a line **parallel to OH**, and through H draw a line **parallel to OA**. They meet at the outer point **S**. Now OHSA has both pairs of opposite sides parallel, and all sides equal (OA = OH, and opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal): a **rhombus**.
> 4. Join O to each outer point S, T, U, … and shade one half of each rhombus.
>
> The points all have the same size because every rhombus has the same side and the same 45° angle.

## 14.6 Arches

**The trefoil arch** (three round lobes) is found in old Indian buildings such as the Red Fort and in parks round the world. Its support lines are a trapezium-like shape ABCD with **AB = CD** and **∠BAD = ∠CDA** (for symmetry).

![Trefoil arch on support lines A, B, C, D: three half-circle lobes on AB, BC and CD](assets/img/class7/math/14/trefoil_arch.svg)

**The pointed arch** needs only two equal segments for support: the two pillars' top line AB. With centre A and radius AB draw an arc upwards from B; with centre B and radius BA draw an arc upwards from A. They meet in a point above the middle.

![Pointed arch: arcs centred at A through B and at B through A meet in a point](assets/img/class7/math/14/pointed_arch.svg)

### Check yourself

**18.** Construct the trefoil arch on support lines.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw the base AD. Construct **equal angles** at A and at D (construct one, then **copy** it at D, opening inwards).
> 2. With the compass, mark B on the arm at A and C on the arm at D with **AB = CD**. Join BC.
> 3. Find the **midpoint** of each of AB, BC and CD (perpendicular bisectors).
> 4. With each midpoint as centre, draw a half circle on that segment, bulging **outwards**.
> 5. Draw the pillars straight down from A and D.
>
> The arch is symmetric because the two sides were made with equal lengths and copied angles. (Change the radii of the side lobes a little if you like the look better.)

**19.** If the midpoints of the two support segments are marked, can you construct a pointed arch? Construct one, and make different arches by changing the radius.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** One way (the most common pointed arch): the arcs centred at **A** and **B** with radius AB, as shown above. The two arcs meet at a point where both distances are AB, so A, B and the tip form an **equilateral** triangle: the tip is exactly above the midpoint of AB.
>
> **Changing the radius:** keep the centres on line AB but move them **inward** (for instance to the midpoints of the two halves of AB) with a smaller radius: the arch becomes **lower and rounder**. Move the centres **outward** (beyond A and B) with a larger radius: the arch becomes **taller and sharper**. In every case the two centres must be at **equal distances** from the middle, so the two arcs are mirror images and meet exactly above the midpoint.

**20.** Make your own arch designs.

> [!ANSWER]
> Any design is fine if it is **symmetric** and every arc has a centre you can point to. Ideas: a horseshoe arch (a circle arc bigger than a half circle sitting on the pillars); a "cusped" arch (a pointed arch with small half circles cut along its inside); an ogee arch (each side made of an outward arc then an inward arc). To keep it symmetric, construct the middle line first (perpendicular bisector of AB) and mirror every centre across it.

## 14.7 Regular hexagons and 60°

A **regular polygon** has all sides equal and all angles equal. We already construct equilateral triangles (3 sides) and squares (4 sides). A regular pentagon is harder (you will see why in later classes), but a regular hexagon is within reach.

**Try this.** Try to draw a pentagon and a hexagon with all sides equal using only a compass and ruler. Are their angles equal too? (Usually not: equal sides alone do not make a polygon regular; think of a square pushed over into a rhombus.)

### Six equilateral triangles

**Worked example.** If we put **six** congruent equilateral triangles round a point O, do they fit with no gap and no overlap, and do they make a regular hexagon?

![Six equilateral triangles around O making a regular hexagon ABCDEF; each angle at O is 60° and each hexagon angle is 120°](assets/img/class7/math/14/hexagon_triangles.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Angles fit around a point exactly when they add up to 360°** (a full turn). Then they cover the region round the point with no gaps and no overlaps.

Each angle of an equilateral triangle is **60°**, and 6 × 60° = 360°, so **six fit exactly**. The outside is a hexagon whose sides are all equal (they are sides of congruent triangles) and whose angles are each 60° + 60° = **120°** (two triangle corners meet at each hexagon corner). So it is a **regular hexagon**, and its side equals the distance from O to each corner.

### Constructing 60°

**Worked example.** To make 60° at A on a line AX:
1. With centre A and any radius, draw an arc cutting AX at **B**.
2. With centre B and the **same** radius, cut the first arc at **C**.
3. Join AC. **∠CAX = 60°.**

![60° construction: arc from A cuts B, same arc from B cuts the first at C; ∠CAB = 60°](assets/img/class7/math/14/sixty.svg)

**Why:** AB = AC (same arc from A) and BC = BA (same radius from B). So AB = BC = CA: ∆ABC is **equilateral**, and every angle of an equilateral triangle is 60° (its three equal angles add to 180°).

And the angle next to it on the straight line is 180° − 60° = **120°**: the angle of a regular hexagon.

### Check yourself

**21.** Around a point O, angles of 40°, 60°, 50°, 30°, 40° and 90° are placed side by side. Will a 70° angle fit into the gap that is left? What is the gap?

![Angles 40°, 60°, 50°, 30°, 40° and 90° around a point, with a gap](assets/img/class7/math/14/angles_round_point.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> All the angles round O must add to **360°**:
> 40° + 60° + 50° + 30° + 40° + 90° + gap = 360°, so 310° + gap = 360°, and **gap = 50°**.
> A **70° angle does not fit**: it is 20° too big and would overlap a neighbour.

**22.** In the hexagon made of six equilateral triangles, why are AOD, BOE and COF straight lines?

> [!ANSWER]
> Take A, O, D. Between OA and OD there are **three** triangle corners at O (the triangles AOB, BOC, COD going one way round), each 60°: 3 × 60° = **180°**. An angle of 180° is a straight angle, so A, O and D lie on **one straight line**. The same reasoning works for BOE and COF.

**23.** Construct a regular hexagon of side 4 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> The side of the hexagon equals the distance from the centre to each corner, so:
> 1. Open the compass to **4 cm** (measure on a scale; that is allowed here) and draw a circle with centre O.
> 2. Mark any point A on the circle. With the **same** 4 cm opening and centre A, cut the circle at B; from B cut at C; then D, E, F.
> 3. Join A, B, C, D, E, F in order.
>
> ![Regular hexagon: circle of radius r, the radius stepped round the circle six times](assets/img/class7/math/14/hexagon_construction.svg)
>
> **Why it works:** each ∆OAB has OA = OB = AB = 4 cm, so it is equilateral with a 60° angle at O. Six steps of 60° make 360°, so the sixth step lands exactly back on A, and all six sides are 4 cm with 120° angles.

**24.** Why is ∠CAX = 60° in the construction above? Is there an equilateral triangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes: ∆ABC.** AB = AC because B and C are on the same arc centred at A. BC = BA because C is on the arc centred at B with the same radius. So all three sides are equal. In an equilateral triangle the three angles are equal (it looks the same from every corner) and add to 180°, so each is 180° ÷ 3 = **60°**; in particular ∠CAB = ∠CAX = 60°.

**25.** Construct a regular hexagon of side 5 cm, this time using 120° angles.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw AB = **5 cm** (scale and compass).
> 2. At B, construct **120°**: construct 60° on the extension of AB beyond B; the angle on the other side of the new arm, between it and BA, is 180° − 60° = 120°.
> 3. Mark C on that arm with BC = 5 cm (compass).
> 4. Repeat at C, D, E: construct 120° and mark 5 cm each time, always turning the same way.
> 5. The sixth side FA should close the shape. Check: FA = 5 cm and the angle at A is 120°.
>
> **Why it closes:** a hexagon's angles add up to 6 × 120° = 720°, which is exactly what any hexagon's angles must add to (it splits into 4 triangles: 4 × 180° = 720°), so a hexagon with equal 5 cm sides and all angles 120° is possible and it closes up.

**26.** How will you construct 30° and 15°?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **30°:** construct 60°, then **bisect** it.
> - **15°:** bisect the 30° angle.
>
> ![60° bisected to 30°, and 30° bisected to 15°](assets/img/class7/math/14/thirty_fifteen.svg)
>
> (Other routes: 90° − 60° = 30°; 45° − 30° = 15°.)

**27.** Construct a 6-pointed star with rotational symmetry. (Hint: do you see a hexagon in it?)

![A six-pointed star with outer points A to F and inner corners G to L forming a hexagon](assets/img/class7/math/14/star6.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> The star is **two equilateral triangles**, ACE and BDF, one upside down on the other. Their corners are the 6 corners of a regular hexagon.
> 1. Construct a circle and step the radius round it 6 times to get A, B, C, D, E, F (as in the hexagon construction).
> 2. Join A, C, E (every second point): an equilateral triangle.
> 3. Join B, D, F: another equilateral triangle.
>
> The star looks the same after turning it by 60°, because each turn of 60° moves every corner to the next one round the circle.

**28.** Are the six small triangles at the points of the star, ∆AGH, ∆BHI, ∆CIJ, ∆DJK, ∆ELK and ∆FLG, equilateral? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Take ∆AGH.
> 1. The angle at A is a corner of the big equilateral triangle ACE, so **∠A = 60°**.
> 2. Line GH is part of the side FB of the other big triangle BDF, and ACE and BDF are placed so that FB is **parallel** to EC (both are perpendicular to the line through A and D, by symmetry). So ∠AGH = ∠AEC = **60°** (corresponding angles).
> 3. The third angle is 180° − 60° − 60° = **60°**.
>
> All three angles are 60°, so ∆AGH is equilateral. Turning the star by 60° carries this triangle onto each of the others, so all six are equilateral (and congruent). The inner corners G, H, I, J, K, L then form a regular hexagon.

## 14.8 Tiling a region

**Tangrams** are an old puzzle from China: a square cut into **7 pieces** (two large triangles, one medium triangle, two small triangles, a square and a parallelogram), which can be rearranged into hundreds of shapes such as an arrow, a cat or a running man.

**Try this: tangrams.** Draw a square of side 16 cm on card and cut it into the 7 pieces like this: draw one diagonal; join the midpoints of two neighbouring sides that are **not** on the diagonal; and so on, as in any tangram picture. Then make: (a) an arrow, (b) a letter of the alphabet, (c) a cat, (d) a boat, (e) a fish, (f) a running person. **Tips:** place the two large triangles first (they are half the area); remember all 7 pieces must be used with no overlaps; and the small square and parallelogram usually go last. If you are stuck, notice that two small triangles can always replace the square, the parallelogram or the medium triangle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Tiling** a region means covering it **completely** with shapes, with **no gaps** and **no overlaps**.

### Tiling grids with dominoes

**Worked example.** A **4 × 6 grid** has 4 rows and 6 columns of unit squares. Can we tile it with **2 × 1 tiles** (dominoes), which we may turn either way? Yes, in many ways; here is one:

![A 4 × 6 grid tiled with 12 dominoes](assets/img/class7/math/14/tiling_4x6.svg)

**General strategy when both sides are even:** cover every column with **upright** dominoes. A column has an even number of squares, so the dominoes fit exactly.

### Check yourself

**29.** Can a 4 × 7 grid be tiled with dominoes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Each column has **4** squares (even), so cover every column with two upright dominoes: 7 × 2 = 14 dominoes cover all 28 squares.
>
> ![A 4 × 7 grid tiled column by column with upright dominoes](assets/img/class7/math/14/tiling_4x7.svg)

**30.** Show that a 5 × 7 grid **cannot** be tiled with dominoes.

> [!ANSWER]
> The grid has 5 × 7 = **35** squares. Each domino covers exactly **2** squares, so any number of dominoes covers an **even** number of squares (2, 4, 6, …). 35 is **odd**, so whatever we do, either a square is left uncovered or a domino hangs over. It cannot be tiled.

**31.** Is an m × n grid tileable with dominoes if **one** of m and n is even and the other odd? If so, give a strategy.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, always.** Say the number of **rows** m is even. Then every **column** has an even number of squares, so fill each column with upright dominoes (m ÷ 2 of them). If instead the number of **columns** is even, fill each **row** with flat dominoes.
>
> ![A 4 × 5 grid: 4 rows (even), so every column is filled with two upright dominoes](assets/img/class7/math/14/tiling_3x5_even_odd.svg)

**32.** Is an m × n grid tileable with dominoes if **both** m and n are odd?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.** odd × odd is **odd** (for instance 3 × 5 = 15, 5 × 7 = 35), and dominoes can only cover an even number of squares. So there is always one square too many.

**33.** A 5 × 3 grid with its **top-right corner** removed has 14 squares (even). Can it be tiled with dominoes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** One tiling:
>
> ![5 × 3 grid with the top-right square removed, tiled with 7 dominoes](assets/img/class7/math/14/removed_corner.svg)

**34.** A 5 × 3 grid with the **middle square of the second row** removed. Can it be tiled?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** One tiling:
>
> ![5 × 3 grid with the centre of the second row removed, tiled with 7 dominoes](assets/img/class7/math/14/removed_centre.svg)

**35.** A 5 × 3 grid with the **middle square of the top row** removed. Can it be tiled? How can we be **sure**?

![5 × 3 grid with the middle square of the top row removed](assets/img/class7/math/14/removed_edge.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Trying and failing many times does not prove it; here is a proof.
> **Colour the grid like a chessboard**, so that every square's neighbours (up, down, left, right) are the other colour.
>
> ![The same region coloured like a chessboard: 8 white and 6 black squares](assets/img/class7/math/14/removed_edge_coloured.svg)
>
> 1. A domino always covers **two neighbouring** squares, so it always covers **one white and one black**.
> 2. So any tiled region must have **equal numbers** of white and black squares.
> 3. This region has **8 white and 6 black**. Not equal, so it can **never** be tiled.

**36.** If the plain region can be tiled, can the black-and-white region be tiled with black-and-white dominoes (black on black, white on white)? And the other way round?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, both ways.**
> - Plain → coloured: every domino in a plain tiling covers two neighbouring squares, which on the chessboard colouring are always one black and one white. So just turn each domino so that its black half is on the black square: it fits.
> - Coloured → plain: forget the colours; the same dominoes cover the same squares.
>
> So the two problems are really **the same problem**; the colours only make it easier to **see** when tiling is impossible.

**37.** Which other single square, removed from a 5 × 3 grid, makes it impossible to tile?

> [!ANSWER]
> Colour the full 5 × 3 grid like a chessboard with the **corners white**: it has **8 white and 7 black** squares.
> - Remove a **black** square: 8 white and 6 black, unequal, so **not tileable**. The black squares are the middle of the top and bottom rows, the ends of the 2nd and 4th rows, and the middle of the 3rd row (and the middle of the 2nd and 4th rows is white).
> - Remove a **white** square: 7 and 7. A search over all cases shows each of these **can** be tiled.
>
> ![5 × 3 grid: ✓ where removing that square leaves a tileable region, ✗ where it does not](assets/img/class7/math/14/which_squares.svg)
>
> So, besides the top-middle square, removing **any of the other 6 black squares** (✗) makes the grid non-tileable.

## 14.9 Tiling the whole plane

Can copies of one shape cover a whole flat surface, going on forever, with no gaps or overlaps? **Squares** can, obviously. What about other **regular polygons**?

![Tilings of the plane by equilateral triangles (6 meet at a point), squares (4) and regular hexagons (3)](assets/img/class7/math/14/plane_tilings.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Where tiles meet at a point, their angles must add up to **360°**. So a regular polygon can tile the plane by itself only if its angle **divides 360° exactly**:
> - equilateral triangle, 60°: 360 ÷ 60 = **6** meet at each point ✓
> - square, 90°: 360 ÷ 90 = **4** ✓
> - regular hexagon, 120°: 360 ÷ 120 = **3** ✓

### Check yourself

**38.** Can regular pentagons tile the plane? Can any regular polygon other than triangles, squares and hexagons?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Regular pentagons cannot.** Each angle of a regular pentagon is **108°** (its angles add up to 3 × 180° = 540°, and 540 ÷ 5 = 108). Three of them make 324°, leaving a **36° gap**; four would make 432°, more than 360°, so they would overlap.
>
> ![Three regular pentagons round a point leave a 36° gap](assets/img/class7/math/14/pentagons_gap.svg)
>
> **No other regular polygon works either.** With 7 or more sides the angle is **more than 120°** (but less than 180°), so 3 of them make more than 360° while 2 make less than 360°. Only 60°, 90° and 120° divide 360 exactly among regular-polygon angles.

> [!NOTE]
> **Beyond regular tiles.** The plane can also be tiled with two or more shapes together, or with irregular shapes. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher (1898–1972) tiled it with birds, fish and lizards. Mathematicians are still finding new tilings: in **2023** a single shape (nicknamed "the hat") was found that tiles the whole plane but **never** in a repeating pattern. In nature, the honeycomb of bees is a tiling by **hexagons**: no space is wasted between the cells.

**Try this.** Look for tilings around you: floor tiles, brick walls, paving stones, a honeycomb, a football. For each, name the shapes and count how many meet at one corner. Check that their angles add up to 360°.

**Question 1.** Construct these figures (ruler and compass).

**(a)** An inflexed arc (a doorway whose top is two arcs curving **inward** to a point in the middle).

![Inflexed arc: two pillars; the top is two quarter circles centred at P and Q above the pillars, meeting at T](assets/img/class7/math/14/inflexed_arc.svg)

> 4. With centre P and radius w, draw the quarter circle from L to T (PT = w because PQ = LR = 2w). With centre Q and radius w, draw the quarter circle from R to T.

**(b)** A flower: a circle with six round petals around it. It can be drawn with **only a compass**: how?

![A circle with six round petals centred on six equally spaced points of the circle](assets/img/class7/math/14/flower.svg)

>
> Each arc is a petal; all six are equal because they have equal radii and their centres are equally spaced. If the opening is a little more than half of r, neighbouring petals just overlap, as in the picture. **No ruler was needed at any step.**

**2.** Optical illusion: in this figure we seem to see a bright white triangle lying on top of three black discs and a triangle outline. Why? Recreate it.

**3.** Construct a regular hexagon with a 6-pointed star inside it, the star's points at the hexagon's corners. (Hint: find the angles in the figure.)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Angles:** the hexagon's angles are **120°**; each star point is **60°**.
> 1. Construct the regular hexagon ABCDEF (circle, radius stepped round 6 times; join in order).
> 2. Join every second corner: A to C to E to A, an equilateral triangle.
> 3. Join the other three: B to D to F to B.

**4.** Draw a line l and a point P not on it. Construct the perpendicular from P to l.

![Perpendicular from P: arc from P cuts l at X and Y; arcs from X and Y meet at Q below; PQ is perpendicular to l](assets/img/class7/math/14/perp_from_P.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Idea (from the hint):** find a segment on l whose perpendicular bisector passes through P.
> 1. With centre P and a radius big enough, draw an arc cutting l at **X** and **Y**. Now **PX = PY**, so P is on the perpendicular bisector of XY.
> 2. From X and from Y, with equal radii, draw arcs on the **other** side of l; they meet at **Q**. Q is also equally far from X and Y.

**6.** Can a chessboard (8 × 8) with **two opposite corners removed** be tiled with dominoes?

![Chessboard with the top-right and bottom-left corners removed: 32 white and 30 black squares](assets/img/class7/math/14/board_corners.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The board has 62 squares (even), so counting alone does not settle it. Use the **colours**:
> 1. Opposite corners of a chessboard are the **same colour** (going from one corner to the opposite one you take 7 steps right and 7 down, 14 steps in all, an even number, and each step changes colour).
> 2. Removing them leaves **32 squares of one colour and 30 of the other**.
> 3. Each domino covers one of each colour, so 31 dominoes would need 31 of each.

## In a nutshell

- **Perpendicular bisector of XY:** arcs of equal radius from X and Y meet above (A) and below (B); AB cuts XY at its **midpoint** at **90°**. **Why:** SSS then SAS congruence.
- **Any point equally far from X and Y lies on the perpendicular bisector**; two such points fix it.
- **90° at a point O on a line:** mark X, Y equally far from O, then one pair of arcs.
- **Angle bisector:** arc from O gives A, B; equal arcs from A, B meet at C; OC bisects (**SSS**).
- **Copy an angle:** same arc at both vertices, carry the chord length (**SSS**).
- **Parallel line:** copy the angle a transversal makes (**equal corresponding angles**).
- **60°:** two arcs of the same radius make an **equilateral triangle**. Then 120° = 180° − 60°, 30° and 15° by bisecting.
- **Regular hexagon:** step the radius round a circle 6 times (6 equilateral triangles, 6 × 60° = 360°).
- **Tiling:** no gaps, no overlaps. Dominoes need an **even** number of squares, and a chessboard colouring needs **equal** black and white.
- A regular polygon tiles the plane only if its angle divides 360°: **triangles, squares, hexagons**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** using **different radii** for the two arcs of one pair (the meeting point is then not on the bisector) · changing the compass opening between the two steps of copying an angle · choosing a radius too small for the arcs to meet · thinking an even number of squares means a region can always be tiled (check the **colours**) · forgetting that the angles round a point must add up to exactly **360°** · trusting how a construction **looks** instead of the **reason** it works.
