## Statistical thinking

Your teacher will meet two friends, one 5 feet tall and one 6 feet tall. Who's the man? You'd guess the 6-footer — and you could be wrong, but **experience** (data!) says men are usually taller. That's **statistical thinking**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A statistical question is one answered by collecting data that varies.**
> "How tall are Grade 7 students in our school?" ✔ — heights differ, so we collect and summarise them.
> "Do *you* like reading?" ✘ — one person, one answer.
> **Statistics** = collecting, organising, analysing, interpreting and presenting data.

**Book Math Talk — statistical or not?** Price of a tennis ball in India ✔ (prices vary across shops) · ages of dogs on this street ✔ · fraction of your class who like walking uphill ✔ · "Do you like reading?" ✘ · roughly how many bricks in this wall — ✘ (one wall, one count) · best bowler yesterday — ✘ (decided from one match's figures) · Barmer's rainfall pattern last year ✔.

## A single number to represent a group — the mean

**Who batted better?**

| | M1 | M2 | M3 | M4 | M5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shubman | 23 | 7 | 10 | 52 | 18 |
| Yashasvi | 26 | 53 | 2 | — | 15 |

Shubman's total (110) is bigger than Yashasvi's (96) — but Yashasvi **played one match fewer**. Totals are unfair when group sizes differ. Share the runs out equally over the matches played:
- Shubman: 110 ÷ 5 = **22 runs per match**
- Yashasvi: 96 ÷ 4 = **24 runs per match** → Yashasvi did better on average.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Mean (average, arithmetic mean) = sum of all the values ÷ number of values.**
> **What it means — a fair share:** if everything were shared out equally, each would get the mean.

**Guavas (book):** Shreyas's group (5 people) collected 3, 8, 10, 5, 4 = 30 → **6 each**. Parag's group (6 people) collected 5, 4, 6, 3, 4, 8 = 30 → **5 each**. Same total, different fair share.

**Hibiscus:** 2, 7, 9, 4, 3 flowers over 5 days → 25 ÷ 5 = **5 a day** on average (what would bloom daily if the same number bloomed every day).

> [!NOTE]
> **Indian names for the mean** all mean "equal": *samarajju* (Brahmagupta, 628 CE), *samīkaraṇa* (levelling — Mahāvīrāchārya, 850 CE), *sāmya* (Śrīpati, 1039 CE), *samamiti* (Bhāskarāchārya, 1150 CE).

**Book Figure it Out:**
- Bounces 6, 2, 9, 5, 4, 6, 3, 5 → 40 ÷ 8 = **5**.
- 100 m times: Nikhil 17, 18, 17, 16, 19, 17, 18 (total 122); Sunil 20, 18, 18, 17, 16, 16, 17 (total 122). **Same average, 17 3/7 ≈ 17.4 s** — neither is quicker on average!
- School enrolment 1555, 1670, 1750, 2013, 2040, 2126 → 11,154 ÷ 6 = **1859**.

## Comparing two sets of data

**Onion prices (₹/kg), January to December:**
- Yahapur: 25, 24, 26, 28, 30, 35, 39, 43, 49, 56, 59, 44
- Wahapur: 19, 17, 23, 30, 38, 35, 42, 39, 53, 60, 52, 42

Different children compare differently: Khushboo looks at the **maximum** (Wahapur's 60). Nafisa uses the **total** (Yahapur 458, Wahapur 450). Vishal counts values in the 50s. Sampat compares **month by month** (Yahapur higher 6 times, Wahapur 5, same once). Jithin uses the **range**, max − min (Yahapur 59 − 24 = 35, Wahapur 60 − 17 = 43).
**Means:** Yahapur 458 ÷ 12 ≈ **₹38.2**, Wahapur 450 ÷ 12 = **₹37.5** — Yahapur slightly costlier on average, though Wahapur's prices swing more.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Ways to describe data:** minimum, maximum, **range** (max − min, how spread out), total, **mean**, **median**. No single number tells the whole story.

### Dot plots

A **dot plot** draws a number line and stacks one dot per value above it. It **sorts** the data and shows at a glance where values cluster, how spread out they are, and the extremes — but loses the original order (you can't tell which dot was January).

> [!TIP]
> The number line in a dot plot needn't start at 0 — for onion prices it runs 10 to 60 because there's nothing outside. Keep the **spacing equal**.

## Outliers and the median

**Family heights (book):**
- Yaangba's family: 169, 173, 155, 165, 160, 164 cm → mean **164.3 cm**
- Poovizhi's family: 170, 173, 165, 118, 175 cm → mean **160.2 cm**

Poovizhi's family looks *shorter* on average, yet 4 of its 5 members are taller than 160! The young child's 118 cm drags the mean down. That 118 is an **outlier**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An outlier is a value far away from the rest of the data.** Outliers pull the mean towards them.
> **Median = the middle value when the data is sorted.** With an even number of values, it's the mean of the two middle ones.
> **Why the median resists outliers:** it depends only on *position*, not on how extreme the end values are. Change 118 to 18 and the median doesn't move.

- Poovizhi's sorted: 118, 165, **170**, 173, 175 → median **170**.
- Yaangba's sorted: 155, 160, **164, 165**, 169, 173 → median (164 + 165) ÷ 2 = **164.5**.
- Without the outlier, Poovizhi's mean jumps to **170.75** and the median to **171.5** — now they agree.

| Situation | What you see |
|---|---|
| data balanced, no outliers | mean ≈ median |
| outlier at the **low** end | **mean < median** |
| outlier at the **high** end | **mean > median** |

**Newspaper pages Monday–Sunday:** 16, 18, 20, 22, 26, 16, 10 → mean 128 ÷ 7 ≈ **18.3**, median (sorted 10, 16, 16, **18**, 20, 22, 26) **18** — close, because the data is fairly balanced.

> [!NOTE]
> **Mean and median are "measures of central tendency"** — numbers that show where the data piles up.

### How tall is your class? (book)

Grade 5 heights: boys from 128 to 158 cm (mean ≈ 142.9, median 144); girls from 136 to 156 cm (mean ≈ 146.9, median 148); whole class mean **144.5**, median 145.
- The tallest **and** the shortest are boys — boys' heights are more spread out.
- On average the girls are taller — but that doesn't mean every girl is taller than every boy!
- In both groups mean < median → a few short values pull the mean down.
- **15** students (8 of them boys) are taller than the class average.

## Zero is not the same as "no value"

A player scores 57, 13, 0, 84, —, 51, 27. The 0 was a real innings; the "—" means **didn't play**. So divide by **6 matches**: 232 ÷ 6 ≈ **38.7**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Count a 0 as a value; leave out a "—".** And think about what makes sense: Sita's mango tree gives 0, 0, 8, 24, 41, 16, 5, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0 mangoes a month. A sensible "mangoes per month" uses just the season (8, 24, 41, 16, 5): mean **18.8**, median **16**.

**Zero median, big total:** can a team's median score per player be 0 but the total 407? Yes — if 6 of the 11 players scored 0 and a few scored hugely. (Mean = (407 − 19 extras) ÷ 11 ≈ 35.3.)

**A mean foot (1500s Europe):** 16 men stood toe to heel; that line was the 16-foot "rod", and 1/16 of it was a "foot" — the **mean** foot length, before the word existed.

**Book Figure it Out:**
1. **Median onion prices:** Yahapur sorted 24 … 59 → middle two 35 and 39 → **37**; Wahapur → middle two 38 and 39 → **38.5**.
2. **Pets** (two students absent, so 20 values): 0, 1, 0, 4, 8, 0, 0, 2, 1, 1, 5, 3, 4, 0, 0, 10, 25, 2, 2, 4 → total 72, **mean 3.6**, **median 2**. The 25 (and 10) are high outliers pulling the mean above the median; most students have 0–4.
3. **29 date palms:** mean ≈ **55.9 ft**, median **56 ft**, **13** trees are shorter than the mean. Heights cluster between 43 and 67 ft. Quick mean: pick a "base" like 55, add up the differences (+/−), divide, and add back.
4. **Tap water** 5.6, 8, 3.09, 12.9, 6.5, 12.1, 11.3, 20.5, 7.4 L: mean ≈ **9.71**, median **8**. Can either be 25–30? **No** — every value is at most 20.5. **The mean and median always lie between the minimum and the maximum** (a fair share can't be more than the biggest share).
5. **Newborn weights:** boys mean ≈ 3.43, median 3.45; girls mean 3.35, median 3.4 kg — very similar.
6. **Sumo vs ballet:** means ≈ 240.4 kg and 42.4 kg → a sumo wrestler is roughly **6 times** as heavy.

## Visualising: clustered (double) bar graphs

Put the Yahapur and Wahapur bars **side by side** for each month and colour them differently: a **clustered (double) column graph**. Month-by-month comparison becomes instant. (Patterns like dots or stripes inside the bars help readers who can't tell colours apart, and work in black-and-white print.)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A 2-step way to read any graph:**
> **Step 1 — Identify what's given:** What's on each axis? What's the **scale** (e.g. 1 unit = 20 rockets)? What do colours mean? What patterns do you see?
> **Step 2 — Infer:** What do those patterns tell you? What can (and can't) be concluded? What new questions does it raise?

**Rocket launches 2021–2023 (book):** SpaceX roughly doubled from 2021 to 2022 and rose about 35 more in 2023; Arianespace fell every year; United Launch Alliance rose then fell. Leading countries: USA, China, Russia.

**Daylight hours — summer and winter at the same time (book):** City 1's average daylight per day rises to about 17–18 hours in June and falls to about 6 in December; City 2 does the **opposite** (about 9 hours in June, 15 in December). City 1 is **Helsinki** (Northern Hemisphere) and City 2 is **Wellington** (Southern) — in June the north is tilted towards the Sun. Average daylight per day = monthly hours ÷ days in the month (June in Helsinki: 564 ÷ 30 ≈ 18.8 hours).

**Runs-per-over graph:** a double bar graph of two teams' runs in each over (dots on top = wickets) lets you "watch" a whole match in a minute.

> [!WARNING]
> **Check where the axis starts.** A graph of heights starting at 145 cm "zooms in" — small differences look huge. Always read the scale before comparing bar lengths.

## Data detective — heights of Indian children (book)

A survey table gives average heights of boys and girls aged 5–19 in 1989, 1999, 2009 and 2019. Some things it shows:
- Average heights at almost every age **increased** from 1989 to 2019.
- **Girls are taller than boys at ages 10–12** (e.g. 12-year-olds in 2019: girls 143.8, boys 142.2) — girls' growth spurt comes earlier — but boys overtake from 13–14 and end much taller (at 19: boys 166.5, girls 155.2).
- So "the average boy is taller than the average girl at every age" **can't** be justified.
- And statements like "**all** 13-year-old girls are taller than **all** 11-year-old girls" can never be justified from averages.
- The table stops at 19, so it can't tell us whether boys keep growing after that.

> [!TIP]
> **Means describe groups, not individuals.** "Girls in this class are taller on average" ≠ "every girl is taller".

**More from the book's exercises:**
- **Player C** played 3 of 4 games (8, 11, —, 13): divide by **3** → mean 32 ÷ 3 ≈ 10.7. Player B's 0 was a real game → divide by **4** → mean 18 ÷ 4 = 4.5. Player A: 50 ÷ 4 = **12.5** — the best performer.
- **Quiz groups:** group 1 (10 students) mean **73**, median **78.5** (low scores 39 and 48 pull the mean down); group 2 (9 students) mean ≈ **75.7**, median **79** — group 2 did slightly better.
- **17 students' heights** — to split into two equal groups by height, use the **median, 115 cm** (8 below, 8 above). Heights around 101–125 cm match ages about 5–7 in the survey table.
- **Two sections**, one with mean height 154.2 cm: the other section's mean **cannot be determined** from this.
- **Aditi's Sudoku times:** week 1 mean and median **360 s**; week 2 mean ≈ **279 s**, median **275 s** — she's getting faster.

## In a nutshell

- **Statistical question** → collect data that varies.
- **Mean** = total ÷ count = the **fair share**.
- **Median** = middle of the sorted data (average of the two middle ones if the count is even).
- **Outliers** drag the mean; the median barely moves. Low outlier → mean < median; high outlier → mean > median.
- Mean and median always lie **between min and max**.
- 0 counts as a value; "didn't play / absent" doesn't.
- **Dot plots** show spread and clusters; **clustered bar graphs** compare categories side by side.
- Read graphs in 2 steps: **identify**, then **infer**. Always check the **scale**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** comparing totals when group sizes differ · forgetting to **sort** before finding the median · dividing by the wrong count when a value is missing · thinking a group average describes every member · not noticing a graph's axis doesn't start at 0.
