## What this chapter is about

Every day we hear sentences like these:

- "She has been batting really well this year, so we can expect a big score tomorrow."
- "It takes me about 15 minutes to cycle home from school."
- "The village has about 100 fewer people than ten years ago."

Each one sums up a lot of **numbers** (scores, times, counts) in a short sentence. Turning a pile of numbers into a sensible summary, and then **asking good questions** about it, is what this chapter is about. You will learn to:

- find **one number that represents a whole group** (the **mean** and the **median**), and know when each one is fair;
- **see** data with **dot plots** and **clustered bar graphs**;
- read any graph in **two steps**: first notice what it shows, then work out what it means.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** **Data** is a collection of values (numbers, or answers like "yes / no"). Each single value is a **data value**. **Statistics** is the study of collecting, organising, analysing, interpreting and presenting data.

## 13.1 Questions that need data

Your teacher says they will meet two old friends tonight. One is 5 feet tall, the other 6 feet tall. Guess which one is a woman and which one a man.

Most people guess the 5-foot friend is the woman. You **could** be wrong, but experience tells us that, more often, men are taller than women. Guessing from **what usually happens** is called **statistical thinking**.

A **statistical statement** is a claim or summary about something, said with numbers, fractions, chances or predictions ("about 15 minutes", "about 100 fewer people").

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A statistical question is a question we answer by collecting data that varies.**
> "How tall are the Grade 7 students in our school?" is statistical: heights **differ** from student to student, so we must collect many heights and summarise them.
> "Do you like reading?" is **not**: it is asked to one person, who gives one answer.
> **Why the "varies" matters:** if every answer were the same, one answer would settle the question and there would be nothing to summarise.

## 13.2 One number for a whole group: the mean

**Worked example: who batted better?** Two batters played four matches:

| | Match 1 | Match 2 | Match 3 | Match 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shubman | 0 | 17 | 21 | 90 |
| Yashasvi | 67 | 55 | 18 | 35 |

Different people look at different things. One says Shubman, because he made the **highest score**, 90. Another says Yashasvi, because his **total** is 175 against Shubman's 128. A third notices that Yashasvi is more **consistent**: the gap between his highest and lowest score (67 − 18 = 49) is much smaller than Shubman's (90 − 0 = 90).

Now a second series:

| | M1 | M2 | M3 | M4 | M5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shubman | 23 | 7 | 10 | 52 | 18 |
| Yashasvi | 26 | 53 | 2 | — | 15 |

The "—" means Yashasvi **did not play** Match 4.

Shubman's total is 110 and Yashasvi's is 96. But comparing totals is **unfair** here: Shubman had **5** chances to score and Yashasvi only **4**. We need a number that does not depend on how many matches were played.

**Idea:** share the runs out **equally** over the matches played. Add up all the runs, then divide by the number of matches.

- Shubman: 110 ÷ 5 = **22 runs per match**.
- Yashasvi: 96 ÷ 4 = **24 runs per match**.

So, match for match, **Yashasvi scored more** in this series.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Mean** (also called the **average** or **arithmetic mean**):
>
> **Mean = (sum of all the values) ÷ (number of values)**
>
> **Why it is a fair single number:** if the total were shared out so that every value became the same, each would be exactly the mean. Highs and lows **balance out**.

### The mean as a fair share

**Worked example.** Shreyas and 4 friends picked guavas: 3, 8, 10, 5 and 4. Parag and 5 friends picked 5, 4, 6, 3, 4 and 8. Each group shares its guavas equally. In which group does each person get more?

- Shreyas's group: 3 + 8 + 10 + 5 + 4 = 30 guavas, shared by 5 people: 30 ÷ 5 = **6 each**.
- Parag's group: 5 + 4 + 6 + 3 + 4 + 8 = 30 guavas, shared by 6 people: 30 ÷ 6 = **5 each**.

Same total, but Shreyas's group has fewer people, so each gets **1 more guava**.

![Five piles of guavas (3, 8, 10, 5, 4) levelled into five piles of 6](assets/img/class7/math/13/fair_share.svg)

**Why this works:** levelling only **moves** guavas from tall piles to short ones; none are added or lost. So the total stays 30, and 5 equal piles of 30 must be 6 each. The mean is the height of the piles **after levelling**.

**Worked example.** Vaishnavi counts the hibiscus flowers that bloom in her garden each day: 2, 7, 9, 4, 3.

1. Total flowers: 2 + 7 + 9 + 4 + 3 = 25.
2. Number of days: 5.
3. Mean: 25 ÷ 5 = **5 flowers a day**.

Meaning: if the same number bloomed every day, it would be 5 a day.

**Try this: see the mean with blocks.** Make 5 towers of building blocks (or coins) with 3, 8, 10, 5 and 4 in them. Without adding or removing any, move blocks from tall towers to short ones until all towers are the same height. You will always end with 6 in each. Now try towers of 1, 2 and 9: you get 4 each, and 4 = (1 + 2 + 9) ÷ 3.

> [!NOTE]
> **Old Indian names for the mean** all contain *sama*, "equal": *samarajju* (Brahmagupta, 628 CE), *samīkaraṇa*, "levelling" (Mahāvīrācārya, 850 CE), *sāmya*, "equality" (Śrīpati, 1039 CE) and *samamiti*, "mean measure" (Bhāskarācārya, 1150 CE). They saw the mean exactly as we just did: the value that **levels** a collection.

> [!TIP]
> **Means around us:** "average rainfall per day in July", "my scooter's average mileage is 45 km per litre", "an average Indian produces about 0.45 kg of waste a day". Each one shares a total out equally.

## 13.3 Comparing two sets of data

**Worked example.** The price of onions (₹ per kg) each month in two towns:

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yahapur | 25 | 24 | 26 | 28 | 30 | 35 | 39 | 43 | 49 | 56 | 59 | 44 |
| Wahapur | 19 | 17 | 23 | 30 | 38 | 35 | 42 | 39 | 53 | 60 | 52 | 42 |

Where are onions costlier? Five children compare in five ways:

- **Khushboo** looks at the **highest** price: Wahapur reached ₹60, so Wahapur.
- **Nafisa** adds up the year: Yahapur ₹458, Wahapur ₹450, so Yahapur.
- **Vishal** counts prices in the 50s: Wahapur has 3, Yahapur 2, so Wahapur.
- **Sampat** compares month by month: Yahapur is higher in 6 months, Wahapur in 5, equal in 1 (June), so Yahapur.
- **Jithin** looks at the gap between highest and lowest: Yahapur 59 − 24 = 35, Wahapur 60 − 17 = 43. Wahapur's prices **swing more**.

All of these are honest ways to look. Each tells you **something different**, so no single one settles the question.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Ways to describe data:** the **minimum** (smallest value), the **maximum** (largest value), the **range** = maximum − minimum (how spread out the values are), the **total**, the **mean**, and the **median** (coming soon).
> **Why the range shows spread:** it measures the distance from the lowest value to the highest; a big range means the values are scattered far apart.

### Dot plots

A **dot plot** draws a number line and puts **one dot for each value** above it. Equal values stack up.

![Dot plots of monthly onion prices: Yahapur (green) and Wahapur (purple), from ₹10 to ₹60](assets/img/class7/math/13/onion_dotplot.svg)

- The line runs from 10 to 60 (not from 0) because no price is below 10 or above 60. The marks must be **equally spaced**.
- A dot plot **sorts** the data for you, so the lowest, highest and any clusters jump out. You can see at once that Wahapur's dots are **more spread out**, and that only Wahapur has prices below 20.
- It **loses the order**: you can no longer tell which dot was January.

**Try this.** Measure the hand span (thumb tip to little-finger tip, hand stretched) of everyone at home or in your class, to the nearest cm. Draw a dot plot. Where do the dots pile up? What are the minimum, maximum and range?

> [!TIP]
> **Drawing a good dot plot:** (1) find the smallest and largest values; (2) draw a line a little wider than that, with **equal** steps; (3) put one dot per value, stacking repeats **neatly in columns** so the heights can be compared; (4) give it a title and say what the numbers measure.

> 2. Wahapur: total ₹450 over 12 months, mean = 450 ÷ 12 = **₹37.50 per kg**.

**7.** Can you think of another way to compare the two towns? And does the dot plot keep all the information in the table? Can it tell you Yahapur's price in January?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Other ways** (any sensible one is fine): compare the **medians**; count months above ₹40; compare the **first half** of the year with the second half (both towns get costlier towards October–November); look at how much the price **jumps** from one month to the next.
>

## 13.4 When the mean misleads: outliers and the median

**Worked example.** The heights of two families:

- **Yaangba's family:** 169, 173, 155, 165, 160, 164 cm.
- **Poovizhi's family:** 170, 173, 165, 118, 175 cm.

Means: Yaangba's 986 ÷ 6 ≈ **164.3 cm**; Poovizhi's 801 ÷ 5 = **160.2 cm**.

So is Yaangba's family taller? Look closer: **4 of Poovizhi's 5 family members are taller than 164 cm**. The youngest child (118 cm) is far shorter than everyone else and drags the mean down. Poovizhi's mean, 160.2 cm, is **less than the heights of 4 of the 5 people** it is meant to represent.

A value that is very far from the rest of the data is called an **outlier**. Here 118 is an outlier.

### The median: the middle value

Another way to pick one number: **sort the values and take the one in the middle**.

- Poovizhi's family sorted: 118, 165, **170**, 173, 175. The middle (3rd of 5) is **170 cm**.
- Yaangba's family sorted: 155, 160, **164, 165**, 169, 173. Six values have **no single middle**, so take the mean of the two middle ones: (164 + 165) ÷ 2 = **164.5 cm**.

![Dot plots of the two families' heights with mean (solid) and median (dashed) lines](assets/img/class7/math/13/family_heights.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Median = the middle value after sorting.** For an **odd** number of values it is the middle one; for an **even** number it is the **mean of the two middle values**.
> **Why the median shrugs off outliers:** it depends only on **which value sits in the middle position**, not on how extreme the end values are. Change 118 to 18 or to 150: the middle of the sorted list is still 170.
> **Why the mean does not:** the mean uses the **total**, and one extreme value changes the total a lot.

Now the median says Poovizhi's family (170) is **taller** than Yaangba's (164.5), which matches what we see. In Yaangba's family, with no outlier, the mean (164.3) and median (164.5) are almost equal.

**Try this.** Write any five numbers on cards, say 4, 6, 7, 9, 10. Find the mean (7.2) and median (7). Now change the 10 into 100 and find them again. Which one changed? Change the 4 into −50: which one changed now?

> [!TIP]
> **Which to use?** If the data has an outlier, or is lopsided (a few huge or tiny values), the **median** gives a fairer "typical" value. If the data is balanced, the **mean** is good, and it uses every value.

> [!NOTE]
> **Mean and median are "measures of central tendency"**: numbers that show where the values tend to pile up, the "centre" of the data.

**12.** In the three examples (family heights, stories, newspaper pages), what do the data look like when (a) the mean and median are close, (b) mean < median, (c) mean > median?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **(a) Close** (newspaper pages; Yaangba's family): the data is **balanced**, spread fairly evenly, with no outlier.
> - **(b) Mean < median** (Poovizhi's family): there is an outlier on the **low** side (118). It pulls the total, and so the mean, **down**.
> - **(c) Mean > median** (stories): there is an outlier on the **high** side (40). It pulls the mean **up**.
>
> **Rule of thumb:** the mean is dragged **towards** the outlier; the median stays near the middle of the pile.

**13.** What happens to the mean and median when there are outliers on **both** sides? Make up an example.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take 20, 21, 22, 23, 24: mean 22, median 22.
> Now replace the ends with outliers on both sides: **2**, 21, 22, 23, **44**.
> 1. Median: still the middle, **22** (outliers never move the middle position).
> 2. Mean: total = 2 + 21 + 22 + 23 + 44 = 112, so mean = 112 ÷ 5 = **22.4**.
>
> The low outlier pulls the mean down and the high one pulls it up, so their effects **partly cancel**. If they are equally far from the centre, the mean does not move at all; if one is farther, the mean shifts towards that one. The median ignores both.

## 13.5 The ends and the middle: how tall is your class?

**Worked example.** Heights (cm) of a Grade 5 class:

- **Boys (17):** 147, 135, 130, 154, 128, 135, 134, 158, 155, 146, 146, 142, 140, 141, 144, 145, 150
- **Girls (11):** 143, 136, 150, 144, 154, 140, 145, 148, 156, 150, 150

| | Number | Lowest | Highest | Mean | Median |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boys | 17 | 128 | 158 | 2430 ÷ 17 ≈ 142.9 | 144 |
| Girls | 11 | 136 | 156 | 1616 ÷ 11 ≈ 146.9 | 148 |
| Whole class | 28 | 128 | 158 | 4046 ÷ 28 = 144.5 | 145 |

![Dot plots of girls', boys' and whole-class heights with mean and median lines](assets/img/class7/math/13/class_heights.svg)

What can we say?

- The boys' heights are **more spread out** (128 to 158, range 30) than the girls' (136 to 156, range 20). The tallest **and** the shortest students are both boys.
- On average the **girls are taller** (146.9 against 142.9). That does **not** mean every girl is taller than every boy: the tallest student is a boy!
- In both groups **mean < median**: a few short values pull the mean a little below the middle.

## 13.6 Zero is not the same as "no value"

**Worked example.** A batter's scores in a series: 57, 13, 0, 84, —, 51, 27. The **0** is a real innings: the batter played and scored nothing. The **—** means the batter **did not play**. So we divide by **6** matches, not 7:

Mean = (57 + 13 + 0 + 84 + 51 + 27) ÷ 6 = 232 ÷ 6 ≈ **38.7 runs per match**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Count a 0 as a value; leave out a "—" (missing).** A 0 is something that happened; a missing value is something that did not happen.

**Worked example.** Sometimes we must also **think about which values make sense**. Sita's mango tree gave these mangoes each month from January to December: 0, 0, 8, 24, 41, 16, 5, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0. Mango trees only fruit in summer. To describe "how many mangoes a month in the season", use only March to July: 8, 24, 41, 16, 5.

- Mean = 94 ÷ 5 = **18.8 mangoes a month**; sorted 5, 8, **16**, 24, 41 → median **16**.
- If we used all 12 months, the mean would be 94 ÷ 12 ≈ 7.8 and the median 0, which describes no real month of the season.

> [!NOTE]
> **A mean foot.** In Europe in the early 1500s, a "rod" for measuring land was 16 feet long, and a foot was a real person's foot. But feet differ! So 16 men stood in a line, toe to heel; that line was the rod, and one-sixteenth of it was "a foot". That is the **mean** foot length, found long before anyone used the word "mean".

### Check yourself

**16.** A newspaper says: "Yesterday the **median** score of the team's players was 0, and yet the team scored 407 runs all out." Can that be true?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** A cricket team has 11 batters. Sort their scores: the median is the **6th** one. If **6 or more** players scored 0, the 6th sorted score is 0, so the median is 0.
>
> The other 5 (or fewer) players can still score heavily: for instance 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 30, 58, 85, 101, 114 add up to **388**, and with **19 extras** (runs not scored by any batter) the team total is 388 + 19 = **407**. ✓
>
> The **mean** runs per player would be 388 ÷ 11 ≈ **35.3**: very different from the median of 0. Most players failed, a few scored big; the median shows the first fact, the mean hides it.

**17.** Find the median onion price in Yahapur and in Wahapur.

> [!ANSWER]
> Twelve values, so the median is the mean of the **6th and 7th** sorted values.
> 1. Yahapur sorted: 24, 25, 26, 28, 30, **35, 39**, 43, 44, 49, 56, 59 → (35 + 39) ÷ 2 = **₹37**.
> 2. Wahapur sorted: 17, 19, 23, 30, 35, **38, 39**, 42, 42, 52, 53, 60 → (38 + 39) ÷ 2 = **₹38.50**.

**19.** Rintu looks after date palms. Their heights (feet): 50, 45, 43, 52, 61, 63, 46, 55, 60, 55, 59, 56, 56, 49, 54, 65, 66, 51, 44, 58, 60, 54, 52, 57, 61, 62, 60, 60, 67. Make a dot plot, find the mean and median, and describe the heights. Is there a quicker way to find the mean? How many trees are shorter than the mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Count:** 29 trees. **Median:** sorted, the middle is the 15th value = **56 ft**.
>
> **Mean, the quick way:** pick a convenient "base" near the middle, say **55**, and add up how far each tree is above (+) or below (−) it:
> −5, −10, −12, −3, +6, +8, −9, 0, +5, 0, +4, +1, +1, −6, −1, +10, +11, −4, −11, +3, +5, −1, −3, +2, +6, +7, +5, +5, +12. These add up to **+26**.
> So the total is 29 × 55 + 26 = 1595 + 26 = **1621**, and the mean = 55 + 26 ÷ 29 ≈ 55 + 0.9 = **55.9 ft**.
> **Why the shortcut works:** each height = 55 + its difference, so the total = 29 × 55 + (sum of differences). Sharing that out equally gives 55 + (sum of differences) ÷ 29. Small numbers are much easier to add than 29 two-digit heights.
>
> ![Dot plot of 29 palm heights from 43 to 67 ft, mean about 55.9 and median 56](assets/img/class7/math/13/palm_dotplot.svg)
>
> **Description:** the heights run from 43 to 67 ft (range 24 ft) and are spread fairly evenly, with a small pile at 60 ft (4 trees). Mean (55.9) and median (56) almost agree, so there is no outlier.
>
> **Shorter than the mean:** the heights below 55.9 are 43, 44, 45, 46, 49, 50, 51, 52, 52, 54, 54, 55, 55 → **13 trees**.

**Question 20.** The water used from a tap each day (litres): 5.6, 8, 3.09, 12.9, 6.5, 12.1, 11.3, 20.5, 7.4.

**(a)** Can the mean or the median daily use be between 25 and 30 litres? Explain using what mean and median mean.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The **largest** day is only 20.5 litres.
> - The **mean** is a fair share: if all the water were shared equally over the 9 days, each day would get the mean. Sharing out can never give a day **more** than the biggest day had, so the mean is at most 20.5. (In fact the mean = 87.39 ÷ 9 ≈ **9.71 L**.)
> - The **median** is one of the middle values of the sorted list (here the 5th of 9: 3.09, 5.6, 6.5, 7.4, **8**, 11.3, 12.1, 12.9, 20.5 → **8 L**), so it is one of the data values, at most 20.5.

**(b)** Can the mean or median ever be less than the smallest value or more than the largest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.**
> - **Mean:** levelling moves amounts from bigger values to smaller ones until all are equal. The tallest pile can only go down and the shortest can only go up, so the level ends up **between** them.
> - **Median:** it is a middle value of the sorted list (or halfway between two middle values), so it lies **between** the first and last values.
>
> So **minimum ≤ mean ≤ maximum** and **minimum ≤ median ≤ maximum**, always. (They equal the min or max only if all values are the same.)

## 13.7 Clustered (double) bar graphs

We can draw a bar graph of Yahapur's prices and another for Wahapur's. But to **compare** month by month it is easier to put the two bars for each month **side by side**:

![Clustered bar graph of monthly onion prices, Yahapur green and Wahapur purple](assets/img/class7/math/13/onion_bars.svg)

This is a **clustered bar graph**; with two bars in each cluster it is also called a **double bar graph**. The **scale** here is 1 grid step = ₹10. In each month the taller bar shows the costlier town, and the grid lets you estimate the difference. (Printed books often fill the bars with dots or stripes as well as colour, so that people who find colours hard to tell apart, and black-and-white copies, can still read them.)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The 2-step way to read any graph.**
> **Step 1, identify what is given:** What is on each axis? What is the **scale** (how much one grid step is worth)? What do the colours mean? Where does each axis start? What patterns do you see?
> **Step 2, infer:** What do those patterns **tell** you? What can, and cannot, be concluded? What new questions does it raise?
> **Why two steps:** if you jump to conclusions before reading the scale and labels, you can misread a graph completely (for instance, thinking a bar twice as long means twice as much when the axis does not start at 0).

**Try this.** Ask 10 friends or family members two questions: "How many hours did you sleep last night?" and "How many hours do you **want** to sleep?" Draw a double bar graph with one cluster per person. Which bar is usually taller?

> [!TIP]
> **Drawing a double bar graph:** choose a scale so the biggest value fits (round the top up to a nice number); keep the two bars of each cluster **touching**, with a gap between clusters; use the **same colour order** in every cluster; add a key (legend) and label both axes.

### Summer and winter at the same time

**Worked example.** The hours of daylight (Sun at least partly above the horizon) in each month, for two cities:

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| City 1 | 210 | 257 | 372 | 441 | 536 | 564 | 555 | 465 | 394 | 310 | 222 | 186 |
| City 2 | 459 | 384 | 381 | 327 | 304 | 276 | 295 | 318 | 369 | 409 | 435 | 468 |

Months have different numbers of days, so to compare fairly we find the **average daylight per day** = monthly hours ÷ days in the month (June in City 1: 564 ÷ 30 = 18.8 hours).

![Double bar graph of average daylight hours per day for City 1 and City 2 in each month](assets/img/class7/math/13/daylight.svg)

**Step 1.** Months across the bottom; hours per day up the side, 1 grid step = 5 hours. June is the **highest** month for City 1 and the **lowest** for City 2.

**Step 2.**

- City 1's days grow longer from January to about **19 hours** in June, then shorter, down to about **6 hours** in December.
- City 2 does the **opposite**: about 9 hours in June, about 15 hours in December.
- City 1's extremes are more extreme: its longest days are longer and its shortest days shorter than City 2's.
- In June, City 1 has daylight for about 3/4 of the 24-hour day (18 ÷ 24 = 3/4); in December–January only about 1/4 (6 ÷ 24).

So where are they? City 1 must be far **north** of the Equator and City 2 far **south**. In June the northern half of the Earth is tilted **towards** the Sun (long summer days) and the southern half **away** (short winter days). City 1 is **Helsinki** (Finland) and City 2 is **Wellington** (New Zealand). Near the poles, in summer, the Sun does not set at all: the "midnight Sun".

> 2. **0 6 4:** one digit is correct; 0 and 6 are ruled out, so **4 is in the code**, but **not in the 3rd place**.
> 3. **5 4 2:** two digits are correct. 4 is one of them, so exactly one of 5 and 2 is in the code. Also 4 is not in the 2nd place (wrong place here). So 4 is in the **1st place** (not 2nd, not 3rd).
> 4. **2 6 5:** one digit correct **and in its place**; 6 is out. If it were 2 (1st place), it would clash with 4 being 1st. So **5 is in the code, in the 3rd place**. Then from step 3, 2 is **not** in the code.
> 5. **2 7 1:** one digit correct but in the wrong place; 2 is out, so it is 7 or 1. The only place left is the **2nd**. 7 in this clue is already in the 2nd place, so if 7 were the digit it would be "well placed", which the clue says it is not. So the digit is **1**, and it goes in the 2nd place.

## In a nutshell

- A **statistical question** is answered by collecting data that **varies**.
- **Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values** = the **fair share** if everything were levelled.
- **Median = middle of the sorted data** (mean of the two middle values if there is an even number).
- An **outlier** is far from the rest. It drags the **mean** towards itself; the **median** hardly moves. Low outlier → mean < median; high outlier → mean > median; balanced data → mean ≈ median.
- Mean and median always lie **between the minimum and the maximum**.
- Describe data with **min, max, range (max − min), total, mean, median**; no single number tells the whole story.
- **0 is a value; a missing value ("—", absent, did not play) is not.** Divide by the number of real values.
- **Dot plots** show spread, clusters and outliers; **clustered (double) bar graphs** compare groups side by side.
- Read a graph in **2 steps**: **identify** (axes, scale, colours, start of the axis, patterns), then **infer**.
- Averages describe **groups**, never every individual.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** comparing totals when the groups have different sizes · forgetting to **sort** before finding the median · with an even count, forgetting to average the two middle values · dividing by the wrong count when a value is missing (or leaving out a real 0) · thinking an average describes **every** member · trusting bar lengths without checking where the axis **starts** · saying "the fastest / tallest in the world" when the graph shows only some data.
