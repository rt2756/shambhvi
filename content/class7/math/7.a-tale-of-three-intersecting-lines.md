## What this chapter is about

Take three straight lines that cross each other in three different points. The piece they fence in is a **triangle**, the simplest closed shape there is. In this chapter we ask:

- How can we **draw** a triangle exactly, when we know its sides, or some sides and angles?
- Can **any** three lengths make a triangle? (No! We will find a quick test.)
- Why do the three angles of **every** triangle add up to exactly **180°**?
- What is the **height** of a triangle, and how do we draw it?

By the end you will be able to say whether a triangle exists **before** you draw it, and you will know the reason why.

## 7.1 Triangles and their names

A triangle has:
- **three vertices** (corner points), and
- **three sides**, the line segments joining each pair of vertices.

We name a triangle by its vertices with the sign **∆** in front: ∆ABC. The order does not matter: ∆ABC, ∆BCA and ∆CAB are all the same triangle.

![Three triangles named by their vertices: ∆ABC, ∆YZX and ∆WUV](assets/img/class7/math/7/triangles_named.svg)

At each corner two sides meet and make an angle. In ∆ABC the three angles are ∠CAB, ∠ABC and ∠BCA. We usually write them short as **∠A, ∠B and ∠C**, naming each angle by the vertex where it sits.

### Check yourself

**1.** What happens if the three "vertices" A, B and C all lie on one straight line?

![A, B and C on one straight line](assets/img/class7/math/7/collinear.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **There is no triangle.** The "sides" AB, BC and AC all lie along the same line, one on top of another, so they don't fence in any space and there are no corners. The longest side AC is exactly AB + BC. Keep this picture in mind: later we will see that **one side equal to the sum of the other two means "flat", not a triangle**.

## 7.2 Drawing an equilateral triangle

An **equilateral** triangle has **all three sides equal**. It is the most symmetric triangle of all. Let's draw one with every side 4 cm.

**With only a ruler** you can do it, but it takes guessing. Draw the base AB = 4 cm. Now you need a point C that is 4 cm from A **and** 4 cm from B. You can put the ruler at A and mark a point 4 cm away, but then BC may come out 3.6 cm or 4.3 cm, and you have to rub it out and try again.

**With a compass** there is no guessing. The key fact:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A circle (or an arc) is the set of all points at one fixed distance from its centre.** So an arc of radius 4 cm drawn from A contains **every** point that is 4 cm from A, and nothing else.

![Three steps: arc from A, arc from B, join to the crossing point C](assets/img/class7/math/7/equi_steps.svg)

1. Draw the base **AB = 4 cm**.
2. Set the compass to 4 cm and draw a long arc with centre **A**. C must be somewhere on this arc.
3. Keeping the same opening, draw an arc with centre **B**. C must be on this arc too.
4. The arcs cross at **C**. Join AC and BC.

**Why it works:** C lies on the first arc, so AC = 4 cm. C also lies on the second arc, so BC = 4 cm. And AB = 4 cm because we drew it so. All three sides are 4 cm. The crossing point is the **only** kind of point that is on both arcs, which is exactly what we needed.

## 7.3 Drawing a triangle from its three sides

Now a triangle that isn't equilateral: sides **4 cm, 5 cm and 6 cm**.

The same idea works. Pick one length as the base, say **AB = 4 cm**, and decide that AC = 5 cm and BC = 6 cm.

- Every point 5 cm from A lies on the circle with centre A and radius 5 cm. C is on that circle.
- Every point 6 cm from B lies on the circle with centre B and radius 6 cm. C is on that circle too.
- So **C is where the two circles cross**.

![Circle of radius 5 cm about A and arc of radius 6 cm about B cross at C](assets/img/class7/math/7/sss_456.svg)

You don't need the full circles: an arc near where C will be is enough.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Construction from three sides (SSS):**
> 1. Draw the base AB with one of the lengths.
> 2. From A, draw an arc with the second length as radius.
> 3. From B, draw an arc with the third length as radius, crossing the first arc.
> 4. The crossing point is C. Join AC and BC.
>
> **Why:** C is on both arcs, so its distance from A is the first radius and its distance from B is the second radius.

> [!TIP]
> The arcs cross at **two** points, one above AB and one below. Either one gives the triangle: the lower one is just the upper triangle **flipped over** AB, same shape and size.

**Names by sides.** A triangle with **all three sides equal** is **equilateral**. One with **two equal sides** is **isosceles** ("iso" = equal, "sceles" = legs). One with **all sides different** is **scalene**. In figures, sides with the **same tick marks** are equal.

**(g)** Two circles of the same size have centres A and B, and each circle passes through the other's centre. Use the centres and points on the circles to make isosceles and equilateral triangles. Then do the same for three such circles with centres A, B and C.

![Two equal circles with centres A and B](assets/img/class7/math/7/two_circles_q.svg)
![Three equal circles with centres A, B and C](assets/img/class7/math/7/three_circles_q.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two circles.** Call the radius r. Since each circle passes through the other's centre, **AB = r**.
>
> ![Equilateral triangles ABC and ABD, and an isosceles triangle ABE](assets/img/class7/math/7/two_circles_a.svg)
>
> - **Equilateral:** let C and D be the points where the circles cross. C is on the circle about A, so AC = r; C is on the circle about B, so BC = r. With AB = r, all three sides are r: **∆ABC is equilateral**, and so is ∆ABD.
> - **Isosceles:** take any other point E on the circle about B. Then BA = BE = r (both radii of that circle), so **∆ABE is isosceles**. Points on the circle about A work the same way.
>
> **Three circles.** Now A, B and C are all one radius apart (each centre lies on the other circles), so **∆ABC is equilateral**. Any point G on the circle about C gives CA = CG = r, so **∆CAG is isosceles**.
>
> ![Equilateral triangle ABC from the three centres, and an isosceles triangle CAG](assets/img/class7/math/7/three_circles_a.svg)

## 7.4 Can any three lengths make a triangle?

**Try this.** Try to construct a triangle with sides **3 cm, 4 cm and 8 cm**. Draw the base 8 cm, an arc of 3 cm from one end and an arc of 4 cm from the other.

![Base 8 cm with arcs of 3 cm and 4 cm that never meet](assets/img/class7/math/7/no_meet_348.svg)

The arcs **never meet**, so there is no point C. Some sets of lengths make triangles and some don't. Trying every set by drawing is slow. Is there a quicker test?

### Check yourself

**1.** Why couldn't the arcs for 3 cm, 4 cm and 8 cm meet?

> [!ANSWER]
> The arc from A reaches only 3 cm along the base, and the arc from B reaches only 4 cm back along it. Together they cover 3 + 4 = **7 cm** of the 8 cm base, so a **1 cm gap** is left in the middle. Arcs that can't even reach each other along the straight base certainly can't reach each other anywhere else.

**2.** Check whether a triangle with sides 2 cm, 3 cm and 6 cm is possible.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not possible.** On a base of 6 cm, the arcs of 2 cm and 3 cm together reach only 2 + 3 = 5 cm, leaving a 1 cm gap. They never meet.

**3.** Find more sets of lengths for which a triangle is impossible. What pattern do they share?

> [!ANSWER]
> For instance 1, 2, 5 · 2, 2, 7 · 3, 5, 10 · 4, 4, 9. **Pattern: the two shorter lengths add up to less than the longest one.** The two short sides together are too short to reach across the long side.

### The shortest path

Picture a flat field with a **tent**, a **tree** and a **pole**. You walk from the tent to the tree. Which is shorter: walking **straight** to the tree, or walking to the pole first and **then** to the tree?

![Tent, tree and pole: the direct path and the path via the pole](assets/img/class7/math/7/tent_tree.svg)

Clearly the straight path. **A straight line is the shortest path between two points.** Any detour through a third point is longer.

Now think of a triangle ABC. Going straight from B to C is one side, BC. Going "the long way" from B to C means going through A: BA + AC. So:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In every triangle, each side is shorter than the other two sides added together.**
> BC < BA + AC, AB < AC + CB, CA < CB + BA.
> **Why:** a side is the straight path between two vertices, and the other two sides together are a detour through the third vertex. The straight path is always the shortest.

**Worked example.** Can a triangle have sides **10 cm, 15 cm and 30 cm**?

Suppose it could. Draw a rough sketch (not to size) with BC = 10, BA = 15, AC = 30, and test all three paths.

![A rough sketch of a "triangle" with sides 10, 15 and 30 cm](assets/img/class7/math/7/rough_10_15_30.svg)

1. B to C: direct 10; via A, 15 + 30 = 45. Direct is shorter. ✔
2. A to B: direct 15; via C, 30 + 10 = 40. Direct is shorter. ✔
3. C to A: direct **30**; via B, 10 + 15 = **25**. The direct path is **longer** than the detour. ✘

A straight path can never be longer than a detour, so this is impossible. **No triangle has sides 10, 15 and 30 cm.** We found this without drawing anything, only by reasoning.

### Check yourself

**1.** Can a triangle have sides 3 cm, 3 cm and 7 cm? Check by reasoning, then by drawing.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The detour around the 7 cm side is 3 + 3 = 6 cm, which is shorter than the 7 cm "straight" side. That is impossible. By drawing: on a 7 cm base, arcs of 3 cm from each end cover only 6 cm and leave a 1 cm gap, so they don't meet.

**2.** In the rough sketch for 10, 15 and 30, could we write the three lengths on the sides in a **different order** so that every direct path is shorter than its detour?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Whichever side gets the 30, its detour is made of the other two sides, 10 + 15 = 25, which is shorter than 30. Moving the numbers around changes which side is 30, but never this sum. So no arrangement works, and no such triangle exists.

**3.** Earlier we found by drawing that 3, 4, 8 and 2, 3, 6 make no triangle. Could you have found this without drawing?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 3 + 4 = 7 is less than 8, and 2 + 3 = 5 is less than 6. In each case the longest side is longer than the detour made by the other two, which is impossible in a triangle.

**Question 4.** Is a triangle possible with these sides? Explain.

**(a)** 10 km, 10 km and 25 km

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 10 + 10 = 20, which is less than 25. The 25 km side would be longer than the detour around it.

**(b)** 5 mm, 10 mm and 20 mm

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 5 + 10 = 15, less than 20.

**(c)** 12 cm, 20 cm and 40 cm

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 12 + 20 = 32, less than 40.

**5.** For 10, 15, 30 two of the three tests passed (10 < 15 + 30 and 15 < 10 + 30) and one failed. For **any** three lengths, will at least two tests always pass?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, always.** Take a shorter length, say 15. The longest length (30) alone is already more than 15, so 15 < 30 + (anything) for sure. The same is true for the other shorter length. So the two **shorter** lengths always pass; only the **longest** can fail. For instance with 7, 10, 15: 7 < 25 ✔ and 10 < 22 ✔ automatically, and 15 < 17 ✔ too. With 12, 14, 18: 12 < 32 ✔, 14 < 30 ✔, 18 < 26 ✔.

**6.** Can you tell, without adding, which lengths will **surely** pass the test?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes: put the lengths in order.** Every length except the longest is smaller than the longest length on its own, so it is certainly smaller than the longest plus another length. Only the longest needs to be checked.

**7.** So, given three lengths, what is the one comparison we need to make?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Compare the longest length with the sum of the other two.** If the longest is **less than** that sum, all three tests pass. If it is equal or more, the test fails.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The triangle inequality.** Three lengths **satisfy the triangle inequality** when **each** length is less than the sum of the other two. 3, 4, 5 satisfies it; 10, 15, 30 does not.
> **Shortcut:** only check the longest length: is **longest < sum of the other two**?
> Lengths that fail the triangle inequality can **never** be the sides of a triangle.

### Does passing the test guarantee a triangle?

Take **4 cm, 5 cm and 8 cm**. The longest is 8, and 8 < 4 + 5 = 9, so the test passes. That means no path is "too long". But does it **prove** the arcs will cross? Let's picture the construction.

Draw the longest side as the base, **AB = 8 cm**. Draw the full circle of radius 4 cm about A and the circle of radius 5 cm about B. The first circle crosses AB at a point X with AX = 4 cm, so **XB = 8 − 4 = 4 cm**.

![Circles of radius 4 cm about A and 5 cm about B crossing at C and C′](assets/img/class7/math/7/circles_458.svg)

The circle about B has radius 5 cm, but X is only 4 cm from B. So X is **inside** the circle about B, while other points of the circle about A (like its far left point) are outside it. A closed curve that has points both inside and outside the other circle must **cross** it. The circles cross at two points C and C′, and ∆ABC has sides 8, 4 and 5. **The triangle exists.**

### Check yourself

**1.** For 4, 5, 8, why did we only check 8 < 4 + 5 and not the other two lengths?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because 4 and 5 are each **smaller than 8 on its own**, so 4 < 5 + 8 and 5 < 4 + 8 are automatically true. Only the longest length can ever fail.

**2.** In the picture, AX = 4 cm and AB = 8 cm. What is BX, and how does it show that the circles cross?

> [!ANSWER]
> **BX = 8 − 4 = 4 cm.** The circle about B has radius 5 cm, which is more than 4 cm, so X lies **inside** the circle about B. The circle about A passes through X (inside) and also goes round to points far on the other side of A (outside), so it must cross the circle about B. Crossing points give the third vertex.

**Question 3.** Which of these can be the sides of a triangle? (Same unit in each set.)

**(a)** 2, 2, 5

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Longest is 5; 2 + 2 = 4, and 5 is more than 4.

**(b)** 3, 4, 6

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Longest is 6; 3 + 4 = 7, and 6 < 7. (The other two checks, 3 < 10 and 4 < 9, pass automatically.)

**(c)** 2, 4, 8

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 2 + 4 = 6, less than 8.

**(d)** 5, 5, 8

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 5 + 5 = 10, more than 8. (An isosceles triangle.)

**(e)** 10, 20, 25

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 10 + 20 = 30, more than 25.

**(f)** 10, 20, 35

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 10 + 20 = 30, less than 35.

**(g)** 24, 26, 28

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 24 + 26 = 50, more than 28.

### Three ways two circles can sit

Is it **always** true that passing the test makes the circles cross? Put the longest length as the base AB and draw circles about A and B with the two shorter lengths as radii. Only three things can happen:

![Three cases: circles touching at one point, circles apart, circles crossing at two points](assets/img/class7/math/7/three_cases.svg)

| Two shorter lengths compared with the longest | What the circles do | Triangle? |
|---|---|---|
| sum **equal** to the longest | **touch** at one point on AB | **No:** the "triangle" is flat |
| sum **less** than the longest | **don't meet** | **No** |
| sum **more** than the longest | **cross** at two points | **Yes** |

**Why the last row:** along the base, one radius starts at A and the other at B. If the two radii add up to more than AB, they **overlap** along the base: AB is made of one full radius plus only **part** of the other. That overlap means each circle pokes into the other, so they cross, and the crossing points give a triangle.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Conclusion.** Three lengths make a triangle **exactly when** they satisfy the triangle inequality.
> - Satisfied (longest < sum of the other two) → the circles cross → a triangle **exists**.
> - Not satisfied (longest ≥ sum of the other two) → **no** triangle.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Equal" fails too.** 3, 6, 9: 3 + 6 = 9 exactly. The circles only **touch**, on the base line itself, so all three "vertices" are on one line. That is the flat picture from Section 7.1, not a triangle.

### Check yourself

**1.** Give three sets of lengths for which the two circles **just touch**.

> [!ANSWER]
> The two shorter lengths must add up **exactly** to the longest: for instance **3, 5, 8** · **2, 4, 6** · **1, 1, 2**. (Any a, b and a + b works.)

**2.** Give three sets of lengths for which the circles **don't meet at all**.

> [!ANSWER]
> The two shorter lengths must add up to **less** than the longest: for instance **3, 4, 8** · **1, 2, 5** · **2, 3, 10**.

**3.** Write a complete procedure for deciding whether three lengths make a triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Put the three lengths in order and pick out the **longest**.
> 2. Add the other two.
> 3. If the longest is **less than** this sum, a triangle exists (and you can construct it with the compass method, using the longest side as the base).
> 4. If the longest is **equal to or more than** the sum, no triangle exists.
>
> **Why step 3 is enough:** each shorter length is already less than the longest alone, so its own test passes without any checking.

**Question 4.** Does a triangle exist with these sides?

**(a)** 1, 100, 100

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Longest is 100; 1 + 100 = 101, more than 100. (A very thin isosceles triangle.)

**(b)** 3, 6, 9

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 3 + 6 = 9, exactly equal to the longest. The circles only touch: the three points lie on a line.

**(c)** 1, 1, 5

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 1 + 1 = 2, much less than 5.

**(d)** 5, 10, 12

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 5 + 10 = 15, more than 12.

**5.** Does an equilateral triangle with sides 50, 50, 50 exist? Does an equilateral triangle exist for **every** side length?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes to both.** For 50, 50, 50: 50 < 50 + 50 = 100. For any side length s: s is always less than s + s = 2s, because 2s is s plus another positive amount. So the triangle inequality is always satisfied, and an equilateral triangle of any size exists.

**Question 6.** Give at least five possible third sides so that a triangle exists (decimals are allowed):

**(a)** sides 1 and 100

> [!ANSWER]
> For instance **99.5, 100, 100.5, 99.4, 100.7.** Every value works if it is **more than 99** and **less than 101** (see the next question for why).

**(b)** sides 5 and 5

> [!ANSWER]
> For instance **1, 3, 4, 4.9, 5** (also 9, 9.9 …). Any value more than 0 and less than 10 works.

**(c)** sides 3 and 7

> [!ANSWER]
> For instance **5, 6, 6.4, 7, 8.** Any value more than 4 and less than 10 works.

**7.** For each part of the previous question, describe **all** possible third sides.

> [!ANSWER]
> The third side must be **more than the difference** of the two given sides and **less than their sum**.
> - **(a)** 1 and 100: strictly between 100 − 1 = **99** and 100 + 1 = **101**.
> - **(b)** 5 and 5: strictly between **0** and **10**.
> - **(c)** 3 and 7: strictly between 7 − 3 = **4** and 3 + 7 = **10**.
>
> **Why "less than the sum":** the third side can't be the longest side and still be as long as the detour round it. **Why "more than the difference":** with 3 and 7, if the third side were 4 or less, then 3 + (third side) would be 7 or less, and the 7 would fail the test.

## 7.5 Triangles from sides and angles

Instead of three sides, we can be given **some sides and some angles**.

### Two sides and the included angle (SAS)

The **included angle** is the angle **between** the two given sides: the corner where they meet.

**Worked example.** Construct ∆ABC with **AB = 5 cm, AC = 4 cm and ∠A = 45°.**

1. Draw the base **AB = 5 cm**.
2. At A, use a protractor to draw a ray making **45°** with AB. Side AC lies along this ray.
3. Mark **C** on the ray so that **AC = 4 cm**.
4. Join **BC**.

![Triangle with AB 5 cm, AC 4 cm and a 45° angle at A](assets/img/class7/math/7/sas_steps.svg)

**Why there is no guessing:** the angle fixes the **direction** of AC and the length fixes **how far** to go, so C has exactly one place.

**(a)** 3 cm, 75°, 7 cm

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw AB = 7 cm.
> 2. At A, draw a ray at 75° to AB with the protractor.
> 3. Mark C on the ray with AC = 3 cm, and join BC.
>
> If you measure, BC comes to about **6.9 cm** (a check that you drew it right).
>
> ![SAS triangle: 7 cm, 75°, 3 cm](assets/img/class7/math/7/sas_a.svg)

**(b)** 6 cm, 25°, 3 cm

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw AB = 6 cm.
> 2. At A, draw a ray at 25°.
> 3. Mark C on it with AC = 3 cm, and join BC.
>
> BC measures about **3.5 cm**: a long, flat triangle, because the angle is small.
>
> ![SAS triangle: 6 cm, 25°, 3 cm](assets/img/class7/math/7/sas_b.svg)

**(c)** 3 cm, 120°, 8 cm

> ![SAS triangle: 8 cm, 120°, 3 cm](assets/img/class7/math/7/sas_c.svg)

**(d)** Is there any set of "two sides and the included angle" for which **no** triangle can be made?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only if the angle is 180° or more.** At 180° the two sides point in opposite directions along one line, so the three points are on a line (flat). For any angle **between 0° and 180°** the two sides make a real corner, and joining their free ends always closes a triangle, whatever the lengths. So, unlike three sides, two sides with an angle between them almost always work.

### Two angles and the included side (ASA)

Now we know **two angles** and the side **between** them (the side that is an arm of both angles).

**Worked example.** Construct ∆ABC with **AB = 5 cm, ∠A = 45° and ∠B = 80°.**

1. Draw the base **AB = 5 cm**.
2. At A draw a ray at **45°**; at B draw a ray at **80°**, on the same side of AB.
3. The rays cross at **C**.

![Rays at 45° from A and 80° from B meet at C](assets/img/class7/math/7/asa_steps.svg)

> 2. Draw a 120° ray at A (leaning back over A) and a 30° ray at B.
> 3. They meet at C, with **30°** at C.
>
> Two equal angles (30° and 30°), and indeed AC = AB = 6 cm: isosceles again.
>
> ![ASA triangle: 120°, 6 cm, 30°](assets/img/class7/math/7/asa_c.svg)

**(d)** Find some "two angles and the included side" for which **no** triangle is possible.

> [!ANSWER]
> For instance **100°, 5 cm, 95°**, or **90°, 4 cm, 90°**: if both angles are 90° or more, the rays from A and B go straight up or lean **away** from each other, so they never meet. Also **40°, 5 cm, 140°** or **40°, 5 cm, 150°**: see below for why.

### When do the rays meet?

Keep **∠A = 40°** and let ∠B grow. At first the ray from B meets line l (the ray from A) at C. As ∠B gets bigger, the ray from B tips further to the right and C moves further away. Eventually the ray from B stops meeting l at all.

![∠A = 40°: at ∠B = 100° the rays meet at C; at ∠B = 140° the ray from B is parallel to l; beyond that it leans away](assets/img/class7/math/7/asa_limit.svg)

The **first** ray from B that doesn't meet l is the one **parallel** to l (call it m). Now AB is a **transversal** of the parallel lines l and m, and ∠A and ∠B are **interior angles on the same side**. For parallel lines these add to **180°**, so ∠B = 180° − 40° = **140°**.

So with ∠A = 40°:
- ∠B **less than 140°** → the rays meet → triangle.
- ∠B **140° or more** → parallel or leaning apart → no triangle.

Notice the **length AB played no part**: making AB longer or shorter just slides B along; the angles decide everything.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two angles can be angles of a triangle exactly when their sum is less than 180°.**
> **Why:** if the sum is exactly 180°, the two rays are parallel (same-side interior angles adding to 180°), so they never meet. If the sum is more than 180°, they lean even further apart. If it is less, they lean towards each other and must meet.

## 7.6 The angle sum of a triangle

**Try this.** Construct a triangle with angles **60° and 70°** on a base of 5 cm and measure the third angle. Now do it again on a base of 7 cm. What do you notice?

Both times the third angle comes out at about **50°**. The bigger base gives a bigger triangle, but the **same shape**. Once two angles are fixed, the length of the base doesn't change the third angle. So there must be a rule linking the third angle to the other two. Let's find it **without** measuring.

**Worked example.** In ∆ABC, ∠B = 50° and ∠C = 70°. Find ∠A.

The trick: through A, draw the line **XY parallel to BC**.

![Line XY through A parallel to BC; the 50° and 70° angles appear again at A](assets/img/class7/math/7/angle_sum_50_70.svg)

1. AB is a transversal of the parallel lines XY and BC. ∠XAB and ∠B are **alternate angles**, so **∠XAB = ∠B = 50°**.
2. AC is also a transversal. ∠YAC and ∠C are alternate angles, so **∠YAC = ∠C = 70°**.
3. At A, the three angles ∠XAB, ∠BAC and ∠YAC sit side by side along the straight line XY, so they add to **180°**: 50° + ∠BAC + 70° = 180°.
4. 120° + ∠BAC = 180°, so **∠BAC = 60°**.

### The angle sum property

Do the same with **any** triangle, using letters instead of numbers.

![Proof: the angles b and c are copied up to A beside angle a, filling the straight line XY](assets/img/class7/math/7/angle_sum_proof.svg)

- Draw XY through A, parallel to BC.
- **∠XAB = ∠B** and **∠YAC = ∠C** (alternate angles).
- ∠XAB, ∠A and ∠YAC together make the straight angle XY, so they add to 180°.
- Replace ∠XAB by ∠B and ∠YAC by ∠C: **∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Angle sum property: the three angles of every triangle add up to 180°.**
> **Why:** a line through the top vertex, parallel to the base, copies the two base angles up beside the top angle (alternate angles are equal). Together the three angles exactly fill a straight line, which is 180°. Nothing in this argument depends on the size of the triangle, so it works for **every** triangle.

At first the third angle seemed to have no simple link with the other two. One clever extra line made the answer obvious. This idea appears in a famous old work called *The Elements*, written by the Greek mathematician **Euclid** around 300 BCE.

**Try this: check it by folding.** Cut out a paper triangle ABC. Fold it so that A touches the base BC at D, the point straight below A (the crease runs parallel to BC). Then fold B and C in so they also land on D.

![Folding a paper triangle so that A, B and C all meet at D on the base](assets/img/class7/math/7/fold_triangle.svg)

**What you will see:** the three corners fit together exactly around D, along the straight base. **Why:** they fill a straight angle with no gaps and no overlaps, so their sum is 180°.

> [!NOTE]
> The angle sum explains the 140° limit from Section 7.5. If ∠A = 40°, then ∠B + ∠C = 140°. Since ∠C must be bigger than 0°, ∠B must be less than 140°.

### Check yourself

**Question 1.** Two angles of a triangle are given. Find the third angle, using a line parallel to the base.

**(a)** 36°, 72°

> [!ANSWER]
> Call the triangle ABC with ∠B = 36° and ∠C = 72°. Draw XY through A parallel to BC.
> 1. ∠XAB = ∠B = 36° and ∠YAC = ∠C = 72° (alternate angles).

>

**(b)** 150°, 15°

**(c)** 90°, 30°

**2.** Can a triangle have all three angles 70°? If two of its angles are 70°, what is the third? If all three angles must be equal, how big is each?

> [!ANSWER]

## 7.7 Exterior angles

Extend side BC beyond C to a point D. The angle **∠ACD**, between the extension and side CA, is called an **exterior angle** of the triangle. The two angles **inside** the triangle at A and B are the **interior opposite angles** of this exterior angle (they are the ones not touching it).

![Triangle with BC extended to D; the exterior angle ACD is 110°](assets/img/class7/math/7/exterior.svg)

**Worked example.** ∠A = 50° and ∠B = 60°. Find ∠ACD.

1. Angle sum: 50° + 60° + ∠ACB = 180°, so ∠ACB = 70°.
2. ∠ACB and ∠ACD together make the straight line BD, so ∠ACD = 180° − 70° = **110°**.

Notice that **110° = 50° + 60°**. Try other values, say ∠A = 40° and ∠B = 75°: ∠ACB = 65° and ∠ACD = 115° = 40° + 75°. It happens every time. Here is why.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles:** ∠ACD = ∠A + ∠B.
> **Why:** ∠A + ∠B + ∠ACB = 180° (angle sum) and ∠ACD + ∠ACB = 180° (straight line). Both ∠A + ∠B and ∠ACD make 180° together with the same ∠ACB, so they must be equal.

**Worked example.** An exterior angle of a triangle is 125° and one interior opposite angle is 48°. Find the other interior opposite angle and the interior angle next to the exterior angle.

1. Exterior angle = sum of the two opposite angles: 125° = 48° + x, so **x = 77°**.
2. The interior angle next to it makes a straight line with the 125°: 180° − 125° = **55°**.
3. Check with the angle sum: 48° + 77° + 55° = 180°. ✔

## 7.8 Altitudes: the heights of a triangle

We talk about the height of a person, a tree or a building. Height is always measured **straight up**, at right angles to the ground. A triangle has heights too.

The **height of vertex A above side BC** is the length of the segment AD drawn from A **perpendicular** to BC. This segment AD is an **altitude** of the triangle. From B and C we get two more altitudes, BE and CF, each perpendicular to the opposite side.

![A triangle with its three altitudes AD, BE and CF](assets/img/class7/math/7/altitudes.svg)

When we say "the height of the triangle", we mean the altitude to whichever side we call the base.

**What if the foot falls outside?** In a triangle with an obtuse angle at B, the perpendicular from A doesn't land on BC at all. Then we **extend CB** beyond B and drop the perpendicular from A onto the extended line.

![Obtuse angle at B: CB is extended and the altitude AD meets the extension](assets/img/class7/math/7/altitude_obtuse.svg)

**Drawing an altitude with a set square.** A ruler alone gives only a rough right angle. For an accurate one:
1. Lay a ruler along the base BC.
2. Put a set square on the ruler, one edge of its right angle resting on the ruler.
3. Slide the set square along the ruler until its upright edge touches A.
4. Draw along the upright edge from A down to BC.

![A set square sliding along a ruler until its upright edge touches A](assets/img/class7/math/7/set_square.svg)

**Why it works:** the set square's corner is exactly 90°, and the ruler keeps that corner sitting on the line BC, so the edge you draw along is perpendicular to BC.

### Check yourself

**1.** Cut out a paper triangle and fold it so that the crease is the altitude from the top vertex to the base. Why must the crease be perpendicular to the base?

> [!ANSWER]
> **How to fold:** fold so that the base **folds onto itself** (one part of the base lies exactly along the other part) and the crease passes through the top vertex.
>
> **Why the crease is perpendicular:** where the crease meets the base, it makes two angles, one on each side. Folding puts one of these angles exactly on top of the other, so they are **equal**. Together they make a straight line, 180°. Two equal angles adding to 180° are each **90°**.

**2.** Can a side of a triangle also be an altitude? Sketch such a triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, when the triangle has a right angle.** If ∠B = 90°, then side AB is already perpendicular to BC, so **AB is the altitude from A to BC**. In the same way, BC is the altitude from C to AB.
>
> ![Right angle at B: side AB is also the altitude from A to BC](assets/img/class7/math/7/right_altitude.svg)
>
> A triangle with one right angle is called a **right-angled triangle**, or simply a **right triangle**.

## 7.9 Types of triangles

**By sides** (we have met these already):

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Equilateral** | all three sides equal |
| **Isosceles** | two sides equal |
| **Scalene** | all three sides different |

**By angles:**

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Acute-angled** | **all three** angles are acute (less than 90°) |
| **Right-angled** | one angle is exactly 90° |
| **Obtuse-angled** | one angle is more than 90° |

![Triangles by sides (equilateral, isosceles, scalene) and by angles (acute, right, obtuse)](assets/img/class7/math/7/types.svg)

> [!NOTE]
> A triangle can have **only one** right angle or obtuse angle. **Why:** two angles of 90° or more would already add up to 180° or more, leaving nothing for the third angle.

### Check yourself

**1.** Could we define an acute-angled triangle as "a triangle with **one** acute angle"? Why not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No, because every triangle has at least two acute angles.** By the note above, at most one angle can be 90° or more, so the other two are always less than 90°. "Has an acute angle" would then describe **every** triangle, right and obtuse ones included, and tell us nothing. So an **acute-angled** triangle must have **all three** angles acute.

### 4. Special triangles

**(a)** Is it possible to construct an **equilateral** triangle that is (i) right-angled, (ii) obtuse-angled?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neither.** An equilateral triangle has three equal angles adding to 180°, so each is **60°** (Section 7.6, Check yourself Q2). Every angle is 60°, which is acute, so it can never have a 90° or obtuse angle. (Every equilateral triangle is acute-angled.)

**(b)** Construct an **isosceles** triangle that is (i) right-angled, (ii) obtuse-angled.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both are possible.**
> - **(i) Right-angled isosceles:** angles **45°, 45°, 90°**. Draw a base, say 4 cm, and rays at 45° from both ends; they meet at a right angle. Check: 45 + 45 + 90 = 180.
> - **(ii) Obtuse-angled isosceles:** angles **30°, 30°, 120°**. Draw a base, say 5 cm, and rays at 30° from both ends; they meet at 120°. Check: 30 + 30 + 120 = 180.
>
> ![A 45°-45°-90° isosceles triangle and a 30°-30°-120° isosceles triangle](assets/img/class7/math/7/ex4.svg)
>
> Equal angles at the base give equal sides going up to the top vertex: the picture is the same on both sides of the middle, so the two slanted sides are equally long.

### 5. Puzzle: shortest path in a box

A spider sits in a bottom corner of a closed box. It wants to reach the opposite top corner (the one farthest away). It can't fly, so it must walk on the faces of the box. What is its shortest path? Take a box of length 4, width 2 and height 1 (any unit).

> [!ANSWER]
> **Unfold the box.** Lay the front face and the top face flat, side by side, so they form one rectangle. On a flat sheet the shortest path between two points is a **straight line** (the tent-and-tree idea).
>
> ![The front and top faces unfolded into a 4 by 3 rectangle; the straight path from spider to target is 5 units](assets/img/class7/math/7/spider_net.svg)
>
> The unfolded rectangle is 4 long and 1 + 2 = 3 high. Measure the straight line across it: **5 units**. Walking along the edges instead would be 4 + 1 + 2 = **7 units**. Then fold the box back up: the straight line becomes a path that crosses the edge between the two faces at a slant.
>
> **Try this with a real cardboard box:** mark the path with a string. Different ways of unfolding (front + top, side + top, …) give different straight lines; the shortest of them is the answer. For this box, the 4 by 3 unfolding gives the shortest, 5 units.

## In a nutshell

- A triangle has 3 vertices, 3 sides and 3 angles; name it ∆ABC in any order of vertices.
- A **circle** is all points at one distance from its centre, so the **crossing of two arcs** is the point at the right distance from both ends. That is how we construct a triangle from **three sides**.
- **Triangle inequality:** each side is less than the sum of the other two, because a straight path is shorter than any detour. Just check **longest < sum of the other two**.
- Three lengths make a triangle **exactly when** they pass this test. Equal (like 3, 6, 9) gives a flat line, not a triangle.
- A third side must be **more than the difference** and **less than the sum** of the other two.
- Triangles can also be built from **two sides and the angle between them** and from **two angles and the side between them**.
- Two angles fit in a triangle only if their **sum is less than 180°**; at exactly 180° the rays are parallel.
- **The angles of every triangle add up to 180°** (draw a line through the top vertex parallel to the base).
- **An exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles.**
- An **altitude** is the perpendicular from a vertex to the opposite side (extended if needed).
- By sides: equilateral, isosceles, scalene. By angles: acute-angled (all three acute), right-angled, obtuse-angled.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 3, 6, 9 is **not** a triangle (the sum must be **more than**, not equal to, the longest side) · you only need to test the **longest** side · two angles adding to exactly 180° make **no** triangle · three 70° angles are impossible · an exterior angle is the sum of the two **opposite** interior angles, not of all three · "acute-angled" means **all three** angles acute · an equilateral triangle can never be right-angled or obtuse · when the altitude misses the base, **extend** the base.
