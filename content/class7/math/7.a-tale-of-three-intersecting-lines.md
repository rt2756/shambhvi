## A triangle is three lines meeting

A **triangle** is the simplest closed shape: **3 vertices** (corner points) and **3 sides** joining them. We write **∆ABC** (the vertices can be listed in any order: ∆BCA is the same triangle). Its angles are ∠CAB, ∠ABC, ∠BCA — written simply **∠A, ∠B, ∠C**.

> [!NOTE]
> **If the three points lie on one straight line, there is no triangle** — the "sides" just lie on top of each other. That idea comes back below as the triangle inequality.

## Constructing an equilateral triangle

**Equilateral triangle** = all three sides equal. To draw one with sides 4 cm:

With just a ruler you'd have to keep guessing where C goes so that **both** AC and BC are 4 cm. A **compass** solves it:

1. Draw the base **AB = 4 cm**.
2. With the compass set to 4 cm, draw an arc centred at **A**. Every point on this arc is exactly 4 cm from A.
3. Draw another 4 cm arc centred at **B**. Every point on it is 4 cm from B.
4. The arcs cross at **C**. Join AC and BC.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why it works:** a circle (or arc) is the set of all points at a fixed distance from its centre. C lies on **both** arcs, so it is 4 cm from A **and** 4 cm from B — exactly what we need.

## Constructing a triangle from three sides

**Sides 4 cm, 5 cm, 6 cm:**
1. Draw base **AB = 4 cm**.
2. Arc of radius **5 cm** from **A** (C must be 5 cm from A).
3. Arc of radius **6 cm** from **B**, crossing the first arc.
4. The crossing point is **C**. Join AC and BC.

> [!TIP]
> You don't need whole circles — only arcs long enough to cross. And you can choose any of the three lengths as the base; taking the **longest** as the base makes the picture easiest to see.

**Isosceles triangle** = **two** equal sides. **Scalene** = all three sides different. (Book constructions: 4, 4, 6 and 1, 5, 5 are isosceles; 3.5, 3.5, 3.5 equilateral; 3, 4, 5 and 4, 6, 8 scalene.)

> [!TIP]
> **Isosceles triangles from a circle:** join the centre to any two points on the circle. Both of those sides are radii, so they are equal. With two equal circles through each other's centres, the centres and a crossing point make an **equilateral** triangle (every side is one radius).

## Can any three lengths make a triangle?

Try **3, 4 and 8 cm**: the arcs of 3 cm and 4 cm from the ends of an 8 cm base **never meet**. Same with 2, 3, 6. Why?

**The shortest path idea:** walking straight from your tent to a tree is always shorter than going via a pole. A straight line is the shortest path between two points.

In any triangle, going **directly** from one vertex to another must be shorter than going the **roundabout** way through the third vertex.

**Can 10, 15, 30 be a triangle?** Suppose it could:
- From B to C directly 10; via A: 15 + 30 = 45 ✔ shorter.
- From A to B directly 15; via C: 30 + 10 = 40 ✔.
- From C to A directly **30**; via B: 10 + 15 = **25** ✘ — the direct path would be *longer* than the roundabout one. Absurd!

So **no triangle** with sides 10, 15, 30.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Triangle inequality: each side must be LESS than the sum of the other two.**
> **Shortcut:** you only need to check the **longest** side. If longest < sum of the two shorter ones, the other two checks pass automatically (each shorter side is already less than the longest side alone).
> 4, 5, 8: is 8 < 4 + 5 = 9? Yes → triangle.

### Why passing the test guarantees a triangle

Put the longest side AB as the base and draw circles from A and B with the two shorter lengths as radii. Three things can happen:

| Two shorter sides vs longest | What the circles do | Triangle? |
|---|---|---|
| sum **less than** longest | don't reach each other | **No** |
| sum **equal to** longest | just touch at one point on AB | **No** (flat — all three points on a line) |
| sum **greater than** longest | overlap and cross at two points | **Yes** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Conclusion:** three lengths make a triangle **if and only if** they satisfy the triangle inequality. If they do, the arcs cross and the construction works; if they don't, no triangle can exist.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Equal" fails too.** 3, 6, 9: 3 + 6 = 9 exactly → the arcs only touch on the base line → a flat line, not a triangle.

**Book Figure it Out:**
- 10, 10, 25 km → 10 + 10 = 20 < 25 → **no** · 5, 10, 20 mm → **no** · 12, 20, 40 cm → **no**
- 2, 2, 5 **no** (4 < 5) · 3, 4, 6 **yes** (7 > 6) · 2, 4, 8 **no** · 5, 5, 8 **yes** (10 > 8) · 10, 20, 25 **yes** (30 > 25) · 10, 20, 35 **no** (30 < 35) · 24, 26, 28 **yes**
- 1, 100, 100 **yes** (101 > 100) · 3, 6, 9 **no** (equal) · 1, 1, 5 **no** · 5, 10, 12 **yes**
- **Equilateral of side 50?** 50 < 50 + 50 → yes. **Any side s works**, since s < 2s always.

**What can the third side be?** (Book.) It must be **more than the difference** and **less than the sum** of the two given sides.
- 1 and 100 → strictly between **99 and 101** (e.g. 99.5, 100, 100.7)
- 5 and 5 → between **0 and 10** (e.g. 1, 3, 4.9, 5, 9)
- 3 and 7 → between **4 and 10** (e.g. 5, 6, 6.4, 7, 8)

> [!NOTE]
> **Why "more than the difference":** with sides 3 and 7, if the third side were 4 or less, then 3 + (third) ≤ 7 — the 7 would fail the test.

## Constructing from sides and angles

### Two sides and the included angle (SAS)

The **included angle** is the one *between* the two given sides. **Construct ∆ABC with AB = 5 cm, AC = 4 cm, ∠A = 45°:**
1. Draw AB = 5 cm.
2. At A, draw a 45° angle with a protractor (that's the direction of AC).
3. Mark C on that arm with AC = 4 cm.
4. Join BC.

> [!NOTE]
> **Is SAS ever impossible?** Only if the "angle" is 180° or more — then the arms don't make a corner. Any angle between 0° and 180°, with any two lengths, gives a triangle.

### Two angles and the included side (ASA)

**Construct ∆ABC with AB = 5 cm, ∠A = 45°, ∠B = 80°:** draw AB, draw a 45° ray at A and an 80° ray at B on the same side; they meet at **C**.

**But do the rays always meet?** If both angles are 90° or more, the rays lean apart and never meet. More interesting: keep ∠A = 40°. How big can ∠B get?

The two rays fail to meet exactly when the ray from B is **parallel** to (or leaning away from) the ray from A. AB is then a transversal of two parallel lines, and same-side interior angles add to 180° → ∠B = 180° − 40° = **140°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two angles can belong to a triangle only if their sum is less than 180°.** If the sum is 180° or more, the rays are parallel or spread apart and never meet — and the length of the included side makes no difference.

**Book Figure it Out:**
- With **30°**: possible with 50°, 90° (anything below 150°); impossible with 150°, 170°.
- With **70°**: possible with 60°, 80°; impossible with 110°, 140°.
- With **54°**: possible with 64°, 90°; impossible with 126°, 154°.
- With **144°**: possible with 20°, 35°; impossible with 36°, 90°.
- Pairs: 35° & 150° **no** (185°) · 70° & 30° **yes** · 90° & 85° **yes** (175°) · 50° & 150° **no**.

## The angle sum property

Draw triangles with ∠B = 50° and ∠C = 70° on bases of 5 cm or 7 cm — ∠A always comes out the same, about **60°**. The third angle depends only on the other two. But **why**?

**Euclid's clever trick:** through vertex A, draw line XY **parallel to BC**.

```
      X ----------- A ----------- Y
                  /   \
                /       \
              /           \
            B ------------- C
```

- AB is a transversal of XY ∥ BC → **∠XAB = ∠B** (alternate angles).
- AC is a transversal → **∠YAC = ∠C** (alternate angles).
- At A, the three angles ∠XAB, ∠BAC, ∠YAC sit along the straight line XY → they add to **180°**.

So **∠B + ∠A + ∠C = 180°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Angle sum property: the three angles of every triangle add up to 180°.**
> **Why it must be true:** the parallel line through the top vertex copies ∠B and ∠C up beside ∠A, and the three together exactly fill a straight angle. This works for every triangle — no measuring needed. (The idea is in Euclid's *Elements*, ~300 BCE.)

> [!TIP]
> **Paper-folding check:** cut out a triangle, fold the top vertex down to touch the base, then fold the other two corners in to meet it. The three corners fit together along a straight line — 180°.

**Book Figure it Out — find the third angle:**
- 36°, 72° → 180 − 108 = **72°** · 150°, 15° → **15°** · 90°, 30° → **60°** · 75°, 45° → **60°**
- **All three 70°?** 210° ≠ 180° → impossible. If two are 70°, the third is **40°**. If all three are equal, each is 180 ÷ 3 = **60°**.
- ∠A = 50° and ∠B = ∠C → ∠B + ∠C = 130° → each **65°**.

> [!NOTE]
> The angle sum explains the 140° limit above: if ∠A = 40°, then ∠B + ∠C = 140°, and ∠C must be more than 0°, so ∠B must be less than 140°.

## Exterior angles

Extend side BC past C to D. The angle **∠ACD** between the extension and side AC is an **exterior angle**.

**Example (book):** ∠A = 50°, ∠B = 60° → ∠ACB = 180 − 110 = 70° → ∠ACD = 180 − 70 = **110°**. Notice 110 = 50 + 60!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior angles opposite it.**
> **Why:** ∠A + ∠B + ∠ACB = 180° (angle sum) and ∠ACD + ∠ACB = 180° (linear pair). Both make 180° with ∠ACB, so ∠ACD = ∠A + ∠B.

## Altitudes — the height of a triangle

The **height** of vertex A above side BC is the length of the **perpendicular** from A to BC. That segment AD is an **altitude**. Every triangle has **three** altitudes, one from each vertex to the opposite side.

- If the foot of the perpendicular would fall **outside** BC (in a triangle with an obtuse angle at B or C), **extend BC** and drop the perpendicular onto the extended line.
- **Paper folding:** fold the triangle so the base folds onto itself and the crease passes through the top vertex. The crease is perpendicular to the base because the two halves of the base land exactly on each other, making two equal angles that together are 180° → each 90°.
- **With a set square:** keep a ruler along BC, slide the set square along it until its upright edge touches A, then draw.

> [!NOTE]
> **In a right-angled triangle, two sides are also altitudes** — each leg is perpendicular to the other. (Book exercise: ∆ABC with ∠B = 90°: AB is the altitude from A to BC.)

## Types of triangles

| By sides | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Equilateral** | all 3 sides equal |
| **Isosceles** | 2 sides equal |
| **Scalene** | all sides different |

| By angles | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Acute-angled** | **all three** angles acute (less than 90°) |
| **Right-angled** | one angle exactly 90° |
| **Obtuse-angled** | one angle more than 90° |

> [!WARNING]
> **"Acute-angled" means ALL angles acute**, not one. Every triangle has at least two acute angles (two angles of 90° or more would already make 180°), so "one acute angle" says nothing.

**Can an equilateral triangle be right-angled or obtuse?** No — its angles are all 60°. **An isosceles triangle can be either:** 45°, 45°, 90° (right) or 30°, 30°, 120° (obtuse).

**Right triangle with ∠B = 90° and AC = 5 cm** — how many? Infinitely many: ∠A can be anything between 0° and 90°, with ∠C = 90° − ∠A.

**Shortest path in a box (puzzle):** a spider at one corner of a box wants the far opposite corner, walking on the faces. Unfold the box flat — on the flat net, the shortest route is a **straight line**.

## In a nutshell

- Arcs/circles give all points at a fixed distance → **compass constructions** for triangles.
- **Triangle inequality:** each side < sum of the other two (check the longest). Passes → triangle exists; fails (even equality) → none.
- Third side lies **between the difference and the sum** of the other two.
- Triangles can be built from **SSS**, **SAS**, **ASA**. Two angles work only if their **sum < 180°**.
- **Angle sum = 180°** (draw a parallel through the top vertex).
- **Exterior angle = sum of the two opposite interior angles.**
- **Altitude** = perpendicular from a vertex to the opposite side (extended if needed).
- Sides: equilateral, isosceles, scalene. Angles: acute (all), right, obtuse.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 3, 6, 9 is NOT a triangle (sum equal, not greater) · just check the longest side · an exterior angle is the sum of the two **opposite** angles, not of all three · three 70° angles can't make a triangle · "acute-angled" needs all three acute · an equilateral triangle can never be right-angled.
