## What this chapter is about

Shabnam is always 3 years older than Aftab. When Aftab is 4, she is 7; when he is 10, she is 13; when he is 23, she is 26. We could make a never-ending table, or we could say it **once, for every age**:

> Shabnam's age = Aftab's age + 3.

This chapter is about writing rules like that in a short, exact way, using **letters that stand for numbers**. You will learn:

- what a **letter-number** and an **algebraic expression** are, and how to turn a rule in words into a **formula**;
- how to **find the value** of an expression when the letters are given numbers;
- how to **simplify** expressions by collecting **like terms** and opening brackets (the same rules you used for numbers in Chapter 2);
- how to use expressions to **describe patterns**, and even to **prove** that a pattern will always hold.

> [!NOTE]
> **The big idea.** A letter-number is just a number we have not fixed yet. So **every rule that works for numbers also works for letter-numbers**: swapping and grouping terms, opening brackets, the distributive property. Nothing new to memorise, only new things to do with old rules.

## 4.1 Letters that stand for numbers

**Worked example.** Shabnam is 3 years older than Aftab. Aftab is now 18. How old is Shabnam? How do we find her age for **any** age of Aftab?

1. Say the rule in words: "add 3 to Aftab's age". So Shabnam is 18 + 3 = **21**.
2. Choose short names. Let **a** stand for Aftab's age and **s** for Shabnam's age.
3. Write the rule with the letters: **s = a + 3**.

| Aftab's age | Shabnam's age |
|---|---|
| 4 | 4 + 3 |
| 10 | 10 + 3 |
| 23 | 23 + 3 |
| a | a + 3 |

If a = 23, we **replace** a by 23: s = 23 + 3 = **26 years**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - A **letter-number** is a letter (or short word) that stands for a number. Here a and s are letter-numbers.
> - An **algebraic expression** is an expression that contains letter-numbers, such as a + 3 or 2 × n.
> - A **formula** is a rule written with letter-numbers, such as s = a + 3.
>
> **Why use letters?** One short line covers **every** case at once. The table above could go on forever; "s = a + 3" is the whole table in five symbols.

The same rule can be turned round. Aftab is 3 years **younger** than Shabnam, so Aftab's age is 3 less than Shabnam's: **a = s − 3**.

**Worked example (matchstick Ls).** Parthiv makes a row of Ls from matchsticks. Each L uses 2 matchsticks.

![Five matchstick Ls in a row: 2 × 5 = 10 matchsticks](assets/img/class7/math/4/ls_pattern.svg)

- 5 Ls need 5 × 2 sticks, 7 Ls need 7 × 2, 45 Ls need 45 × 2.
- In words: number of matchsticks = 2 × number of Ls.
- With n for the number of Ls: **matchsticks = 2 × n**.

**Why it works:** every L is built the same way, so each new L adds exactly 2 more sticks. n Ls means n lots of 2.

**Try this.** Make Ls with matchsticks, ear-buds or pencils. Count the sticks for 1, 2, 3, 4 Ls and write them in a table next to 2 × n. **What you will see:** the table and the formula agree every time. **Why:** the formula is just a short way of saying "2 sticks for each L".

**Worked example (Ketaki's laddus).** A coconut costs ₹35 and 1 kg of jaggery costs ₹60. What does Ketaki pay for 10 coconuts and 5 kg jaggery? What is the formula for c coconuts and j kg jaggery?

1. 10 coconuts cost 10 × 35 = ₹350; 5 kg jaggery costs 5 × 60 = ₹300; total **₹650**.
2. In words: cost = (number of coconuts × 35) + (kg of jaggery × 60).
3. In letters: **c × 35 + j × 60**. This has two terms: c × 35 and j × 60.

When c and j change, the value of the expression changes; the **rule** stays the same.

**Worked example (perimeter).** A square has 4 equal sides. If each side is q, the perimeter is **4 × q**. For q = 7 cm, the perimeter is 4 × 7 = 28 cm.

> [!TIP]
> **How to write a formula, every time:**
> 1. Say the rule **in words**.
> 2. Try it with one or two **actual numbers**.
> 3. Only then replace the words by **letters**.
>
> If you cannot say it in words, you are not ready to write it in letters.

## 4.2 The old rules still work

In Chapter 2 we learnt to read arithmetic expressions by splitting them into **terms**, to **swap and group** terms, to open **brackets** (a minus in front changes the sign of every term inside), and the **distributive property**. Here is a quick warm-up.

**Worked example.** 23 − 10 × 2. The terms are 23 and −10 × 2. Work out the second term first: 23 + (−20) = **3**.

**Worked example.** 83 + 28 − 13 + 32. Terms 83, 28, −13, 32. Group the friendly ones: (83 − 13) + (28 + 32) = 70 + 60 = **130**.

**Worked example.** 68 − (18 + 13), two ways:
- Bracket first: 68 − 31 = **37**.
- Open the bracket: 68 − 18 − 13 = 50 − 13 = **37**.

Algebraic expressions follow exactly the same rules, because the letters **are** numbers. When we put a number in place of each letter, an algebraic expression becomes an arithmetic expression with a value.

### Check yourself

**1.** Find the values: (a) 34 − 14 + 20 (b) 42 + 15 − (8 − 7) (c) 7 × 4 + 9 × 6 (d) 20 + 8 × (16 − 6)

> [!ANSWER]
> - (a) 34 − 14 = 20; 20 + 20 = **40**.
> - (b) Bracket first: 8 − 7 = 1. Then 42 + 15 − 1 = **56**.
> - (c) Two terms: 28 + 54 = **82**.
> - (d) Bracket first: 16 − 6 = 10. Then the term 8 × 10 = 80. So 20 + 80 = **100** (not 28 × 10: the 8 × (…) is one term, added to 20).

## 4.3 Dropping the × sign

Look at the multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, … The 3rd number is 4 × 3, the 29th is 4 × 29, and the **nth** is **4 × n**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **We write 4 × n as 4n.** Number first, then the letter, with no sign between.
> - 7k means 7 × k. If k = 4, 7k = 7 × 4 = 28.
> - 5m + 3 means 5 × m + 3. If m = 2, it is 5 × 2 + 3 = **13**.
>
> **Why we can only do this with letters:** "4n" cannot be misread. But between two numbers we must keep the sign: "43" already means forty-three, not 4 × 3.

> [!WARNING]
> **When you put a number in place of a letter, put the multiplication back.** If d = 6, then 3d is 3 × 6 = 18, **not** "36". And if a = −4, write it in brackets: 10 − a = 10 − (−4) = 14.

In each item a letter has been replaced by a number. Is the value right? If not, say what went wrong and correct it.

> [!ANSWER]

**2.** If d = 6, then 3d = 36.

> **Wrong.** 3s − 2 has two terms, 3 × s and −2. So it is 21 − 2 = **19**. The mistake: 3 × (7 − 2) = 15, as if there were a bracket.

**4.** If r = 8, then 2r + 1 = 29.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** 2r + 1 = 2 × 8 + 1 = **17**. The mistake: 2r was read as "28", then 1 added.

**5.** If j = 5, then 2j = 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Correct.** 2j = 2 × 5 = 10.

**6.** If m = −6, then 3(m + 1) = 19.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** Bracket first: m + 1 = −6 + 1 = −5. Then 3 × (−5) = **−15**. The mistake: the minus sign of m was lost and the bracket was ignored (3 × 6 + 1 = 19).

**7.** If f = 3 and g = 1, then 2f − 2g = 2.

**8.** If t = 4 and b = 3, then 2t + b = 24.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** 2t + b has terms 2 × t and b: 8 + 3 = **11**. The mistake: everything was multiplied, 2 × 4 × 3 = 24, though b is **added**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** Bracket first: 3 − n = 3 − 6 = −3. Then 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = **8**. Another way: open the bracket, changing both signs inside: h − 3 + n = 5 − 3 + 6 = 8. The answer 4 comes from a lost sign somewhere (say 5 − 3 − 6 = −4, then dropping the minus).

## 4.4 Simplifying expressions

**Worked example (perimeter of a rectangle).** Walk round a rectangle of length l and breadth b: the perimeter is

p = l + b + l + b.

The terms are numbers, so we may add them in any order: l + l + b + b. And l + l is 2 × l = 2l, b + b = 2b. So **p = 2l + 2b**.

Check with l = 3, b = 4: l + b + l + b = 3 + 4 + 3 + 4 = 14 and 2l + 2b = 6 + 8 = 14. ✓

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two expressions are equal if they give the same value for every choice of numbers for the letters.** To **simplify** an expression is to rewrite it in a shorter form that is still equal. We get the shorter form using only the rules for numbers, so the two forms **must** agree for every choice.

**Worked example (pencils and erasers).** A shop sells pencils at c rupees each and erasers at d rupees each.

| | Day 1 | Day 2 | Day 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pencils (price c) | 5 | 3 | 10 |
| Erasers (price d) | 4 | 6 | 1 |

1. Pencil money: 5c on Day 1, 3c on Day 2, 10c on Day 3. Total 5c + 3c + 10c.
2. This is "c added 5 times, then 3 times, then 10 times", so c is added 5 + 3 + 10 = 18 times: **18c**.
3. Eraser money: 4d + 6d + 1d = **11d**.
4. Total: **18c + 11d**. This cannot be shortened: c and d are different prices.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why 5c + 3c + 10c = 18c:** this is the distributive property, 5 × c + 3 × c + 10 × c = (5 + 3 + 10) × c. Think of 5 bags of c marbles, then 3 more bags, then 10 more: 18 bags of c marbles.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Like terms** have the same letter-number part: 5c, 3c, 10c; or 12n and −4n.
> - **Unlike terms** have different letter-numbers: 18c and 11d.
> - **Add like terms by adding their number parts:** 5c + 3c + 10c = 18c. Unlike terms stay separate.
>
> **Why unlike terms cannot be joined:** 18 pencils' worth of money and 11 erasers' worth do not make "29 of" anything, because a pencil and an eraser have different prices.

## 4.5 The distributive property as an area picture

A rectangle of height v is split into two rectangles of widths 4 and 3.

![A rectangle of height v split into widths 4 and 3: areas 4v and 3v, total 7v](assets/img/class7/math/4/area_split.svg)

- Add the two parts: 4v + 3v.
- Or use the whole width, 4 + 3 = 7: area 7v.

The same rectangle, two ways of counting, so **4v + 3v = 7v**.

Now take rectangle ABCD with AB = 12 and height n, and cut off the strip EBCF of width 4.

![Rectangle ABCD, 12 by n, with strip EBCF of width 4 removed, leaving AEFD of area 8n](assets/img/class7/math/4/area_cut.svg)

- Big rectangle minus strip: 12n − 4n.
- Or use the width of AEFD directly, 12 − 4 = 8: area 8n.

So **12n − 4n = 8n**.

## 4.6 Brackets with a minus in front

**Worked example (renting furniture).** A shop rents a chair for ₹40 and a table for ₹75. On return it pays back ₹6 per chair and ₹10 per table. What does it cost to rent x chairs and y tables?

1. Paid at the start: **40x + 75y**. Paid back: **6x + 10y**.
2. Cost = what you pay − what comes back = (40x + 75y) − (6x + 10y).
3. Open the second bracket. A minus in front means "take away the **whole** bracket", so take away each term: 40x + 75y − 6x − 10y.
4. Group like terms: (40x − 6x) + (75y − 10y) = **34x + 65y**.

**Check with numbers:** x = 1, y = 1: (40 + 75) − (6 + 10) = 115 − 16 = 99, and 34 + 65 = 99. ✓

> [!NOTE]
> **Same rule as Chapter 2:** − (6x + 10y) = −6x − 10y. So we could also write (40x + 75y) + (−6x − 10y). Taking away a sum means taking away each part.

**Worked example (Charu's quiz).** In three quiz rounds Charu scored 7p − 3q, 8p − 4q and 6p − 2q, where p is the score for a correct answer and q is the penalty for a wrong one.

- **Meaning:** 7p − 3q means 7 correct answers and 3 wrong answers.
- **Round 1 with p = 4, q = 1:** 7 × 4 − 3 × 1 = 28 − 3 = **25**.
- **Total:** (7p − 3q) + (8p − 4q) + (6p − 2q). With a + in front, brackets can just be removed. Group like terms: (7 + 8 + 6)p − (3 + 4 + 2)q = **21p − 9q**.

**Worked example.** Simplify 4(x + y) − y.
By the distributive property 4(x + y) = 4x + 4y. So 4x + 4y − y = 4x + (4 − 1)y = **4x + 3y**.

## 4.7 Equal or not?

Are **5u** and **5 + u** equal? 5u means 5 **times** u; 5 + u means 5 **more than** u. Try numbers:

| u | 2 | 5 | 8 | 11 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5u | 10 | 25 | 40 | 55 |
| 5 + u | 7 | 10 | 13 | 16 |

They give different values, so they are **not** equal. (One disagreement is enough to show two expressions are not equal. To show they **are** equal, we need a reason that works for every number, not just a few checks.)

> [!WARNING]
> **10y − 3 is not 10(y − 3).** 10y − 3 means "3 less than 10 times y". 10(y − 3) means "10 times (3 less than y)" = 10y − 30. With y = 2: 17 against −10.

**Worked example (adding a picture of numbers).** Some of the numbers in this picture are unknown, so they are written as letter-numbers. Find the sum of all of them.

| 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| | r | s | |
| | r | s | |
| 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 |

There are eight 3s, two r's and two s's. Three ways to add:
1. Row by row: (4 × 3) + (r + s) + (r + s) + (4 × 3).
2. Like terms together: (8 × 3) + (r + r) + (s + s).
3. Top half, doubled: 2 × (4 × 3 + r + s).

All three simplify to **2r + 2s + 24**. Different ways of counting, same total.

## 4.9 Formula detective

A **number machine** takes two numbers a (left) and b (right), does the same operations every time, and gives one answer.

| a | b | answer |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 2 | 8 |
| 8 | 1 | 15 |
| 9 | 11 | 7 |
| 10 | 10 | 10 |
| 6 | 4 | ? |

**Worked example.** Find the machine's formula.
1. Guess from the first row: 8 could be 5 + 2 + 1, or 2 × 5 − 2, or …
2. Test each guess on **every** row. "a + b + 1" gives 8 + 1 + 1 = 10 for the second row, not 15. ✗
3. "2a − b": 2 × 5 − 2 = 8 ✓, 16 − 1 = 15 ✓, 18 − 11 = 7 ✓, 20 − 10 = 10 ✓.
4. So the formula is **2a − b**, and the last answer is 2 × 6 − 4 = **8**.

> [!TIP]
> A guess that fits **one** row proves nothing. A formula is found only when it fits **every** row.

**Try this.** Invent your own number machine (for example "a × b − a"), write four input pairs with their answers, and challenge a friend to find the formula. **What you will see:** your friend needs several rows to be sure. **Why:** many formulas fit one or two rows; only the true one fits them all.

## 4.10 Repeating patterns: the saree border

A saree border repeats three designs A, B, C over and over.

![A saree border: designs A, B, C repeating at positions 1 to 9](assets/img/class7/math/4/saree.svg)

- C is at positions 3, 6, 9, …: the multiples of 3. The nth C is at **3n**.
- Each B sits just before a C: positions 2, 5, 8, 11, … The nth B is at **3n − 1**.
- Each A sits two before a C: positions 1, 4, 7, … The nth A is at **3n − 2**.

**Which design is at position 122?** Divide by 3 and look at the **remainder**:

| Position | ÷ 3 | Remainder | Design |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99 | 33 | 0 | C |
| 122 | 40 | 2 | B |
| 148 | 49 | 1 | A |

> [!NOTE]
> **Why the remainder works:** after every complete group of 3 (A B C) the pattern starts again. Remainder 1 means one step into a new group (A), remainder 2 means two steps (B), remainder 0 means the group just finished (C).

## 4.11 Patterns in a calendar

Pick any 2 × 2 square of dates, say 12, 13 / 19, 20. Add the diagonals: 12 + 20 = 32 and 13 + 19 = 32. Equal! Will this happen in **every** 2 × 2 square, even in a calendar that goes on forever?

We cannot check infinitely many squares one by one. Instead, call the top-left number **a** and describe the others in words:
- the number to the right is 1 more (the next day): **a + 1**;
- the number below is 7 more (a week later): **a + 7**;
- the number diagonally below-right is 8 more: **a + 8**.

| | |
|---|---|
| a | a + 1 |
| a + 7 | a + 8 |

Diagonal sums: a + (a + 8) = **2a + 8**, and (a + 1) + (a + 7) = **2a + 8**.

Both diagonals give 2a + 8 **whatever a is**, so the sums are equal in every 2 × 2 square.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Checking examples is not proving.** Ten examples that work only tell us about those ten squares. Writing the numbers with a letter covers **every** square at once, so it is a **proof**.

**Try this.** Take a real calendar. Pick three different 2 × 2 squares and add their diagonals. Then work out 2a + 8 using each top-left number a. **What you will see:** both diagonal sums always equal 2a + 8. **Why:** the shape of a calendar (7 days per row) fixes how the four numbers are related, whatever a is.

## 4.12 Matchstick triangles

![Rows of matchstick triangles: Step 1 has 3 sticks, Step 2 has 5, Step 3 has 7, Step 4 has 9](assets/img/class7/math/4/triangles.svg)

Step 1 has 1 triangle, Step 2 has 2 triangles, and so on. Each new triangle needs only **2 more** sticks, because it shares one side with the triangle before it.

| Step | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Matchsticks | 3 | 5 | 7 | 9 | 11 | 13 |
| As a sum | 3 | 3 + 2 | 3 + 2 + 2 | 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 | 3 + 2 × 4 | 3 + 2 × 5 |

**Worked example.** Find the matchsticks for Step y.
1. In words: start with 3, then add a 2 for every step after the first.
2. Step 10 has nine 2s: 3 + 2 × 9. Step 11 has ten 2s: 3 + 2 × 10.
3. Step y has (y − 1) twos: **3 + 2 × (y − 1)**.
4. Simplify: 3 + 2y − 2 = **2y + 1**.

**Why 2y + 1 also makes sense on its own:** the very first stick on the left is extra; after that, **every** triangle brings 2 sticks. So y triangles need 2y sticks, plus the 1 first stick.

**Another way to see it.** Sort the sticks by direction (orange flat ones, blue slanting ones in the picture). Step y has **y** flat sticks and **y + 1** slanting sticks. Total y + (y + 1) = 2y + 1 again.

**Try this.** Build Steps 1 to 5 with matchsticks or toothpicks. For each step, count flat and slanting sticks separately. **What you will see:** flat = step number, slanting = one more. **Why:** each triangle has one flat side, and the slanting sides sit between the triangles, plus one at each end, which is always one more than the number of triangles.

| 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 |
| 13 | 14 | 15 | 16 |

**(a)** Give expressions for all the numbers in each column.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each row adds 4 to the number above. Row r starts after (r − 1) full rows of 4, that is after 4(r − 1) numbers.
> - Column 1: **4(r − 1) + 1**
> - Column 2: **4(r − 1) + 2**
> - Column 3: **4(r − 1) + 3**
> - Column 4: **4(r − 1) + 4**, which simplifies to 4r (the multiples of 4).

**(b)** In which row and column do (i) 124, (ii) 147, (iii) 201 appear?

> [!ANSWER]
> Write each number as 4 × (r − 1) + c, where c is from 1 to 4.
> - (i) 124 = 4 × 30 + 4 → r − 1 = 30: **row 31, column 4**.
> - (ii) 147 = 4 × 36 + 3 → **row 37, column 3**.
> - (iii) 201 = 4 × 50 + 1 → **row 51, column 1**.

**(c)** What number is in row r, column c?

> [!ANSWER]
> **4(r − 1) + c.** **Why:** the rows before row r hold 4(r − 1) numbers, and then we count c more along row r.

**(d)** Look at where the multiples of 3 appear. What patterns do you see?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Multiples of 3: 3 (col 3), 6 (col 2), 9 (col 1), 12 (col 4), 15 (col 3), 18 (col 2), … The columns repeat **3, 2, 1, 4**. **Why:** moving on by 3 is "one row down, one column left", and wrapping round after column 1.
> - Other patterns: column 4 holds the multiples of 4; even numbers are only in columns 2 and 4; odd numbers only in columns 1 and 3.

## In a nutshell

- A **letter-number** stands for a number; an **algebraic expression** contains letter-numbers; a **formula** is a rule written with them.
- Say the rule **in words** first, test it on numbers, then write it with letters.
- **4n means 4 × n.** Put the × back (and brackets round negatives) when you replace letters by numbers.
- **Like terms** (same letter part) add by adding their numbers: 5c + 3c = 8c. **Unlike terms** stay separate.
- All the old rules work: swap and group terms, a **minus in front of a bracket changes every sign inside**, and the **distributive property** 4(x + y) = 4x + 4y.
- Two expressions are **equal** if they agree for every value of the letters; one disagreement shows they are not.
- For a repeating pattern of length k, the **remainder** on dividing by k tells you the place in the repeat.
- A letter-number lets us **prove** a pattern for every case at once (calendar diagonals: both 2a + 8).

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 3d with d = 6 is 18, not 36 · 10 − a with a = −4 is 14 · 3a + 2b is not 5 (or 5ab) · 6(p + 2) is 6p + 12, not 6p + 8 · −(2 − 6z) is −2 + 6z · 7p − p is 6p, not 7p · 5u is not 5 + u, and 10y − 3 is not 10(y − 3) · the start-up time is added once: 10 + 8y, not (10 + 8)y · a customer who bought both flowers still gets one flag.
