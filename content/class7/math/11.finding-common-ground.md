## The greatest of all — HCF

**Sameeksha's floor (book):** a room 12 ft × 16 ft is to be covered with equal square tiles, whole feet on a side, using as few tiles as possible.

- To fit the 12 ft side exactly, the tile side must be a **factor of 12**: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.
- To fit the 16 ft side, a **factor of 16**: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16.
- So it must be a **common factor**: 1, 2 or 4. Bigger tiles mean fewer tiles → **4 ft**.
- Number of tiles: (12 ÷ 4) × (16 ÷ 4) = 3 × 4 = **12 tiles**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The Highest Common Factor (HCF)** of two or more numbers is the largest number that divides all of them exactly. It is also called the **Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)**. HCF(12, 16) = 4.

> [!NOTE]
> **What if tiles could have fractional sides?** The side s still needs 12 ÷ s and 16 ÷ s to be whole numbers. A side bigger than 4 (like 4.5 or 6) fails for one of the walls, so 4 ft is still the largest. The answer doesn't change.

**Lekhana's rice (book):** 84 kg from one farm, 108 kg from another, packed in equal whole-kg bags (each bag from one farm only), as few bags as possible.
Common factors of 84 and 108: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 → choose the **largest, 12 kg** → 7 + 9 = **16 bags**.

**Jump Jackpot (book):** the longest jump from 0 that lands on both treasures is a number that divides both → the **HCF**: (14, 30) → **2** · (7, 11) → **1** · (30, 50) → **10** · (28, 42) → **14**.

## Primes and prime factorisation

A **prime** is a number greater than 1 whose only factors are 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …). Every number can be written as a **product of primes** — keep splitting composite factors until only primes remain.

90 = 3 × 30 = 3 × 2 × 15 = 3 × 2 × 3 × 5. Start differently (90 = 2 × 45 = 2 × 3 × 15 = …) and you reach **the same primes**, maybe in another order.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every whole number greater than 1 has exactly one prime factorisation** (apart from the order of the factors). That is what makes it a reliable "fingerprint" of the number.

**The division method:** divide by a prime, write the quotient below, repeat until you reach 1 (or a prime).

```
 3 | 105         2 | 1200
 5 |  35         2 |  600
   |   7         2 |  300
                 2 |  150
105 = 3 × 5 × 7  3 |   75
                 5 |   25
                   |    5
         1200 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 × 5
```

> [!WARNING]
> **Anshu's conjecture — "a bigger number has a longer prime factorisation" — is false.** Counterexample: 96 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 (6 primes) but the bigger 121 = 11 × 11 (only 2). A **conjecture** is a claim not yet proved; one **counterexample** is enough to disprove it.

## Every factor is a "subpart" of the prime factorisation

840 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 × 7. **Is 28 = 2 × 2 × 7 a factor?** Regroup: 840 = (2 × 2 × 7) × (2 × 3 × 5) = 28 × 30. **Yes.**
- 14 = 2 × 7 → yes (a subpart). 8 = 2 × 2 × 2 → yes. **27 = 3 × 3 × 3 → no**: 840 has only one 3.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A number is a factor of N exactly when its prime factors can be found inside N's prime factorisation** (each prime used no more often than it appears in N).
> **Why:** if the primes are "inside", the leftover primes multiply to the other factor. If you need a prime more times than N has it, nothing can supply the extra.

**All factors of 225 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 5:** take every possible subpart: 1; 3, 5; 9, 15, 25; 45, 75; 225 → **1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25, 45, 75, 225**.

**Book Figure it Out:**
- 90: **1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 15, 18, 30, 45, 90**
- 105: **1, 3, 5, 7, 15, 21, 35, 105**
- 132: **1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 11, 12, 22, 33, 44, 66, 132**
- 360 has 24 factors and 840 has 32 factors.

> [!TIP]
> **Count factors without listing them:** 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5 means you can use 2 zero to three times (4 choices), 3 zero to two times (3 choices), 5 zero or once (2 choices) → 4 × 3 × 2 = **24** factors. 840 = 2³ × 3 × 5 × 7 → 4 × 2 × 2 × 2 = **32**.

## HCF using prime factorisation

Common factors must be subparts of **both** factorisations.

**45 = 3 × 3 × 5 and 75 = 3 × 5 × 5.** Common subparts: 3, 5, 3 × 5 → common factors 1, 3, 5, 15 → **HCF 15**.
**112 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 7 and 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7** → HCF = 2 × 2 × 7 = **28**.
**96 = 2⁵ × 3 and 275 = 5 × 5 × 11** → nothing in common → **HCF 1**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **HCF: take each prime that appears in ALL the numbers, as many times as it appears in the number where it appears LEAST.**
> 30 = 2 × 3 × 5, 72 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 → one 2 and one 3 → **HCF 6**.
> 225 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 5, 750 = 2 × 3 × 5 × 5 × 5 → one 3, two 5s → **HCF 75**.
> **Why the minimum:** a common factor must fit inside *each* number, so it can't use a prime more often than the number that has fewest copies of it.

**Book Figure it Out:**
- HCF(50, 60) = **10** · HCF(140, 275) = **5** · HCF(77, 725) = **1** · HCF(370, 592) = **74** · HCF(81, 243) = **81**
- HCF(24, 180) = **12** · HCF(42, 75, 24) = **3** · HCF(240, 378) = **6** · HCF(400, 2500) = **100** · HCF(300, 800) = **100**
- **72 = 6 × 12 and 144 = 8 × 18** — does that show they share no factor? **No!** Those are not prime factorisations. In primes: 72 = 2³ × 3², 144 = 2⁴ × 3² → HCF 72.

## The least common multiple — LCM

**Torans (book):** Anshu uses 6 cm strips, Guna 8 cm strips. Equal-length torans must be a **common multiple** of 6 and 8: 24, 48, 72, … The shortest is **24 cm**.

**Free gajak (book):** free every Monday (every 7 days); Kabamai visits every 10 days. Both happen together after a common multiple of 7 and 10 — first after **70 days**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)** of two or more numbers is the smallest number that is a multiple of all of them.
> There is **no** largest common multiple — you can always add another LCM.

**Idli-Vada (book):** "idli-vada" is first called at the LCM: (4, 6) → **12** · (7, 11) → **77** · (14, 30) → **210** · (15, 55) → **165**.

### LCM using prime factorisation

A multiple of 36 contains all of 36's primes: 648 = 36 × 18 = (2 × 2 × 3 × 3) × (2 × 3 × 3).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **LCM: take every prime that appears in ANY of the numbers, as many times as it appears in the number where it appears MOST.**
> 14 = 2 × 7, 35 = 5 × 7 → 2 × 5 × 7 = **70**.
> 96 = 2⁵ × 3, 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5 → 2⁵ × 3² × 5 = **1440**.
> **Why the maximum:** a common multiple must contain each number's full factorisation; using the most copies of each prime covers both, and using any fewer would leave one number out. Any more would not be the *lowest*.

**Book Figure it Out:** LCM(30, 72) = **360** · LCM(36, 54) = **108** · LCM(105, 195, 65) = **1365** · LCM(222, 370) = **1110**.

## Patterns and general statements

**When is the HCF one of the two numbers?** HCF(6, 18) = 6 — whenever **one number is a factor of the other**. In algebra: **HCF(n, 5n) = n**; for m the other number could be 2m, 3m, …; for 7k it could be 14k, 21k, or k.

**General statements (book):**
| Pair | HCF | LCM |
|---|---|---|
| two consecutive numbers (8, 9) | **1** | their **product** |
| two consecutive odd numbers (7, 9) | **1** | their product |
| two consecutive even numbers (8, 10) | **2** | product ÷ 2 |
| two even numbers | **at least 2** | — |
| two co-prime numbers | **1** | their **product** |
| one a multiple of the other (3, 24) | the smaller | the **larger** (LCM(3, 24) = 24) |
| two multiples of 3 | multiple of 3 | multiple of 3 |

> [!NOTE]
> **Why consecutive numbers have HCF 1:** any common factor would also divide their difference, which is 1. Consecutive odd (or even) numbers differ by 2, so their HCF divides 2 — it's 1 for odd numbers, 2 for even ones.

**Doubling both numbers doubles the HCF.** 270 and 50 → HCF 10; 540 and 100 → HCF 20. Each number gains one extra 2, and that 2 joins the common part.

**Multiples of the same number:** 14 × 6 and 14 × 9 have HCF **42**, not 14 — because 6 and 9 still share a 3. HCF(18 × 10, 18 × 15) = **90** · HCF(10 × 38, 10 × 21) = **10** · HCF(5 × 13, 5 × 20) = **5** · HCF(12 × 16, 12 × 20) = **48**. The HCF equals the common multiplier exactly when the other two multipliers are **co-prime**.

## A pretty procedure: HCF and LCM together

Divide **both** numbers by a common factor, write both quotients, repeat until the two numbers share nothing.

```
 2 | 84, 180         2 | 630, 770
 2 | 42,  90         5 | 315, 385
 3 | 21,  45         7 |  63,  77
   |  7,  15           |   9,  11
```

- **HCF = product of the divisors on the left.** HCF(84, 180) = 2 × 2 × 3 = **12**. HCF(630, 770) = 2 × 5 × 7 = **70**.
- **LCM = divisors on the left × the numbers left at the bottom.** LCM(84, 180) = 12 × 7 × 15 = **1260**. LCM(630, 770) = 70 × 9 × 11 = **6930**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why it works:** the left column collects exactly the primes the two numbers share — the HCF. The bottom row is what's left of each number, with nothing in common. The LCM needs the shared part once plus both leftovers.
> **You may divide by any common factor, not just primes** Guna divides 300 and 150 by 50 straight away (→ 6, 3), then by 3 (→ 2, 1): HCF = 50 × 3 = **150**, LCM = 150 × 2 × 1 = **300**.

Try: HCF(90, 150) = **30**, LCM = **450** · HCF(84, 132) = **12**, LCM = **924**.

## HCF × LCM = product of the two numbers

105 = 3 × 5 × 7 and 95 = 5 × 19. LCM = 3 × 5 × 7 × 19, and 105 × 95 = 3 × 5 × 5 × 7 × 19 = LCM × **5** — and 5 is the HCF!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For two numbers: HCF × LCM = the product of the numbers.**
> **Why:** the shared primes appear twice in the product (once from each number). The LCM takes them once and the HCF takes them once — between them, twice. The non-shared primes appear once in the product and once in the LCM. So HCF × LCM uses exactly the same primes as the product.
> Checks: 45 × 105 = 315 × 15 · 275 × 352 = 8800 × 11 · 222 × 370 = 1110 × 74.

> [!WARNING]
> **This only works for TWO numbers.** For three numbers it fails: HCF(2, 4, 8) × LCM(2, 4, 8) = 2 × 8 = 16, but 2 × 4 × 8 = 64.

**Is the LCM ever bigger than the product?** No — the product itself is always a common multiple, so the *lowest* one can't be bigger.

> **Adding fractions = an LCM problem.** The smallest common denominator is the LCM of the denominators — that's the "efficient" way the book hints at.

## In a nutshell

- **HCF** = biggest common factor; **LCM** = smallest common multiple.
- Every number has **one** prime factorisation; every factor is a **subpart** of it.
- HCF: shared primes, **fewest** copies. LCM: all primes, **most** copies.
- Ladder method: left column → HCF; left column × bottom row → LCM.
- **HCF × LCM = product** (for two numbers).
- Consecutive or co-prime numbers: HCF 1, LCM = product.
- "Biggest equal pieces/tiles/bags" → HCF. "Next time together / shortest common length" → LCM.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 6 × 12 is not a prime factorisation · HCF can't be bigger than the smaller number; LCM can't be smaller than the bigger number · HCF × LCM = product only for **two** numbers · co-prime numbers needn't be prime (8 and 9) · 1 is not a prime.
