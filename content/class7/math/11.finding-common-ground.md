## What this chapter is about

Two strips of cloth, two piles of rice, two friends visiting a shop on different days: many problems ask what two numbers have **in common**.

- The **biggest number that divides both** is their **HCF** (Highest Common Factor).
- The **smallest number that both divide** is their **LCM** (Lowest Common Multiple).

Listing factors and multiples works for small numbers but gets slow and easy to get wrong. So we learn a better tool: **prime factorisation**, writing a number as a product of primes. With it, the HCF and LCM can be *read off* almost at a glance, and you will see **why** the methods work.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** A **factor** of a number divides it exactly (3 is a factor of 12). A **multiple** of a number is that number times a counting number (24 is a multiple of 12). A **prime** is a number greater than 1 whose only factors are 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …). A number greater than 1 that is not prime is **composite**. Two numbers are **co-prime** if their only common factor is 1.

## 11.1 The greatest of all

**Worked example (tiles).** Sameeksha's room is 12 ft by 16 ft. She wants to cover the floor with equal **square tiles** whose side is a whole number of feet, using **as few tiles as possible**. What size should she buy?

1. Tiles must fit the 12 ft side exactly, so the side must be a **factor of 12**: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.
2. They must also fit the 16 ft side, so the side must be a **factor of 16**: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16.
3. So the side must be a **common factor**: 1, 2 or 4 ft.
4. Fewest tiles means biggest tiles: **4 ft**.

![A 16 ft by 12 ft room covered by 4 ft square tiles: 4 across and 3 down](assets/img/class7/math/11/room_tiles.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The **Highest Common Factor (HCF)** of two or more numbers is the largest number that is a factor of all of them. It is also called the **Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)**. Here HCF(12, 16) = 4.

**Worked example (rice bags).** Lekhana buys 84 kg of rice from one farm and 108 kg from another. She packs it in bags that all weigh the same whole number of kg, with each bag holding rice from only one farm.

1. Factors of 84: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 12, 14, 21, 28, 42, 84.
2. Factors of 108: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 27, 36, 54, 108.
3. Common factors: **1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12**. Any of these bag sizes works for both farms.

**Try this.** On squared paper, draw a 16 × 12 rectangle (one square = 1 ft). Try to cover it with squares of side 2, 3, 4 and 6. **What you will see:** 2 and 4 fit exactly; 3 fits the 12 side but leaves a strip of 1 on the 16 side; 6 fits neither way. **Why:** a square fits a side exactly only when its side length is a factor of that side, so it must be a factor of both 12 and 16.

### Check yourself

**1.** How many 4 ft tiles does Sameeksha need?

> [!ANSWER]
> **12 tiles.**
> 1. Along the 16 ft side: 16 ÷ 4 = 4 tiles.
> 2. Along the 12 ft side: 12 ÷ 4 = 3 tiles.
> 3. Total: 4 × 3 = **12**. (Check by area: 16 × 12 = 192 sq ft, and each tile is 4 × 4 = 16 sq ft; 192 ÷ 16 = 12 ✔.)

**2.** Why should she pick the **largest** common factor?

> [!ANSWER]
> The floor area is fixed (192 sq ft). Number of tiles = floor area ÷ area of one tile. A **bigger** tile has a bigger area, so we divide by a bigger number and need **fewer** tiles. With 2 ft tiles she would need 8 × 6 = 48; with 1 ft tiles, 192. So the largest common factor gives the fewest tiles.

**3.** What if the tile side did not have to be a whole number of feet? Could she use a bigger tile?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No: 4 ft is still the biggest.**
> 1. The tiles must fit exactly, so 12 ÷ (side) and 16 ÷ (side) must both be whole numbers of tiles.
> 2. A side bigger than 4 ft would fit fewer than 3 times along the 12 ft side, so it would fit exactly 1 or 2 times, which makes the side 12 ft or 6 ft.
> 3. Neither fits the 16 ft side exactly (16 ÷ 12 and 16 ÷ 6 are not whole numbers).
> 4. Fraction sizes such as 2/3 ft or 1/2 ft do fit, but they are **smaller**, so they need more tiles.

**4.** Which bag weight should Lekhana choose so she uses as few bags as possible? How many bags will that be?

> [!ANSWER]
> **12 kg bags; 16 bags in all.**
> 1. Fewest bags means heaviest bags, so choose the **largest common factor, 12**.
> 2. Farm 1: 84 ÷ 12 = 7 bags. Farm 2: 108 ÷ 12 = 9 bags.
> 3. Total: 7 + 9 = **16 bags**.

**Question 5.** *Jump Jackpot.* Treasure is placed on two numbers. Jumpy starts at 0 and always jumps the same size. Find the **longest** jump size that lands on both treasures.

![Jumps of 14 from 0 land on 14, 28 and 42](assets/img/class7/math/11/jumps.svg)

**(a)** 14 and 30

> [!ANSWER]
> **2.** Jumpy lands only on multiples of the jump size, so the jump must divide 14 **and** 30. Common factors of 14 (1, 2, 7, 14) and 30 (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30): 1 and 2. Longest: **2**.

**(b)** 7 and 11

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** 7 and 11 are both prime and different, so their only common factor is 1. Jumpy must hop 1 step at a time.

**(c)** 30 and 50

> [!ANSWER]
> **10.** Factors of 30: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30. Factors of 50: 1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50. Common: 1, 2, 5, 10. Longest: **10** (10, 20, 30, 40, 50 are all landing spots).

**(d)** 28 and 42

> [!ANSWER]
> **14.** Factors of 28: 1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28. Factors of 42: 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 14, 21, 42. Common: 1, 2, 7, 14. Longest: **14** (the picture above shows the jumps 14, 28, 42).

**6.** Is the longest jump size always the HCF of the two numbers? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
> 1. Starting from 0, a jump of size j lands on j, 2j, 3j, …: exactly the **multiples of j**.
> 2. To land on a treasure at n, n must be a multiple of j, that is, **j must be a factor of n**.
> 3. To land on both treasures, j must be a **common factor**, and the longest such jump is the **highest** common factor.

## 11.2 Primes and prime factorisation

Listing every factor is slow for numbers like 30 and 50, and it is easy to miss one. Primes give a quicker, more reliable way.

**Any number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes**: keep breaking composite factors until only primes are left. This is its **prime factorisation**.

**Worked example.** 90 = 9 × 10 = 3 × 3 × 2 × 5. We could also start 90 = 3 × 30 = 3 × 2 × 15 = 3 × 2 × 3 × 5, or 90 = 2 × 45 = 2 × 3 × 15 = 2 × 3 × 3 × 5.

![Factor tree of 90: 90 splits into 2 and 45, 45 into 3 and 15, 15 into 3 and 5](assets/img/class7/math/11/factor_tree_90.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **However you start, you always reach the same primes** (only their order may change). For 90 it is always 2 × 3 × 3 × 5. A prime's factorisation is just the prime itself.
> **Why it can't come out differently:** this is a deep fact about numbers (proved in higher classes), but you can feel why: a prime cannot be split, so each prime is a "brick". 90 is built from one 2, two 3s and one 5; whichever way you take the wall apart, you get the same bricks.

**Try this.** Ask two friends to draw factor trees for 72, one starting with 8 × 9 and the other with 2 × 36. **What you will see:** the trees look different, but the leaves are always three 2s and two 3s: 72 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3. **Why:** each branch only splits a number into smaller pieces; the primes at the bottom cannot be split any further, so both trees end at the same set of primes.

### The division method

To factorise, **divide by a prime** that goes in exactly, write the quotient underneath, and repeat until you reach a prime (or 1). The primes on the left, together with the last number, are the prime factors.

```
3 | 105          2 | 30
5 |  35          3 | 15
  |   7            |  5

105 = 3 × 5 × 7     30 = 2 × 3 × 5
```

**Why it works:** each line says "this number = the prime on its left × the number below it" (105 = 3 × 35, 35 = 5 × 7). Putting the lines together gives 105 = 3 × 5 × 7.

> [!TIP]
> Divide by the **smallest prime first** (2, then 3, then 5, …) and keep using it until it no longer divides. Then you never miss a factor, and the primes come out in order.

### Check yourself

**1.** Find the prime factorisation of 1200 by the division method. Is it easier than splitting 1200 = 40 × 30 = 5 × 8 × 5 × 6 = …?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1200 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 × 5.**
>
> ```
> 2 | 1200
> 2 |  600
> 2 |  300
> 2 |  150
> 3 |   75
> 5 |   25
>   |    5
> ```
>

> 2. 75 is odd, so try 3: 75 ÷ 3 = 25. Then 25 ÷ 5 = 5, which is prime.
> 3. **Easier?** Yes for most people: each step is one small division and you can't forget a factor. The splitting way (5 × 8 × 5 × 6, then 8 = 2 × 2 × 2, 6 = 2 × 3) gives the same primes but needs you to keep track of many pieces at once.

## 11.3 All the factors from the prime factorisation

840 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 × 7. **Is 2 × 2 × 7 = 28 a factor of 840?** Re-order the primes (order does not change a product):

840 = (2 × 2 × 7) × (2 × 3 × 5) = 28 × 30.

So **28 is a factor**, and its partner is 30.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every factor of a number is a "sub-part" of its prime factorisation**: a product of some of its primes (each used no more often than it appears). Multiplying the left-over primes gives the partner factor. And **1** is always a factor (the sub-part with no primes).
> **Why:** if you pick some of the primes, the rest of them multiply to a whole number, so (your pick) × (the rest) = the number. And a factor can't use a prime the number doesn't have, or use it more often, because the primes of a product are just the primes of its two parts put together.

**Worked example: all factors of 225.** 225 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 5. List the sub-parts in order of how many primes they use:

- no primes: 1
- one prime: 3, 5
- two primes: 3 × 3 = 9, 3 × 5 = 15, 5 × 5 = 25
- three primes: 3 × 3 × 5 = 45, 3 × 5 × 5 = 75
- four primes: 3 × 3 × 5 × 5 = 225

So the factors of 225 are **1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25, 45, 75, 225** (9 factors).

> [!TIP]
> **Counting check:** for 225 = 3² × 5² (two 3s, two 5s) we may use 0, 1 or 2 threes (3 choices) and 0, 1 or 2 fives (3 choices), so there are 3 × 3 = **9** factors. In general add 1 to the count of each prime and multiply.

## 11.4 The HCF from prime factorisations

Every factor of a number is a sub-part of its prime factorisation. So a **common factor** must be a sub-part of **both** factorisations, and the **HCF is the biggest common sub-part**.

**Worked example: 45 and 75.** 45 = 3 × 3 × 5 and 75 = 3 × 5 × 5. Common sub-parts: 3, 5 and 3 × 5 = 15 (and 1). **HCF = 15.**

**Worked example: 112 and 84.** 112 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 7 and 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7. Common sub-parts: 2, 7, 2 × 2, 2 × 7, 2 × 2 × 7 (and 1). **HCF = 2 × 2 × 7 = 28.**

**Worked example: 96 and 275.** 96 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 and 275 = 5 × 5 × 11. No prime appears in both, so the only common factor is 1. **HCF = 1**: 96 and 275 are co-prime.

### The quick rule

**Worked example: HCF of 30 and 72.** 30 = 2 × 3 × 5 and 72 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3.

1. Only primes that appear in **both** can be in the HCF: **2 and 3** (5 is only in 30).
2. How many 2s? 30 has only one 2, so a common sub-part can't have more than one: **one 2**.
3. How many 3s? 30 has only one 3: **one 3**.
4. HCF = 2 × 3 = **6**.

**Worked example: HCF of 225 and 750.** 225 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 5 and 750 = 2 × 3 × 5 × 5 × 5. Common primes 3 and 5. Fewest 3s: one (in 750). Fewest 5s: two (in 225). **HCF = 3 × 5 × 5 = 75.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **HCF rule:** take the primes common to all the numbers, each as many times as it appears in the number that has it the **fewest** times. Multiply them.
> **Why:** the HCF has to fit inside *every* factorisation. A prime used more often than the poorest number allows would not be a sub-part of that number.

## 11.5 Least, but not last: the LCM

**Worked example (torans).** Anshu makes torans from 6 cm strips of cloth placed end to end; Guna uses 8 cm strips. What is the shortest toran length they can both make?

1. Anshu's lengths are multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, **24**, 30, 36, 42, **48**, …
2. Guna's are multiples of 8: 8, 16, **24**, 32, 40, **48**, …
3. Both can make the **common multiples** 24, 48, 72, …; the shortest is **24 cm**.

![Four 6 cm strips and three 8 cm strips both reach 24 cm](assets/img/class7/math/11/torans.svg)

**Worked example (free gajak).** A sweet shop gives free gajak every Monday. Today is Monday and Kabamai got some; she visits the shop every 10 days. Free gajak days come every 7 days: 7, 14, 21, …, 63, **70**. Kabamai's visits: 10, 20, …, 60, **70**. She next gets free gajak **70 days** from today.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)** of two or more numbers is the smallest number that is a multiple of all of them. LCM(6, 8) = 24 and LCM(7, 10) = 70.

**Try this.** On a 1–60 number chart, draw a circle round every multiple of 4 and a square round every multiple of 6. **What you will see:** the numbers with both marks are 12, 24, 36, 48, 60: every one is a multiple of 12, and 12 comes first. **Why:** a number with both marks is a common multiple, and every common multiple is a multiple of the LCM (it must contain 2 × 2 from 4 and 2 × 3 from 6, that is, 2 × 2 × 3 = 12).

### Check yourself

**1.** Is there a **largest** common multiple of 6 and 8?

> [!ANSWER]

**Question 2.** *Idli-Vada.* Players count 1, 2, 3, … and say "idli" for multiples of the first number, "vada" for multiples of the second, and "idli-vada" for multiples of both. Find the first "idli-vada".

**(a)** 4 and 6

> [!ANSWER]
> **12.** Multiples of 4: 4, 8, **12**. Multiples of 6: 6, **12**. First common one: 12.

**(b)** 7 and 11

> [!ANSWER]
> **77.** 7 and 11 are different primes, so a common multiple must contain both: 7 × 11 = 77.

**(c)** 14 and 30

> [!ANSWER]
> **210.** Multiples of 30: 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, **210**. The first one divisible by 14 is 210 = 14 × 15.

**(d)** 15 and 55

> [!ANSWER]
> **165.** Multiples of 55: 55, 110, **165**. 55 is not a multiple of 15, nor is 110, but 165 = 15 × 11 is.

**3.** Is the first "idli-vada" always the LCM? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** "Idli-vada" is called exactly on numbers that are multiples of **both** numbers, that is, common multiples. Counting up from 1, the first one we meet is the smallest common multiple: the LCM.

## 11.6 The LCM from prime factorisations

Compare a number with one of its multiples: 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3, and its multiple 648 = 36 × 18 = (2 × 2 × 3 × 3) × (2 × 3 × 3). The multiple **contains the whole factorisation of 36, plus more primes**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every multiple of a number contains that number's prime factorisation as a sub-part.**
> **Why:** a multiple is (the number) × (something), so its primes are the number's primes together with the something's primes.

So a **common multiple** of two numbers must contain **both** factorisations, and the **LCM is the smallest number that does**.

**Worked example: LCM of 14 and 35.** 14 = 2 × 7 and 35 = 5 × 7. A common multiple must contain 2 × 7 and 5 × 7. For instance 2 × 7 × 5 × 7 × 3 and 2 × 2 × 5 × 7 × 7 × 11 both do. The smallest is **2 × 5 × 7 = 70**: it contains 2 × 7 and also 5 × 7 (the 7 is shared), and removing any prime from it breaks one of them.

**Worked example: LCM of 96 and 360.** 96 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 and 360 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5.

1. **2s:** 96 has five, 360 has three. Five 2s cover both, so take **five**.
2. **3s:** 96 has one, 360 has two. Take **two**.
3. **5s:** 96 has none, 360 has one. Take **one**.
4. LCM = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = **1440**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **LCM rule:** take every prime that appears in any of the numbers, each as many times as it appears in the number that has it the **most** times. Multiply them.
> **Why:** the LCM must contain each number's factorisation, so for each prime it needs as many copies as the "richest" number has. Any extra copy would just make it bigger without being needed.

## 11.7 Patterns and general statements

The HCF of 6 and 18 is 6, one of the two numbers. When does that happen? Exactly when **one number is a factor of the other** (so the other is a multiple of the first): HCF(n, 5n) = n, because n divides both and nothing bigger than n can divide n.

A statement that is true in **every** case, such as "HCF(n, k × n) = n", is a **general statement**, and finding one is called **generalising**. Letters let us say it for all numbers at once.

### Check yourself

For pairs where one number is the HCF:

**(a)** If one number is m, what could the other be?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Any multiple of m** (2m, 3m, 10m, …), and then m is the HCF. (Or any factor of m, and then that factor is the HCF.)

**(b)** If one number is 7k, what could the other be?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Any multiple of 7k**, such as 14k or 21k (then the HCF is 7k), **or any factor of 7k**, such as 7, k or 1 (then that smaller number is the HCF).

**Question 1.** Make a general statement about the HCF of each kind of pair, and explain why it holds.

**(a)** Two consecutive even numbers

> [!ANSWER]
> **The HCF is always 2.** Examples: HCF(4, 6) = 2, HCF(10, 12) = 2, HCF(36, 38) = 2.
> **Why:** any common factor of two numbers also divides their **difference** (if d goes into both, it goes into what is left when you take one from the other). The difference is 2, so the HCF is 1 or 2; both numbers are even, so it is 2.

**(b)** Two consecutive odd numbers

> [!ANSWER]
> **The HCF is always 1.** Examples: (5, 7), (9, 11), (21, 23).
> **Why:** a common factor divides the difference, 2, so it is 1 or 2. Odd numbers are not divisible by 2, so it is 1: they are co-prime.

**(c)** Two even numbers

> [!ANSWER]
> **The HCF is even (at least 2).** Examples: HCF(4, 10) = 2, HCF(12, 18) = 6, HCF(8, 24) = 8.
> **Why:** both factorisations contain a 2, so the biggest common sub-part contains that 2.

**(d)** Two consecutive numbers

> [!ANSWER]
> **The HCF is always 1.** Examples: (8, 9), (14, 15).
> **Why:** a common factor divides their difference, which is 1. The only factor of 1 is 1.

**(e)** Two co-prime numbers

> [!ANSWER]
> **The HCF is 1.** That is exactly what co-prime means: no common prime, so the only common factor is 1. Examples: (8, 15), (9, 20).

**Question 2.** The LCM of 3 and 24 is 24, one of the two numbers.

**(a)** Find more such pairs.

> [!ANSWER]
> (2, 8): LCM 8 · (5, 15): LCM 15 · (4, 20): LCM 20 · (7, 7): LCM 7 · (6, 36): LCM 36. In each pair the bigger number is a multiple of the smaller.

**(b)** Make a general statement and write it with algebra.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The LCM is one of the numbers exactly when that number is a multiple of the other.** With letters: **LCM(n, k × n) = k × n** for any counting numbers n and k.
> **Why:** k × n is already a multiple of n (and of itself), and no common multiple can be smaller than k × n, because every multiple of k × n is at least k × n.

**Question 3.** Make a general statement about the LCM of each kind of pair, and explain why.

**(a)** Two multiples of 3

> [!ANSWER]
> **The LCM is a multiple of 3.** Example: LCM(6, 9) = 18, LCM(12, 15) = 60.
> **Why:** the LCM is a multiple of each number, and each number is a multiple of 3, so the LCM is too (a multiple of a multiple of 3 is a multiple of 3).

**(b)** Two consecutive even numbers

> [!ANSWER]
> **The LCM is half their product.** Examples: LCM(4, 6) = 12 = 24 ÷ 2; LCM(10, 12) = 60 = 120 ÷ 2.
> **Why:** their HCF is 2 (Question 1a), and HCF × LCM = product (see 11.9), so LCM = product ÷ 2.

**(c)** Two consecutive numbers

> [!ANSWER]
> **The LCM is their product.** Examples: LCM(4, 5) = 20, LCM(8, 9) = 72.
> **Why:** consecutive numbers are co-prime, so they share no primes. The LCM must contain all the primes of both, with nothing shared, which is the whole product.

**(d)** Two co-prime numbers

> [!ANSWER]
> **The LCM is their product.** Examples: LCM(8, 15) = 120, LCM(9, 20) = 180.
> **Why:** no prime appears in both, so the LCM rule takes every prime of both numbers, which multiplies out to the product.

### Doubling and common multipliers

**What happens to the HCF if both numbers are doubled?** Each number gets one extra 2 in its factorisation, so the common sub-part gets one extra 2 as well: **the HCF doubles**.

**Worked example.** 270 = 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 5 and 50 = 2 × 5 × 5, so HCF = 2 × 5 = 10. Doubled: 540 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 5 and 100 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 5, so HCF = 2 × 2 × 5 = **20**.

**Worked example.** The HCF of 14 × 6 and 14 × 9. Clearly 14 is a common factor, but it is not the highest: 14 × 6 = 2 × 7 × 2 × 3 and 14 × 9 = 2 × 7 × 3 × 3 share 2 × 7 × 3. **HCF = 14 × 3 = 42.** The 6 and the 9 had a common factor 3 of their own.

### Check yourself

Find the HCF.

**(a)** 18 × 10 and 18 × 15

> [!ANSWER]
> **90.** 10 and 15 share 5, so HCF = 18 × 5 = 90. (Check: 180 and 270; 270 − 180 = 90, and 90 divides both.)

**(b)** 10 × 38 and 10 × 21

> [!ANSWER]
> **10.** 38 = 2 × 19 and 21 = 3 × 7 share nothing, so HCF = 10 × 1 = 10.

**(c)** 5 × 13 and 5 × 20

> [!ANSWER]
> **5.** 13 and 20 are co-prime, so HCF = 5.

**(d)** 12 × 16 and 12 × 20

> [!ANSWER]
> **48.** 16 and 20 share 4, so HCF = 12 × 4 = 48.

**(e)** In which of these is the HCF the same as the common multiplier? When does that happen?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In (b) and (c).** The HCF of n × a and n × b is n × HCF(a, b). It equals n exactly when **a and b are co-prime** (HCF(a, b) = 1), as 38 and 21, and 13 and 20 are. In (a) and (d) the other parts (10 and 15; 16 and 20) share a factor, so the HCF is bigger than the common multiplier.

## 11.8 A quick procedure for HCF and LCM together

Write the two numbers side by side and keep dividing **both** by a common prime until the two numbers left have no common factor:

```
2 | 84, 180
2 | 42,  90
3 | 21,  45
  |  7,  15
```

- **HCF** = product of the numbers on the left: 2 × 2 × 3 = **12**.
- **LCM** = product of the numbers on the left **and** the two numbers at the bottom: 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 × 15 = **1260**.

**Why the HCF:** the left column is exactly the primes that both numbers share (84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 and 180 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 15). We stop when 7 and 15 share nothing, so nothing more is common.
**Why the LCM:** 84 = (left primes) × 7 and 180 = (left primes) × 15. The number (left primes) × 7 × 15 contains both, and it can't be smaller, because the shared primes appear once and 7, 15 are co-prime.

**Try this.** Pick two numbers of your own, such as 48 and 72, and find their HCF and LCM in two ways: by the ladder and by comparing prime factorisations. **What you will see:** both ways give HCF 24 and LCM 144. **Why:** the ladder is the prime-factorisation method written compactly: the left column collects the shared primes, and the bottom row holds the primes that are not shared.

More examples:

```
2 | 300, 150            2 | 630, 770
5 | 150,  75            5 | 315, 385
5 |  30,  15            7 |  63,  77
3 |   6,   3              |   9,  11
  |   2,   1
HCF = 2 × 5 × 5 × 3 = 150       HCF = 2 × 5 × 7 = 70
LCM = 150 × 2 × 1 = 300         LCM = 70 × 9 × 11 = 6930
```

**Bigger steps are fine.** We don't have to divide by one prime at a time; any common factor we can spot works. Guna divides 300 and 150 by 50 first (giving 6 and 3), then by 3 (giving 2 and 1): HCF = 50 × 3 = 150 and LCM = 50 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 300. Anshu divides 630 and 770 by 10 (63 and 77), then by 7 (9 and 11): HCF = 10 × 7 = 70 and LCM = 10 × 7 × 9 × 11 = 6930. **This works** because a common factor such as 50 is just several common primes (2 × 5 × 5) removed in one step.

### Check yourself

Use the quick procedure, with big steps where you can.

**(a)** 90 and 150

> [!ANSWER]
> **HCF 30, LCM 450.**
> 1. Both end in 0, so divide by 10: 9 and 15.
> 2. 9 and 15 share 3: divide by 3 → 3 and 5, which share nothing.
> 3. HCF = 10 × 3 = **30**; LCM = 10 × 3 × 3 × 5 = **450**.

**(b)** 84 and 132

> [!ANSWER]
> **HCF 12, LCM 924.**
> 1. Both are divisible by 12: 84 ÷ 12 = 7 and 132 ÷ 12 = 11, which share nothing.
> 2. HCF = **12**; LCM = 12 × 7 × 11 = **924**.

## 11.9 HCF × LCM = product

**Is the LCM ever bigger than the product of the two numbers?** No: the product a × b is itself a common multiple (it is a times b and b times a), and the LCM is the **lowest** common multiple, so **LCM ≤ a × b**.

**Worked example.** 105 = 3 × 5 × 7 and 95 = 5 × 19. LCM = 3 × 5 × 7 × 19. The product is 105 × 95 = 3 × 5 × 5 × 7 × 19 = LCM × **5**. And 5 is the HCF!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> For any two numbers, **HCF × LCM = the product of the two numbers.**
> **Why:** sort the primes of the two numbers into "shared" ones and "only in one" ones. The product a × b contains the **shared primes twice** (once from each number) and the others once. The LCM takes the shared primes **once** and all the others once. The HCF is exactly the shared primes, once. So LCM × HCF has the shared primes twice and the others once: the same as a × b.

![Venn picture: 84's primes 2, 2, 3, 7 and 180's primes 2, 2, 3, 3, 5; the overlap 2, 2, 3 is the HCF 12; everything together is the LCM 1260](assets/img/class7/math/11/venn_84_180.svg)

### Check yourself

**1.** Is the LCM of 45 and 105 a factor of their product? What must it be multiplied by?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, × 15.**
> 1. 45 = 3 × 3 × 5 and 105 = 3 × 5 × 7. LCM = 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 = 315.
> 2. Product = 45 × 105 = 4725 = 315 × 15.
> 3. 15 = 3 × 5 is the HCF.

**2.** Do the same for 275 and 352.

> [!ANSWER]
> **× 11.**
> 1. 275 = 5 × 5 × 11 and 352 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 11. LCM = 2⁵ × 5 × 5 × 11 = 8800.
> 2. Product = 275 × 352 = 96,800 = 8800 × 11.
> 3. 11 is the HCF.

**3.** Do the same for 222 and 370.

> [!ANSWER]
> **× 74.**
> 1. 222 = 2 × 3 × 37 and 370 = 2 × 5 × 37. LCM = 2 × 3 × 5 × 37 = 1110.
> 2. Product = 222 × 370 = 82,140 = 1110 × 74.
> 3. 74 = 2 × 37 is the HCF. **Pattern:** the multiplier is always the HCF.

**4.** Does HCF × LCM = product work for **three** numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not in general.**
> 1. Try 2, 4, 8: HCF = 2, LCM = 8, so HCF × LCM = 16, but the product is 2 × 4 × 8 = 64.
> 2. Why it breaks: a prime shared by all three numbers appears **three** times in the product, but only twice in HCF × LCM.
> 3. It does work for some sets, such as 2, 3, 5 (HCF 1, LCM 30, product 30), where no prime is shared at all.

**Try this.** Make a "Mystery Colours" chart. Give each prime its own colour (2 red, 3 blue, 5 green, 7 yellow, and a new colour for each bigger prime). For each number from 2 to 30, draw a ring split into as many equal pieces as it has prime factors, and colour each piece by its prime. **What you will see:** primes are single-colour rings; 12 is red, red, blue; numbers that share a colour share a factor. **Why:** the ring is just the prime factorisation drawn in colour, so the HCF of two numbers is the colours their rings have in common.

Solve each question on paper first, then press **Show answer** to check.

### 1. Two rows of stars repeat their colours. Row 1 repeats every 6 stars and Row 2 every 4 stars, and both have a blue star at position 4. At which position do the blue stars meet next?

![Row 1 repeats yellow, green, orange, blue, pink, grey; Row 2 repeats green, orange, yellow, blue](assets/img/class7/math/11/stars.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **At star 16.**
> 1. Row 1 has blue at 4, 10, 16, 22, … (every 6).
> 2. Row 2 has blue at 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, … (every 4).
> 3. After star 4, both blues line up again after a number of steps that is a multiple of 6 and of 4. The smallest is LCM(6, 4) = 12, so the next meeting is at 4 + 12 = **16**.

### 2. Multiples and factors

**(a)** Is 5 × 7 × 11 × 11 a multiple of 5 × 7 × 7 × 11 × 2?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** A multiple must contain the other number's whole factorisation. 5 × 7 × 11 × 11 has only **one** 7 and **no** 2, but 5 × 7 × 7 × 11 × 2 needs two 7s and a 2.

**(b)** Is 5 × 7 × 11 × 11 a factor of 5 × 7 × 7 × 11 × 2?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** A factor must be a sub-part. 5 × 7 × 11 × 11 uses **two** 11s, but 5 × 7 × 7 × 11 × 2 has only one 11.

### 3. Find the HCF and LCM, as prime factorisations.

**(a)** 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 7 and 12 × 7 × 11

> [!ANSWER]
> First make the second one fully prime: 12 × 7 × 11 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 × 11.
> 1. **HCF:** common primes 3 and 7; fewest 3s: one; fewest 7s: one. **HCF = 3 × 7** (= 21).
> 2. **LCM:** most 2s: two; most 3s: two; most 5s: one; most 7s: two; most 11s: one. **LCM = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 7 × 11** (= 97,020).

**(b)** 45 and 36

> [!ANSWER]
> 45 = 3 × 3 × 5 and 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3.
> 1. **HCF = 3 × 3** (= 9).
> 2. **LCM = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5** (= 180).
> 3. Check: 9 × 180 = 1620 = 45 × 36 ✔.

### 4. Find two numbers whose HCF is 1 and LCM is 66.

> [!ANSWER]
> **For instance 6 and 11** (also 2 and 33, 3 and 22, or 1 and 66).
> 1. HCF × LCM = product, so the two numbers multiply to 1 × 66 = 66.
> 2. 66 = 2 × 3 × 11. The numbers must share no prime (HCF 1), so split these primes between them: 6 = 2 × 3 and 11.
> 3. Check: HCF(6, 11) = 1, LCM = 66 ✔.

### 5. A cowherd's cows split equally through 3 gates, then through 5 gates, then through 7 gates. He has fewer than 200 cows. How many?

> [!ANSWER]
> **105 cows.**
> 1. Equal groups through 3, 5 and 7 gates means the number is a multiple of 3, of 5 and of 7.
> 2. These are different primes, so the common multiples are multiples of 3 × 5 × 7 = 105: 105, 210, …
> 3. Fewer than 200 (and more than 0) leaves only **105**. (35 through each of 3 gates, 21 through each of 5, 15 through each of 7.)

### 6. A box is 12 cm long, 18 cm wide and 36 cm high. Which cubes fill it with no gaps?

**(a)** 9 cm

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The edge must divide 12, 18 and 36 exactly. 12 ÷ 9 is not a whole number.

**(b)** 6 cm

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 12 ÷ 6 = 2, 18 ÷ 6 = 3, 36 ÷ 6 = 6, so 2 × 3 × 6 = 36 cubes fill it. (6 is the HCF of 12, 18 and 36: the biggest cube that fits.)

**(c)** 4 cm

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 18 ÷ 4 = 4.5, not whole.

**(d)** 3 cm

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 3 divides 12, 18 and 36 (4 × 6 × 12 = 288 cubes).

**(e)** 2 cm

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 2 divides 12, 18 and 36 (6 × 9 × 18 = 972 cubes). Every common factor of 12, 18, 36 works: 1, 2, 3, 6.

### 7. Which is the largest number that divides both 306 and 36? (a) 36 (b) 612 (c) 18 (d) 3 (e) 2 (f) 360

> [!ANSWER]
> **(c) 18.**
> 1. 306 = 2 × 3 × 3 × 17 and 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3. HCF = 2 × 3 × 3 = 18.
> 2. 36 does not divide 306 (306 ÷ 36 = 8.5); 612 and 360 are bigger than 306, so they can't divide it. 3 and 2 do divide both but are smaller than 18.

### 8. Find the smallest number that is divisible by 3, 4, 5 and 7 but leaves remainder 10 when divided by 11.

> [!ANSWER]
> **2100.**
> 1. Divisible by 3, 4, 5, 7 means a multiple of LCM = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 = **420**.
> 2. Check multiples of 420 for remainder 10 on division by 11. 420 = 11 × 38 + 2, so 420 leaves 2; each extra 420 adds 2 more to the remainder: 420 → 2, 840 → 4, 1260 → 6, 1680 → 8, **2100 → 10**.
> 3. Check: 2100 = 11 × 190 + 10 ✔.

### 9. In "Fire in the Mountain", children form groups of the number called; anyone not in a full group is out. When 6 was called, no one was out; when 9 was called, no one was out; when 10 was called, some were out. How many children could there be? (a) 72 (b) 90 (c) 45 (d) 3 (e) 36 (f) none of these

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a) 72 or (e) 36.**
> 1. No one out at 6 and at 9 means the number is a multiple of 6 and of 9, so a multiple of LCM(6, 9) = 18.
> 2. Someone out at 10 means it is **not** a multiple of 10.
> 3. 72 = 18 × 4 ✔ and not a multiple of 10 ✔. 36 = 18 × 2 ✔ ✔. 90 is a multiple of 10 ✗. 45 is not a multiple of 6 ✗. 3 is not a multiple of 18 ✗.

### 10. m and n are two different primes. Their LCM can be: (a) less than both, (b) between the two, (c) greater than both, (d) less than m × n, (e) greater than m × n. Which are correct?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only (c).**
> 1. Different primes share no factor except 1, so LCM(m, n) = **m × n**.
> 2. m × n is bigger than m and bigger than n (each prime is at least 2), so (c) is true and (a), (b) are false.
> 3. It is exactly m × n, so neither less (d) nor greater (e).

### 11. A rabbit has a 150 ft head start. Each time it jumps 7 ft, the dog chasing it jumps 9 ft. After how many leaps does the dog catch it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **75 leaps.**
> 1. In each leap the dog gains 9 − 7 = 2 ft on the rabbit.
> 2. It must make up 150 ft: 150 ÷ 2 = **75 leaps**.
> 3. Check: dog 75 × 9 = 675 ft; rabbit 150 + 75 × 7 = 675 ft ✔.
> (Seen with multiples: the dog's positions are multiples of 9, the rabbit's are 150 + multiples of 7; they first meet at 675.)

### 12. What is the smallest number that is a multiple of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10?

> [!ANSWER]
> **360.**
> 1. Primes needed: 2s — the most is three (in 8 = 2 × 2 × 2); 3s — the most is two (in 9 = 3 × 3); 5s — one (in 5 and 10).
> 2. LCM = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = **360**. (1, 2, 4, 6, 10 add nothing new.)

### 13. A problem from Mahāvīrāchārya (850 CE): add 8/15, 1/20, 7/36, 11/63 and 1/21. How can this be done efficiently?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The sum is exactly 1.**
> 1. To add fractions, rewrite them with a **common denominator**; the smallest one is the LCM of the denominators.
> 2. 15 = 3 × 5, 20 = 2 × 2 × 5, 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3, 63 = 3 × 3 × 7, 21 = 3 × 7. LCM = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 = **1260**.
> 3. Convert: 8/15 = 672/1260 (× 84); 1/20 = 63/1260 (× 63); 7/36 = 245/1260 (× 35); 11/63 = 220/1260 (× 20); 1/21 = 60/1260 (× 60).
> 4. Add: 672 + 63 + 245 + 220 + 60 = 1260, so the sum is 1260/1260 = **1**.

## In a nutshell

- **HCF** = the biggest common factor; **LCM** = the smallest common multiple.
- **Prime factorisation:** every number > 1 is a product of primes, always the same primes. Use the division method, smallest primes first.
- **Factors are sub-parts** of the prime factorisation; **multiples contain** the whole factorisation.
- **HCF:** common primes, each the **fewest** times it appears. **LCM:** all primes, each the **most** times it appears.
- Quick procedure: divide both numbers by common factors until nothing is common. **HCF** = the divisors; **LCM** = divisors × what is left.
- **HCF × LCM = product** of the two numbers, so **LCM ≤ product**.
- Consecutive numbers and co-prime numbers: HCF 1, LCM = product. Consecutive even numbers: HCF 2.
- Use the HCF when you **cut or share into the biggest equal pieces**; use the LCM when things **repeat and you want them to meet again**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** the HCF can never be bigger than the smaller number, and the LCM can never be smaller than the bigger number · for the HCF take the **fewest** copies of each common prime, for the LCM the **most** copies of every prime · compare **prime** factorisations, not composite ones (72 = 6 × 12 and 144 = 8 × 18 still share 72) · HCF × LCM = product works for **two** numbers, not three · 1 is neither prime nor composite.
