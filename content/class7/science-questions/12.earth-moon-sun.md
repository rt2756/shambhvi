<!-- ===== Rotation ===== -->

Define **rotation**, and state how long the Earth takes for one rotation and in which direction.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Rotation** = the motion of an object in which all its parts move in circles around an imaginary line passing through it — the **axis of rotation** (through the North and South Poles).
>
> The Earth completes one rotation in about **24 hours**, turning **West to East** (anti-clockwise viewed from above the North Pole).

---

Does the Sun really travel across the sky? Use the merry-go-round to explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — the Earth turns, and the Sun only *appears* to move.**
>
> On a merry-go-round turning **anti-clockwise**, the trees around you appear to sweep past in the **opposite** direction, entering your view on the left and leaving on the right.
>
> The Earth turns **West→East** in just the same way, so the Sun **appears** to rise in the East, cross the sky, and set in the West.

---

Explain how the Earth's rotation causes **day and night**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Sunlight falls on **only half** the Earth at any moment. That half has **day**; the other half has **night**.
>
> As the Earth rotates, your location moves **into** the lit half (**sunrise**) and later **into** the dark half (**sunset**) — one full cycle every 24 hours.

---

When it is daytime in India, it is night in the USA. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because sunlight reaches only **half the Earth at a time**, and India and the USA are on **opposite sides** of the globe.
>
> When India is on the lit half, the USA is on the dark half — so the Earth's single rotation gives them opposite times of day.

---

Sunrise happens earlier in some parts of India than others. Which part sees it first, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **eastern** part (e.g. sunrise is earlier in the east than the west).
>
> The Earth rotates **West to East**, so eastern places turn into the sunlight **first**.

---

Why does the **Pole Star** appear stationary while other stars seem to move?

> [!ANSWER]
> The Earth's **axis of rotation points very close to the Pole Star** in the Northern Hemisphere.
>
> The Earth spins about that axis, so the Pole Star stays almost in the same place while every other star appears to **circle around it**.
>
> Long-exposure photographs capture this as curved **star trails**.

---

What did **Aryabhata** say about the apparent motion of the stars?

> [!ANSWER]
> In the *Aryabhatiya* (~5th century CE) he compared it to a boat: **just as a man in a moving boat sees stationary objects moving backwards, people see the stationary stars moving west.**
>
> His figure for one rotation, about **23 h 56 min 4.1 s**, is remarkably close to the modern value.

---

<!-- ===== Revolution ===== -->

What is the difference between **rotation** and **revolution**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Rotation:** an object spinning about **its own axis** — the Earth does this in ~24 hours, giving day and night.
>
> **Revolution:** the motion of one object **around another** — the Earth around the Sun, taking about **365 days and 6 hours**. The path is called its **orbit**.

---

Why do different stars appear in the night sky at different times of year?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the Earth **revolves** around the Sun.
>
> Each month the Earth is at a different point in its orbit, so the **night side faces a different direction in space** — showing a different part of the sky after sunset.

---

Padmashree saw Orion nearly overhead at 8 pm yesterday. Roughly when will she see it overhead today?

> [!ANSWER]
> **About 4 minutes earlier** — close to 7:56 pm.
>
> The Earth's revolution shifts the stars slightly each night, so a given star returns to the same position about four minutes earlier every day (adding up to ~2 hours in a month).

<!-- ===== Seasons ===== -->

What actually causes the **seasons**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **tilt of the Earth's axis of rotation**, together with the Earth's **spherical shape** — and the Earth keeps that same tilt all the way around its orbit.

---

Explain why the Northern Hemisphere has summer in June, giving **both** reasons.

> [!ANSWER]
> In June the Northern Hemisphere is tilted **towards** the Sun, and two things follow:
>
> **1. More intense sunlight** — because of the Earth's curved surface, the same amount of sunrays is **spread over a smaller area**, heating it more.
>
> **2. Longer days** — that hemisphere receives sunlight for **more than 12 hours** of the 24-hour rotation.
>
> More intense light, for longer → summer.

---

Two common explanations for seasons are **wrong**. Name them and say why.

> [!ANSWER]
> ✗ "The hemisphere tilted toward the Sun is **closer** to it."
> ✗ "The Earth's **oval orbit** brings it nearer the Sun at some times of year."
>
> **Both distance differences are far too small** to cause seasons. The clinching fact: **the Earth is closest to the Sun in January** — the middle of the northern winter.
>
> The real cause is the **tilt**.

---

The Indian cricket team tours Australia in December. Should they pack winter or summer clothes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Summer clothes.**
>
> Australia is in the **Southern Hemisphere**, where the seasons are **reversed**. In December the Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun → summer there, while India has winter.

---

Name the four key dates in the Northern Hemisphere and what happens on each.

> [!ANSWER]
> **~21 June — summer solstice:** the **longest day**.
>
> **~22 December — winter solstice:** the **shortest day and longest night**.
>
> **~21 March — spring equinox** and **~23 September — autumn equinox:** day and night are each **12 hours**.

---

How many hours of sunlight does the **North Pole** get during one rotation in June? In December?

> [!ANSWER]
> **June: all 24 hours** — continuous sunshine.
>
> **December: 0 hours** — continuous darkness.
>
> The poles get about **six months of daylight followed by six months of darkness** (the South Pole being opposite).

---

Why are the seasons not very noticeable in the southern states of India?

> [!ANSWER]
> They lie **close to the equator**, where there is always about **12 hours of sunlight and 12 hours of darkness**, and little difference in the **intensity** of the sunrays from month to month.
>
> (Local geography and nearness to the sea also shape the weather there.)

---

If the Earth's axis were **not tilted**, what would happen to the seasons?

> [!ANSWER]
> **There would be no seasons** as we know them.
>
> Every place would receive sunlight of the same intensity for the same length of time all year round — day and night would be roughly 12 hours everywhere, always. Since the tilt *is* the cause, removing it removes the effect.

<!-- ===== Apparent size & solar eclipse ===== -->

The Moon is far smaller than the Sun. How can it hide the Sun completely?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because of **apparent size** — how big something looks depends on **both its real size and its distance** from you.
>
> The Moon is much smaller than the Sun but also **much closer to us**, so their **apparent sizes in our sky are nearly the same** — just enough for the Moon to cover the Sun's disc.
>
> Same reason your **thumb** can cover a friend's head from 5 metres away.

---

Venus is much bigger than the Moon. Why can't it block the Sun?

> [!ANSWER]
> It is **much farther from the Earth** than the Moon is, so its **apparent size is tiny**.
>
> When Venus passes between the Sun and the Earth it shows only as a **small black dot** crossing the Sun — the rare **Transit of Venus**.

---

Describe the positions of Sun, Moon and Earth during a **solar eclipse**, and the difference between total and partial.

> [!ANSWER]
> Order: **Sun → Moon → Earth** — the Moon comes between the Sun and the Earth, and its **shadow falls on a small area** of the Earth.
>
> **Total solar eclipse:** in that small area no part of the Sun can be seen — it goes dark for a few minutes.
>
> **Partial solar eclipse:** in the surrounding regions, where the Moon covers only part of the Sun.

---

Why does a total solar eclipse last only a few minutes?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the Moon's shadow does not stay still — the **Earth's rotation** and the **Moon's motion in its orbit** make the shadow **sweep across** the Earth's surface.
>
> Any one place is inside the shadow only briefly.

---

Four friends watch a solar eclipse: Ravikiran uses eclipse goggles, Jyothi projects the Sun's image with a mirror, Adithya looks directly at the Sun, Aruna attends a planetarium event. Who was careless, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Adithya.**
>
> Even during an eclipse the Sun is **intense enough to damage the eyes and cause blindness**. Looking directly must be **strictly avoided** — and sunglasses, binoculars or telescopes are **not** safe either.
>
> The other three used safe methods: proper solar goggles, mirror projection, and an organised viewing event.

---

<!-- ===== Lunar eclipse ===== -->

Describe the positions during a **lunar eclipse**, and how it looks.

> [!ANSWER]
> Order: **Sun → Earth → Moon** — the Earth comes between them, and the **Earth's shadow falls on the full Moon**.
>
> **Total lunar eclipse:** the Moon is completely in the shadow and turns a **dark red** colour.
>
> **Partial lunar eclipse:** only part of the Moon is in the shadow.

---

Which eclipse is safe to watch with the naked eye, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **lunar** eclipse is safe.
>
> You are looking at the **dim, eclipsed Moon** — not at the Sun. A solar eclipse still involves looking towards the intensely bright Sun, which can blind you.

---

Why can a lunar eclipse be seen from a large part of the Earth, while a total solar eclipse is visible from only a small area?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Lunar:** the **Earth's shadow is large** and falls on the whole Moon, so **anyone on the night side of the Earth** who can see the Moon sees the eclipse.
>
> **Solar:** the **Moon's shadow is small** and lands on only a narrow strip of the Earth's surface — so only observers inside that strip see totality.

---

What is an eclipse called in Sanskrit, and which ancient text gives calculations to predict them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Grahan.** The best-known text is the **Surya Siddhanta**, written in classical Sanskrit poetry as rhythmic shlokas.
>
> Eclipses were once feared and surrounded by superstition; now that the cause is understood, scientists travel the world to observe them — they reveal phenomena that cannot be studied any other way.
