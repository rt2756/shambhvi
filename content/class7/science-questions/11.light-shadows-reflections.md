<!-- ===== Luminous & non-luminous ===== -->

Which of these are **luminous**: Sun, Moon, Pole Star, Venus, a mirror, a firefly?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Luminous (emit their own light): the Sun, the Pole Star** (a star), and a **firefly**.
>
> **Non-luminous: the Moon, Venus and the mirror** — they only **reflect** light that falls on them.

---

Is moonlight the Moon's own light? Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The Moon is **non-luminous** — moonlight is **sunlight reflected** off its surface.
>
> The same is true of every planet in the solar system: they shine only by reflecting the Sun's light.

---

<!-- ===== Straight-line travel ===== -->

Three matchboxes with holes at the same height are lined up and a light spot falls on a screen. One box is nudged sideways. What happens, and what does it prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The spot disappears.**
>
> **Why it proves light travels straight:** if light could bend, it would still reach the screen through the misaligned hole. The spot vanishing means light only got through when all three holes lay on **one straight line**.

---

You can see a candle flame through a straight pipe but not through a bent one. What does this show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Light travels in a straight line.**
>
> Through the straight pipe there is a clear straight path from the flame to your eye. Bend the pipe and that straight path is broken — the light cannot follow the curve, so you see nothing.

---

<!-- ===== Materials ===== -->

Classify as transparent, translucent or opaque, and say how much light passes: **glass · tracing paper · cardboard**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Glass — transparent:** light passes **almost completely**.
>
> **Tracing paper — translucent:** light passes **partially**.
>
> **Cardboard — opaque:** light does **not pass at all**.

---

Which type of material makes the **darkest** shadow, and can a transparent object cast one?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Opaque objects** form the **darkest** shadows — they block the light completely.
>
> **Translucent objects** make **lighter** shadows, and some **transparent objects can cast faint shadows** too.

---

<!-- ===== Shadows ===== -->

What is a shadow, and what **three things** are needed to see one?

> [!ANSWER]
> A shadow is the **dark patch where light does not reach** because an object blocked it.
>
> You need: a **source of light**, an **opaque object**, and a **screen** (a wall, the floor, the ground) to catch it. Remove any one and there is no shadow to observe.

---

A red ball casts a shadow. What colour is the shadow, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Dark/black — not red.** Changing the colour of an object does **not** change the colour of its shadow.
>
> **Why:** a shadow is simply the **absence of light**, not an image of the object. There is no light there to carry any colour.

---

Keeping the torch and screen fixed, you move an object **closer to the torch**. What happens to the shadow?

> [!ANSWER]
> The shadow becomes **larger** (and blurrier).
>
> Closer to the light, the object blocks a wider spread of the beam. Moving it toward the **screen** instead makes the shadow **smaller and sharper**.

---

Can you always tell what an object is from its shadow?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The shadow's shape depends on the object's **position and tilt** relative to the light — tilt it and the outline can change completely.
>
> Sometimes the shadow reveals the object clearly; sometimes it is unrecognisable. This is exactly what **shadow puppetry** exploits.

---

Name two Indian traditions of shadow puppetry.

> [!ANSWER]
> Any two of: **Charma Bahuli Natya** (Maharashtra) · **Tholu Bommalata** / **Keelu Bomme** (Andhra Pradesh) · **Togalu Gombeyaata** (Karnataka) · **Ravana Chhaya** (Odisha) · **Tholpavakoothu** (Kerala) · **Bommalattam** (Tamil Nadu).
>
> Flat cut-out puppets are moved between a light source and a screen — used for entertainment and to share messages with the community.

---

<!-- ===== Reflection ===== -->

What is **reflection of light**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **change in direction of light** when it falls on a shiny surface or mirror.
>
> The light still travels in straight lines — the mirror simply sends it off in a **new** straight direction.

---

A mirror in sunlight throws a bright spot onto a shaded wall. Tilting the mirror moves the spot. What does this show?

> [!ANSWER]
> That a mirror **changes the direction** of the light falling on it — and that we can **control** that direction by turning the mirror.
>
> It also shows light keeps travelling **straight** after reflecting, which is why the spot lands somewhere definite rather than spreading everywhere.

---

<!-- ===== Plane mirror image ===== -->

State the **four properties** of an image formed by a plane mirror.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** Same **size** as the object · **2. Erect** (upright) · **3.** **Cannot be obtained on a screen** · **4. Laterally inverted** (left–right swapped).
>
> It also appears **as far behind** the mirror as the object stands in front of it.

---

You stand closer to a mirror. What happens to your image?

> [!ANSWER]
> The image also **comes closer** to the mirror — it always appears **as far behind the mirror as you are in front of it**.

---

What is **lateral inversion**? Test it by raising your left arm.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **left–right reversal** in a mirror image.
>
> Raise your **left** arm and the image raises what looks like its **right**; touch your right ear and the image touches its left.

---

Why is **"AMBULANCE"** written reversed on the front of an ambulance?

> [!ANSWER]
> A driver ahead sees it in their **rear-view mirror**, which **laterally inverts** it — flipping the reversed writing back the right way round, so it reads **AMBULANCE** and they know to give way.

---

A screen is placed behind, then in front of, a plane mirror. Can the image be caught on it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — in neither position.** The image formed by a plane mirror **cannot be obtained on a screen**.
>
> It only *appears* to be behind the mirror; no light actually reaches that place, so there is nothing for a screen to catch.

---

<!-- ===== Pinhole camera ===== -->

What is a **pinhole camera**, and how is its image oriented?

> [!ANSWER]
> A device where light rays from an object pass through a **tiny hole** and form an image on a screen.
>
> The image is **inverted (upside down)** — and it shows the object's **colours**.

---

Why is a pinhole camera's image upside down?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **light travels in straight lines**.
>
> A ray from the **top** of the object passes through the pinhole and carries on **downwards**, landing at the **bottom** of the screen. A ray from the bottom crosses **upwards** to the top. The rays **cross at the hole**, so the whole picture is flipped.

---

How is the image in a pinhole camera different from the image in a plane mirror?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Pinhole camera: upside down (inverted).**
>
> **Plane mirror: erect, but laterally inverted** (left–right swapped, not top–bottom).
>
> Two different effects from two different causes — don't mix them up.

---

<!-- ===== Devices ===== -->

How does a **periscope** work, and where is it used?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two plane mirrors** fixed in a **Z-shaped box**. Light from an object reflects off the first mirror down to the second, and then to your eye.
>
> The two reflections let you see objects that are **not directly visible** — used in **submarines, tanks**, by soldiers in bunkers, or to see over a crowd.

---

Why does a **kaleidoscope** show a new pattern every time you turn it?

> [!ANSWER]
> It has **three mirror strips in a triangle**, so you get **reflections of reflections** — many images at once, forming a symmetric pattern.
>
> Turning the tube **rearranges the loose coloured pieces**, and since the arrangement is never repeated exactly, every look gives a **different pattern**. Designers and artists use them for new pattern ideas.
