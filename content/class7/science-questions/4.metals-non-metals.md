<!-- ===== The five properties ===== -->

Name the property behind each: **(a)** silver foil on a sweet · **(b)** a copper wire · **(c)** the ringing of a school bell.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a) Malleability** — beaten into thin sheets.
>
> **(b) Ductility** — drawn out into wires.
>
> **(c) Sonority** — metals ring when struck.

---

An iron nail is hammered and flattens. A lump of sulfur is hammered and shatters. A block of wood does neither. Describe each material.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Iron → malleable** (flattens into a sheet).
>
> **Sulfur → brittle** (breaks into pieces).
>
> **Wood → neither malleable nor brittle** — it just stays as it is.
>
> ⚠️ This is why "not malleable" ≠ "brittle": brittle is an *active* shattering, and wood does neither thing.

---

Which two metals are the **most malleable**, and how ductile is gold?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Gold and silver** are the most malleable — which is why silver foil can be beaten thin enough to eat on a sweet.
>
> Gold is so ductile that **1 gram can be drawn into a 2 km-long wire**.

---

A metal spoon and a wooden spoon of the same size are left in hot water for a few minutes. Which end is hotter to touch, and what does that show?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **metal spoon's** upper end is hotter.
>
> Heat travels **through** the metal from the hot water to the far end — that transfer through a material is **conduction**, and metals are **good conductors** of heat. Wood conducts poorly, so its far end stays cool.

---

Why is a cooking vessel made of metal but its handle of wood or plastic?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two jobs needing **opposite** properties: the metal body must **let heat through** to cook the food (good conductor), while the handle must **block heat** so your hand is safe (poor conductor).
>
> Same design logic as the electrician's plastic-covered screwdriver — right material for each job.

---

Why does an electrician wear **rubber gloves and shoes** and use tools with plastic handles?

> [!ANSWER]
> Rubber and plastic are **poor conductors of electricity**, so current cannot pass through them into the body.
>
> The metal parts do the conducting where it's wanted; the insulating layers stop it where it would be dangerous.

---

<!-- ===== Exceptions ===== -->

"All metals are hard solids." Give two facts that disprove this.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** **Sodium and potassium** are metals so **soft** they can be cut with a knife.
>
> **2.** **Mercury** is a metal that is **liquid** at room temperature (used in thermometers).
>
> The properties are *general tendencies*, not unbreakable laws — which is why we test rather than assume.

---

Are plastic, glass and wood metals or non-metals?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neither.** Those two labels only sort **elements** — substances that cannot be broken down into simpler substances (118 are known).
>
> Plastic, glass, wood, rubber and paper aren't elements at all, so the question doesn't apply to them.

---

<!-- ===== Rusting ===== -->

In the three-bottle rusting experiment, why is **silica gel** put in bottle A, and why is the water in bottle B **boiled and covered with oil**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each trick removes **one** ingredient so the bottle tests one condition cleanly:
>
> **Silica gel** absorbs moisture → bottle A has **dry air, no water**.
>
> **Boiling** drives out dissolved air and the **oil layer** stops more air dissolving → bottle B has **water, no air**.
>
> Bottle C (partly dipped, open) has **both**. Change one thing at a time — that's what makes the result trustworthy.

---

Which bottle showed rust, and what does the result prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> Only **bottle C** — the one exposed to **both air and water**.
>
> A and B stayed clean, so air alone can't rust iron and water alone can't either. **Both together (moist air) are needed** — the whole point of testing them separately.

---

What is **corrosion**? Give the colour it produces on copper and on silver.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **gradual deterioration of a metal surface** caused by air, water or other substances. Rusting is corrosion's iron-specific version.
>
> **Copper → green** coating · **silver → black** coating.

---

Name three ways of preventing rusting, and the one idea behind all of them.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Painting, oiling/greasing, galvanisation** (coating the iron with **zinc**).
>
> **The single idea:** rusting needs air *and* water touching the iron — every method puts a **barrier** between the iron and the moist air. Remove a necessary ingredient and the process stops.

---

What is remarkable about the **Iron Pillar of Delhi**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Made over **1600 years ago** (time of Chandragupta II), about **8 m** tall and over **6000 kg**, it has stood in the open through wind and rain with **barely any rust**.
>
> It shows how advanced ancient Indian metallurgy was — they made rust-resistant iron long before modern chemistry explained it.

<!-- ===== Oxides and litmus ===== -->

Magnesium ribbon is burnt. Describe what you see, name the product, and give its nature with litmus.

> [!ANSWER]
> It burns with a **dazzling white flame** leaving a **white powder — magnesium oxide** (magnesium + oxygen from the air).
>
> Dissolved in warm water it turns **red litmus blue** → **basic**.

---

Sulfur is burnt and the gas is dissolved in water. What forms, and is it acidic or basic?

> [!ANSWER]
> Burning sulfur gives **sulfur dioxide** gas; dissolved in water it forms **sulfurous acid**.
>
> It turns **blue litmus red** → **acidic**.

---

An unknown element is burnt, and the oxide turns **red litmus blue**. Metal or non-metal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A metal.** Red → blue means the oxide is **basic**, and **metal oxides are basic** while **non-metal oxides are acidic**.
>
> This is why the litmus test from Chapter 2 doubles as an element-sorting tool: burn it, dissolve the oxide, read the colour.

---

<!-- ===== Storage ===== -->

Why is **sodium stored in kerosene** but **phosphorus stored in water**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sodium** reacts vigorously with air and water, releasing a lot of heat — kerosene keeps **both** away from it.
>
> **Phosphorus** catches fire in ordinary air — water keeps **air** away from it.
>
> Note the neat reversal: the metal must be kept *out* of water, the non-metal must be kept *in* it. Each is stored in whatever it does **not** react with.
