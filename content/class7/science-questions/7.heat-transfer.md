<!-- ===== The three processes ===== -->

Name the three ways heat is transferred, and say which one needs **no medium**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Conduction, convection and radiation.**
>
> **Radiation needs no medium** — it can cross empty space, which is how the Sun's heat reaches Earth. Conduction and convection both need a material whose particles carry the heat.

---

What is the key difference between **conduction** and **convection**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Conduction:** each particle passes heat to its neighbour, but **the particles stay in their positions** — only the heat travels along.
>
> **Convection:** the heated particles **themselves move** from one place to another, carrying the heat with them.
>
> That's why conduction rules in **solids** (particles are locked in place) and convection in **liquids and gases** (particles are free to travel).

---

<!-- ===== Conduction ===== -->

Four pins are fixed with wax along a metal strip, and one end is heated. Predict what happens and explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> The pin **nearest the flame falls first**, then the second, third and fourth **in order**.
>
> Heat travels along the strip from the hot end to the cold end. As it arrives at each pin, the **wax there melts** and the pin drops.
>
> **Why the order matters:** if they all fell together, heat would be reaching everywhere at once. Falling one by one proves heat moves **gradually through the material** — the definition of conduction.

---

The same experiment is done with a **wooden** strip. What happens, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The pins do not fall.** Wood is a **poor conductor (insulator)** of heat — it doesn't let heat pass through easily, so the wax never gets hot enough to melt.

---

Why is tea served in **clay or porcelain** cups but cooked in **metal** vessels?

> [!ANSWER]
> Opposite jobs need opposite materials.
>
> **Metal is a good conductor** — heat passes quickly from the flame into the food.
>
> **Clay and porcelain are poor conductors** — they slow heat escaping, so the tea **stays hot longer**.

---

Why do woollen clothes keep us warm?

> [!ANSWER]
> Wool **traps air in its pores**, and **air is a poor conductor of heat**.
>
> That trapped air blocks heat flowing from the body to the cold surroundings — so the warmth stays in. (It's the *air*, not the wool itself, doing most of the work.)

---

Why are **two thin blankets** warmer than one thick blanket?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because a **layer of air gets trapped between them**, and air is a poor conductor — so you gain an extra insulating layer that a single thick blanket doesn't have.

---

How do **hollow bricks** keep a house warm in winter and cool in summer?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **air trapped inside the hollows** is a poor conductor, so it slows heat passing through the wall **in either direction** — heat can't escape easily in winter, and outside heat can't get in easily in summer.

---

<!-- ===== Convection ===== -->

Why does hot air rise?

> [!ANSWER]
> When air is heated it **expands** and takes up more space, which makes it **lighter** — so it rises. Cooler, heavier air sinks to take its place.
>
> Same reason smoke from a fire or an incense stick goes up: it's warmer than the air around it.

---

A candle is placed under one of two inverted paper cups hanging from a stick. What happens?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That cup rises**, tilting the stick.
>
> The air below it is heated, expands, becomes lighter and rises — pushing the cup up as it goes.

---

A grain of potassium permanganate sits at the bottom-centre of a beaker of water heated from below. Describe and explain the coloured streak.

> [!ANSWER]
> The colour **rises up through the middle and comes down along the sides** — a continuous loop.
>
> **Why:** water at the bottom is heated, expands, becomes lighter and **rises**. The cooler, heavier water at the sides **sinks** to take its place, gets heated in turn, and rises. The cycle repeats until all the water is hot.
>
> The dye simply makes the movement visible — this is **convection**, heat carried by the actual movement of water.

---

Which heats up faster — **soil or water**? Which cools faster?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Soil does both** — it heats faster than water **and** cools faster than water.
>
> (Shown by leaving a bowl of soil and a bowl of water in sunlight with thermometers: after 20 minutes the soil's temperature has risen more; bring them indoors and the soil also cools quicker.)

---

Explain the **sea breeze**, and why coastal houses have windows facing the sea.

> [!ANSWER]
> **During the day** the land heats faster than the sea. The warm air above the land **rises**, and **cooler air moves in from the sea to the land** — that's the sea breeze.
>
> Windows face the sea to catch it, because in hot coastal places this breeze is what relieves the heat.

---

Explain the **land breeze**, and why the wind direction reverses at night.

> [!ANSWER]
> **At night** there's no sunlight, and the land **cools faster** than the sea. Now the air above the **sea** is warmer and rises, so **cooler air moves from the land towards the sea** — the land breeze.
>
> The reversal happens because the *same* property (land changes temperature faster than water) works in **opposite directions** when heating and when cooling.

---

<!-- ===== Radiation ===== -->

How does the Sun's heat reach the Earth, and why can't it be conduction or convection?

> [!ANSWER]
> By **radiation** — heat travelling directly from the hot object, **needing no material medium**.
>
> Conduction and convection both need particles to carry the heat, and there's essentially **empty space** between the Sun and the Earth. Only radiation can cross it.

---

A hot utensil is taken off the flame. Why does it cool down?

> [!ANSWER]
> It **radiates heat to its surroundings** — **all objects radiate heat**, not just fires and the Sun. Losing that heat is what cools it.

---

Why wear **light-coloured** clothes in summer and **dark** ones in winter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Light colours reflect** most of the heat falling on them — comfortable when you want to stay cool.
>
> **Dark surfaces absorb** more heat — comfortable when you want to stay warm.

---

<!-- ===== All three together ===== -->

Water is being heated in a pan on a flame. Identify where each of the three processes is happening.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Flame → pan: conduction** (heat passing through the metal).
>
> **Through the water: convection** (hot water rising, cooler water sinking).
>
> **The warmth you feel standing nearby: radiation** (no contact needed).
>
> One everyday scene, all three processes at once.

---

<!-- ===== Water cycle ===== -->

Name the four main steps of the **water cycle** and what drives it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Evaporation → condensation → precipitation → flow/seepage back to water bodies**, with **transpiration** from plants adding vapour too.
>
> **Driven by the Sun** — its heat is what evaporates water from oceans, rivers and lakes in the first place.

---

Why do wet clothes dry faster on a sunny day?

> [!ANSWER]
> The Sun's heat makes **evaporation faster** — more water turns to vapour in the same time.
>
> It's the same process that lifts water from oceans and lakes into the sky, just on a clothesline.

---

Why does water vapour turn into clouds as it rises?

> [!ANSWER]
> As the vapour rises it **cools down**, and cooling makes it **condense** back into tiny water droplets — which we see as **clouds**.
>
> When those clouds release the water as **rain, snow or hail**, that's **precipitation**.

---

What two things does the water cycle achieve?

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** It **redistributes and replenishes** water in rivers, lakes and oceans — melted snow flows down as rivers in summer and is restored by fresh snowfall in winter.
>
> **2.** It **conserves the total amount of water on Earth** — water is moved around and changed in state, but none is lost.

---

Who was **Varahamihira**?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **sixth-century CE** astronomer and mathematician from Ujjaini (modern **Ujjain**, Madhya Pradesh).
>
> In his *Brihatsamhita* he gave methods for **predicting seasonal rainfall**, based on cloud formation, wind patterns and the positions of stars and the Moon.
