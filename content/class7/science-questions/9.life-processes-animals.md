<!-- ===== The canal ===== -->

List the parts of the alimentary canal in order, and name the two associated organs.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mouth → oesophagus (food pipe) → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → rectum → anus.**
>
> Associated organs: the **liver** and the **pancreas** — they aren't part of the tube, but they pour digestive secretions into it.

---

What is **digestion**, and why is it necessary at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> Breaking **complex food components** (carbohydrates, proteins, fats) into **simpler forms**.
>
> **Why necessary:** the body can't use or absorb the complex forms — only simple ones can pass from the intestine into the blood and be carried to the cells.

---

What is **mechanical digestion**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The initial breakdown of food into **fine pieces** by crushing and chewing with the teeth.
>
> It doesn't change what the food *is* — it just makes the pieces small enough for the digestive juices to work on efficiently.

---

<!-- ===== Saliva & the iodine test ===== -->

Why does a chapati begin to taste sweet if you chew it long enough?

> [!ANSWER]
> Chapati contains **starch** (a carbohydrate). **Saliva contains a digestive juice that breaks starch down into sugar**.
>
> Chew long enough and enough sugar forms for your tongue to taste it — digestion is literally starting in your mouth.

---

Test tube A has boiled rice; test tube B has boiled rice chewed for 30–60 seconds. Iodine is added to both. Predict and explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A turns blue-black** — iodine gives a blue-black colour with **starch**, so starch is present.
>
> **B shows no colour, or only a faint one** — the starch has already been **broken down into simple sugars by the saliva**.
>
> A faint colour means a little starch remains; chewing for longer would break down more.

---

<!-- ===== Down the canal ===== -->

How does food move down the food pipe? Does it need gravity?

> [!ANSWER]
> The walls **contract and relax in a wave-like motion**, pushing the food along.
>
> **No, gravity isn't needed** — that wave happens throughout the whole alimentary canal, which is why you could swallow lying down.

---

Name the three secretions in the stomach and the job of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Digestive juice** — breaks down **proteins**.
>
> **Acid** — helps break down proteins **and kills many harmful bacteria**.
>
> **Mucus** — **protects the stomach lining** from being damaged by its own acid.

---

Why doesn't the stomach's acid damage the stomach itself?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because of the **mucus**, which coats and protects the inner lining.
>
> Without it the acid strong enough to digest proteins would start digesting the stomach wall — which is itself made of protein.

---

How did Dr **William Beaumont** learn about digestion in the stomach?

> [!ANSWER]
> In **1822** he treated **Alexis St. Martin**, who had been shot in the stomach. The wound never fully healed, leaving a **small permanent hole**.
>
> Through it Beaumont could **watch digestion happening** — testing how different foods were broken down, and even how emotions affected it.

---

<!-- ===== Small intestine ===== -->

How long is the small intestine, and why is it called "small" when it's the longest part?

> [!ANSWER]
> About **6 metres** — the **longest** part of the alimentary canal.
>
> "Small" refers to its **width, not its length**: it is narrower than the large intestine. (The large intestine is only ~1.5 m but is **wider**.)

---

Name the three sources of digestive juices in the small intestine.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** Its own **inner lining** · **2.** the **liver** (bile) · **3.** the **pancreas** (pancreatic juice).

---

Give two jobs of **bile**, and say why its basic nature matters.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** It **neutralises the acid** arriving from the stomach — bile is **mildly basic**, so this is a **neutralisation reaction** (Chapter 2).
>
> **2.** It **breaks fats into tiny droplets**, making them easier to digest — smaller droplets give the digestive juices more surface to act on.

---

The inner lining of the small intestine has thousands of **finger-like projections**. What are they for?

> [!ANSWER]
> They greatly **increase the surface area** for absorbing nutrients.
>
> **Why that matters:** absorption happens across the lining's surface, so more surface in the same length of intestine means far more nutrients passing into the blood.

---

What is **absorption**, and where do the absorbed nutrients go?

> [!ANSWER]
> The passage of **digested nutrients from the small intestine into the blood** in the vessels of its walls.
>
> The blood then carries them to **all parts of the body**, where they provide energy and support growth and repair.

---

What is **celiac disease**, and which grains are a safe alternative?

> [!ANSWER]
> A condition where the body **reacts to gluten** (a protein in wheat, barley and rye), damaging the **inner lining of the small intestine** so nutrients can't be absorbed properly.
>
> The only management is **avoiding gluten**. **Millets — jowar, bajra, ragi — are naturally gluten-free.**

---

<!-- ===== Large intestine ===== -->

What does the large intestine do, and what is **egestion**?

> [!ANSWER]
> It **absorbs water and some salts** from the undigested food, making the waste **semi-solid** — this is **stool**, stored in the **rectum**.
>
> **Egestion** is the expelling of that waste through the **anus**.

---

Why are fibre-rich and fermented foods good for digestion?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fibre** (fruits, vegetables, whole grains) makes the **stool easier to pass**.
>
> The large intestine also contains helpful **bacteria** that break down fibre and produce essential nutrients — **fermented foods** like curd, buttermilk, kanji, pickles, gundruk and poita bhat support them.

---

<!-- ===== Other animals ===== -->

What is **rumination**, and name two ruminants.

> [!ANSWER]
> Grass-eaters partially chew grass and swallow it; it is **partially digested in the stomach**, then brought **back to the mouth** for thorough chewing before passing down again.
>
> **Cows and buffaloes** — a cow spends about **8 hours a day** chewing.

---

Birds have no teeth. How do they break down food?

> [!ANSWER]
> In a chamber called the **gizzard**, whose walls **contract and relax** to grind the food — often helped by **grit (small stones)** the bird deliberately swallows.
>
> The stones do the job teeth would: a grinding surface.

---

<!-- ===== Breathing vs respiration ===== -->

What is the difference between **breathing** and **respiration**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Breathing** = inhaling and exhaling air — a **physical** process.
>
> **Respiration** = using oxygen to break down glucose and **release energy** — a **chemical** process happening inside the body.
>
> Breathing **supplies** the oxygen; respiration **spends** it.

---

Write the word equation for respiration.

> [!ANSWER]
> **glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy**
>
> The energy released is what lets us walk, run, play and think.

---

Trace the path of inhaled air, and say why we should breathe through the nose.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Nostrils → nasal passages → windpipe → two branches → finer branches → alveoli** (in the lungs, protected by the rib cage).
>
> **Breathe through the nose** because the **tiny hair and mucus in the nostrils trap dust and dirt** — the mouth has no such filter.

---

Where does gas exchange take place, and what makes that structure suitable?

> [!ANSWER]
> In the **alveoli** — small balloon-like sacs at the ends of the finest branches.
>
> They have **thin walls surrounded by fine blood vessels**, so carbon dioxide can pass out of the blood into the alveoli and oxygen can pass from the alveoli into the blood.

---

Describe what the **ribs** and **diaphragm** do during inhalation and exhalation.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Inhalation:** ribs move **up and outwards**, diaphragm moves **down** → **more space** in the chest → air is drawn **in**.
>
> **Exhalation:** ribs move **down and inwards**, diaphragm moves **up** → **less space** → air is pushed **out**.

---

In the bottle-and-balloons model of breathing, what do the **balloons** and the **rubber sheet** represent?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Balloons = the lungs.** **Rubber sheet = the diaphragm.**
>
> Pulling the sheet down inflates the balloons (inhalation); releasing it upwards deflates them (exhalation) — exactly how the diaphragm changes the space in the chest.

---

You blow through a straw into lime water in test tube B, while ordinary air is pushed into test tube A with a syringe. What happens, and what does it prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **B turns milky; A does not.**
>
> Lime water turns milky with **carbon dioxide**, so **exhaled air contains much more carbon dioxide than inhaled air** — proof that the body produces it and breathes it out.

---

Give the approximate percentages of oxygen and carbon dioxide in inhaled and exhaled air. What does this show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Inhaled:** ~**21%** oxygen, ~**0.04%** carbon dioxide.
>
> **Exhaled:** ~**16–17%** oxygen, ~**4–5%** carbon dioxide.
>
> It shows that **not all the oxygen is used up** — exhaled air still holds plenty, which is why mouth-to-mouth resuscitation can save a life.

---

<!-- ===== Breathing in other animals ===== -->

How do fish, frogs and earthworms breathe?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fish:** through **gills**, richly supplied with blood vessels, exchanging gases with the **oxygen dissolved in water**.
>
> **Frogs:** **tadpoles use gills**; **adults use lungs** on land and **skin** for gas exchange in water — an adaptation to living in both places.
>
> **Earthworms:** through their **moist skin**.

---

<!-- ===== Circulation ===== -->

What does the **circulatory system** consist of, and what does it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **heart, blood and blood vessels**.
>
> The heart **pumps blood** through the vessels, delivering **oxygen and nutrients** to every part of the body and **carrying waste products away**.

---

Why is smoking harmful both to the smoker and to those nearby?

> [!ANSWER]
> It **damages the lungs**, raising the risk of **lung cancer** and other respiratory illness, and causes persistent coughing and frequent infections.
>
> It also releases **toxic chemicals into the air**. Others who inhale it experience **passive smoking**, which is especially dangerous for **children, pregnant women and the elderly**.
