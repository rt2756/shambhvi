<!-- ===== The test ===== -->

What is the **one question** that decides whether a change is physical or chemical?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Is a new substance formed?**
>
> **No new substance** (only shape, size or state changed) → **physical**. **A new substance formed** → **chemical**.

---

Classify: **(a)** crushing chalk to powder · **(b)** rusting of iron · **(c)** melting ice · **(d)** burning magnesium ribbon.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a) Physical** — still chalk, just smaller pieces.
>
> **(b) Chemical** — a new brown substance (**rust**, iron oxide) forms.
>
> **(c) Physical** — still water, in a different state.
>
> **(d) Chemical** — a new white substance (magnesium oxide) forms.

---

"A change you can't undo must be chemical." Show with two examples that this is **wrong**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Crushing chalk into powder** can't be undone, but no new substance forms → still **physical**.
>
> **Chopping vegetables** likewise — irreversible, but physical.
>
> Reversibility and new-substance-formation are **two separate questions**. Only the second one decides physical vs chemical.

---

<!-- ===== Lime water and CO2 ===== -->

You blow through a straw into (A) tap water and (B) lime water. What happens in each, and which is a chemical change?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A:** bubbles only, water looks unchanged → **physical**.
>
> **B:** bubbles, and the lime water turns **milky**; a white solid settles → a **new substance** formed → **chemical**.
>
> The new substance is **calcium carbonate**, which is insoluble in water — undissolved white solid is exactly what "milky" looks like.

---

Write the reaction between lime water and the carbon dioxide you breathe out.

> [!ANSWER]
> **calcium hydroxide (lime water) + carbon dioxide → calcium carbonate + water**
>
> Because only carbon dioxide does this, **"lime water turns milky" is the standard test for carbon dioxide**.

---

Vinegar is added to baking soda; the gas produced is passed through lime water, which turns milky. Name the gas and say what kind of change occurred.

> [!ANSWER]
> The milky lime water identifies the gas as **carbon dioxide**.
>
> A **new substance** (the gas) was produced → **chemical change**.

---

<!-- ===== Combustion ===== -->

Define **combustion** and name three combustible substances.

> [!ANSWER]
> A chemical reaction in which a substance reacts with **oxygen**, producing **heat and/or light**.
>
> Combustible substances (fuels): **wood, paper, cotton, kerosene** (among others).

---

Write the reaction for burning magnesium ribbon, including what is given out.

> [!ANSWER]
> **magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide + heat + light**
>
> Heat and light given out is the signature of combustion; the white powder is the new substance that makes it a chemical change.

---

Name the **three requirements** for combustion (the fire triangle).

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. Fuel** (a combustible substance) · **2. Oxygen** · **3. Heat** enough to reach the ignition temperature.
>
> **Why a triangle:** all three legs are needed at once. Remove **any one** and the fire stops — which is how every method of putting out a fire works.

---

What is **ignition temperature**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **lowest temperature at which a substance catches fire**.
>
> Below it a substance can sit in oxygen forever without burning; at or above it, it ignites.

---

A sheet of paper sits in air for months without burning, yet catches fire in a minute under a magnifying glass in sunlight. Explain using the fire triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> Lying in air, the paper already has **fuel** and **oxygen** — the missing leg is **heat**.
>
> The magnifying glass focuses sunrays onto a tiny bright spot, heating the paper until it passes its **ignition temperature** — then it smokes and burns, with no flame ever touching it.
>
> A lit matchstick does it instantly because the match is already hotter than the paper's ignition temperature.

---

Two identical lit candles: one is covered with a glass tumbler. Predict and explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **covered** candle goes out after a short while; the open one keeps burning.
>
> The tumbler cuts off the **continuous supply of air**, so the **oxygen** leg runs out.
>
> Proof of what happened: put lime water in the dish — it turns **milky**, showing **carbon dioxide** was formed from the wax's carbon and the air's oxygen.

---

A person's clothes catch fire. What should you do, and which leg of the fire triangle does it remove? What must you *not* use?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrap them in a blanket or thick cloth** — this cuts off the **air (oxygen)**, so the fire is starved out.
>
> ⚠️ **Never use a synthetic blanket or cloth** — it can **melt and stick to the skin**, making the injury far worse.

---

<!-- ===== The candle: both at once ===== -->

Which changes in a burning candle are physical and which are chemical?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Physical:** the wax **melting**, flowing, **solidifying** again, and **evaporating** — it's still wax throughout.
>
> **Chemical:** the wax **vapour burning** in the flame — new substances form.
>
> So a single candle shows **both kinds of change happening together** — which is exactly why Michael Faraday used it to teach science.

---

<!-- ===== Reversible & desirable ===== -->

Which of these can be reversed: melting ice · making popcorn · boiling water · chopping vegetables?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Reversible:** melting ice (refreeze it), boiling water (condense the vapour).
>
> **Not reversible:** popcorn, chopped vegetables — no way back to the original corn kernel or whole vegetable.

---

Give an example of a change that is **undesirable in one situation but desirable in another**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Food decaying.** In storage it's undesirable — the food is spoiled and wasted.
>
> But decomposing food waste into **compost** is desirable — it feeds the soil.
>
> The chemistry is the same; only the **situation** decides whether we want it.

---

<!-- ===== Weathering & erosion ===== -->

What is **weathering**? Give one physical and one chemical example.

> [!ANSWER]
> The breaking down of rocks (in place) by physical and chemical changes — it eventually forms **soil**.
>
> **Physical:** water **freezing inside cracks**, temperature changes, or growing tree roots splitting rock into **sediments**.
>
> **Chemical:** water reacting with black **basalt** rock, whose iron becomes **iron oxide** — leaving a **red** layer.

---

What is **erosion**, and how is it different from weathering?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Erosion** is the **carrying away** of broken rock, soil and sediment by natural forces — **wind and flowing water**. It's a **physical** change.
>
> **The difference: weathering breaks it, erosion moves it.** Weathering happens where the rock stands; erosion is the transport afterwards.
>
> It's why river pebbles are smooth — constant flowing water wearing them down.

---

Why do sediments pile up at the bottom of a lake or ocean, and what happens to them over time?

> [!ANSWER]
> Erosion carries material along while the water or wind is fast. Where the **speed drops** — in a lake or ocean — the material can no longer be carried, so it **settles** at the bottom.
>
> Over **thousands of years** these sediments harden into **new rock**. These changes cannot be reversed.

---

Fireflies glow in the evening. Is this a chemical change, and what is it called?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — a chemical change**, producing **light without heat**. It is called **bioluminescence**.
>
> A useful reminder that chemical changes don't have to involve burning or heat.
