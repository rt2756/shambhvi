<!-- ===== Cell & battery ===== -->

On an electric cell, which part is the **positive terminal** and which is the **negative**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The small protruding **metal cap = positive (+)**; the flat **metal disc = negative (−)**.
>
> Every cell has exactly these two terminals — they are the two "doors" the current uses to leave and return.

---

What is a **battery**? How must the cells be arranged?

> [!ANSWER]
> A combination of **two or more cells**, connected so the **positive terminal of one cell touches the negative terminal of the next**.
>
> **Why this order:** all the cells then push the current in the **same direction** — like rowers rowing the same way. (Everyday speech also calls a single cell a "battery", as in a phone battery.)

---

Nihal put two cells into his torch and one cell was **reversed**. The lamp didn't glow. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Reversed, the cell pushes the current **against** its partner instead of with it — the two pushes cancel, so no useful current flows and the lamp stays dark.
>
> Fix: match **+ to −**, cell after cell, so the pushes add up.

---

Why do many devices use **two or more cells** instead of one?

> [!ANSWER]
> More cells provide energy for a **longer time and/or more energy** — some devices simply need a stronger push than one cell can give.

---

<!-- ===== Lamps ===== -->

Which part of an **incandescent lamp** actually glows, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **filament** — the thin wire fixed in the middle of the glass bulb.
>
> When current passes through it, the filament **gets hot and glows** — the light is a side-effect of the heat. Its two ends connect to the lamp's two terminals: the metal **case** and the metal **tip** at the base.

---

A torch lamp is connected properly to a working cell but does not glow. The lamp is "fused". What has happened inside?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **filament has broken**. A broken filament leaves a **gap** in the path, current cannot cross the gap, and no current means no heat and no light.
>
> A fused lamp is really a tiny broken bridge inside the bulb.

---

How is an **LED** different from an incandescent lamp? Name two differences.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. No filament** — an LED makes light without a glowing hot wire.
>
> **2. Direction matters** — an LED lets current through in **one direction only**. An incandescent lamp glows connected either way; an LED glows only when its **+ meets the battery's +**.

---

How do you identify the **positive terminal of an LED**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **longer wire is positive**, the shorter is negative.
>
> Connect long wire → battery +, short wire → battery −. Swap them and the LED simply stays dark (current can't pass backwards through it).

---

An LED connected to a battery does not glow, but the same battery lights an incandescent lamp. What is the most likely mistake?

> [!ANSWER]
> The LED is connected **the wrong way round** — its long (+) wire is on the battery's − side.
>
> The incandescent lamp can't reveal this mistake (it works both ways); the LED is the fussy one. **Swap the two LED wires** and it should glow.

---

<!-- ===== The circuit loop ===== -->

What is an **electrical circuit**, and when does the lamp glow?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **complete, unbroken path**: one terminal of the cell → wire → lamp → wire → back to the other terminal.
>
> The lamp glows **only while current flows**, and current flows **only when the loop is complete**. One gap anywhere — loose wire, broken filament, open switch — stops the current *everywhere* in the loop.

---

In which direction is the **electric current** said to flow in a circuit?

> [!ANSWER]
> From the **positive terminal to the negative terminal** of the cell (outside the cell, through the wires and lamp).
>
> That agreed direction is why the + and − markings matter when placing cells and LEDs.

---

A wire is connected from a cell's + terminal to a lamp, but the lamp's other terminal is left **unconnected**. Will the lamp glow?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The path dead-ends at the unconnected terminal — the loop never closes, so no current flows at all.
>
> Current is not like water filling a pipe from one end; it only moves when the **whole ring** is closed.

---

<!-- ===== Switch ===== -->

Describe the homemade **safety-pin switch** and how it turns a lamp on and off.

> [!ANSWER]
> A safety pin is pinned through its ring to a cardboard by one drawing pin; a second drawing pin sits where the pin's free end can **touch** it. One wire connects to each drawing pin.
>
> **ON:** rotate the safety pin to touch the second drawing pin → the metal pin **bridges the gap** → circuit **closed** → current flows → lamp glows.
>
> **OFF:** swing it away → **gap** in the path → circuit **open** → no current → dark.
>
> A switch is exactly this: a movable bridge over a deliberate gap.

---

Does it matter **where** in the circuit the switch is placed?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No** — anywhere in the loop works.
>
> **Why:** the current needs the **entire** loop to be closed. A gap at any point — next to the cell, next to the lamp, anywhere — breaks the whole path equally. Home light switches work the same way, just packaged differently.

---

<!-- ===== Circuit diagrams ===== -->

What is a **circuit diagram**, and why do we use symbols instead of drawings?

> [!ANSWER]
> A representation of a circuit using **standard symbols** for each component (cell, lamp, switch, wire…).
>
> **Why symbols:** they're faster to draw, impossible to misread, and **standard across the world** — an engineer anywhere reads your diagram exactly as you meant it. Realistic torch sketches can't promise that.

---

In the **symbol for a cell**, which line is the positive terminal?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **long line = positive (+)**; the short line = negative (−).
>
> Memory hook: "**long**er line, **plus** sign has more ink too."

---

In the **LED symbol**, what do the triangle and the two small arrows mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **triangle points in the direction the current can flow** — a built-in reminder that an LED is one-way.
>
> The **two arrows pointing away** mean "light comes **out** of this component" (emitting).

---

<!-- ===== Conductors & insulators ===== -->

What is a **conduction tester**, and how does it classify materials?

> [!ANSWER]
> A cell + lamp circuit with **two free wire ends**. Touch the free ends to the two ends of an object:
>
> **Lamp glows → conductor** (current crossed the object and completed the loop) · **stays dark → insulator** (the object is the gap that stops the loop).
>
> The lamp is the verdict — the object is literally being asked to become part of the circuit.

---

Sort into conductors and insulators: **metal key, plastic scale, glass bangle, coin, rubber eraser, pencil lead, wooden stick, aluminium foil**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Conductors:** metal key, coin, aluminium foil (all metals) — and **pencil lead (graphite)**, the famous non-metal exception.
>
> **Insulators:** plastic scale, glass bangle, rubber eraser, wooden stick.
>
> The pattern: **metals conduct**; wood, plastic, glass, rubber, wax and paper block.

---

Why are electric wires made of **metal covered with plastic or rubber**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two jobs, two materials: the **metal core** is a conductor — it *carries* the current; the **plastic/rubber skin** is an insulator — it *stops* the current leaking out into your hand.
>
> Same reason electricians' tools have rubber grips: conductor where current should go, insulator where it must not.

---

<!-- ===== Safety ===== -->

Why must you experiment **only with cells and batteries**, never with the mains supply?

> [!ANSWER]
> The electricity from wall sockets and poles is **far more powerful** than a cell's — enough to badly injure or kill. That's what the danger signs on poles warn about.
>
> Torch cells give a small, safe push — all the circuit-building of this chapter works with them. **Even portable generators are dangerous.**
