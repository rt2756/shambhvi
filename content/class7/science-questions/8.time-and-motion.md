<!-- ===== Timekeeping ===== -->

What is the one idea every clock — ancient or modern — is built on?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A process that repeats continuously**, marking equal intervals of time.
>
> Sunrise/sunset for a day, a swinging pendulum, or the rapid vibrations of a quartz crystal or atoms — the technology changes, the principle doesn't.

---

Say what repeats or flows in each: **sundial · water clock · hourglass · candle clock**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sundial** — the position of a **shadow** cast by the Sun.
>
> **Water clock** — **water** flowing out of a marked vessel (or into a floating bowl until it sinks).
>
> **Hourglass** — **sand** flowing from one bulb to the other.
>
> **Candle clock** — a marked **candle burning** down.

---

The outflow water clock was replaced by the sinking-bowl **Ghatika-yantra**. What was wrong with the older design?

> [!ANSWER]
> As the water level in the vessel **dropped, the flow rate decreased** — so equal marks no longer meant equal times, and the clock ran inaccurately.
>
> The sinking bowl avoided this: the bowl simply filled through a fixed hole and sank, always taking the same time.

---

What was a **ghati**, and how many made a day?

> [!ANSWER]
> The time for the Ghatika-yantra's bowl to fill and sink — **24 minutes**.
>
> A 24-hour day was divided into **60 ghatis**. (Check: `60 × 24 min = 1440 min = 24 h` ✓)

---

What is remarkable about the **Samrat Yantra** at Jaipur?

> [!ANSWER]
> It is the **world's largest stone sundial** — built about 300 years ago at Jantar Mantar, **27 metres** tall.
>
> Its shadow moves about **1 mm per second**, and its scale is marked finely enough to read intervals as short as **2 seconds**.

---

<!-- ===== Pendulum ===== -->

Define **one oscillation** and the **time period** of a pendulum.

> [!ANSWER]
> **One oscillation:** the bob goes from its **mean position → one extreme → the other extreme → back to the mean position**. (Equivalently: from one extreme, across to the other, and back again.)
>
> **Time period:** the time taken to complete **one oscillation**.

---

Does the time period of a pendulum depend on the **mass of the bob**? On its **length**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mass: NO.** **Length: YES.**
>
> So all pendulums of the **same length** at the same place have the **same time period** — whatever the bob is made of.
>
> That constancy is precisely what makes a pendulum good for measuring time.

---

A pendulum takes **20 seconds for 10 oscillations**. Find its time period. Why time 10 rather than 1?

> [!ANSWER]
> Time period = `20 ÷ 10 = ` **2 s**.
>
> **Why 10:** your reaction time in starting and stopping the watch is an error of maybe a few tenths of a second. Timing 10 oscillations and dividing by 10 **divides that error by 10 too** — a far more accurate result than timing a single swing.

---

What did Galileo notice about a swinging lamp, and what did he use as his clock?

> [!ANSWER]
> Sitting in a church, he noticed the lamp took the **same time for each swing**, no matter how wide the swing.
>
> He timed it **using his own pulse**. Experimenting further, he concluded a pendulum of a given length always takes the same time for one oscillation — the idea **Christiaan Huygens** turned into the pendulum clock (invented 1656).

---

<!-- ===== Units ===== -->

What is the **SI unit of time** and its symbol? Give the relations to minutes and hours.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **second**, symbol **`s`**.
>
> `60 s = 1 min` and `60 min = 1 h`.

---

Correct these: **`5 Sec.`** · **`3 hrs`** · **`10m/s`**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`5 s`** — lowercase, no full stop, and "sec" is not a valid symbol.
>
> **`3 h`** — "hrs" is incorrect; symbols are singular and lowercase.
>
> **`10 m/s`** — always leave a **space** between the number and the unit.

---

<!-- ===== Speed ===== -->

Write the formula for speed and give its SI unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Speed = total distance covered ÷ total time taken**
>
> SI unit: **metre/second (m/s)**. Also commonly **km/h**.

---

Two runners cover different distances. Why can't you tell who is faster from the times alone?

> [!ANSWER]
> A longer time might just mean a **longer distance**, not slower running.
>
> Speed fixes this by putting both on the same footing — **distance covered in one unit of time** — so races of any length can be compared fairly.

---

Swati's school is **3.6 km** away and she cycles there in **15 minutes**. Find her speed in **m/s**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Convert both units first: `3.6 km = 3600 m` and `15 min = 15 × 60 = 900 s`.
>
> Speed = `3600 ÷ 900 = ` **4 m/s**
>
> The conversion must come first — mixing km with minutes would give a number in neither unit.

---

A bus travels at **50 km/h for 2 hours**. How far does it go?

> [!ANSWER]
> Rearrange the formula: **distance = speed × time**
>
> `= 50 × 2 = ` **100 km**

---

A train travels at **90 km/h**. How long does it take to cover **360 km**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **time = distance ÷ speed** = `360 ÷ 90 = ` **4 h**
>
> Sense-check: at 90 km each hour, four hours gives 360 km ✓

---

A car covers **150 km in 3 hours**. Find its speed in km/h, then in m/s.

> [!ANSWER]
> Speed = `150 ÷ 3 = ` **50 km/h**.
>
> In m/s: `50 km = 50 000 m` and `1 h = 3600 s` → `50000 ÷ 3600 ≈ ` **13.9 m/s**.

---

Why is the speed we calculate really the **average speed**?

> [!ANSWER]
> An object hardly ever moves at one steady speed for the whole journey — it speeds up and slows down along the way.
>
> Dividing **total distance by total time** smooths all of that into a single number: the speed it *would* have needed to keep constantly to make the same journey in the same time.

---

What do a **speedometer** and an **odometer** each measure?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Speedometer** — the vehicle's **speed**, in km/h, right now.
>
> **Odometer** — the total **distance travelled**, in km.

---

<!-- ===== Uniform vs non-uniform ===== -->

Define **uniform** and **non-uniform linear motion**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Uniform linear motion:** moving along a **straight line at constant speed** — covering **equal distances in equal intervals of time**.
>
> **Non-uniform linear motion:** moving along a straight line but with **changing speed** — covering **unequal distances in equal intervals of time**.

---

A train leaves station A slowly, speeds up, runs steadily from B to C, then slows to a stop at D. Which parts are uniform?

> [!ANSWER]
> **B to C is uniform** — constant speed along a straight track.
>
> **A to B (speeding up) and C to D (slowing down) are non-uniform** — the speed is changing.

---

Train X covers 8 km in every 10-minute interval. Train Y covers 6 km, then 9 km, then 7 km. Which is in uniform linear motion?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Train X** — **equal distances in equal intervals of time** is exactly the test for uniform motion.
>
> Train Y's distances vary between intervals, so its speed is changing → **non-uniform**.

---

Why is uniform linear motion called an **idealisation**?

> [!ANSWER]
> In everyday life we **seldom** find anything moving at a truly constant speed over long distances or long intervals — traffic, friction and turns all change the speed.
>
> That is exactly why we work with **average speeds** instead.
