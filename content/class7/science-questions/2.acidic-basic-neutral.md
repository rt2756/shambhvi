<!-- ===== The three families ===== -->

Sort into **acidic / basic / neutral**: lemon juice, soap solution, salt solution, vinegar, lime water, sugar solution.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Acidic:** lemon juice, vinegar (contain acids, taste sour).
>
> **Basic:** soap solution, lime water (bitter, soapy feel).
>
> **Neutral:** salt solution, sugar solution (neither family — they leave both litmus papers unchanged).

---

Why should you **never taste or touch** a substance to find out its nature?

> [!ANSWER]
> An unknown substance could be harmful — strong acids and bases can burn skin, and many chemicals are poisonous.
>
> That is exactly the job of **indicators**: they announce the nature by a **colour change**, keeping your tongue and fingers out of the experiment.

---

Bitter gourd (karela) tastes bitter. Is it basic?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Bases *generally* taste bitter, but the arrow doesn't reverse: **not everything bitter is a base**.
>
> Taste is only a rough clue; the reliable test is an indicator — and bitter gourd doesn't turn red litmus blue.

---

<!-- ===== Litmus ===== -->

Where does **litmus** come from, and in what forms is it available?

> [!ANSWER]
> From **lichens** — an organism that is a partnership of a **fungus and an alga**, growing on rocks and trees where the air is clean.
>
> It is used as a **solution** or as **paper strips**, in two colours: **blue and red**.

---

A drop of a liquid turns **blue litmus paper red**. What is the liquid's nature? Give two examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Acidic** — acid turns **blue → red**.
>
> Examples from the chapter: lemon juice, amla juice, tamarind water, vinegar.

---

A drop of another liquid turns **red litmus paper blue**. Its nature? Two examples?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Basic** — base turns **red → blue** (the opposite swap).
>
> Examples: soap solution, baking soda solution, lime water, washing powder solution.

---

Tap water changes **neither** litmus paper. What does that tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> It is **neutral** — neither acidic nor basic.
>
> An indicator only reacts to the two "strong personalities"; a neutral substance gives it nothing to react to, so no colour change **is itself the result**.

---

What is an **acid–base indicator**?

> [!ANSWER]
> A substance that shows **different colours in acidic and basic solutions** — litmus, red rose extract and turmeric are the chapter's examples.
>
> Because the colour depends on the nature of what it meets, the indicator works as a messenger: look at the colour, know the nature.

---

How is **lime water** prepared? (Careful — not the fruit!)

> [!ANSWER]
> Mix **lime (chuna — calcium oxide)** in water, leave it about an hour, then **filter** — the clear filtrate is lime water (a solution of calcium hydroxide).
>
> ⚠️ The word trap: **lime the fruit** (like lemon) is *acidic*; **lime the mineral** makes a *basic* solution. Same word, opposite families.

---

<!-- ===== Other indicators ===== -->

How do you prepare **red rose indicator**, and what colours does it show?

> [!ANSWER]
> Crush fallen red-rose petals, soak them in **hot water** till the water is coloured, then **filter** — the filtrate is the indicator.
>
> It turns a shade of **red in acids** and **green in bases**, and stays unchanged in neutral substances — the same three-way sorting litmus gives, from a garden flower.

---

**Turmeric paper** stays yellow in lemon juice AND in sugar solution, but turns red in soap solution. What can turmeric detect — and what can't it?

> [!ANSWER]
> Turmeric turns **red only with bases** → it **detects basic substances**.
>
> But acids and neutrals both leave it yellow → it **cannot tell acidic from neutral** — two different natures, one same colour, no information.
>
> Litmus is the better all-rounder; turmeric is a one-question specialist.

---

Why does a **curry stain** on a white shirt turn **red when soap** is applied?

> [!ANSWER]
> Curry contains **turmeric (haldi)** — a natural indicator. Soap solution is a **base**, and base turns turmeric **red**.
>
> The stain fades back to yellow on rinsing as the soap washes away. Your laundry is accidentally running the chapter's experiment!

---

At the science fair, blank sheets sprayed with a liquid showed the words **"Welcome to the Wonderful World of Science."** Explain the trick.

> [!ANSWER]
> The words were written with **soap solution** — a base, invisible on white paper when dry.
>
> The spray was **turmeric solution** — an indicator. Wherever it met the basic writing, it turned **red**, revealing the message.
>
> Indicator + hidden base = invisible ink you can build from the kitchen.

---

What is an **olfactory indicator**? Describe the onion test.

> [!ANSWER]
> An indicator that signals with a **change of smell** instead of colour ("olfactory" = to do with smelling).
>
> Cloth strips kept overnight with chopped **onion** pick up its smell. Add a **base** (baking soda solution) → the onion smell **fades**; add an acid (tamarind water) → the smell **stays**.
>
> Useful exactly where colour can't help — for someone who can't see colours, or for coloured liquids that would hide a colour change.

---

**Hydrangea** plants give blue flowers in one garden and pink in another. What's going on?

> [!ANSWER]
> The flower colour reads the **soil's nature**: **acidic soil → blue** flowers, **basic soil → pink/red**.
>
> The plant is a living indicator — a gardener can even shift the colour by making the soil more acidic or more basic.

---

<!-- ===== Acids in foods ===== -->

Name the main acid in: **lemon, curd, tamarind, vinegar, amla**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Lemon → **citric acid** · curd → **lactic acid** · tamarind → **tartaric acid** · vinegar → **acetic acid** · amla → **ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)** (with citric acid too).
>
> All of them taste **sour** — the shared sourness is the acid family's signature.

---

<!-- ===== Neutralisation ===== -->

What is a **neutralisation reaction**? Write its word equation.

> [!ANSWER]
> Mixing an acid with a base **in sufficient quantity** so the result is **neither acidic nor basic**:
>
> **Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat**
>
> Note the **heat** — the mixture warms slightly; the reaction *releases* energy while the two opposites cancel.

---

Lemon-juice solution with blue litmus added turns red. **Lime water is added drop by drop** and the colour slowly returns to blue. Explain each colour change.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Red first:** the solution is acidic (lemon juice), and acid shows litmus red.
>
> **Back to blue:** each drop of lime water (a base) **neutralises** some acid. When enough base has gone in, the solution is no longer acidic — litmus swings to its basic colour.
>
> The litmus is a live commentator: it reports the takeover from acid to base as it happens.

---

<!-- ===== Neutralisation in daily life ===== -->

A red ant bites Keerthi and the skin stings. Her brother rubs **moist baking soda** on it. Why does this help?

> [!ANSWER]
> The ant injects **formic acid** — that acid causes the sting.
>
> Baking soda is a **base**, so it **neutralises** the acid on the skin: acid + base → salt + water, and the sting's cause is cancelled.
>
> Opposite nature, applied on purpose — neutralisation as first aid.

---

A farmer's soil has turned **acidic** from overusing chemical fertilisers, and crops are failing. What should be added — and what if the soil were **basic** instead?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Acidic soil → add lime** (a base) to neutralise it.
>
> **Basic soil → add organic matter / manure** — as it rots it releases **acids** that neutralise the base.
>
> Same principle both ways: measure the soil's nature, add the **opposite**. (If neutral soil still grows poor crops, suspect missing nutrients instead.)

---

Fish are dying in a lake near a factory. The factory's waste is acidic. What should the factory do, and why does it work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neutralise the waste with basic substances before releasing it.**
>
> Acidic waste makes the lake water acidic, which the fish can't survive. Adding a base cancels the acid (neutralisation), so the water reaching the lake is close to neutral — the nature fish are built for.

---

Who is called the **"Father of Modern Indian Chemistry"**, and what did he found?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Acharya Prafulla Chandra Ray (P.C. Ray)** — he established **India's first pharmaceutical company** in **1901**, and wrote the history of chemistry in ancient India.
