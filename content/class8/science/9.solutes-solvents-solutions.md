## Solutions

**Why does every sip of ORS taste the same?** Because sugar and salt in water form a mixture in which the components are **evenly distributed throughout** — a **uniform** mixture.

**Chalk powder, sand or sawdust in water** are **non-uniform** — the components are not evenly spread.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A SOLUTION is a uniform mixture.**
>
> - The **SOLUTE** is the substance that dissolves (usually the solid)
> - The **SOLVENT** is the substance it dissolves in (usually the liquid)
>
> **`Solute + Solvent → Solution`**

**When two liquids mix**, it isn't obvious which dissolves which. **The one present in the SMALLER amount is the solute; the one in the LARGER amount is the solvent.**

> [!NOTE]
> **The chashni of gulab jamun** is a large amount of sugar in a small amount of water — yet **water is still called the solvent and sugar the solute**, because sugar is the solid being dissolved.

**Air is a solution too** — a uniform mixture of gases.

## How much will dissolve?

**Add salt a spoon at a time to a glass of water, stirring each time.** At first every spoonful dissolves. Eventually the salt **stops dissolving and settles at the bottom**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Term | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Unsaturated solution** | more solute **can** still be dissolved at that temperature |
> | **Saturated solution** | the solute **stops dissolving** and settles at the bottom, at that temperature |
> | **Concentration** | the amount of solute in a fixed quantity of solution (or solvent) |
> | **Dilute** | **less** solute in a fixed quantity |
> | **Concentrated** | **more** solute in a fixed quantity |
> | **Solubility** | the **maximum** amount of solute that dissolves in a fixed quantity of solvent |

> [!WARNING]
> **"Dilute" and "concentrated" are RELATIVE terms.** One spoon of salt in water is dilute *compared to* two spoons — there's no absolute cut-off.
>
> **Compare properly:** 4 spoons in 50 mL is **more concentrated** than 2 spoons in 100 mL — that's 4 times as much per unit of water.

> [!TIP]
> **"Saturated" always needs a temperature attached.** A solution saturated at 20 °C is *not* saturated at 50 °C.

## Temperature and solubility

**Experiment:** dissolve baking soda in 50 mL of water at **20 °C** until some remains undissolved. Heat to **50 °C** — **the leftover dissolves**. Add more until it saturates again, then heat to **70 °C** — **that dissolves too**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **For most substances, SOLUBILITY INCREASES WITH TEMPERATURE.**
>
> **So a saturated solution at one temperature behaves as an unsaturated solution if you heat it.**

**That's the answer to "too much sugar in my tea":** heat it, or use hotter tea.

> [!NOTE]
> **Water is the main solvent in Ayurveda, Siddha and other traditional Indian systems of medicine.** Formulations also use **hydro-alcoholic extracts** of herbs, and traditional texts refer to **oils, ghee and milk** as solvents chosen to bring out a drug's therapeutic benefit.

> [!NOTE]
> **Asima Chatterjee** developed **anti-epileptic and anti-malarial drugs**, using solvents and solutions extensively to **extract and isolate compounds from medicinal plants**. She was the **second Indian woman to earn a Doctorate of Science** (after Janaki Ammal), the **first woman to receive the Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar Award** in chemical science, and was honoured with the **Padma Bhushan**.

## Gases dissolve too — but the opposite way

**Many gases dissolve in water, including oxygen** — only to a small extent, but **that dissolved oxygen sustains all aquatic life**: plants, fish and other organisms.

**It is a uniform mixture**, since the gases dissolve evenly.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The solubility of GASES generally DECREASES as temperature increases** — the exact opposite of most solids.
>
> **Cold water holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water.**

> [!WARNING]
> **This is why warming water is dangerous for aquatic life.** As a lake or river warms, it can hold **less dissolved oxygen** — so fish may suffocate even though nothing has been added to the water.

## Why things float or sink — density

**Husk floats while rice sinks; oil floats on water.**

People say floaters are "lighter" and sinkers "heavier" — but a wooden stick and an iron rod may be the **same size** and still feel very different. The real property is **DENSITY**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **`Density = Mass ÷ Volume`** — the mass present in a unit volume.
>
> **Density is INDEPENDENT of an object's shape or size**, but **depends on temperature and pressure**.

**Think of a crowded bus versus an almost empty one, or a dense forest versus a sparse one** — same space, different amount packed in.

### Units

| | |
|---|---|
| SI unit | **kilogram per cubic metre (kg/m³)** |
| For liquids | **g/mL** or **g/cm³** (more convenient) |
| Conversion | `1 kg/m³ = 1 g/L` |
| Handy fact | **1 mL of water ≈ 1 g** at room temperature, so 100 mL of water ≈ 100 g |

**Worked:** an aluminium block of mass **27 g** and volume **10 cm³** has density `27 ÷ 10 =` **2.7 g/cm³**.

**RELATIVE DENSITY** compares a substance to water:

```
relative density = density of the substance ÷ density of water at that temperature
```

Aluminium's relative density is **2.7** — a **pure number with no units**, meaning aluminium is 2.7 times denser than water.

> [!TIP]
> **A 1-litre pack of oil weighing only 910 g** tells you the oil's density is `910 ÷ 1000 =` **0.91 g/mL** — **less than water's 1 g/mL**. That is exactly why oil floats.

> [!WARNING]
> **Density alone does not decide floating.** A steel ship floats even though steel is far denser than water — its **shape** encloses a great deal of air, lowering the *average* density of the whole vessel.

## Measuring mass and volume

### Mass — the digital balance

1. Switch it on and check it reads **zero**; press **tare/reset** if not.
2. Place a **dry, clean watch glass** on the pan and note the reading.
3. **Tare again** so it reads zero with the watch glass on it.
4. Place the object on the watch glass and read the mass.

> [!TIP]
> **Why tare with the watch glass on?** So the balance subtracts the container's mass automatically, and you read the object alone.

> [!WARNING]
> **Most balances actually measure WEIGHT, not mass** — but their scales are marked in grams and kilograms. Only a **two-pan balance** truly compares masses, because it balances the object against known masses and gravity cancels from both sides.

### Volume of a liquid — the measuring cylinder

**Units:** SI is **m³**; smaller volumes in **dm³** or **cm³** (= **cc**); liquids in **litres** (1 L = 1 dm³) and **millilitres** (**1 mL = 1 cm³**).

**Finding a cylinder's least count** — exactly as with a thermometer:

1. **Range:** a cylinder marked 100 mL measures up to **100 mL**.
2. Difference between two big marks: **10 mL**. Divisions between them: **10**.
3. **Least count = `10 ÷ 10 =` 1 mL.**

| Cylinder | Typical least count |
|---|---|
| 10 mL or 25 mL | **0.1 mL** |
| 100 mL | **1 mL** |
| 250 mL | **2 mL** |
| 500 mL | **5 mL** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Choosing the right cylinder is a real skill.** To measure **70 mL**:
>
> - a **50 mL** cylinder needs **two steps** (50 + 20) — inconvenient and error-prone
> - a **250 or 500 mL** cylinder does it in one step but with **poorer accuracy** (bigger least count)
> - a **100 mL** cylinder is **best** — one step, finest division
>
> **Pick the smallest cylinder that still holds the whole amount.**

**The meniscus.** Liquid in a narrow tube forms a **curved surface**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Read the BOTTOM of the meniscus for water and other colourless liquids; the TOP for coloured liquids.**
>
> **And keep your eye LEVEL with that point** — looking from above or below gives a wrong reading.

> [!TIP]
> **Why measuring cylinders are tall and narrow, not short and wide.** In a narrow tube, a small change in volume produces a **large change in height**, so the graduations are far apart and can be read precisely. In a wide beaker the same volume change barely moves the level.
>
> The same reasoning explains why water bottles are tall cylinders rather than spheres.

### Volume of a solid

**Regular shape (cuboid):** `Volume = length × width × height`
A notebook 25 cm × 18 cm × 2 cm has volume `25 × 18 × 2 =` **900 cm³**.

**Irregular shape (a stone) — displacement:**

1. Fill a measuring cylinder to a known level, say **50 mL** (initial, A).
2. Tie the object with a thread and **lower it in slowly**.
3. Read the new level, say **55 mL** (final, B).
4. **Volume of the object = B − A =** `55 − 50 =` **5 mL = 5 cm³**

**Why it works:** the object **pushes aside exactly its own volume** of water, and the rise in level measures that displaced water.

**Now the density:** a stone of mass **16.400 g** and volume **5 cm³** has density
`16.400 ÷ 5 =` **3.28 g/cm³** — denser than water, so it sinks.

## Density in the world

> [!NOTE]
> **The Earth's layers get denser as you go inwards** — crust (**lightest**), upper mantle, lower mantle, outer core (liquid), inner core (solid).
>
> **Why:** as you go deeper, **both pressure and temperature rise sharply**, making the materials **heavier and more compact**.

**Temperature and density.** **Density generally DECREASES on heating and INCREASES on cooling.**

**Why (from Chapter 7):** heating makes the particles move faster and **spread farther apart**, so the **same mass occupies a larger volume** — and `mass ÷ larger volume` is a smaller density.

**Pressure and density.** **Pressure mainly affects GASES**; its effect on solids and liquids is negligible.

**Why:** gas particles have large spaces between them that pressure can squeeze out. Solid and liquid particles are already close together.

> [!NOTE]
> **Before large ships existed**, people crossed rivers and seas on **bamboo and wooden rafts**. **Bamboo is light and hollow**, so it floats easily; strong wooden logs were hollowed out into boats or lashed into rafts. Similar traditional boats are still used today — for transport and as tourist attractions.
