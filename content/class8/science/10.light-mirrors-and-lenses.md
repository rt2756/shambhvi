## Spherical mirrors

**Look at your face in a shiny metal spoon:**

| Surface | Image |
|---|---|
| **Inner** curved surface (curves inwards) | **inverted** |
| **Outer** surface (bulges outwards) | **erect but smaller** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Spherical mirrors have reflecting surfaces shaped like part of a hollow sphere.**
>
> - **CONCAVE mirror** — reflecting surface curves **inwards**
> - **CONVEX mirror** — reflecting surface curves **outwards**
>
> (In diagrams, the **non-reflecting** side is shown shaded.)

> [!NOTE]
> **Spherical mirrors are not made by slicing a glass sphere.** A flat piece of glass is **ground and polished** into a curved surface, then given a reflective coating (usually a thin layer of **aluminium**).
>
> **Coating on the OUTER curved surface → concave mirror. Coating on the INNER curved surface → convex mirror.**

**To tell them apart:** lay both flat with reflecting surfaces up and **look from the side, eye at their level** — you can see whether the surface curves in or out.

## Images formed by spherical mirrors

**Place an object near a concave and a convex mirror, then move it away.**

| Mirror | Object close | Object far |
|---|---|---|
| **Plane** | erect, **same size** | erect, same size — **never changes** |
| **Concave** | **erect and ENLARGED** | becomes **INVERTED**; enlarged at first, then gets **smaller and smaller** |
| **Convex** | **erect and DIMINISHED** | still erect and diminished, **slightly smaller** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A convex mirror ALWAYS gives an erect, diminished image** — no exceptions.
>
> **A concave mirror's image depends on the object's distance** — it can be enlarged or diminished, erect or inverted.
>
> **Lateral inversion is seen in all three types of mirror.**

> [!TIP]
> **You can identify a mirror just by looking at an image in it.**
> Same size, always erect → **plane**. Always small and erect → **convex**. Changes with distance, and flips → **concave**.

### Where they're used

**Concave mirrors** — because they **concentrate** light and **enlarge** nearby objects:

- **Reflectors** of torches, car and scooter headlights
- **Dental mirrors** — an enlarged view of teeth when held close
- The **main mirror of a reflecting telescope**

**Convex mirrors** — because they give an **erect image over a wide area**:

- **Side-view mirrors** of vehicles
- **Road safety mirrors** at intersections and sharp bends, so drivers can see round the corner
- **Surveillance mirrors** in big stores, to monitor a large area

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **"Objects in mirror are closer than they appear."**
>
> A convex side-view mirror makes everything look **smaller** — and we instinctively read "small" as "far away". The warning exists because the diminished image tricks your judgement of distance.
>
> **The trade-off is worth it:** the outward curve gives a **much wider view of the road behind**, which matters more than exact distance judgement.

## The laws of reflection

**Set-up:** a comb with all slits blacked out except one, a torch, and a plane mirror on white paper — giving a **thin beam of light**.

**Terms:**

```
      incident ray  \  | normal  / reflected ray
                     \ |i     r /
      ────────────────\|/──────────────  mirror
                       O   (point of incidence)
```

- **Incident ray** — the ray falling on the mirror
- **Reflected ray** — the ray coming back
- **Normal** — the line at **90° to the mirror** at the point of incidence
- **Angle of incidence (i)** — between the **normal** and the incident ray
- **Angle of reflection (r)** — between the **normal** and the reflected ray

> [!WARNING]
> **Both angles are measured from the NORMAL, not from the mirror surface.** This is the most common mistake in the whole chapter.
>
> If a ray makes 40° with the **normal**, it makes `90 − 40 =` **50° with the mirror**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **First law of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.**
>
> **`i = r`**
>
> If light falls **along the normal**, both angles are **zero** — the light comes straight back.

**The second law — the bending-paper experiment.** Put the mirror on a stiff sheet with part of it hanging over the table edge. The reflected beam is visible on that extended part. **Bend the extended part down and the reflected beam disappears; flatten it and the beam returns.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Second law of reflection: the incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray ALL LIE IN THE SAME PLANE.**
>
> **Why the experiment proves it:** bending the paper creates a **new plane**, breaking the alignment — so the reflected beam is no longer on the paper.

**Both laws are valid for ALL mirrors — plane, concave and convex.**

## Converging and diverging

**Shine MULTIPLE parallel beams** (a comb with many slits open) at each mirror:

| Mirror | Reflected beams |
|---|---|
| **Plane** | stay **parallel** |
| **Concave** | come closer — they **CONVERGE** |
| **Convex** | spread out — they **DIVERGE** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every single ray still obeys the laws of reflection.** It is the **curved shape of the surface** that makes the family of parallel rays converge or diverge — because the normal points in a different direction at each point of a curved mirror.

**The burning experiment.** Hold a **concave mirror** facing the Sun and adjust the distance to a sheet of paper until you get a **sharp bright spot**. Hold it steady — **the paper starts to smoke and burn**.

**Why:** the mirror **concentrates the Sun's light into one small point**, producing enough heat there to ignite the paper.

> [!WARNING]
> **Never look at the Sun, or at a mirror reflecting it.** Aim the reflected light only at paper — never at anyone's face or eyes.

> [!NOTE]
> **Solar concentrators** use mirrors and lenses to concentrate sunlight into a small area. The heat produces **steam to generate electricity**, or provides heat for **large-scale cooking** and **solar furnaces** — which are even used for **melting steel**.

## Lenses

**The water-drop experiment.** Rub a thin coat of oil or wax on a flat glass strip (so the water beads up), place a **drop of water** on it, and hold printed text underneath.

**The letters under the drop look ENLARGED.** The drop's surface is **curved outwards** — it is acting as a simple lens.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A LENS is a piece of transparent material (glass or plastic) with curved surfaces.**
>
> - **CONVEX lens** — **thicker in the middle**, thinner at the edges
> - **CONCAVE lens** — **thicker at the edges**, thinner in the middle

> [!TIP]
> **The key difference from a mirror:** light **passes THROUGH** a lens. **You see things THROUGH a lens, but IN a mirror.**

### Images through lenses

| Lens | Object close | Object far |
|---|---|---|
| **Convex** | **erect and ENLARGED** | becomes **INVERTED**; enlarged at first, then **diminishing** |
| **Concave** | **erect and DIMINISHED** | still erect and diminished |

> [!TIP]
> **Notice the pattern:** a **convex lens behaves like a concave mirror**, and a **concave lens like a convex mirror**. Both "concentrate" and both "spread" — one by reflection, the other by transmission.

### Converging and diverging

Shine **multiple parallel beams** through each:

| | Result |
|---|---|
| **Thin flat glass plate** | passes straight through, unchanged |
| **Convex lens** | **CONVERGES** the light → called a **converging lens** |
| **Concave lens** | **DIVERGES** the light → called a **diverging lens** |

**A convex lens can burn paper too** — exactly like a concave mirror, by concentrating sunlight to a point.

> [!WARNING]
> **Never look at the Sun directly or through a lens** — it can damage your eyes permanently.

### Where lenses are used

- **Eyeglasses** — to correct vision
- **Cameras**, including smartphone cameras
- **Telescopes** and **microscopes**
- **Magnifying glass** — a convex lens making print appear bigger
- **Your own eye** contains a **convex lens** — a remarkable one that **changes its shape**, which is what lets you switch between reading a book and looking into the distance

> [!TIP]
> **Why some reading glasses have a curved line across them.** They are **bifocals** — two different lens strengths in one, with the line marking the boundary. The upper part is for distance, the lower part for reading.
