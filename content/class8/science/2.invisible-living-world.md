## Why we couldn't see it for so long

The human eye can only see things above a certain size. Everything smaller stayed **completely unknown**.

Then people noticed that a **curved piece of glass** made small things look bigger. It was shaped like a **lentil seed** — thick in the middle, thin at the edges — so they called it a **lens** (Latin *lens* = lentil).

**Try it:** fill a round-bottom flask with water, cork it, and stand it on a page. **The letters look bigger** — the water-filled flask works as a magnifying glass.

> [!NOTE]
> **Robert Hooke, 1665.** In his book *Micrographia* he drew what he saw through an early microscope that magnified **200–300 times**. Looking at a thin slice of **cork**, he found it was full of tiny empty compartments like a honeycomb. He called each one a **cell** — **the first use of the word in science**.
>
> **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek**, in the same decade, ground much better lenses and was the **first to see and describe bacteria and blood cells**. He is called the **Father of Microbiology**.

## What a cell is

**All living beings are made of cells.** A cell is to an organism what a **brick is to a wall**.

### Seeing them yourself

**Onion peel:** pull the thin transparent layer off an onion, stain it with **safranin** (red), rinse, mount in **glycerin**, cover, and look. You see **nearly rectangular cells packed with no gaps**.

**Cheek cells:** scrape gently inside your cheek with a blunt toothpick, spread on a slide, stain with **methylene blue**, add glycerin, cover. You see **polygon-shaped cells**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why stain?** Cells are nearly transparent. A stain **increases contrast** so the parts stand out.
>
> **Why glycerin?** It stops the cells **drying out** and makes the image clearer.
>
> **Why lower the coverslip at an angle?** To avoid trapping **air bubbles**, which look like dark circles and hide the cells.

### The parts of a cell

| Part | What it does | Why that matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Cell membrane** | encloses everything; **porous** | lets useful materials **in** and waste **out**, and separates one cell from the next |
| **Cytoplasm** | jelly holding carbohydrates, proteins, fats, mineral salts | **most life processes happen here** |
| **Nucleus** | controls all the cell's activities and its growth | the cell's control room |
| **Cell wall** (plants, fungi, bacteria only) | rigid outer layer **outside** the membrane | gives **rigidity and strength** — which is why plant cells look firm and packed |
| **Chloroplasts** (plants) | plastids containing **chlorophyll** | make food by **photosynthesis**; non-green plastids store substances |
| **Vacuole** (large in plants) | big fluid-filled space | **stores substances, dumps waste, keeps the cell's shape** — the plant's support |
| **Mitochondria** | | release energy |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Plant cell vs animal cell — three differences:**
>
> | | Plant | Animal |
> |---|---|---|
> | Cell wall | **yes** | no |
> | Chloroplasts | **yes** | no |
> | Vacuole | one **large** one | absent or very small |
>
> Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus.

### Shape follows function

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A cell's shape and size are matched to the job it does.**

- **Cheek cells** — thin and flat → form a **protective lining**
- **Muscle cells** — **spindle-shaped**, thin and flexible → contract and relax, pushing food down the food pipe in waves
- **Nerve cells (neurons)** — very **long and branched** → reach distant parts of the body and **carry messages quickly**
- **Some plant cells** — long **tubes** → carry water up the plant

**In the stomach**, muscle cells churn the food while lining cells make digestive juices and acid — **different cells, different shapes, one organ**.

## Levels of organisation

```
Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ system → Organism
```

- **Tissue** — a group of **similar** cells
- **Organ** — different tissues working together
- **Organ system** — several organs doing one major job
- **Organism** — all the systems together

**Multicellular organisms** (plants, animals, humans) start from a **single cell — the egg** — which divides again and again.

> [!NOTE]
> **The largest cell known** is the **yolk of an ostrich egg** — a single cell **130–170 mm** across. The shell and the white are non-cellular extras: protection and nourishment.

## Microorganisms

**Living things too small to see with the naked eye** (*micro* = very small). Also called **microbes**.

**Found everywhere** — water, soil, air, on leaves and stems, in hot springs, in snow, and inside your own gut.

| Group | Cells | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | unicellular | spherical, rod, comma or spiral shaped; **no proper nucleus** |
| **Protozoa** (Amoeba, Paramecium) | unicellular | Amoeba has an **irregular, changing** shape; Paramecium moves using special structures |
| **Algae** | unicellular or multicellular | **green** — they contain chlorophyll and make their own food |
| **Fungi** | unicellular (yeast) or multicellular (mould) | branched filaments, **no chlorophyll** — cannot make their own food |

**To see them you need a microscope** magnifying **100–400 times**. A low-cost **foldable paper microscope** shows less detail than a laboratory one, but it puts the microscopic world within everyone's reach.

### Bacteria are different from everything else

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Bacteria have NO well-defined nucleus and no nuclear membrane.** Their genetic material sits loose in a region called the **nucleoid**.
>
> **This one feature separates bacteria from yeast, protozoa, algae, fungi, plants and animals** — all of which have a proper nucleus.

### Viruses aren't quite alive

**Viruses are acellular** — they aren't made of cells at all. **They can only multiply inside a living cell**, which they invade. They may infect plants, animals or even bacteria, and can cause disease.

> [!TIP]
> That's why viruses sit awkwardly on the boundary of "living": outside a host they do nothing at all.

## What microbes do for us

### They clean up — decomposition

**Bury fruit and vegetable peels under soil, wait 2–3 weeks, and they turn into dark, nutrient-rich manure.**

**Why:** fungi and bacteria in the soil **break complex substances down into simpler, nutrient-rich ones** — a process called **decomposition**. Those nutrients return to the soil, and plants use them again.

Microbes also decompose **dead animals and animal dung**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Without microorganisms, dead leaves, bodies and waste would simply pile up forever, and the nutrients locked inside them would never come back.** Decomposition is what makes the nutrient cycle a *cycle*.

**Manure forms best at an optimal temperature with the right moisture** — microbes need suitable conditions like any other living thing.

> [!NOTE]
> **Ancient Indian texts**, particularly the **Vedas**, use the word **'Krimi'** for tiny entities — both **'Drishya'** (visible) and **'Adrishya'** (invisible) — and the **Atharvaveda** discusses their beneficial and harmful effects.

### Biogas

Some bacteria and fungi live **without oxygen** and can decompose plant, animal and household waste. In doing so they release **biogas** — mostly **methane**, with carbon dioxide.

Biogas is used for **cooking, heating, generating electricity and even running vehicles**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Ananda Mohan Chakrabarty** (1938–2020) developed in 1971 a bacterium that could **break down oil spills**. It received a patent in 1980 — a **patent** being a right that stops others copying, using or selling an invention without permission. His work showed microbes could solve **pollution** problems.

### They make our food

**Yeast and dough.** Add yeast, sugar and warm water to flour and the dough **rises, turns fluffy and smells different** within hours. Dough without yeast does not.

**Why:** yeast is a **fungus** that **respires**, breaking down the sugar for energy. This releases **carbon dioxide**, whose bubbles make the dough soft and fluffy, plus a little **alcohol**, which gives the smell.

> [!TIP]
> **Why sugar?** It's the yeast's food. **Why warm water?** Yeast grows well in **warm** conditions — cold slows it right down.
>
> This is how **bread, cakes and bhatura** are made. **Lactobacillus** bacteria ferment the batter for **idli and dosa**.

**Curd.** Add a spoon of curd to **lukewarm** milk kept warm, and it sets into curd and turns slightly sour. The same in **cold** milk in a refrigerator does not set.

**Why:** **Lactobacillus** in the curd feeds on the milk sugar (**lactose**), multiplies, and produces **lactic acid** — which is what makes curd sour and makes it set. And these bacteria **grow well only in warm conditions**.

> [!TIP]
> **Yeast makes alcohol and CO₂; Lactobacillus makes lactic acid.** Same idea — fermentation — different product, so one puffs up bread and the other sets curd.

### They fertilise soil — Rhizobium

**Legumes** (beans, peas, lentils) have swollen **root nodules** containing **Rhizobium** bacteria.

**These bacteria trap nitrogen from the air and make it usable by the plant.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **This is why farmers grow legumes in rotation with other crops** — and why a bean farmer doesn't need nitrogen fertiliser while a wheat farmer does.
>
> Air is 78% nitrogen, but **plants cannot use nitrogen gas directly**. Rhizobium converts it into a form the plant can absorb, and the leftover enriches the soil for the next crop.

### Microalgae

**Microscopic plant-like organisms** in water, soil, air and on trees. They make their own food using sunlight, and in doing so **release more than half of the Earth's oxygen supply**.

- Food for many aquatic animals
- **Spirulina, Chlorella, Diatoms** used as health supplements and medicines
- Help **clean water** and are used to make **biofuel**

> [!NOTE]
> **Spirulina** is called a superfood — over **60% protein** by weight, very little fat or sugar, and a good source of **vitamin B12**. It can be grown at home in a glass tank of pond water kept out of direct sunlight, stirred twice a week, and harvested through fine cloth after 3–6 weeks.

**Pollution, climate change and habitat destruction threaten microalgae** — and with them, a large share of the planet's oxygen.

### Why don't microbes spoil pickles and murabbas?

**Because of the salt and sugar.** A **high concentration of salt or sugar** stops microorganisms growing — that's what makes them **preservatives**.

## The cell as the basic unit of life

- **Unicellular** — the whole organism is **one cell** doing everything needed to survive: bacteria, Amoeba, Paramecium, **yeast**.
- **Multicellular** — many cells, each **specialised**, cooperating: plants, animals, **mould**.

> [!TIP]
> **Specialisation is the advantage of being multicellular.** A single cell must do every job adequately; a body of many cells can have some doing nothing but carrying messages and others doing nothing but churning food — each far better at its own task.

**An electron microscope** magnifies about **10,00,000 times**, revealing structures inside the cell that an ordinary microscope cannot show.
