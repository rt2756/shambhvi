## Matter is made of tiny particles

**Break a stick of chalk again and again, then grind it in a mortar.** Every speck of powder is **still chalk** — grinding is a **physical change**, not a chemical one; only the **size** has changed.

**Imagine grinding forever.** Eventually you reach units that **cannot be broken down any further**. These are the **constituent particles** of chalk.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A constituent particle is the basic unit that makes up a larger piece of a substance.**
>
> One stick of chalk is made of an **enormous number** of them. So are the grains of sand and clay that rivers grind out of mountain rock.

**The sugar experiment.** Put sugar in water without stirring — the top layer isn't sweet. Stir until it dissolves and **the top layer tastes sweet**, but **no sugar can be seen**.

**Why:** the sugar has broken up into its **constituent particles**, far too small to see, and spread through the water.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Matter is composed of a very large number of extremely small particles** — so small they cannot be seen even through an ordinary microscope.

**Where did the sugar go?** Into the **INTERPARTICLE SPACES** — the gaps between the water particles.

> [!NOTE]
> **Acharya Kanad**, an ancient Indian philosopher, first proposed the idea of the **Parmanu (atom)** — that matter is made of tiny, indivisible, eternal particles. He wrote it in the **Vaisheshika Sutras**.

## What holds the particles together

**INTERPARTICLE ATTRACTIONS** are attractive forces between the constituent particles.

**Their strength depends on:**
- the **nature of the substance**
- the **interparticle distance** — and **even a slight increase in distance decreases the force drastically**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The strength of these forces decides the physical state of a substance.**

## Solids

**Definite shape AND definite volume.**

**Why:** the particles are **tightly packed** with **very strong** interparticle attractions, holding them in **fixed positions**. They can only **vibrate to and fro** about those positions — they **cannot move past each other**.

### Melting

```
solid heated
    ↓
particles vibrate more vigorously
    ↓
vibrations become so vigorous that particles leave their positions
    ↓
interparticle forces weaken
    ↓
LIQUID
```

**The MELTING POINT is the minimum temperature at which a solid melts to a liquid at atmospheric pressure.**

**Weak interparticle forces → low melting point. Strong forces → high melting point.**

| Solid | Melting point |
|---|---|
| Ice | **0 °C** |
| Urea | **133 °C** |
| Iron | **1538 °C** |

> [!TIP]
> **The melting point is a direct measure of how strongly the particles are held.** Iron's 1538 °C tells you its attractions are enormously stronger than ice's.

> [!WARNING]
> **Ice is an exception to the usual rule.** Normally a liquid's particles are farther apart than the solid's — but **ice's particles are farther apart than water's**, which is why ice floats.

## Liquids

**No definite shape, but a definite volume.**

**Experiment:** mark 200 mL in three differently shaped containers and pour the same water from one to the next.

- The **shape** changes every time → **no fixed shape**
- The **level stays at 200 mL** → **fixed volume**

**Why:** the particles are **free to move, but only within a limited space**. The attractions are **slightly weaker than in solids** but still strong enough to keep the particles close.

**Move your finger through water and it closes up again** — you displace it only **temporarily**. You could never do that to a solid.

### Boiling and evaporation

**The BOILING POINT is the temperature at which a liquid boils and turns to vapour at atmospheric pressure.**

At that temperature the particles move so vigorously that they **move apart, the attractions weaken, and particles escape** into the gaseous state.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Boiling vs evaporation:**
>
> | | **Boiling** | **Evaporation** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Temperature | only at the **boiling point** | at **any** temperature |
> | Where | **throughout** the liquid (bubbles form inside) | only at the **surface** |
> | Speed | **fast** | **slow** |
>
> That's why spilled water disappears without ever being heated.

## Gases

**No definite shape AND no definite volume.**

**Experiment:** trap incense smoke in gas jar A, place empty jar B on top, remove the separating plate.

**The smoke fills the whole of jar B as well.**

**Why:** gas particles **move freely in all directions** and the interparticle attractions are **negligible** — so a gas expands to occupy **all the available space**.

> [!TIP]
> **Smoke is a visible stand-in for the invisible.** The tiny smoke specks are constantly **hit by invisible gas particles**, and watching them move lets us see the motion of the gas. (Iodine vapour works too.)

**FLUIDS.** Liquids and gases both **flow** and **do not retain a fixed shape** — so both are classed as **fluids**, distinct from solids.

## Interparticle spacing — the key comparison

**Gases can be squeezed; liquids cannot.**

**Syringe experiment:** seal the nozzle with your thumb and push the plunger. **The air's volume decreases** — and springs back when you release it.
**Repeat with water: it is practically incompressible.**

**Why:** gas particles have **a great deal of space between them**, which external pressure can reduce. Liquid particles are already close together.

**Sugar-in-water experiment:** adding sugar raises the water level, but after it dissolves **the level drops again**.

**The volume of the solution is LESS than water plus sugar separately** — proving there **are spaces between the water particles**, which the sugar particles slip into.

**Compare with sand:** sand doesn't dissolve, so it just **settles at the bottom and takes up space**, increasing the total volume.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Even solids have some space between their particles** — and that space contains **nothing at all**, not air.

| | **Solid** | **Liquid** | **Gas** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Interparticle spacing** | **minimum** | a little more than solids | **maximum** |
| **Packing** | closely packed | a little loosely packed | free |
| **Interparticle attraction** | **maximum** | slightly weaker than solids | **minimum (negligible)** |
| **Movement** | negligible — only vibrations | restricted to a limited space | throughout all available space |
| **Shape** | definite | takes the container's shape | takes the container's shape |
| **Volume** | definite | definite | not definite |
| **Compressible?** | no | practically not | **yes** |

> [!WARNING]
> **"Particle" means different things in different contexts.** In air-pollution reports, **Suspended Particulate Matter (SPM)** means tiny **dust** particles floating in air — which are themselves made of an enormous number of constituent particles. Dust specks are gigantic compared to atoms and molecules.

## Particles are always moving

**Potassium permanganate experiment:** drop a grain into still water. Pink streaks spread out, and eventually **the whole tumbler turns a uniform pink** — with no stirring at all.

**Why:** the **water particles are in constant motion**. They first **pull the potassium permanganate particles out of the grain**, then **hit them repeatedly**, spreading them everywhere.

**Why sand doesn't dissolve:** in some substances the constituent particles are held together **so strongly that water particles cannot pull them out**.

### Temperature changes the speed

Drop the same grain into **hot, room-temperature and ice-cold water**:

| Water | Spreading speed |
|---|---|
| Hot | **fastest** |
| Room temperature | medium |
| Ice-cold | **slowest** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Heating makes particles move faster.** That single fact explains melting, boiling, evaporation and diffusion all at once.

### Gases move too

**Light an incense stick in one corner of a room** and shortly the fragrance reaches every corner.

**Why:** the **air particles are moving constantly**, hitting the fragrance particles and spreading them throughout the room. It's the same reason perfume reaches you from across a room.

> [!NOTE]
> **How soap cleans oil.** Many **soap particles surround the oil particles** on the fabric. **One end of a soap particle attaches to the oil; the other end mixes with the water** — so the oil is lifted off and washed away.

## The unifying idea: thermal energy

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **It is the THERMAL (heat) ENERGY of the particles that determines the physical state of matter.**

```
SOLID   — low thermal energy → particles stay close
          → strong attractions → motion limited to small vibrations

at the MELTING POINT, thermal energy is used up overcoming the attractions

LIQUID  — particles leave fixed positions; interparticle distance
          increases slightly → forces weaken → particles move around,
          but within a limited space

GAS     — enough energy to completely overcome the attractions
          → particles move freely in all directions
```

**Answering the opening questions:**

- **Why can you pile up sand but not water?** Sand is a **solid** — strong attractions hold each grain's particles in fixed positions, so a heap keeps its shape. Water's particles are free to move, so it flows and levels out.
- **Why does water take the shape of cupped hands and lose it when released?** A liquid has **no fixed shape** — its particles move freely and take whatever container is offered.
- **How does invisible air add weight to a balloon?** Because air is **matter** — made of particles, and particles have mass, whether or not you can see them.
