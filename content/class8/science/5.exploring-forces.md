## What a force is

**A force is a push or a pull on an object, resulting from the object's interaction with another object.**

**SI unit: the newton, symbol N** (small n for the unit, capital N for the symbol — units named after people follow this rule).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **At least TWO objects must interact for a force to exist.**
>
> When you push a table, your hand is one object and the table is the other. **You feel a force on your hand too** — and the moment you stop pushing, that force disappears.
>
> **Whenever two objects interact, EACH experiences a force from the other.**

## What a force can do

A force applied to an object may:

1. **Make it move from rest** — pushing a stationary box
2. **Change its speed** — a friend holding your bicycle from behind slows it
3. **Change its direction of motion** — hitting a moving ball with a bat
4. **Change its shape** — pressing an inflated balloon, rolling a chapati
5. **Cause several of these at once**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The rule runs backwards too: whenever speed, direction or shape changes, a force MUST be acting.** Nothing changes on its own.

> [!TIP]
> **Does an object at rest mean no force acts on it?** No — it means the forces on it are **balancing one another**. A book on a table is pulled down by gravity and pushed up by the table.

## Contact forces

**Forces that act only when the objects are physically touching.** The contact may be **direct** (your hand) or **indirect** (a stick, a rope).

### Muscular force

**The force produced by muscles contracting and elongating.**

Used in walking, running, lifting, pushing, jumping, stretching — by humans, animals, birds, fish and insects. Humans have long used **animals' muscular force** for ploughing and carrying.

> [!NOTE]
> **Muscular force works inside you too.** It chews food and pushes it along the alimentary canal, and the **contraction and expansion of heart muscles circulates the blood** — without which you couldn't survive.

### Friction

**A ball rolling on flat ground stops by itself. A bicycle slows when you stop pedalling — and stops sooner on a rough road than a smooth one.**

Since speed changes, **a force must be acting**. That force is **friction**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Friction is the force that comes into play when an object moves, or tries to move, over another surface.**
>
> **It always acts OPPOSITE to the direction of motion (or attempted motion)** — which is why it slows things down.
>
> It is a **contact force**, since it arises from two surfaces touching.

**Why friction exists:** **every surface has minute irregularities**, even ones that look perfectly smooth. When two surfaces touch, **the irregularities of one lock into those of the other**, opposing any attempt to slide.

**Friction depends on the nature of the surfaces** — it is **greater on rough surfaces**. Push the same object across glass, cloth, wood, tile and sand and it travels a **different distance each time**.

> [!TIP]
> **Friction acts in liquids and gases too.** That is why aeroplanes, ships, boats and high-speed trains are given **streamlined shapes** — to reduce the friction from the air or water they move through.

## Non-contact forces

**Forces whose effect is felt even when the objects are NOT touching.**

### Magnetic force

**The force exerted by a magnet on another magnet or on a magnetic material.**

- **Like poles repel** (N–N, S–S); **unlike poles attract** (N–S)
- Slide two ring magnets onto a vertical stick with **like poles facing**, and **the upper one floats** — held up by a force with no contact at all. Push it down and you feel the force resisting.
- **Reverse one magnet** and it drops onto the other instead.

### Electrostatic force

**Rub a plastic scale or straw with polythene and hold it near small pieces of paper — the paper jumps up and sticks to it.**

**Why:** when certain materials are rubbed together, **electrical charges build up on their surfaces**. These are **static charges** because they don't move by themselves; the object is then **charged**.

**A charged object exerts a force on an uncharged object** — without touching it.

**The two-balloon experiment:**

| Set-up | Result | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Two balloons rubbed with **the same** woollen cloth, hung near each other | they **move apart** | **like charges repel** |
| A rubbed balloon and the **woollen cloth** that rubbed it | they **attract** | **unlike charges attract** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **There are two kinds of static charge: positive and negative.**
>
> **Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.**
>
> **Both the rubbing object and the rubbed object become charged — with OPPOSITE kinds of charge.** That's why the balloon and the cloth attract while the two balloons repel.

**The force exerted by a charged body on another charged or uncharged body is the ELECTROSTATIC FORCE** — a non-contact force.

> [!TIP]
> **When these charges move, they constitute an electric current** — the same current that lights a lamp and produces the heating and magnetic effects of Chapter 4. Static electricity and current electricity are the same charges, still or moving.

### Gravitational force

**Throw a ball up — however hard — and it always comes back down.**

**The force with which the Earth attracts objects towards itself is the GRAVITATIONAL FORCE**, also called the **force of gravity** or simply **gravity**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Gravitational force is ALWAYS attractive** — unlike magnetic and electrostatic forces, which can attract **or** repel.

**Vertical motion.** Drop an object and it falls straight down, speeding up. Throw it straight up and it **slows down, stops momentarily at the top, changes direction, then speeds up coming down**.

> [!TIP]
> That momentary stop at the top is the answer to the swing question: **you feel "light" at the top because your motion has reversed there** — you were rising, and now you're falling.

## Weight and mass

**WEIGHT is the force with which the Earth pulls an object towards itself.**

Since weight is a **force**, its **SI unit is also the newton (N)**.

**Hang different objects from a spring** and the stretch differs each time — so **the Earth pulls different objects with different forces**.

### The spring balance

A spring fixed at one end with a hook at the other. Hanging an object stretches the spring, and the **amount of stretch gives the weight**, read off a scale marked in newtons (usually with a second scale showing mass in grams).

**Reading any instrument — the two things to check first:**

1. **The range** — a balance marked 0 to 10 N can measure at most **10 N**.
2. **The least count** — the smallest value it can read.

**Worked:** between two big marks the difference is **1 N**, and there are **5 small divisions** between them.
`1 N ÷ 5 =` **0.2 N** — the smallest weight this balance can read.

> [!WARNING]
> **Never hang an object heavier than the balance's maximum** — it can damage the spring permanently.

### Weight vs mass — the key distinction

| | **Mass** | **Weight** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | the **amount of matter** in an object | the **gravitational force** pulling it |
| Unit | gram (g), kilogram (kg) | **newton (N)** |
| Changes with place? | **No — same everywhere** | **Yes** — varies with gravity |

**The same 1 kg object:**

| | Earth | Moon | Mars | Venus | Jupiter |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mass** | 1 kg | 1 kg | 1 kg | 1 kg | 1 kg |
| **Weight** | 10 N | **1.6 N** | 3.8 N | 9 N | **25.4 N** |

> [!WARNING]
> **Everyday language gets this wrong on purpose.** We say "the weight of the wheat bag is 10 kg" — but **kg is a unit of mass, not weight**. In science you must use the right term with the right unit.
>
> In daily life we genuinely care about the **amount of matter** (mass), not the pull of the Earth — which is why the shop measures in kilograms.

**Since the Earth's gravity varies only very slightly from place to place, weighing an object is an acceptable practical way to find its mass** — with a spring balance, or by comparing against a known mass on a beam balance.

## Floating and sinking

**Push an empty capped bottle down into water and you feel an upward push; release it and it bounces to the surface.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The upward force applied by a liquid on an object is called UPTHRUST or BUOYANT FORCE.** All liquids do it.

**Two forces act on an object in a liquid:**

```
gravitational force  ↓  (Earth pulling it down)
buoyant force        ↑  (liquid pushing it up)
```

- **Gravity greater than buoyancy → the object SINKS**
- **The two equal → the object FLOATS**

**That's why the mug feels lighter inside water** — the buoyant force is partly cancelling the pull of gravity.

**The buoyant force depends on the density of the liquid** (covered in a later chapter).

> [!NOTE]
> **Archimedes' Principle.** When an object is fully or partly immersed in a liquid, it experiences an **upward force equal to the weight of the liquid it displaces**.
>
> - Weight of liquid displaced **less than** the object's weight → it **sinks**
> - Weight of liquid displaced **equal to** the object's weight → it **floats**

> [!NOTE]
> **A rock that floats: pumice.** Formed when lava full of gas and water vapour **cools quickly**, trapping tiny gas bubbles. The result is a light, porous rock **less dense than water** — so it floats.

## Answering the opening questions

- **Why is it harder to pedal uphill?** You must work against the **gravitational force** pulling you back down the slope, as well as friction.
- **Why is it easier to slip on a wet surface?** Water fills the surface irregularities, so they can no longer lock together — **less friction**.
- **Why does the wind push a cyclist back?** Air exerts a **frictional force** opposing motion; riding against the wind increases it.
- **Why do the bicycles speed up downhill without pedalling?** **Gravity** is pulling them down the slope.
