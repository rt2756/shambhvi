The book's page numbers carry a symbol each:

- **A root** at the bottom of left pages — the **solid foundation** of careful observation, evidence, tradition and heritage.
- **A kite** at the top of right pages — **curiosity taking flight** into the unknown.

> [!TIP]
> **Investigation works only when you have both.** Observation without imagination never asks a new question; imagination without observation never checks whether the answer is true.

## How to investigate anything — the puri example

*Why is one side of a puri thinner than the other? Why does it puff up at all?*

You don't need a laboratory. A **kitchen is a fine place to do science**.

### Step 1 — Ask a question you can actually test

Not "why is cooking interesting?" but **"what changes the way a puri puffs up?"** — something an experiment could answer.

### Step 2 — List what you can CHANGE (the variables you control)

- **thickness** of the rolled dough
- **size** of the circle
- **type of flour** (atta, maida, …)
- **temperature** of the oil
- **how you drop it in** — vertically? at an angle? slowly?

### Step 3 — List what you can OBSERVE or MEASURE

- Did it puff up? (**yes/no**)
- **How long** did it take to puff? (seconds)
- Does a very thick dough still give one thin side?
- Did the oil splatter, smell or smoke?

> [!TIP]
> **Some observations are yes/no; some are numbers.** Numbers are more useful when you have them, because they let you compare "how much", not just "whether".

### Step 4 — Change ONE thing at a time

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **This is the single most important rule in experimenting.**
>
> To test the effect of oil temperature, use dough circles of the **same thickness**, the **same flour**, dropped the **same way**. Only then can any difference be blamed on the temperature.
>
> **If you change two things at once, you can never tell which one caused the result.**

### Step 5 — Keep notes on everything

Not just the thing you're testing — the smell, the splatter, the smoke, anything odd. **Notes let you answer questions you hadn't thought of yet**, and they let someone else repeat exactly what you did.

### Step 6 — Let the results raise new questions

*Do puris puff better from fresh dough or stored dough? What happens if you prick a hole first?*

**That's the whole cycle: question → experiment → observation → better question.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Scientists still don't fully understand why a puri puffs.** A question being everyday doesn't make it easy — and it doesn't make it already answered.

## The habits worth keeping

1. **Start from something you actually noticed** — not from a textbook heading.
2. **Sharpen the question** until an experiment could settle it.
3. **Separate what you control from what you measure.**
4. **Vary one thing at a time.**
5. **Record everything, including the surprises.**
6. **Expect the answer to produce a better question.**
