## The magnetic effect of electric current

**The experiment.** Stretch a wire between two nails, put a **magnetic compass underneath**, and switch the circuit on and off.

| Switch | Compass needle |
|---|---|
| **ON** (current flowing) | **deflects** away from its usual direction |
| **OFF** (no current) | **returns** to its original direction |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When electric current flows through a conductor, it produces a MAGNETIC FIELD around it.** This is the **magnetic effect of electric current**.
>
> **The magnetic field disappears the instant the current stops.**

**Why the compass proves it:** a compass needle is a **tiny magnet**. It deflects only when there's a magnet — or a magnetic field — nearby. Nothing was moved, nothing was brought close; only the current changed. So **the current itself must be producing the field**.

**A magnetic field is the region around a magnet or a current-carrying wire where its magnetic effect can be felt** — for example by the deflection of a compass needle.

> [!TIP]
> This answers the chapter's opening question: **if you have no lamp, a compass will tell you whether current is flowing.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Hans Christian Oersted (1777–1851)** made exactly this discovery in **1820**. While giving a demonstration in Denmark he noticed that a nearby compass needle deflected **whenever a circuit was opened or closed**. He investigated, made sure the effect was real, and published — after which other scientists **repeated his experiment** to check they got the same result.
>
> **That repetition is not a formality.** A result only becomes accepted science once other people can reproduce it.

**Uses:** electromagnets, electric bells, motors, fans, loudspeakers.

## Electromagnets

**Wind a coil of insulated wire around an iron nail and connect it to a cell.**

- Current **ON** → the nail picks up iron paper clips like a magnet
- Current **OFF** → the clips **fall off**

**A current-carrying coil that behaves as a magnet is called an ELECTROMAGNET.**

### What the coil and core each contribute

| Set-up | Effect |
|---|---|
| Coil alone, current on | behaves as a magnet — deflects compass needles |
| Coil with an **iron nail** inside | **much stronger** — bigger deflection, and it attracts clips |
| Current off | magnetic effect **disappears** |

**For practical use, most electromagnets have an iron core to make them stronger.**

### An electromagnet has poles, like any magnet

Bring a compass to end A: whichever compass pole is **attracted** tells you the polarity, because **unlike poles attract**.

If the compass's **north** pole is attracted to end A, then **A is a south pole** — and B is then north.

### How to make it stronger — or flip it

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Strength increases with:**
> - **more current** (more cells in the battery)
> - **more turns** in the coil
> - an **iron core**
>
> **The poles reverse if you reverse the direction of the current.**

**Why more cells helps:** a single cell gives only a small current, so the field is weak — small deflection, few clips. A battery of more cells drives a larger current, giving a stronger field.

> [!TIP]
> **Electromagnet vs permanent magnet — the whole point is the switch.**
> A permanent magnet is always on. An electromagnet can be **turned on and off**, made **stronger or weaker**, and have its **poles reversed** — all electrically, with no moving parts.

### Lifting electromagnets

Strong electromagnets hung from **cranes**. The operator switches the current **ON** to lift iron and steel objects, and **OFF** to release them.

**Used in factories and scrap yards** to move, lift and sort heavy metal.

**Why an electromagnet and not a permanent magnet?** Because a permanent magnet could never **let go**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why is the Earth itself a magnet?** Deep inside, the **movement of liquid iron in the core creates electric currents**, and those currents generate a magnetic field — the same effect as in a wire, on a planetary scale.
>
> Many **migratory birds, fish and animals navigate by it**, and it **shields Earth from harmful particles from space**.

> [!NOTE]
> **The link runs both ways.** Just as electricity produces magnetism, **a moving magnet can produce an electric current**. That second half is what makes **power generators** possible, and you'll meet it in higher classes.

## The heating effect of electric current

**The experiment.** Stretch a **nichrome wire** between two nails and pass a current for about 30 seconds. Touch it briefly (never hold it).

**The wire feels warm.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When electric current flows through any conductor, the conductor faces RESISTANCE — opposition to the flow. This resistance converts some electrical energy into heat.**
>
> That warming is the **heating effect of electric current**.

**Why nichrome?** Different conductors offer **different amounts of resistance**. Nichrome offers **much higher resistance than copper** of the same size and length — so it heats up strongly while the copper connecting wires stay cool.

> [!TIP]
> **That's exactly why appliances use nichrome for the heating element and copper for the wiring.** You want heat where the element is and nowhere else.

### What the heat depends on

- the **material** of the wire
- its **thickness**
- its **length**
- the **current** (2 cells heat it more than 1)
- the **duration** the current flows

### Everyday uses

The **filament** of an incandescent lamp glows because current heats it. All of these contain a **heating element** — a rod or coil of wire:

**electric room heater · electric stove · electric kettle · electric iron · immersion rod · hair dryer**

In some of them the element is visible, glowing **red hot**.

> [!NOTE]
> **In industry:** steel plants use specially designed **high-temperature electric furnaces** to melt and **recycle scrap steel** into usable steel.

### When the heating effect is a problem

> [!WARNING]
> The same effect causes **energy loss in transmission wires**, and **overheating can melt plastic parts of plugs and sockets or start fires**.
>
> That is why household circuits contain **safety devices**, and why you must use wires, plugs and sockets **rated for the current** they will carry.

## How a cell generates electricity

### The Voltaic (Galvanic) cell

```
two ELECTRODES of different metals
        ↓  dipped in
an ELECTROLYTE  (weak acid or salt solution)
        ↓
chemical reaction  →  electric current
```

**Current flows from the positive terminal through the circuit to the negative terminal.**

**Over time the chemicals get used up** and the cell stops working — it is then **"dead"** and cannot supply any more electricity.

> [!NOTE]
> **Galvani vs Volta — a model dispute.** In the late 1700s **Luigi Galvani** noticed a dead frog's leg kicked when touched with **two different metals** (copper and iron). He concluded the electricity came from **the frog**.
>
> **Alessandro Volta** disagreed — he thought it came from **the metals**. To test it, he replaced the frog's leg with **saltwater-soaked paper** and still got a current.
>
> **That single substitution settled it:** the electricity came from **metals + liquid**, not from the animal. It led directly to the **first battery**.
>
> Notice the method: he changed **one thing** — removed the frog — and the effect survived.

**Build your own:** push a **copper wire** and an **iron nail** into a lemon, a small distance apart. Chain five or six lemons (copper of one to nail of the next), and connect an **LED** across the ends.

- **Electrodes:** copper wire and iron nail
- **Electrolyte:** lemon juice (a salt solution also works)
- If the LED doesn't glow, **reverse it** — an LED conducts only when its **longer wire goes to the positive terminal**.

**Common metal pairs:** zinc/copper, zinc/silver, aluminium/copper, iron/copper, magnesium/copper, lead/copper. **Copper tends to act as the positive electrode and zinc as the negative** — because of their chemical properties.

### Dry cells

**Called "dry" because the electrolyte is not a liquid but a thick moist paste.**

```
zinc container   → the NEGATIVE terminal
carbon rod (centre, with a metal cap) → the POSITIVE terminal
paste electrolyte surrounding the rod
```

**A dry cell is single-use** — once used up it must be disposed of.

> [!TIP]
> **Why dry cells replaced Voltaic cells for everyday use:** a paste can't spill, so the cell can be sealed, carried in a pocket and used in any orientation. A glass jar of acid cannot.

### Rechargeable batteries

**Can be recharged and reused many times** — preventing waste and saving money.

Used in **watches, phones, cameras, laptops, inverters and electric vehicles**.

**But they don't last forever.** After many charge–use cycles they **slowly wear out** — which is why a phone battery needs charging more often after a year or two.

> [!NOTE]
> **Lithium-ion (Li-ion)** is today's most common rechargeable battery. It depends on **lithium and cobalt**, mined and processed in only a few parts of the world — so countries are racing to **secure supplies, recycle old batteries and develop new technologies**.
>
> The next step may be **solid-state batteries**, replacing the liquid or paste electrolyte with a **solid**: safer, faster to charge and longer lasting. Better rechargeable batteries matter enormously as the world moves to **environmentally friendly electrical power**.

## The one-line summary

| Effect | What happens | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| **Magnetic effect** | current creates a magnetic field around the conductor | electromagnets, bells, motors, fans, loudspeakers |
| **Heating effect** | resistance turns electrical energy into heat | heater, kettle, iron, hair dryer, lamp filament, steel furnace |

**Both happen at once.** In the electromagnet activity the wire ends also got **warm** — the same current was producing a field *and* heat.
