## Probe and ponder

- With **no lamp** in a circuit, how else could you tell if **current is flowing**?
- Can we make **temporary magnets**?
- How do **electrical appliances** make heat without burning anything?
- How do you know a cell is **dead**? Can every battery be **recharged**?

## Sumana's magic crane

At the school science exhibition, **Mohini and Aakarsh** stop at their senior **Sumana's** model: a **lifting electromagnet**. Instead of a crane hook there's an **iron nail wrapped in wire**, joined to a battery. **Switch ON → the nail picks up iron paper clips. Switch OFF → they drop.** But there's **no magnet** in it — only a circuit! How?

## 1. The magnetic effect of electric current

**Activity 4.1:** stretch a wire between two nails on cardboard, connect it through a **switch** to a cell, and put a **magnetic compass under the wire**.

| Switch | Compass needle |
|---|---|
| **ON** (current flows) | **deflects** (turns away from north–south) |
| **OFF** (no current) | **returns** to north–south |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A compass needle is a tiny magnet — it's moved only by magnetic effects.** So the deflection proves the **current-carrying wire acts like a magnet**. Stop the current, and the effect **vanishes**.
>
> - **MAGNETIC FIELD** = the region around a magnet or a current-carrying wire where its magnetic effect can be felt (e.g. by a compass deflecting).
> - **MAGNETIC EFFECT OF ELECTRIC CURRENT** = **a current flowing through a conductor produces a magnetic field around it.** No current → no field.

> [!TIP]
> **So, no lamp? Use a compass.** If it deflects when you close the switch, **current is flowing**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Hans Christian Oersted (1777–1851)**, a professor in **Denmark**, noticed in **1820** during a demonstration that a nearby compass needle **jumped whenever he closed or opened a circuit**. He investigated until he was sure, then **published** — and other scientists **repeated** his experiment to check. **Electricity and magnetism are linked.**

## 2. Electromagnets

**Activity 4.2:** wind about **50 cm of insulated wire tightly round an iron nail**, connect the ends to a cell (only for a few seconds — or the cell runs down fast). **The nail picks up paper clips; disconnect and they fall.**

**Activity 4.3:** wind **~50 turns** of insulated wire on a **paper tube** the width of a pencil, with **compasses at both ends**.

| Set-up | Result |
|---|---|
| Coil + current | compasses **deflect** — the coil is a **magnet** |
| Coil, current off | needles go back |
| **Coil + iron nail inside** + current | **much bigger deflection**, and it **attracts clips** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ELECTROMAGNET = a current-carrying coil that behaves as a magnet.**
> - It is a **temporary magnet** — magnetic **only while current flows**. (That answers Sumana's puzzle: the clips drop when the switch goes OFF.)
> - Most have an **IRON CORE** — it makes the electromagnet **much stronger**.

### Poles of an electromagnet (Activity 4.4)

Label the coil's ends **A and B** and bring a compass near each. If the compass's **North pole is attracted to A**, then **A is a South pole** (unlike poles attract). **B turns out to be the opposite pole.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An electromagnet has TWO POLES — North and South — just like a bar magnet.**

### Making it stronger — or flipping it

> [!IMPORTANT]
> | Change | Effect |
> |---|---|
> | **more cells** (bigger current) | **stronger** — more deflection, more clips |
> | **more turns** of wire | **stronger** |
> | **iron core** inserted | **much stronger** |
> | **reverse the current's direction** (swap the cell's terminals) | **poles swap** (N ↔ S) |
>
> **Why more current helps:** a single cell gives only a **small current → weak field**. A battery of more cells gives **more current → stronger field**.

> [!TIP]
> **Neha slides the iron nail out of the coil.** Does the coil still deflect the compass? **Yes** — the current-carrying coil is still a magnet — but **less** than before, because the **iron core** was making it stronger.
>
> **Coils of iron, copper, aluminium and nichrome wire, all carrying current:** **all four** deflect a compass. **Any** conductor carrying current makes a magnetic field — the wire **doesn't** have to be magnetic.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why is the Earth a magnet?** **Liquid iron moving in the Earth's core** makes **electric currents**, which produce a **magnetic field**. **Migratory birds, fish and animals** use it to **navigate**; it also **shields** us from harmful particles from space.

### Lifting electromagnets

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Strong electromagnets hung from cranes** in **factories and scrap yards**: **current ON → lifts iron/steel; current OFF → drops it.** The operator controls it with a switch — something an ordinary magnet can't do.
> Other uses of the magnetic effect: **electric bells, motors, fans, loudspeakers**.

> [!NOTE]
> **It works the other way too:** just as electricity makes magnetism, a **moving magnet can produce an electric current** — the basis of **generators**. (Higher grades.)

## 3. The heating effect of electric current

**Activity 4.5 (with a teacher):** tie a **10 cm nichrome wire** (about 0.3 mm thick) between two nails and connect it through a switch to a cell. **Switch ON for 30 s** → touch it **briefly** → it's **warm**. (Don't hold it.)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every conductor offers some opposition — RESISTANCE — to the flow of current.** This resistance turns some **electrical energy into HEAT**.
> **HEATING EFFECT OF ELECTRIC CURRENT = a conductor gets heated when current passes through it.**
>
> **NICHROME** has **much higher resistance than copper** of the same size — so it **gets hotter**. That's why **heating elements are made of nichrome**, while **connecting wires are copper** (they should stay cool).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Heat produced depends on:**
> - the **current** (2 cells → **more heat** than 1 cell in the same time)
> - the wire's **material**
> - its **thickness** and **length**
> - **how long** the current flows

> [!TIP]
> **Uses:** **room heaters, electric stoves, kettles, irons, immersion rods, hair dryers** — each has a **HEATING ELEMENT** (a coil or rod of wire) that sometimes **glows red hot**. An **incandescent lamp** glows because its **filament** is heated — that's why a torch bulb gets warm.
> **Industry:** **electric furnaces** melt **scrap steel** for recycling.

> [!WARNING]
> **Unwanted heating:**
> - **energy is lost** as heat in **transmission wires**
> - **overheating** can **melt plastic** in plugs and sockets, or **cause fires**
>
> Use **wires, plugs and sockets rated for the current**; household circuits have **safety devices** to prevent accidents.

> [!TIP]
> **Electric heaters vs firewood/charcoal:** no **smoke** (cleaner air indoors — better for **health**), **no cutting trees**, **easy to control** temperature, **instant**, cleaner kitchens.

> [!TIP]
> **Sumana forgot to switch off.** After a while the nail stops lifting clips but the wire is **still warm**. Why? The current kept flowing and **ran the cell down** — too little current left for a strong magnetic field (the warmth is the heating effect that was using up the energy). Possibly the cell is nearly **dead**.

## 4. How does a battery make electricity?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A CELL produces electric current from CHEMICAL REACTIONS inside it.** (A **battery** is two or more cells joined.)

### Voltaic (Galvanic) cell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Two rods of DIFFERENT metals** (the **ELECTRODES**), partly dipped in a liquid — the **ELECTROLYTE** (a weak **acid** or **salt solution**). The **chemical reaction** between rods and electrolyte **produces electricity**.
> - Current flows from the **positive terminal** through the circuit to the **negative terminal**.
> - When the chemicals are **used up**, the cell is **"dead"**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Galvani vs Volta (late 1700s):** **Luigi Galvani** saw a **dead frog's leg kick** when touched with **two different metals** (copper and iron) and thought the electricity came **from the frog**. **Alessandro Volta** thought it came **from the metals**. He **tested** it by replacing the frog's leg with **paper soaked in salt water** — and **still got a current**. So it's **two metals + a liquid** → the **first battery**. (A great example of testing a hypothesis!)

**Activity 4.6 — lemon battery:** push a **copper wire and an iron nail** into each of 5–6 **juicy lemons**; join copper of one lemon to the nail of the next; connect an **LED** across the ends. **It glows!** (If not, **reverse the LED** — it only conducts when its **longer leg goes to +**.)
- **Electrodes:** copper and iron. **Electrolyte:** lemon juice (or salt water).

> [!TIP]
> **Copper strip and iron nail in PURE WATER vs in LEMON JUICE?** The LED glows only with **lemon juice** — pure water is a **poor conductor** and isn't a proper electrolyte.
>
> Common metal pairs: **zinc/copper, zinc/silver, aluminium/copper, iron/copper, magnesium/copper, lead/copper**. **Copper** tends to be the **positive** electrode, **zinc** the **negative**.

### Dry cell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Called "**dry**" because the electrolyte is a **thick moist PASTE**, not a liquid — so it's **portable and spill-proof**.
> - **Zinc container** = **negative** terminal
> - **Carbon rod** in the centre with a **metal cap** = **positive** terminal
> - **Paste electrolyte** around the carbon rod
>
> **Single use** — once used up, it must be **thrown away**.

### Rechargeable batteries

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Can be recharged and reused many times** → **less waste**, saves money. Used in **watches, phones, laptops, cameras, inverters, electric vehicles**.
> **But they wear out** — after many charges they hold less, which is why an old phone needs **charging more often**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Lithium-ion (Li-ion)** batteries are the most common; they need **lithium and cobalt**, mined in only a few places — so countries race to **secure supplies** and **recycle**. Next: **solid-state batteries** (solid electrolyte) — **safer, faster-charging, longer-lasting**.

> [!WARNING]
> **A "dead" battery isn't harmless** — it can still contain **acids** and metals like **lead, cadmium, nickel, lithium** that can **cause fires** or **harm the environment** in ordinary garbage. Take used batteries to **e-waste recycling** — many of their materials are **valuable and reusable**.

## Think-it-through questions

1. **Fill in:** the liquid in a Voltaic cell is the **electrolyte**; a current-carrying coil behaves like a **magnet**.
2. **True or false?**
   - *Dry cells are less portable than Voltaic cells.* → **False** — dry cells are **more** portable.
   - *A coil is an electromagnet only while current flows.* → **True.**
   - *An electromagnet with 1 cell attracts more clips than with 2 cells.* → **False** — 2 cells give **more current → stronger**.
3. **Current through a nichrome wire:** it gets **warm** AND a compass under it **deflects** → **both** effects happen.
4. **Match:** Voltaic cell → **chemical reactions**; electric iron → **heating effect**; nichrome wire → **best for heaters**; electromagnet → **magnetic effect**.
5. **Why nichrome in heaters?** It **generates more heat for a given current** (high resistance).
6. **Reverse the battery terminals** → the compass **deflects the opposite way** (the poles swap).

## In a nutshell

- **Current makes a magnetic field** (Oersted, 1820) — the **magnetic effect**.
- **Electromagnet** = current-carrying coil (+ iron core); **temporary**, two poles; stronger with **more current, more turns, iron core**; poles flip if current reverses.
- **Heating effect:** resistance turns electrical energy into heat; **nichrome** for heating elements. Depends on current, material, thickness, length, time.
- **Cells** make electricity by **chemical reactions**: Voltaic (two metals + electrolyte), **dry cell** (zinc case −, carbon rod +, paste), **rechargeable** (Li-ion).
- **Recycle** used batteries as e-waste.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** an electromagnet loses its magnetism when current stops · any current-carrying wire (copper too) makes a magnetic field · nichrome heats up, copper connecting wire shouldn't · dry cell: zinc is −, carbon is + · a cell runs on chemical energy, not stored electricity.
