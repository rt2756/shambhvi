## Why elephants walk into villages

In Odisha, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Assam and Chhattisgarh, **elephants often enter farms and villages** looking for bananas and sugarcane — damaging crops and sometimes harming people and animals.

**The chain of causes:**

```
changes in rainfall and temperature  +  trees cut for roads and buildings
                    ↓
forests dry out and shrink  →  loss of habitat
                    ↓
animals move into human habitats
```

**Elephants are adapted to forest life**, and sudden changes make survival hard.

**The response:** wildlife ecologists have marked **corridors** connecting forest habitats, so animals can travel safely between large forests **without meeting human settlements**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **This one story is the whole chapter: nothing in nature happens on its own.** A change in rainfall reaches a village kitchen through a chain of connections.

## Habitats: biotic and abiotic

**A HABITAT is the place where an organism lives** — it could be a pond, a forest, a farm, or even just **the bark of a tree**.

| | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **BIOTIC components** | the **living** things | plants, animals, microorganisms |
| **ABIOTIC components** | the **non-living** things | air, water, soil, sunlight, temperature |

**A habitat must supply food, oxygen, shelter and space to grow.**

**A fish in a pond** gets its **biotic** needs (food) from small plants and animals, and its **abiotic** needs (oxygen) from the water.

> [!TIP]
> **Two organisms in the same habitat can face quite different conditions.** A forest is warm by day and cool by night — so a **rodent active by day** and a **snake that comes out at night** share a habitat but live in different worlds.
>
> **That is how many organisms coexist in one place: they use the same resources in different ways.**

## Levels of organisation

```
Individual → Population → Community → Ecosystem
```

- **POPULATION** — a group of **the same type** of organism in a habitat at a given time (all the fish of one kind in a pond)
- **COMMUNITY** — **different populations** sharing the same habitat: all the plants, animals and microorganisms together
- **ECOSYSTEM** — **the community plus the abiotic components**, all interacting

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An ecosystem is bigger than a community, which is bigger than a population.**
>
> So "an ecosystem is part of a community" is **wrong** — it's the other way round.

**What if a habitat had only ONE type of organism?** They'd all need the **same food, water and space** — leading to **fierce competition and scarcity**.

**Two main types of ecosystem:**

- **Aquatic** — ponds, rivers, lakes, seas
- **Terrestrial** — forests, grasslands, deserts, farms, even a single large tree

**Ecosystems can be large or small, and they overlap** — a river running through mountains, forest, grassland and farmland has several ecosystems interacting at once.

## Everything affects everything — the fish and flowers study

Researchers compared **Pond A (with fish)** and **Pond B (without fish)**.

```
Pond A has FISH
    ↓  fish eat dragonfly larvae
FEWER dragonflies
    ↓  dragonflies eat bees, flies and butterflies
MORE bees, flies and butterflies
    ↓  these pollinate nearby flowers
MORE seeds produced by plants around Pond A
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A fish in the water changes how many seeds a plant on the bank produces** — through three steps, none of them obvious.
>
> **That is what "indirect effect" means**, and it is why disturbing one part of an ecosystem has consequences you didn't predict.

**So overfishing wouldn't just reduce fish** — it would raise dragonfly numbers, cut pollinator numbers, and lower seed production on the bank.

> [!NOTE]
> **Pollination.** A flower has a stalk, green **sepals**, coloured **petals**, and two reproductive parts — **carpels** (female) and **stamens** (male). Stamens release yellow **pollen grains**, and **wind, water, insects, bats and birds** carry them to the carpels. **Pollination is essential for the formation of fruits and seeds.**

## Types of interaction in an ecosystem

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Abiotic ↔ biotic** | earthworms live in **moist soil**; a fish lays eggs in **water** |
| **Abiotic ↔ abiotic** | sunlight raises the day **temperature**; sunlight makes **water evaporate** |
| **Biotic ↔ biotic** | a frog eats insects; frogs and fish **compete** for insect larvae |

**Biotic depends on abiotic:** sunlight, carbon dioxide and water for photosynthesis; soil for nutrients; air for respiration; water for everything.

**And abiotic depends on biotic:** plants **release oxygen**, roots **hold soil in place and prevent erosion**, and plants **retain soil moisture and cool the atmosphere**.

> [!TIP]
> **The dependence runs both ways.** It's easy to see that living things need soil and air — less obvious that the soil and air are shaped by living things.

## Who eats whom

### Producers, consumers, decomposers

| Group | Also called | How they feed | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Producers** | **autotrophs** (*auto* = self, *troph* = food) | make their own food by **photosynthesis** | green plants, trees |
| **Consumers** | **heterotrophs** (*hetero* = other) | depend on other organisms | animals |
| **Decomposers** | **saprotrophs** (*sapro* = rotten) | feed on **dead matter** | fungi, bacteria |

**Consumers are of three kinds:**

- **Herbivores** — eat only plants: deer, hare, squirrel
- **Carnivores** — eat only animals: leopard, vulture, shikra
- **Omnivores** — eat both: crow, fox, mouse

### Food chains and trophic levels

**A FOOD CHAIN is a simple sequence showing "who eats whom".**

```
Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle
Grass → Hare → Leopard
```

**A TROPHIC LEVEL is an organism's position in the chain** (*troph* = food):

1. **Producers** — green plants
2. **Herbivores** — hares, deer
3. **Small carnivores** — frogs
4. **Large carnivores** — tigers, vultures

**The numbers form a pyramid.** In a crop field there are very many millet plants, fewer mice, and very few eagles — **most at the base, fewest at the top**.

> [!TIP]
> **Why the pyramid narrows.** It takes a great many plants to feed one mouse, and a great many mice to feed one eagle — so each level up supports far fewer individuals.

### Food webs

**Each organism is usually eaten by two or more kinds of organism**, so food chains **interlink into a network — a FOOD WEB**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A food web is more realistic than a single food chain, and it explains resilience.** If one prey species vanishes, a predator with several food sources can switch. A predator with only one cannot.

## What happens to waste

**Mushrooms on dead wood; beetles and flies on elephant dung** — these are **decomposers** at work.

**Microorganisms like fungi and bacteria break complex substances in dead plants and animals into simpler ones**, returning nutrients to the soil.

**This process is DECOMPOSITION**, and it is why **plants can keep growing** — much of the soil's nutrient supply comes from it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In nature, nothing is wasted — everything is reused.**
>
> **If all decomposers disappeared**, dead matter would pile up unbroken, nutrients would stay locked inside it, the soil would lose fertility, and plants — and everything that depends on them — would fail.

## One change leads to another

```
pollution kills pond plants
      ↓
less oxygen produced in the water
      ↓
fish die
      ↓
fewer consumers → insects multiply
      ↓
insects spread to nearby farmland
      ↓
farmers use more pesticides
      ↓
further damage to the environment
```

> [!NOTE]
> **A real example — India's frog legs.** In the 1980s India was a large exporter of frog legs, especially of the **Indian bullfrog**. Large-scale harvesting **cut frog populations**. Since **frogs eat insects**, agricultural **pests rose** — so farmers used **more synthetic pesticides**, harming soil, water, the environment and human health.
>
> **The Government of India banned the export of frog legs** to prevent further ecological damage.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An ecosystem stays in balance when interactions among organisms and their environment keep populations and resources stable.**
>
> **That balance is DYNAMIC, not fixed** — it constantly adjusts, and can be disrupted by natural or human-made changes.

## Relationships between organisms

**Competition** for food, water, space or sunlight **controls population size and keeps the ecosystem balanced.** Without it, one species could multiply too far and unbalance everything.

**Three named relationships:**

| Relationship | Who benefits | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mutualism** | **both** organisms benefit | honeybee and flower — the bee gets nectar, the flower gets pollinated |
| **Commensalism** | one benefits, the other is **unaffected** | orchid on a tree — the orchid gets support, the branch is unharmed |
| **Parasitism** | one benefits, the other is **harmed** | tick on a dog — the tick feeds on blood, the dog gets skin irritation |

> [!NOTE]
> **A.J.T. Johnsingh** was a famous Indian wildlife biologist and a pioneer of **modern tracking systems** for studying wildlife. Working in **Bandipur National Park, Karnataka**, his research showed how predators like tigers and leopards **depend on prey such as deer and wild boar** — proving that **a healthy prey population is key to predator survival**.

## What ecosystems give us

- **Forests** — fresh air, fertile soil, food, fibres, timber, medicines
- **Aquatic ecosystems** — water and food
- **All ecosystems** — aesthetic and recreational value, and climate regulation

**When we overuse or misuse natural resources, we disturb this balance.**

### The Sundarbans

**The largest mangrove forest in the world**, where the Ganges and Brahmaputra meet, between India and Bangladesh.

**What it does:** home to many **endangered** species; **slows strong winds and waves during storms and floods**; the trees **absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen**. **UNESCO declared it a World Heritage Site in 1987.**

**The threats:** mangroves cut for **fuelwood and farming**; **illegal hunting** and overuse of forest resources; **pollution from industrial waste and untreated sewage**.

> [!TIP]
> **Mangroves protect coastal villages from tsunamis and storm surges** by physically **slowing the water and breaking the force of the waves** — which is why villages behind intact mangrove belts suffered far less in the **2004 tsunami** than neighbouring ones.

**Other Indian ecosystems face the same problems:** deforestation, overuse of resources, **invasive species**, unsustainable land use and pollution — damaging forests, rivers, scrublands, wetlands, grasslands and coasts.

> [!NOTE]
> **Protected areas** are parts of land or water set aside to conserve wildlife and habitats — national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves and community conserved areas.
>
> **Jim Corbett** (Uttarakhand) · **Manas** (Assam) · **Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve** (Western Ghats) · **Chilika Lake** (Odisha) · **Eaglenest** (Arunachal Pradesh) · **Hemis** (Leh) · **Keibul Lamjao** (Manipur) · **Pirotan Island Marine National Park** (Gujarat)

> [!NOTE]
> **Migratory birds** fly thousands of miles to India's diverse habitats to escape harsh climates and find food. They act as **pollinators and seed dispersers along their route**, linking two habitats — and they **prey on insect pests**, helping farmers indirectly. **Demoiselle Cranes** visit the water body at **Khichan village, Jodhpur** every winter.

## Human-made ecosystems and farming

**Humans create artificial ecosystems** — fish ponds, farms, parks. Well designed, they can **reduce pollution, support biodiversity and provide recreation** — but **unlike natural ecosystems they need human care and management**.

### The Green Revolution and its cost

Between **1950 and 1965** India faced a food crisis from low crop production. In the mid-20th century **tractors, machines, synthetic fertilisers and pesticides** raised production enormously — the **Green Revolution**, which made India food secure.

> [!WARNING]
> **But those methods are now considered unsustainable:**
>
> | Practice | Consequence |
> |---|---|
> | **Overuse of synthetic fertilisers** | reduces friendly **soil microorganisms** and **humus** (organic matter); without humus the soil doesn't bind and becomes **prone to erosion** |
> | **Overuse of pesticides** | kills **natural predators** of pests, so pest numbers eventually **rise**; some pests develop **resistance** |
> | **Excessive groundwater extraction** | depletes the water table |
> | **Monoculture** — growing one crop repeatedly | **reduces biodiversity**, degrades soil, harms **pollinators** |
> | **Heavy irrigation and repeated ploughing** | disturbs earthworms and snails, which maintain ecological balance |

**Towards sustainability:** many farmers are exploring **organic and natural farming** — reducing synthetic inputs and **interfering minimally with natural ecosystems**.

> [!NOTE]
> **The ancient text Vrikshayurveda** emphasises soil health and nourishment, strongly advocating **continuous nourishment of the soil with organic manure** — including **Kunapa Jala**, a liquid fertiliser made from animal and plant waste by **fermentation**, which breaks complex substances into simpler ones.
