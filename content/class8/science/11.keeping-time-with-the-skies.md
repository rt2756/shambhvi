## Why the Moon changes shape

**The Moon's shape doesn't change — only what we SEE changes.**

**The Moon does not emit light of its own.** It shines by **reflecting sunlight**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Half of the Moon always faces the Sun and is illuminated; the other half is dark.**
>
> **Half of the Moon always faces the Earth.**
>
> **But these are NOT the same half.** As the Moon revolves around the Earth, we see a different *fraction* of the illuminated half — and that fraction is the **phase**.

### The phases

| Phase | Sanskrit / Indian name | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| **Full Moon** | **Purnima** | the entire illuminated half |
| **Gibbous** | | **more than half** of the illuminated portion |
| Half circle | | exactly half |
| **Crescent** | | **less than half** |
| **New Moon** | **Amavasya** | nothing — only the dark half faces us |

**The cycle:** bright portion **shrinks** from full to nothing over about two weeks — the **waning period** (**Krishna Paksha**). Then it **grows** back over two weeks — the **waxing period** (**Shukla Paksha**).

**One full Moon to the next takes about a month** (29.5 days).

> [!WARNING]
> **Phases are NOT caused by the Earth's shadow.** That is the commonest wrong explanation.
>
> Phases come from the **changing relative orientation of Sun, Moon and Earth** as the Moon revolves.
>
> **The Earth's shadow on the Moon causes a LUNAR ECLIPSE** — a rare, brief event, not a monthly cycle.

### Why eclipses don't happen every month

**A lunar eclipse can only happen on a full Moon day; a solar eclipse only on a new Moon day.**

**But they don't happen every month** because the **Moon's orbit is slightly TILTED** relative to the Earth's orbit around the Sun. Usually the Moon passes a little above or below the shadow line.

## Where to find the Moon

| Day | Moon's position relative to the Sun |
|---|---|
| **Full Moon** | **opposite** the Sun — as the Sun rises in the East, the Moon is setting in the West |
| Half (waning) | **overhead at sunrise** |
| Crescent (waning) | **close to the Sun** |
| **New Moon** | **closest** to the Sun — which is why it can't be seen |

> [!TIP]
> **A WAXING Moon is easiest to spot at SUNSET; a WANING Moon at SUNRISE.**
>
> Knowing the phase tells you **where and when to look**.

**Why the Moon can be seen in daytime:** **the Moon rises about 50 minutes LATER each day.** Sometimes moonrise falls in the afternoon (2–4 p.m.), so the Moon is up in the eastern sky in broad daylight.

**Why 50 minutes later:** in the 24 hours the Earth takes to rotate once, **the Moon has moved further along its own orbit**. The Earth must rotate a bit more for the Moon to come back to the same place in the sky.

> [!WARNING]
> **"The Moon rises when the Sun sets" is only true on a full Moon day.** On every other day it rises at a different time — check any newspaper's moonrise column for several days and you'll see the 50-minute drift.

**The ball-and-lamp model.** Hold a ball on a stick at arm's length, slightly above your head, with a lamp 3 m away. **Your head is the Earth, the ball the Moon, the lamp the Sun.** Turn slowly anticlockwise and watch the ball.

- Ball **towards** the lamp → you see the **dark** side → **new Moon**
- Ball **away** from the lamp → you see the **fully lit** side → **full Moon**
- In between → the boundary between lit and unlit appears **curved**, exactly like real Moon phases

**Everyone on Earth sees nearly the same phase on a given day** — because the Earth's rotation (1 day) is **much faster** than the Moon's revolution (about a month), so the Moon barely moves during one Earth day.

## Where calendars come from

**Three natural cycles give three units of time:**

| Cycle | Cause | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Sun returns to its **highest position** in the sky | Earth's **rotation** | **day** |
| Moon completes all its **phases** | Moon's **revolution around the Earth** | **month** |
| Earth completes one cycle of **seasons** | Earth's **revolution around the Sun** | **year** |

**The mean solar day = 24 hours** — the average time from the Sun's highest point one day to its highest point the next.

**Finding the Sun's highest point:** fix a **1 m stick** vertically and mark the tip of its shadow every minute. **The shadow is SHORTEST when the Sun is highest.** The time between shortest shadows on consecutive days is one solar day.

**The Moon's phase cycle takes about 29.5 days** — the basis of the month.
**The Earth's revolution takes about 365¼ days** — the basis of the year.

## Three kinds of calendar

### Lunar calendar

**Day → month of ~29.5 days → year of 12 lunar months.**

Ancient people noticed that **about 12 Moon cycles fit into one cycle of seasons**.

> [!WARNING]
> **The problem:** `12 × 29.5 ≈ 354 days`, but the seasons repeat in about **365 days**.
>
> So a lunar year is about **11 days short**, and **the seasons drift out of step** with the months year after year.

### Solar calendar

**Built to keep the year in step with the SEASONS** — which mattered enormously for agriculture.

**The Gregorian calendar** (used worldwide today) is a solar calendar: months adjusted to total **365 days**, which is why some have 30, some 31, and February only 28.

**Leap years.** The Earth takes an extra **quarter day** beyond 365 — which accumulates to about **one whole day every four years**. So a **leap day** is added: **if a year is divisible by 4, February gets 29 days**.

> [!TIP]
> **The correction is even finer than that.** A quarter day is slightly too much, so:
>
> - **Skip** the leap year every **100 years** — 1700, 1800, 1900
> - But that overcorrects, so **add it back every 400 years** — 1600, 2000
>
> These layered corrections keep the calendar matched to the seasons for centuries.

> [!NOTE]
> **Tropical year vs sidereal year.**
> - **Tropical year** — time between successive **spring equinoxes**. The Gregorian calendar is based on this.
> - **Sidereal year** — time for the **same stars to rise again at sunset**.
>
> The sidereal year is longer by just **20 minutes**, so the difference takes a very long time to show. **Astronomers use the sidereal year** to track the Earth's position in its orbit.

### Luni-solar calendar

**Uses the Moon's phases for days and months, but ADJUSTS to stay in step with the seasons.**

**How:** since 12 lunar months fall short by about **11 days** a year, the shortfall reaches nearly a **whole month every 2–3 years**. So an **extra month — the Adhika Maasa (intercalary month) — is added**.

**Used in many parts of India.**

> [!NOTE]
> **Indian luni-solar month names:** Chaitra, Vaisakha, Jyeshtha, Ashadha, Shravana, Bhadrapada, Ashwin, Kartika, Margashirsha (Agrahayan), Pausha, Magha, Phalguna.
>
> - **Amant** calendars — the month starts the day **after the new Moon** and ends on the new Moon.
> - **Purnimant** calendars — the month starts the day **after the full Moon** and ends on the full Moon.

## Ancient Indian sky-watching

> [!NOTE]
> **People worked all this out without knowing the Earth orbits the Sun, and with no instruments** — purely by **careful, patient observation over years**.
>
> **The Sun does not always rise exactly in the East.** In summer it rises a little **north** of East; in winter a little **south**. The extremes fall on the **solstices**, around **21 June** and **21 December**.
>
> - **Uttarayan** — the Sun's apparent **northward** movement, December to June
> - **Dakshinayan** — its apparent **southward** movement, June to December
>
> The **Taittirīya Saṁhitā** (6.5.3) records: *"Thus the Sun moves southwards for six months and northwards for six months."*
>
> The **Surya Siddhanta** notes that the stars of **Capricorn (Makar)** lay behind the Sun around the winter solstice in ancient times, and describes Uttarayana and Dakshinayana as beginning at the Sun's entry into Capricorn and Cancer.

## The Indian National Calendar

**A solar calendar of 365 days**, used alongside the Gregorian calendar for official purposes.

- **The year begins on 22 March** — the day after the **spring equinox**
- Months have **30 or 31 days** (in a regular year, the 2nd to 6th months have 31, the rest 30)
- Month names come from **traditional Indian calendars**
- **Leap years match the Gregorian calendar**, with the extra day added to **Chaitra**, the first month — so those years begin on **21 March**

> [!NOTE]
> In **1952** the Government of India set up a **Calendar Reform Committee (CRC)** to examine all the calendars in use and recommend an accurate, uniform one. Its **Unified National Calendar** was adopted from **21 March 1956 CE = 1 Chaitra 1878 Saka**, and follows the general principles of the **Surya Siddhanta**.
>
> The CRC was chaired by **Meghnad Saha** (1893–1956), a pioneering Indian astrophysicist who studied stars and their temperatures and developed the famous **Saha equation**. The **Saha Institute of Nuclear Physics**, Kolkata, is named after him.

## Festivals and the sky

**Many Indian festivals are tied to the Moon's phases**, so they fall on **different Gregorian dates each year**.

| Festival | When |
|---|---|
| **Diwali** | new Moon of **Kartika** |
| **Holi** | full Moon of **Phalguna** |
| **Buddha Purnima** | full Moon of **Vaisakha** |
| **Dussehra** | tenth day of **Ashwina** |
| **Eid-ul-Fitr** | after sighting the crescent Moon at the end of **Ramazan** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why some festivals shift a little and others shift a lot.**
>
> **Luni-solar** festivals (Diwali, Holi) shift by **less than a month**, because the **Adhika Maasa** corrects the lunar–solar gap every few years.
>
> **Purely lunar** festivals (Eid-ul-Fitr) have **no such correction**, so they drift about 11 days earlier each year and can fall in **any month** of the Gregorian calendar over time.

> [!NOTE]
> **A few Indian festivals follow a solar SIDEREAL calendar** — Makar Sankranti, Pongal, Bihu, Vaisakhi, Poila Baisakh, Puthandu. These fall on **almost the same Gregorian date every year**, because both calendars are solar.
>
> Long ago they were tied to a **solstice or equinox**. Because the sidereal year is 20 minutes longer than the tropical year, their dates **slowly drift** — **Makar Sankranti moves forward by one day every 71 years**. The cause is the **slow wobble of the Earth's axis**, like a spinning top's axis wobbling.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why a festival's date can differ across India.** Many dates depend on the **exact lunar phase at sunrise** — and sunrise is earlier in eastern India than western India, so the date can shift by a day between regions in the same year.
>
> For uniformity, the **Positional Astronomy Centre** of the Government of India publishes the **Rashtriya Panchang** each year — detailed calculations of the Sun's and Moon's positions for a **central location in India** — and gives advance notice of festival dates for declaring holidays.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Moon in Indian art.** Ragas **Chandrakauns, Chandranandan** and **Shubhapantuvarali** ("auspicious moon") carry the Moon in their names and melodies. Bharatanatyam mudras **Chandrakala** and **Ardhachandran** depict it, as do Kathak, Odissi and Kuchipudi. **Madhubani** and **Warli** paintings, and sculpture and pottery of the **Saura, Gond** and other tribes, show the Moon and Sun prominently.

## Artificial satellites

**The Moon is Earth's NATURAL satellite.** Human-made **artificial satellites** also orbit the Earth, appearing as tiny moving specks in the night sky.

**Most orbit about 800 km above the surface and take roughly 100 minutes for one orbit** — so yes, their motion is periodic too, just much faster than the Moon's.

**What they do:** **communication, navigation, weather monitoring, disaster management, scientific research.**

> [!TIP]
> **Spotting one:** just before sunrise or after sunset, from a place with a clear view, look for a **point of light moving fast and steadily across the sky** — no flashing red/green lights (that would be an aircraft). Apps and websites list when satellites will pass over your location.
>
> **Why just after sunset or before sunrise?** The sky is dark for you, but the satellite high above is **still catching sunlight**.

> [!NOTE]
> **ISRO's missions.** The **Cartosat** series captures high-quality images of Earth to improve maps, plan cities and handle natural disasters; the **Bhuvan** platform uses these to show terrain, soil, land use and vegetation. **AstroSat** observes stars and other celestial objects. India's other missions include **Chandrayaan 1, 2 and 3** to the Moon, **Aditya L1** to study the Sun, and **Mangalyaan** to Mars. ISRO also lets Indian students build and launch small satellites — **AzaadiSat, InspireSat-1, Jugnu**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Space junk.** After their useful life, satellites and spent rocket parts become **space debris**. It **crowds space and can collide with working satellites**. Small pieces **burn up in the atmosphere**, but **larger ones can crash on the ground**. Countries are now working together to remove it.

> [!NOTE]
> **Vikram Ambalal Sarabhai** (1919–1971), a researcher in space science and nuclear physics, is known as the **Father of the Indian Space Programme**. He pioneered the effort to launch India's first artificial satellites. The **Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre (VSSC)** in Thiruvananthapuram — ISRO's rocket and launch-vehicle centre — is named after him.

## Answering the opening questions

- **Why is the Moon sometimes visible in daytime?** Because it **rises about 50 minutes later each day**, so on many days it is above the horizon while the Sun is up.
- **What would a day, month and year mean on the Moon?** They'd be set by the Moon's own rotation and its motion — quite different from Earth's, since the Moon takes about a month to rotate once.
- **If Earth had two moons?** Two independent phase cycles overlapping — the night sky would be far brighter and more variable, and any Moon-based calendar would be much harder to build.
- **Without clocks or calendars, how could we measure time?** Exactly as our ancestors did: the **Sun's shadow** for the day, the **Moon's phases** for the month, and the **cycle of seasons** and rising stars for the year.
