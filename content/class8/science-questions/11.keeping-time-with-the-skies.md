<!-- ===== Why the Moon changes shape ===== -->

Does the Moon emit its own light?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** It shines by **reflecting sunlight** that falls on it.
>
> That's why only the half facing the Sun is bright.

---

Explain in one sentence why the Moon appears to change shape.

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **the half of the Moon facing the SUN and the half facing the EARTH are not the same half** — so as the Moon revolves, we see a different **fraction** of the illuminated half.

---

**True or false:** we can only see the part of the Moon that reflects sunlight towards us.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.**
>
> The unlit half sends no light our way, so it is invisible against the dark sky.

---

Name the phases of the Moon, with their Indian names where they exist.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Full Moon (Purnima)** — the entire illuminated half is visible
> - **Gibbous** — **more than half** of the illuminated portion
> - Half circle
> - **Crescent** — **less than half**
> - **New Moon (Amavasya)** — nothing visible; only the dark half faces us

---

What are Shukla Paksha and Krishna Paksha?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Shukla Paksha** — the **waxing** period, when the bright portion **grows** (about two weeks).
> - **Krishna Paksha** — the **waning** period, when the bright portion **shrinks** (about two weeks).

---

How long is one full cycle of the Moon's phases?

> [!ANSWER]
> **About 29.5 days** — roughly a month, from one full Moon to the next.

---

A student says the Moon's phases are caused by the Earth's shadow falling on it. Is that right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — this is the commonest wrong explanation.**
>
> Phases come from the **changing relative orientation of Sun, Moon and Earth** as the Moon revolves.
>
> **The Earth's shadow on the Moon causes a LUNAR ECLIPSE** — a rare, brief event, not a monthly cycle. If the shadow caused phases, we'd have an eclipse every month.

---

On which day can a lunar eclipse happen? A solar eclipse? Why don't they happen every month?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Lunar eclipse — only on a FULL Moon day**
> - **Solar eclipse — only on a NEW Moon day**
>
> **They don't happen every month because the Moon's orbit is slightly TILTED** relative to the Earth's orbit around the Sun — so the Moon usually passes a little above or below the shadow line.

---

Describe the ball-and-lamp activity and what it demonstrates.

> [!ANSWER]
> Hold a ball on a stick at arm's length, slightly above your head, with a lamp about 3 m away. **Your head is the Earth, the ball the Moon, the lamp the Sun.** Turn slowly anticlockwise.
>
> - Ball **towards** the lamp → you see the **dark** side → **new Moon**
> - Ball **away** from the lamp → you see the **fully lit** side → **full Moon**
> - In between → the boundary between lit and unlit appears **curved**, exactly like real phases
>
> It shows the phases come purely from the **relative positions** of the three bodies.

---

Do people in different parts of the Earth see different phases on the same day?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — nearly the same phase everywhere.**
>
> **Why:** the Earth's rotation period (**1 day**) is far shorter than the Moon's revolution period (**about a month**), so the Moon barely moves in its orbit during a single Earth day.

---

<!-- ===== Locating the Moon ===== -->

Where in the sky is the full Moon at sunrise?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Almost setting in the West.**
>
> On a full Moon day the Moon is **nearly opposite the Sun** — so as the Sun rises in the East, the Moon is going down in the West.

---

Which phase is easiest to spot at sunrise, and which at sunset?

> [!ANSWER]
> - A **WANING** Moon at **sunrise**
> - A **WAXING** Moon at **sunset**
>
> Knowing the phase tells you **where and when to look**.

---

Why is the Moon sometimes visible in broad daylight?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day**.
>
> Sometimes moonrise falls in the **afternoon (2–4 p.m.)**, so the Moon is up in the eastern sky while the Sun is still shining.

---

Why does moonrise come about 50 minutes later each day?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because in the 24 hours the Earth takes to rotate once, **the Moon has moved further along its own orbit**.
>
> The Earth must **rotate a bit more** for the Moon to return to the same place in the sky — and that extra rotation takes about 50 minutes.

---

"The Moon rises when the Sun sets." Is this true?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only on a full Moon day.**
>
> On every other day it rises at a different time. Check any newspaper's moonrise column for several days and you'll see the **50-minute daily drift**.

---

Ravi says he saw a crescent Moon rising in the East as the Sun was setting in the West. Is that possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> A Moon rising in the East as the Sun sets in the West is **opposite the Sun** — that is the position of a **FULL** Moon, not a crescent.
>
> A crescent Moon is **close to the Sun** in the sky, so it sets soon after sunset (waxing) or rises shortly before sunrise (waning).

---

<!-- ===== Calendars ===== -->

Name the three natural cycles that give us the day, the month and the year.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Cycle | Cause | Unit |
> |---|---|---|
> | Sun returns to its highest point in the sky | Earth's **rotation** | **day** |
> | Moon completes all its phases | Moon's **revolution around the Earth** | **month** |
> | Earth completes one cycle of seasons | Earth's **revolution around the Sun** | **year** |

---

What is the mean solar day, and how would you measure it with a stick?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The average time from the Sun's highest position in the sky one day to its highest position the next — 24 hours.**
>
> **Method:** fix a 1 m stick vertically and mark the tip of its shadow every minute through midday. **The shadow is SHORTEST when the Sun is highest.** The time between shortest shadows on consecutive days is one solar day.

---

Why is the shadow shortest when the Sun is highest?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the higher the Sun, the **more steeply its light falls**, and the less the stick's shadow stretches along the ground.
>
> Directly overhead would give almost no shadow at all.

---

How long is a lunar year, and what's the problem with it?

> [!ANSWER]
> `12 × 29.5 ≈` **354 days**.
>
> **The problem:** the seasons repeat in about **365 days**, so a lunar year is about **11 days short** — and the **seasons drift out of step with the months** year after year.

---

Why did people need a solar calendar?

> [!ANSWER]
> **For agriculture** — it was essential to know when the seasons would arrive.
>
> Only a calendar synchronised to the **cycle of seasons** can tell you reliably when to sow and harvest.

---

Why do Gregorian months have 30, 31 or 28 days?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the months are **adjusted to add up to 365 days**, the length of the solar year.
>
> There is no whole number of days that divides evenly into 12 months, so the lengths are staggered.

---

Explain the leap year rule and why it exists.

> [!ANSWER]
> The Earth takes about **365¼** days per revolution. That extra **quarter day** accumulates to about **one whole day every four years**.
>
> **So a leap day is added: if a year is divisible by 4, February gets 29 days.**

---

The simple "every 4 years" rule isn't quite right. What are the further corrections?

> [!ANSWER]
> A quarter day is slightly **too much**, so:
>
> - **Skip** the leap year every **100 years** — 1700, 1800, 1900
> - But that **overcorrects**, so **add it back every 400 years** — 1600, 2000
>
> These layered corrections keep the calendar matched to the seasons for centuries.

---

What is the difference between a tropical year and a sidereal year?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Tropical year** — the time between successive **spring equinoxes**. The **Gregorian calendar** is based on it.
> - **Sidereal year** — the time for the **same stars to rise again at sunset**.
>
> The sidereal year is longer by only **20 minutes**, so the difference takes a very long time to show. **Astronomers use the sidereal year** to track Earth's position in its orbit.

---

What is a luni-solar calendar, and how does it fix the lunar calendar's problem?

> [!ANSWER]
> One that **uses the Moon's phases for days and months, but adjusts to stay in step with the seasons**.
>
> **How:** the 11-day annual shortfall builds up to nearly a **whole month every 2–3 years**, so an extra month — the **Adhika Maasa (intercalary month)** — is added.

---

What is the difference between Amant and Purnimant calendars?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Amant** — the month starts the day **after the new Moon** and ends on the new Moon.
> - **Purnimant** — the month starts the day **after the full Moon** and ends on the full Moon.
>
> Same months, different starting point.

---

Name the twelve months of the Indian luni-solar calendars.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Chaitra, Vaisakha, Jyeshtha, Ashadha, Shravana, Bhadrapada, Ashwin, Kartika, Margashirsha (Agrahayan), Pausha, Magha, Phalguna.**

---

<!-- ===== Indian sky-watching ===== -->

Does the Sun always rise exactly in the East?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** In summer it rises a little **north** of East; in winter a little **south** of East.
>
> The extremes fall on the **solstices**, around **21 June** and **21 December**.

---

What are Uttarayan and Dakshinayan, and which ancient text records them?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Uttarayan** — the Sun's apparent **northward** movement, December to June
> - **Dakshinayan** — its apparent **southward** movement, June to December
>
> The **Taittirīya Saṁhitā** (6.5.3) records: *"Thus the Sun moves southwards for six months and northwards for six months."* The **Surya Siddhanta** describes them as beginning at the Sun's entry into Capricorn (**Makar**) and Cancer.

---

Ancient astronomers had no instruments and didn't know the Earth orbits the Sun. How did they build accurate calendars?

> [!ANSWER]
> Through **careful, patient observation over many years** — noticing repeating **patterns and cycles** in natural events.
>
> By tracking the Sun's rising position, shadow lengths and the stars that rose at sunset, they worked out that the year is about **365 days**.

---

Describe the Indian National Calendar.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **solar calendar of 365 days**, used alongside the Gregorian calendar for official purposes.
>
> - The year **begins on 22 March**, the day after the **spring equinox**
> - Months have **30 or 31 days** (2nd to 6th months have 31, the rest 30)
> - Month names come from **traditional Indian calendars**
> - **Leap years match the Gregorian calendar**, with the extra day added to **Chaitra** — so those years begin on **21 March**

---

Who was Meghnad Saha, and what was the Calendar Reform Committee?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Meghnad Saha** (1893–1956) was a pioneering Indian astrophysicist who studied stars and their temperatures and developed the **Saha equation**. The **Saha Institute of Nuclear Physics**, Kolkata, is named after him.
>
> He **chaired the Calendar Reform Committee**, set up by the Government of India in **1952** to examine all the calendars in use and recommend a uniform one. Its **Unified National Calendar** was adopted from **21 March 1956 CE = 1 Chaitra 1878 Saka**, following the principles of the **Surya Siddhanta**.

---

<!-- ===== Festivals ===== -->

Why do most Indian festivals fall on different Gregorian dates each year?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because they are tied to the **phases of the Moon**, and lunar or luni-solar calendars are **not aligned to the Gregorian solar calendar**.
>
> A festival fixed to a particular Moon phase must move around the solar year.

---

Match each festival to its lunar timing: Diwali, Holi, Buddha Purnima, Dussehra, Eid-ul-Fitr.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Diwali** — new Moon of **Kartika**
> - **Holi** — full Moon of **Phalguna**
> - **Buddha Purnima** — full Moon of **Vaisakha**
> - **Dussehra** — tenth day of **Ashwina**
> - **Eid-ul-Fitr** — after sighting the crescent Moon at the end of **Ramazan**

---

Diwali shifts by less than a month each year, but Eid can fall in any month. Why the difference?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Diwali follows a LUNI-SOLAR calendar**, which adds the **Adhika Maasa** every few years to correct the lunar–solar gap — so its date stays within about a month.
>
> **Eid follows a purely LUNAR calendar** with **no such correction**, so it drifts about **11 days earlier every year** and eventually passes through every Gregorian month.

---

Makar Sankranti falls on nearly the same Gregorian date every year, unlike Diwali. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it follows a **solar sidereal calendar**, and the Gregorian calendar is also **solar** — so the two stay roughly in step.
>
> **But it does drift slowly:** the sidereal year is 20 minutes longer than the tropical year, so **Makar Sankranti moves forward by one day every 71 years**. The cause is the **slow wobble of the Earth's axis**, like a spinning top.

---

Why can a festival's date differ between eastern and western India in the same year?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because many dates depend on the **exact lunar phase at sunrise** — and **sunrise is earlier in eastern India** than in the west.
>
> **The solution:** the **Positional Astronomy Centre** publishes the **Rashtriya Panchang** each year, calculating Sun and Moon positions for a **central location in India**, and gives advance notice of dates so holidays are uniform.

---

Give examples of the Moon appearing in Indian art forms.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Music:** ragas **Chandrakauns, Chandranandan, Shubhapantuvarali** ("auspicious moon")
> - **Dance:** Bharatanatyam mudras **Chandrakala** and **Ardhachandran** — also in Kathak, Odissi and Kuchipudi
> - **Painting and craft:** **Madhubani** and **Warli** paintings, and sculpture and pottery of the **Saura, Gond** and other tribes

<!-- ===== Satellites ===== -->

What is the difference between a natural and an artificial satellite?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Natural satellite** — the **Moon**, orbiting Earth without human involvement.
> - **Artificial satellite** — **human-made**, launched from Earth into orbit.

---

How high do most artificial satellites orbit, and how long does one orbit take?

> [!ANSWER]
> About **800 km** above the Earth's surface, taking roughly **100 minutes** for one orbit.
>
> So their motion is **periodic** too — just far faster than the Moon's.

---

Give five uses of artificial satellites.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Communication, navigation, weather monitoring, disaster management and scientific research.**

---

How would you spot a satellite, and why is just after sunset the best time?

> [!ANSWER]
> Look for a **point of light moving fast and steadily** across the sky — no flashing red or green lights (those mark an aircraft). Apps and websites list pass times for your location.
>
> **Why then:** the sky is **dark for you**, but the satellite high above is **still catching sunlight** — so it stands out brightly.

---

Name four ISRO missions and say what each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Cartosat** — high-quality images of Earth for maps, city planning and disaster management (feeding the **Bhuvan** platform)
> - **AstroSat** — scientific observation of stars and celestial objects
> - **Chandrayaan 1, 2, 3** — missions to the **Moon**
> - **Aditya L1** — studies the **Sun**; **Mangalyaan** — to **Mars**
>
> ISRO also lets Indian students build and launch small satellites — **AzaadiSat, InspireSat-1, Jugnu**.

---

What is space junk, and why is it a problem?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Satellites and spent rocket parts left in orbit after their useful life.**
>
> **The problem:** it **crowds space and can collide with working satellites**. Small pieces **burn up in the atmosphere** when they fall, but **larger ones can crash on the ground**. Countries are now working together to remove it.

---

Who is called the Father of the Indian Space Programme?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Vikram Ambalal Sarabhai** (1919–1971), a researcher in space science and nuclear physics who pioneered the effort to launch India's first artificial satellites.
>
> The **Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre (VSSC)** in Thiruvananthapuram, which develops ISRO's rockets and launch vehicles, is named after him.

---

If we had no clocks or calendars, how could we measure time?

> [!ANSWER]
> Exactly as our ancestors did:
>
> - **The Sun's shadow** (shortest at midday) for the **day**
> - **The Moon's phases** for the **month**
> - **The cycle of seasons** and the stars rising at sunset for the **year**

---

If Earth had two moons instead of one, how would timekeeping change?

> [!ANSWER]
> There would be **two independent phase cycles overlapping**, so the night sky would be far brighter and more complicated.
>
> **A Moon-based calendar would be much harder to build** — you'd have to choose which moon to count by, and the two would constantly go in and out of step with each other.
