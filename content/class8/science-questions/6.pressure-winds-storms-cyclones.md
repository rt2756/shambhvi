<!-- ===== Pressure ===== -->

Define pressure and give its SI unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Pressure = Force ÷ Area** — the force acting per unit area.
>
> SI unit: **newton per square metre (N/m²)**, also called the **pascal (Pa)**.

---

Two identical bags weigh the same, but one has narrow straps and hurts the shoulders. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **same weight acts on a smaller area** with narrow straps, so the **pressure on the shoulders is higher**.
>
> Broad straps spread the same force over a **larger area**, lowering the pressure — which is why they feel more comfortable.

---

A force of 100 N acts on a board of area 2 m². Find the pressure.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Pressure = 100 ÷ 2 =` **50 N/m² (50 Pa)**.

---

A 600 N force acts on 0.5 m². What is the pressure? What if the same force acts on 3 m²?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `600 ÷ 0.5 =` **1200 Pa**
> - `600 ÷ 3 =` **200 Pa**
>
> **Same force, six times the area, one-sixth the pressure.**

---

Why is a nail driven in with its pointed end rather than its head?

> [!ANSWER]
> The point has a **very small area**, so the same hammer force produces **very high pressure** — enough to push into the wood.
>
> The flat head spreads the force over a large area, giving too little pressure to penetrate.

---

Give three everyday examples of deliberately **reducing** pressure, and say how.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Broad straps** on a school bag
> - **Broad handles** on a water bucket
> - A **round cloth pad** under a pot carried on the head
>
> **All three increase the AREA**, so the same weight gives less pressure.

---

<!-- ===== Liquid pressure ===== -->

Two pipes of the same length but different diameters have balloons on the bottom and are filled with water to the same level. What do you observe?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both balloons bulge by exactly the same amount** — even though the wide pipe holds much more water.

---

What does that experiment prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That the bulge is caused by PRESSURE, not by the weight of the water.**
>
> The wide pipe holds far more water (and so more weight), yet the bulge is the same. The only thing the two pipes share is the **height of the water column** — so **pressure depends on the height**, not the quantity.

---

Add more water to one of the pipes. What happens, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The balloon bulges more.**
>
> Increasing the height of the column increases the pressure at the bottom — and higher pressure means a bigger bulge.

---

Why are overhead water tanks placed at a height?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **liquid pressure increases with the height of the column**.
>
> A tank on the roof gives a tall column above the taps, so the pressure at the taps is high and you get a **strong stream of water**.

---

You live on the second floor, your friend on the first, and the tank is on the roof. Who gets the stronger stream?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Your friend on the first floor.**
>
> **Why:** there is **more column height above** the first-floor tap than the second-floor tap, so the pressure there is greater.

---

Four holes are made at the same height near a bottle's base, sealed, and then opened at once when the bottle is full. What happens, and what does it show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Water spurts out of all four holes.**
>
> **It shows liquids exert pressure on the SIDES of a container too** — not just on the bottom. In fact liquids exert pressure **in all directions**.

---

Why does water fountain out of a leaking pipe joint?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **water inside is exerting pressure on the walls of the pipe**.
>
> Wherever there's a weak point, that sideways pressure pushes water out through it.

---

Why is the base of a dam much broader than its top?

> [!ANSWER]
> The stored water pushes **horizontally on the walls**, and that horizontal pressure is **greatest near the bottom** — because the water column above is tallest there.
>
> A **broad base** is needed both to support the structure and to withstand that large pressure.

---

<!-- ===== Air pressure ===== -->

What is the atmosphere made of, and how far does it extend?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **envelope of air surrounding the Earth** — mainly **nitrogen and oxygen**, with **argon, carbon dioxide** and other gases in small quantities.
>
> It extends **many kilometres** above the Earth's surface.

---

An inverted paper plate is harder to lift when covered by an unfolded sheet than by the same sheet folded. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **weight of the sheet is identical** in both cases — only the **area** changed.
>
> The unfolded sheet has a **larger area**, so **air pushes down on more of it**. Same pressure × more area = **more force** to overcome.
>
> This proves **air exerts pressure**.

---

Why does a balloon expand in all directions when you blow into it?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **air exerts pressure in all directions**, pushing outwards on every part of the balloon's wall equally.

---

How large is atmospheric pressure, and why aren't we crushed by it?

> [!ANSWER]
> The air column over just **15 cm × 15 cm** pushes with about **2250 N** — the weight of a **225 kg** object.
>
> **We aren't crushed because the pressure inside our bodies equals the atmospheric pressure**, balancing it from within. That internal pressure comes from the movement of fluids and gases in our tissues and organs.

---

Why does a rubber sucker stick to a smooth surface, and why is it hard to pull off?

> [!ANSWER]
> Pressing it **pushes most of the air out** from beneath the cup, so the **pressure inside is reduced**.
>
> The **surrounding air pressure is now higher**, and pushes the sucker firmly against the surface. To remove it you must apply enough force to **overcome that pressure difference**.

---

Name two practical units of air pressure and give their value in pascals.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **millibar (mb)** and the **hectopascal (hPa)** — **both equal to 100 Pa**.
>
> Weather maps and reports use them.

---

<!-- ===== Winds ===== -->

An inflated balloon and an uninflated one are joined by a straw. What happens, and when does it stop?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Air flows from the inflated balloon into the uninflated one** until both are about the same size, and then the flow **stops**.
>
> **It stops when the pressures become equal** — with no pressure difference, there is nothing to drive the flow.

---

State the rule that experiment demonstrates.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Air moves from a region of HIGH pressure to a region of LOW pressure.**
>
> And **the greater the pressure difference, the faster the air moves.**

---

Why are winds stronger on some days than on others?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **pressure difference** between regions is bigger on some days.
>
> A large difference drives air much faster than a small one.

---

Explain the sea breeze and the land breeze in terms of pressure.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Day:** land heats **faster** than water. Warm air over the land is **lighter and rises**, leaving **low pressure** over the land. Air from the higher-pressure sea blows in → **sea breeze** (sea → land).
> - **Night:** the water is now warmer, so **low pressure forms over the sea**, and wind blows **land → sea** → **land breeze**.
>
> Both are just air moving from high to low pressure.

---

Why does warm air rise?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **warm air is lighter** than the cooler air around it.
>
> As it rises it leaves a **low-pressure region** behind, and cooler high-pressure air rushes in to take its place.

---

<!-- ===== High-speed winds ===== -->

You blow into the gap between two hanging balloons. What happens, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They move towards each other** — and faster if you blow harder.
>
> **Why:** blowing creates a **low-pressure region between them**, and the **higher pressure on the outside pushes them together**.

---

State the rule about high-speed winds and pressure.

> [!ANSWER]
> **High-speed winds are accompanied by REDUCED air pressure.**
>
> The faster the wind, the lower the pressure in that region.

---

Why do high-speed winds blow roofs off houses?

> [!ANSWER]
> Winds passing **over** the roof create **low pressure above it**, while the pressure **inside** the house stays normal.
>
> The resulting **upward push** can lift a weak roof clean off.

---

Why should doors and windows be kept OPEN during a storm with high-speed winds?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because with them open, wind flows **through** the house too, so the **pressure inside also drops**.
>
> **The difference between inside and above the roof is greatly reduced**, so the roof isn't pushed up.
>
> (It feels wrong — you'd instinctively shut everything — but the physics says otherwise.)

---

<!-- ===== Storms and lightning ===== -->

Describe the chain of events that produces a storm.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Land gets heated**; warm moist air, being lighter, **rises** → low-pressure area.
> 2. **Cooler air from surrounding high-pressure areas rushes in**, gets heated, and rises too — a continuous circulation.
> 3. The rising air **expands and cools**; its moisture **condenses** into water droplets → **clouds**.
> 4. Droplets merge into **heavier drops** → **rain, hail or snow**.
>
> **Strong winds with rain = a storm.**

---

Why are storms more frequent in India than in cold, dry regions?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because storms need **heat and moisture**, and India is **hot, humid and tropical** — exactly the conditions that make warm moist air rise strongly.

---

How do electric charges develop inside a thundercloud?

> [!ANSWER]
> If air rises high enough, the **low temperature turns water droplets into ice particles**.
>
> **Strong winds blowing upwards and downwards make the water droplets and ice particles rub against each other** — and rubbing charges objects (Chapter 5). That produces **static charges within the cloud**.

---

How are the charges arranged in a thundercloud, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Positive** charges on the **lighter ice particles** → they move to the **TOP**
> - **Negative** charges on the **heavier water droplets** → they stay at the **BOTTOM**
>
> This is **charge separation**. The negative cloud base then makes the **ground and nearby trees and buildings positively charged**.

---

Air normally keeps opposite charges apart. What has to happen for lightning?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Air is an electrical insulator.** But when the **charge build-up becomes very large, the insulating property of air breaks down**.
>
> A **sudden flow of charges** takes place, producing the bright flash we call **lightning**.

---

Where can lightning occur?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Within a single cloud, between two clouds, or between a cloud and the ground.**

---

What causes thunder?

> [!ANSWER]
> Lightning **rapidly heats the air around it**, making that air **expand violently** — and that produces the loud sound.
>
> A storm with lightning and thunder is a **thunderstorm**.

---

Name two local Indian thunderstorms and say what they're good for.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Kalboishakhi** — West Bengal, Bihar, Jharkhand
> - **Bordoisila** — Assam
>
> Both arrive **before the monsoon** and help **kharif crops** grow.
>
> In Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu they're called **mango showers** because they help mangoes ripen; in Karnataka they also help coffee plants.

---

List four safety rules during lightning, with reasons.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Stay away from tall objects** (trees, poles) — lightning takes the shortest path to the ground, which is usually through the tallest thing around.
> - **Crouch in a low open area; do NOT lie flat** — lying down increases your contact with the ground.
> - **Don't use an umbrella with a metallic rod** — it makes you the tallest metal point nearby.
> - **Get out of water**, and **stay in a bus or car** if you're in one — a vehicle is comparatively safe.

---

How does a lightning conductor work?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **metallic rod** runs along the building's walls, with its **pointed upper end above the highest point** of the building and its **lower end buried deep in the ground**.
>
> It gives the charge an **easy path into the ground** — so the charge travels harmlessly down the rod instead of through the building.

---

<!-- ===== Cyclones ===== -->

Where do cyclones form, and why there?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Over warm ocean waters.**
>
> They need **warm, moist air** to rise, and only a warm ocean can supply enough of it.

---

Explain how a cyclone forms.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Heated ocean water makes **warm moist air rise**.
> 2. The rising vapour **condenses** into raindrops.
> 3. **Condensation releases the heat** taken up during evaporation — warming the ascending air **further**.
> 4. So it rises **even more**, dropping the central pressure **even lower**.
> 5. **Surrounding air rushes in** and it too starts rising.
> 6. **The Earth's rotation makes the moving air spin.**
>
> The result is a very low-pressure area with **high-speed winds revolving around it**.

---

Why is the released heat of condensation called the engine of a cyclone?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it is what makes the cycle **self-strengthening**.
>
> Evaporation takes heat **from the ocean**; condensation gives it **back to the air**, warming it so it rises harder — which lowers the pressure, pulls in more moist air, and repeats.

---

If the Earth stopped rotating, would cyclones still form?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not as we know them.** You would still get warm air rising and air rushing in — but **the Earth's rotation is what makes the moving air SPIN**.
>
> Without rotation you'd have a rising column of air and a storm, but not a revolving cyclone.

---

Why does a cyclone weaken once it reaches land?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **the source of warm moist air is cut off**.
>
> Without evaporation from a warm ocean feeding it, the condensation-heat engine stops and the cyclone gradually loses strength.

---

What is the eye of a cyclone?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The region of LOWEST pressure at the centre**, where the **wind is calm**.
>
> The region **surrounding** the eye experiences the strong winds and heavy rainfall.

---

What is a storm surge, and how big can it get?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Strong cyclone winds push ocean water towards the shore**, creating a **wall of water 3 to 12 metres high**.
>
> It floods coastal areas — and even areas far from the sea.

---

Why does a cyclone hurt farmland long after it's over?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **seawater rushing inland leaves salt in the soil**, and **salt makes soil less fertile**.
>
> Crops suffer for a long time afterwards, quite apart from the immediate flooding.

---

Name four other ways a cyclone disrupts life.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Contaminated drinking water** from seawater intrusion
> - **Rivers overflow** from heavy rainfall, and **landslides** may be triggered
> - **Roads blocked** by fallen trees and debris, so help can't reach
> - **Power outages lasting days**, disrupting emergency services

---

How do we protect ourselves from cyclones today?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Follow **weather reports, alerts and warnings from the India Meteorological Department (IMD)**
> - **Weather monitoring satellites** track cyclones and predict their path, greatly reducing loss of life
> - Keep an **emergency kit** ready if you live in a cyclone-prone area
> - **Move quickly to a designated cyclone shelter**

---

Two boats have base areas 7 m² and 3 m², with 5 people of similar weight in each. Which exerts more pressure on the water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The smaller boat (3 m²).**
>
> The **same total force** acts over a **smaller area**, so `Force ÷ Area` is larger.
>
> This is exactly why boats and ships are built with **broad, flat bottoms** — big area means low pressure, which helps them stay afloat.
