<!-- ===== Lenses and history ===== -->

Why is a lens called a "lens"?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the first ones were shaped like a **lentil seed** — thick in the middle and thin at the edges. *Lens* is Latin for **lentil**.

---

A water-filled round flask placed on a page makes the letters look bigger. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> The curved, water-filled flask **acts as a magnifying glass** — its shape bends the light passing through it, exactly like a convex lens.

---

Who first used the word "cell" in science, and what was he actually looking at?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Robert Hooke**, in his 1665 book ***Micrographia***.
>
> He was looking at a thin slice of **cork** through an early microscope (200–300× magnification) and saw it was full of tiny empty compartments like a **honeycomb**. He called each compartment a **cell**.

---

Who is called the Father of Microbiology, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek**, a Dutch scientist of the 1660s.
>
> He ground **better lenses** than anyone before, which let him build more useful microscopes — and he was the **first person to clearly see and describe bacteria and blood cells**.

---

<!-- ===== Seeing cells ===== -->

Describe how to prepare an onion peel slide, and say what you would see.

> [!ANSWER]
> Peel off the thin transparent layer from the inner surface of an onion piece → stain with **safranin** for 30 seconds → rinse in water → mount on a slide in **glycerin** → lower a coverslip gently.
>
> You would see **nearly rectangular cells packed closely with no gaps between them**.

---

Why do you add a stain like safranin or methylene blue?

> [!ANSWER]
> Cells are almost **transparent**, so they're nearly invisible against a bright background.
>
> A stain **increases the contrast**, making the cell parts stand out clearly.

---

Why is glycerin added, and why is the coverslip lowered at an angle?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Glycerin** stops the cells **drying out** and improves clarity.
> - **Lowering at an angle** avoids trapping **air bubbles**, which appear as dark circles and hide the cells underneath.

---

How would you make a slide of your own cheek cells, and what shape are they?

> [!ANSWER]
> Rinse your mouth, gently scrape the inside of your cheek with the **blunt** end of a clean toothpick, spread the material in a drop of water on a slide, stain with **methylene blue**, add glycerin, cover.
>
> The cells are **polygon-shaped** and form the inner lining of the mouth.

---

<!-- ===== Cell parts ===== -->

Name the three parts common to every cell and say what each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Cell membrane** — encloses the cell, separates it from its neighbours, and being **porous** lets useful materials in and waste out.
> - **Cytoplasm** — the jelly between membrane and nucleus, holding carbohydrates, proteins, fats and mineral salts; **most life processes happen here**.
> - **Nucleus** — **regulates all the cell's activities** and its growth.

---

Which cells have a cell wall, and what does it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Plant, fungal and bacterial cells** — but **not animal cells**.
>
> It sits **outside** the cell membrane and gives **rigidity and strength**, which is why plant cells look firm and are packed together compactly.

---

List three differences between a plant cell and an animal cell.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | Plant cell | Animal cell |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Cell wall** | present | absent |
> | **Chloroplasts** | present | absent |
> | **Vacuole** | one **large** one | absent, or very small |

---

What are plastids and chloroplasts?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Plastids** are tiny rod-shaped structures found in the cells of all parts of a plant.
>
> **Chloroplasts** are plastids containing **chlorophyll**, which makes them green and lets them carry out **photosynthesis**. Plastids in **non-green** parts help **store substances** instead.

---

What does the large vacuole do in a plant cell?

> [!ANSWER]
> It **stores important substances**, **gets rid of waste**, and **maintains the cell's shape** — giving the plant strength and support.
>
> Animal cells either lack vacuoles or have small ones that store substances dissolved in water.

---

<!-- ===== Shape and function ===== -->

State the rule connecting a cell's shape to its job, with two examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A cell's shape, size and structure are matched to the function it performs.**
>
> - **Nerve cells** are **long and branched** → they reach distant parts of the body and carry messages quickly.
> - **Muscle cells** are **spindle-shaped, thin and flexible** → they contract and relax in waves to push food down the food pipe.

---

Why are cheek cells thin and flat?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because they form a **protective lining** on the inner surface of the cheek — a covering needs to be a thin sheet, not a lump.

---

The stomach contains more than one kind of cell. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it does more than one job.
>
> **Muscle cells** in the wall **churn** the food, while cells in the **inner lining produce digestive juices and acid** to break it down. Different jobs need differently shaped, differently equipped cells.

---

<!-- ===== Levels of organisation ===== -->

List the levels of organisation in a living body, and define each.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ system → Organism**
>
> - **Tissue** — a group of **similar** cells
> - **Organ** — different tissues working together
> - **Organ system** — several organs performing one major function
> - **Organism** — all the organ systems together

---

Why is a cell compared to a brick?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because a **brick is the basic unit of a wall**, just as a **cell is the basic unit of a living body** — the wall has no smaller piece that is still a wall, and the body has no smaller piece that is still alive.

---

Where does a multicellular organism's life begin?

> [!ANSWER]
> **From a single cell — the egg.**
>
> It has the remarkable ability to **divide repeatedly** until it forms a complete living being made of many cells.

---

What is the largest known cell?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **yolk of an ostrich egg** — a single cell about **130 to 170 mm** across.
>
> The shell and the white are **non-cellular extras**: protection and nourishment for the developing cell.

---

<!-- ===== Microorganisms ===== -->

What is a microorganism, and where are they found?

> [!ANSWER]
> A living thing **too small to be seen with the naked eye** (*micro* = very small). Also called a **microbe**.
>
> **Everywhere** — water, soil, air, on leaves and stems, in hot springs, in snow, and inside our own bodies (especially the gut).

---

Name four groups of microorganisms and say which are unicellular.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Bacteria** — unicellular
> - **Protozoa** (Amoeba, Paramecium) — unicellular
> - **Fungi** — unicellular (**yeast**) or multicellular (**mould**)
> - **Algae** — unicellular or multicellular
>
> **Plants and animals are always multicellular.**

---

What is the one feature that separates bacteria from every other kind of cell?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Bacteria have no well-defined nucleus and no nuclear membrane.**
>
> Their genetic material lies in a region called the **nucleoid**.
>
> Yeast, protozoa, algae, fungi, plants and animals **all** have a proper nucleus — bacteria alone do not.

---

Fungi have a cell wall like plants. Why can't they make their own food?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **fungi have no chloroplasts**, and therefore no chlorophyll.
>
> Photosynthesis needs chlorophyll to capture sunlight, so fungi must obtain food from elsewhere.

---

How are viruses different from all other microorganisms?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Viruses are acellular** — they aren't made of cells at all.
>
> They can only **multiply inside a living host cell**, which they invade. Outside a host they do nothing, which is why they sit awkwardly on the boundary of "living".

---

Amoeba, Paramecium and algae were seen in pond water. How would you tell them apart?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Amoeba** — single cell, **irregular, changing** shape, moves by flowing
> - **Paramecium** — single cell, moves about using **specialised structures**
> - **Algae** — single cell, **green** because of chlorophyll, moves using specialised structures
>
> **The green colour is the giveaway for algae** — it means they make their own food.

---

What magnification do you need to see microorganisms, and what is a foldable paper microscope for?

> [!ANSWER]
> **100 to 400 times.**
>
> A **foldable paper microscope** is a low-cost design that shows less detail than a laboratory instrument — but it makes the microscopic world **accessible to far more people**.

---

An electron microscope magnifies about 10,00,000 times. What does that let you see?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sub-cellular components** — structures inside the cell far too small for an ordinary light microscope to reveal.

---

<!-- ===== Decomposition ===== -->

Fruit and vegetable peels buried under soil turn dark and crumbly in 2–3 weeks. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fungi and bacteria in the soil break the complex substances of the peels down into simpler, nutrient-rich ones** — a process called **decomposition**.
>
> The result is **manure**, which returns nutrients to the soil and increases its fertility.

---

What would happen if microorganisms did not exist on Earth?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Dead leaves, dead bodies and waste would pile up forever**, and the nutrients locked inside them would **never return to the soil**.
>
> Plants would run out of nutrients, and the nutrient cycle would stop being a cycle at all.

---

Snehal digs two pits. In pit A she mixes fruit and vegetable peels with dried leaves; in pit B she puts the peels alone. Both are covered with soil for 3 weeks. What is she testing?

> [!ANSWER]
> Whether **adding dried leaves changes how well (or how fast) the waste decomposes into manure**.
>
> Everything else — waste type, soil cover, time — is kept the same, so any difference must come from the dried leaves. **That's a fair test.**

---

Manure forms best under certain conditions. Which, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **An optimal temperature and the right level of moisture.**
>
> **Why:** the decomposers are living organisms. Like any living thing they need suitable warmth and water to grow and stay active — too cold, too hot or too dry and they slow down or stop.

---

What is biogas, how is it made, and what is it used for?

> [!ANSWER]
> A mixture of gases — mainly **methane**, with carbon dioxide — released when certain **bacteria and fungi that live without oxygen** decompose plant, animal or household waste.
>
> It is used as a fuel for **cooking, heating, generating electricity, and even running vehicles**.

---

Who was Ananda Mohan Chakrabarty, and what is a patent?

> [!ANSWER]
> An Indian-born scientist (1938–2020) who in **1971 developed a bacterium that could break down oil spills**, showing microbes could solve pollution problems. It was granted a patent in **1980**.
>
> A **patent** is a right given to an inventor so that **no one else may copy, use or sell the invention without permission**.

---

<!-- ===== Food ===== -->

Dough with yeast rises and turns fluffy; dough without yeast doesn't. Explain fully.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yeast is a fungus that respires**, breaking down the sugar for energy.
>
> This releases **carbon dioxide**, whose **bubbles** make the dough soft and fluffy, plus a little **alcohol**, which gives the changed smell.

---

Why do you add sugar and **warm** water to yeast dough?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Sugar** is the yeast's **food** — without it there's nothing to respire.
> - **Warm** water because **yeast grows well in warm conditions**; cold slows it right down.

---

Aanandi puts sugar solution in two test tubes, adds yeast to B only, fits a balloon on each and keeps them warm. B's balloon inflates. Which explanation is correct: (a) water evaporated, (b) warm air expanded, (c) yeast produced a gas, (d) sugar reacted with warm air?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(c) — yeast produced a gas.**
>
> **Why the others fail:** tube A had the **same warmth and the same sugar solution** but its balloon did *not* inflate. So warmth (b) and evaporation (a) can't be the cause — the **only difference was the yeast**.
>
> That's the whole point of having a control tube.

---

She then transfers the gas from the balloon into lime water and shakes it. What is she trying to find out?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Whether the gas is carbon dioxide.**
>
> Lime water **turns milky** in the presence of carbon dioxide — it's the standard test.

---

Milk with a spoon of curd sets in a warm place but not in the refrigerator. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> Curd contains **Lactobacillus** bacteria. They feed on the milk sugar (**lactose**), multiply, and produce **lactic acid**, which makes the milk set and taste sour.
>
> **These bacteria grow well only in warm conditions** — in the cold they barely multiply, so the milk stays milk.

---

Yeast and Lactobacillus both ferment. What's the difference?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Yeast** produces **carbon dioxide and alcohol** → the gas puffs up bread, cake and bhatura.
> - **Lactobacillus** produces **lactic acid** → which sets curd and makes it sour.
>
> **Same process, different product** — which is why one makes food rise and the other makes it set.

---

A student leaves curd out for a day and it becomes more sour. Give two possible explanations.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The **Lactobacillus kept multiplying** in the warm room and produced **more lactic acid**.
> 2. **Other microorganisms from the air** may also have grown in it, adding their own acids.

---

Why don't microbes spoil pickles and murabbas?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because of the **high concentration of salt or sugar** added along with the spices.
>
> Such concentrations **prevent microorganisms from growing**, which is exactly why salt and sugar are called **preservatives**.

---

One bread slice is left near the sink and another in the refrigerator. After three days, what do you expect and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> The slice **near the sink grows mould**; the refrigerated one stays largely unchanged.
>
> **Why:** the area near a sink is **warm and moist** — ideal for fungal growth. The refrigerator is **cold and dry**, so microbes grow far more slowly.

<!-- ===== Rhizobium ===== -->

A wheat farmer adds nitrogen fertiliser but the neighbouring bean farmer doesn't. Why not?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **legumes** like beans have **root nodules containing Rhizobium bacteria**, which **trap nitrogen from the air** and make it usable by the plant.
>
> The bean plant gets its nitrogen free; the wheat plant has no such partner and needs fertiliser.

---

Air is 78% nitrogen. Why do plants still run short of it?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **plants cannot use nitrogen gas directly**.
>
> It must first be converted into a usable form — which is exactly what **Rhizobium** does inside the root nodules.

---

Why do farmers grow legumes in rotation with other crops?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **Rhizobium in the legume's root nodules enriches the soil with nitrogen**.
>
> After the legume is harvested, the next crop finds a **naturally more fertile soil** — saving chemical fertiliser and keeping the land healthy.

---

<!-- ===== Microalgae ===== -->

What are microalgae, and why do they matter so much?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Microscopic plant-like organisms** in water, soil, air and on trees that make their own food using sunlight.
>
> **In doing so they release more than half of the Earth's oxygen supply.** They also feed aquatic animals, help clean water, and are used to make **biofuel**.

---

Name three useful microalgae and what they're used for.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Spirulina, Chlorella and Diatoms** — used as **health supplements and medicines**.
>
> **Spirulina** is called a superfood: over **60% protein** by weight, very little fat or sugar, and a good source of **vitamin B12**.

---

What threatens microalgae, and why should we care?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Pollution, climate change and habitat destruction.**
>
> We should care because they supply **over half the planet's oxygen** — losing them would upset the oxygen balance of the whole Earth, not just their own habitat.

---

<!-- ===== Unicellular vs multicellular ===== -->

What is the advantage of being multicellular?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Specialisation.**
>
> A single cell must do **every** job adequately. A body of many cells can have some doing nothing but carrying messages and others doing nothing but churning food — **each far better at its own task**, which raises the organism's chance of survival.

---

Give a unicellular and a multicellular example from the same group of organisms.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fungi:** **yeast** is unicellular; **mould** is multicellular.
>
> This shows that "unicellular" and "multicellular" cut *across* the groups rather than defining them.

---

Design an experiment to show that microorganisms need warmth, air and moisture to grow.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take **four identical slices of bread**:
>
> - **A** — moistened, in a warm place, open to air (all three conditions)
> - **B** — moistened, in a **refrigerator**, open to air (no warmth)
> - **C** — **dry**, warm, open to air (no moisture)
> - **D** — moistened, warm, sealed in an **airtight** bag (no air)
>
> Observe after 3 days. **Only A should grow mould well.**
>
> **Why this design works:** each slice removes **exactly one** condition, so any slice that fails tells you that condition was needed. Changing two at once would prove nothing.

---

Identify: (i) I live in every environment, including your gut. (ii) I make bread and cakes soft and fluffy. (iii) I live in the roots of pulse crops and feed them.

> [!ANSWER]
> (i) **Bacteria** · (ii) **Yeast** · (iii) **Rhizobium**
