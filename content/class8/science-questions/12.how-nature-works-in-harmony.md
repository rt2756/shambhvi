<!-- ===== Elephants ===== -->

Why do elephants enter farms and villages in states like Odisha and Assam?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **vegetation is scarce and waterholes dry up in their natural habitat**, so they wander into farms and plantations looking for food such as bananas and sugarcane.
>
> The root causes are **changes in rainfall and temperature** affecting vegetation, plus **trees cut for roads and buildings** — together drying and shrinking the forests that are the elephants' home.

---

What is a wildlife corridor, and why does it help?

> [!ANSWER]
> A marked stretch of land **connecting forest habitats**, letting animals travel between large forests **without coming into conflict with human settlements**.
>
> **Why it works:** the animals' problem is not that they want to raid farms — it's that fragmented forest leaves them no safe route between feeding areas. A corridor restores the route.

---

<!-- ===== Habitats ===== -->

Define habitat, and give an unusual example.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The place where an organism lives.**
>
> It can be a pond, a forest or a farm — but also something as small as **the bark of a tree**, which is a complete habitat for the organisms living on it.

---

Distinguish biotic from abiotic components, with three examples each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Biotic** — the **living** components: plants, animals, microorganisms
> - **Abiotic** — the **non-living** components: air, water, soil, sunlight, temperature

---

How does a fish in a pond get its biotic and abiotic needs?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Biotic needs (food)** — from small plants and animals in the pond
> - **Abiotic needs (oxygen)** — from the water
>
> A habitat must supply **food, oxygen, shelter and space to grow**.

---

A rodent active by day and a snake active by night share the same forest. How can that be?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **a forest is warm by day and cool by night** — so although the habitat is the same, **the conditions each animal experiences are quite different**.
>
> **This is how many organisms coexist in one place: they use the same resources at different times or in different ways**, reducing direct competition.

---

<!-- ===== Levels of organisation ===== -->

Define population, community and ecosystem.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Population** — a group of **the same type** of organism in a habitat at a given time
> - **Community** — **different populations** sharing the same habitat: plants, animals and microorganisms together
> - **Ecosystem** — **the community PLUS the abiotic components**, all interacting

---

Which of these is wrong: (i) a community is larger than a population; (ii) a community is smaller than an ecosystem; (iii) an ecosystem is part of a community?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(iii) is wrong.**
>
> The order is **population → community → ecosystem**, so the **community is part of the ecosystem**, not the other way round.

---

What would happen if a habitat contained only one type of organism?

> [!ANSWER]
> They would all need the **same food, water and space** — leading to **fierce competition and scarcity of resources**.
>
> A mix of organisms with different requirements lets a habitat support far more life.

---

Name the two main types of ecosystem, with examples of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Aquatic** — ponds, rivers, lakes, seas
> - **Terrestrial** — forests, grasslands, deserts, farms, even a single large tree
>
> Ecosystems can be **large or small**, and they **overlap** — a river crossing forest, grassland and farmland has several interacting at once.

---

<!-- ===== Indirect effects ===== -->

Pond A has fish and many flowering plants around it; Pond B has no fish and fewer flowers. Explain the connection.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Fish eat dragonfly larvae**, so Pond A has **fewer dragonflies**.
> 2. **Dragonflies eat bees, flies and butterflies**, so Pond A has **more of these**.
> 3. Those insects **pollinate flowers**, moving pollen from one flower to another.
> 4. So plants around Pond A **produce more seeds**.
>
> **A fish in the water changes how many seeds a plant on the bank produces** — through three non-obvious steps.

---

What does that study teach about disturbing an ecosystem?

> [!ANSWER]
> That effects can be **indirect and unexpected**.
>
> **Overfishing** wouldn't just reduce fish — it would raise dragonfly numbers, cut pollinator numbers, and **lower seed production on the bank**. You cannot change one part in isolation.

---

What is pollination, and why does it matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> The transfer of **pollen grains** from the **stamens** (male part) to the **carpels** (female part) of the same or a different flower.
>
> **Wind, water, insects, bats and birds** carry the pollen.
>
> **It is essential for the formation of fruits and seeds** — no pollination, no next generation of plants and no fruit crop.

---

Give one example each of an abiotic–biotic, abiotic–abiotic and biotic–biotic interaction.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Abiotic–biotic:** earthworms live in **moist soil**
> - **Abiotic–abiotic:** **sunlight** makes pond water evaporate faster
> - **Biotic–biotic:** a frog eats insects; frogs and fish **compete** for insect larvae

---

Give two ways living things change the non-living parts of an ecosystem.

> [!ANSWER]
> - Plants **release oxygen** during photosynthesis
> - Roots **hold soil in place and prevent erosion**; plants **retain soil moisture and cool the atmosphere**
>
> **The dependence runs both ways** — it's easy to see that life needs soil and air, less obvious that soil and air are shaped by life.

---

<!-- ===== Feeding ===== -->

Define producers, consumers and decomposers, giving the Greek roots.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Producers (autotrophs)** — *auto* = self, *troph* = food. Make their own food by **photosynthesis**: green plants.
> - **Consumers (heterotrophs)** — *hetero* = other. Depend on other organisms: animals.
> - **Decomposers (saprotrophs)** — *sapro* = rotten. Feed on **dead matter**: fungi, bacteria.

---

Distinguish herbivores, carnivores and omnivores with examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Herbivores** — eat **only plants**: deer, hare, squirrel
> - **Carnivores** — eat **only animals**: leopard, vulture, shikra
> - **Omnivores** — eat **both**: crow, fox, mouse

---

What is a food chain? Give two examples from a grassland.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A simple sequence showing "who eats whom" in an ecosystem.**
>
> - `Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle`
> - `Grass → Hare → Leopard`

---

What is a trophic level? Name the four levels with examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> **An organism's position in a food chain** (*troph* = food).
>
> 1. **Producers** — green plants
> 2. **Herbivores** — hares, deer
> 3. **Small carnivores** — frogs
> 4. **Large carnivores** — tigers, vultures

---

In a crop field there are many millet plants, fewer mice and very few eagles. Why does this form a pyramid?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **it takes a great many plants to feed one mouse, and a great many mice to feed one eagle**.
>
> Each level up can therefore support **far fewer individuals** — so the numbers narrow towards the top.

---

What is a food web, and why is it more realistic than a food chain?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A network formed when food chains interlink**, because **each organism is usually eaten by two or more kinds of organism**.
>
> **It explains resilience:** if one prey species vanishes, a predator with several food sources can switch — one with only a single source cannot.

---

In the chain `Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake`, what happens if the frogs disappear?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Grasshoppers increase** — their predator is gone.
> - **Snakes decrease** — their food is gone (unless they can switch to other prey).
>
> And with more grasshoppers, the **grass suffers** too — the effect passes both up and down the chain.

---

<!-- ===== Decomposition ===== -->

What is decomposition, and who carries it out?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Microorganisms like fungi and bacteria break down complex substances in dead plants and animals into simpler ones**, returning nutrients to the soil.
>
> The organisms doing it are **decomposers (saprotrophs)** — fungi, bacteria, and small insects like beetles and flies on dung.

---

What would happen if all decomposers disappeared from a forest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Dead matter would pile up unbroken**, and the nutrients locked inside it would **never return to the soil**.
>
> Soil fertility would fall, **plants would fail**, and every organism depending on plants would follow.
>
> **In nature nothing is wasted — decomposers are what make the recycling possible.**

---

Where do most of the nutrients in soil come from?

> [!ANSWER]
> Largely from the **decomposition** of dead plants and animals.
>
> That's why forests stay fertile without anyone adding fertiliser.

---

<!-- ===== Chains of change ===== -->

Pollution kills the plants in a pond. Trace the chain of consequences.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> plants die → less oxygen produced in the water
>            → fish die
>            → fewer consumers, so insects multiply
>            → insects spread to nearby farmland
>            → farmers use more pesticides
>            → further environmental damage
> ```
>
> **One change in a pond ends up affecting farmland and human health.**

---

What happened when India exported frog legs in the 1980s?

> [!ANSWER]
> Large-scale harvesting of the **Indian bullfrog** **cut frog populations**. Since **frogs eat insects**, agricultural **pests rose** — so farmers used **more synthetic pesticides**, harming soil, water quality, the environment and human health.
>
> **The Government of India banned the export of frog legs** to prevent further ecological damage.

---

When is an ecosystem "in balance", and is that balance fixed?

> [!ANSWER]
> When **interactions among organisms and their environment keep populations and resources stable**.
>
> **The balance is DYNAMIC, not fixed** — it constantly adjusts, and can be disrupted by natural or human-made changes.

---

<!-- ===== Relationships ===== -->

Why is competition good for an ecosystem?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **it controls population size and keeps the ecosystem balanced**.
>
> Without it, **one species could multiply too far**, using up resources and unbalancing everything else.

---

Define mutualism, commensalism and parasitism, with an example each.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Relationship | Who benefits | Example |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Mutualism** | **both** | honeybee and flower — bee gets nectar, flower gets pollinated |
> | **Commensalism** | one benefits, other **unaffected** | orchid on a tree — orchid gets support, branch unharmed |
> | **Parasitism** | one benefits, other **harmed** | tick on a dog — tick feeds on blood, dog gets skin irritation |

---

Who was A.J.T. Johnsingh, and what did his research show?

> [!ANSWER]
> A famous Indian **wildlife biologist** and a pioneer of **modern tracking systems** for studying wildlife.
>
> Working in **Bandipur National Park, Karnataka**, he showed how predators like **tigers and leopards depend on prey such as deer and wild boar** — proving that **a healthy prey population is key to predator survival**.

<!-- ===== Ecosystem services ===== -->

List the benefits humans get from forests and from aquatic ecosystems.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Forests** — fresh air, fertile soil, food, fibres, timber, medicines
> - **Aquatic ecosystems** — water and food
>
> Both also give **aesthetic and recreational value** and help **regulate climate**.

---

Why are the Sundarbans important, and what threatens them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The largest mangrove forest in the world**, where the Ganges and Brahmaputra meet. It is home to many **endangered species**, **slows strong winds and waves during storms and floods**, and its trees **absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen**. **UNESCO made it a World Heritage Site in 1987.**
>
> **Threats:** mangroves cut for **fuelwood and farming**, **illegal hunting** and resource overuse, and **pollution from industrial waste and untreated sewage**.

---

Selvam's village in Cuddalore was less affected by the 2004 tsunami than nearby villages, thanks to mangroves. How?

> [!ANSWER]
> Mangroves **physically slow the water and break the force of the waves** with their dense tangle of roots and trunks.
>
> By the time the surge reached the village behind them, it had lost much of its energy — so the damage was far less than in villages with no such barrier.

---

Name four kinds of protected area in India, with an example of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> **National parks** (Jim Corbett, Uttarakhand) · **wildlife sanctuaries** (Eaglenest, Arunachal Pradesh) · **biosphere reserves** (Nilgiri, Western Ghats) · **community conserved areas**.
>
> Also **Manas** (Assam), **Chilika Lake** (Odisha), **Hemis** (Leh), **Keibul Lamjao** (Manipur), **Pirotan Island Marine National Park** (Gujarat).

---

Why do migratory birds matter to Indian ecosystems?

> [!ANSWER]
> They act as **pollinators and seed dispersers along their route**, linking two distant habitats — and they **prey on insect pests**, helping farmers control pest populations and indirectly supporting healthy crop growth.
>
> **Demoiselle Cranes** visit the water body at **Khichan village, Jodhpur**, every winter.

<!-- ===== Farming ===== -->

What is a human-made ecosystem, and how does it differ from a natural one?

> [!ANSWER]
> One created by humans to meet their needs — **fish ponds, farms, parks**.
>
> **Unlike natural ecosystems, they need continuous human care and management** to keep working. Well designed, they can reduce pollution, support biodiversity and provide recreation.

---

What was the Green Revolution, and why was it needed?

> [!ANSWER]
> The mid-20th-century use of **tractors, machines, synthetic fertilisers and pesticides** to raise food production.
>
> **Why it was needed:** between **1950 and 1965** India faced a **food crisis** from low crop production. The Green Revolution made the country food secure.

---

Why is overuse of synthetic fertilisers now considered harmful?

> [!ANSWER]
> It **reduces friendly soil microorganisms** and lowers **humus** (organic matter), which binds soil particles together.
>
> **Without enough humus the soil becomes prone to erosion** — so fertility falls in the long run even though yields rise at first.

---

Why can overusing pesticides eventually make the pest problem worse?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two reasons:
>
> 1. Pesticides **kill the natural predators** of pests, so pest numbers **rise** once the spraying stops.
> 2. **Some pests develop resistance**, making them very difficult to control.

---

What is monoculture, and what does it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Growing the same crop repeatedly on the same land.**
>
> It **reduces biodiversity**, degrades the soil, and **harms pollinators** — which are crucial for food production.

---

How do heavy irrigation and repeated ploughing affect soil life?

> [!ANSWER]
> They **disturb soil organisms like earthworms and snails**, which are important for maintaining ecological balance — they aerate the soil and help break down organic matter.

---

What is Kunapa Jala, and what does Vrikshayurveda say about soil?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Kunapa Jala** is a **liquid fertiliser made from animal and plant waste by fermentation**, which breaks complex substances into simpler ones.
>
> The ancient text **Vrikshayurveda** emphasises soil health and strongly advocates **continuous nourishment of the soil with organic manure** and composted materials.

---

'Human-made ecosystems like agricultural fields are necessary, but they must be made sustainable.' Comment.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Necessary** because a growing population cannot be fed from natural ecosystems alone — farming is what makes food security possible.
>
> **But they must be sustainable**, because the Green Revolution's methods — chemical overuse, groundwater depletion, monoculture — **degrade the very soil and pollinators that farming depends on**. Organic and natural farming methods aim to keep production high while interfering minimally with natural ecosystems.

---

Why can't an ecosystem exist with only producers, and no consumers or decomposers?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **nutrients would never be recycled**.
>
> Producers take nutrients from the soil to grow. Without **decomposers**, dead plant matter would pile up and those nutrients would stay locked inside it — so the soil would eventually run out and the producers themselves would fail.

---

Students notice fewer butterflies in the school garden than last year. Suggest possible reasons and remedies.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Possible reasons:** fewer flowering plants; **use of pesticides**; loss of the specific plants caterpillars feed on; habitat cleared or paved; changes in weather.
>
> **Remedies:** plant more **nectar-rich flowering plants** and the host plants caterpillars need; **stop using pesticides**; leave some wild corners undisturbed; provide a shallow water source.

---

If the Indian hare population drops because of disease, what happens to other organisms?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Predators of hares** (foxes, eagles) **decrease** — or switch to other prey, putting pressure on those species instead.
> - **Grasses and plants the hares ate increase** — which may then affect other herbivores.
>
> **The effect spreads both up and down the food web**, which is exactly why one species' health matters to the whole ecosystem.
