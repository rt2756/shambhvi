<!-- ===== Magnetic effect ===== -->

If you have no lamp, how can you tell whether current is flowing in a circuit?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Put a magnetic compass near the wire.**
>
> When current flows the needle **deflects**; when it stops the needle **returns** to its usual direction.

---

A compass under a wire deflects when the switch is turned ON and returns when it is turned OFF. What does this prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That an electric current produces a magnetic field around the conductor.**
>
> **Why the proof works:** a compass needle is a **tiny magnet** and deflects only near a magnetic field. Nothing was moved and no magnet was brought near — **the only thing that changed was the current**.

---

Define "magnetic field".

> [!ANSWER]
> **The region around a magnet or a current-carrying wire where its magnetic effect can be felt** — for example, by the deflection of a compass needle.

---

What happens to the magnetic field when the current stops?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It disappears immediately.**
>
> The field exists only while current flows — which is exactly what makes electromagnets switchable.

---

Who discovered the link between electricity and magnetism, and how?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Hans Christian Oersted**, in **1820**, in Denmark.
>
> While giving a demonstration he noticed a nearby **compass needle deflected whenever a circuit was opened or closed**. He investigated until he was certain, then published.

---

After Oersted published, other scientists repeated his experiment. Why does that matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **a result only becomes accepted science once other people can reproduce it**.
>
> One person's observation could be a mistake, a coincidence or a faulty instrument. **Repetition by others is the check.**

---

Name four devices that depend on the magnetic effect of electric current.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Electromagnets, electric bells, motors, fans and loudspeakers.**

---

<!-- ===== Electromagnets ===== -->

What is an electromagnet?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A current-carrying coil that behaves as a magnet.**
>
> For practical use, most electromagnets have an **iron core** inside the coil to make them stronger.

---

Wire is wound round an iron nail and connected to a cell. Paper clips cling when the current is on and fall when it is off. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> Current through the coil creates a **magnetic field**, which magnetises the iron nail — so it attracts the iron clips.
>
> Switch the current off and **the field disappears**, so the nail is no longer a magnet and the clips fall.

---

A coil alone deflects a compass, but the deflection is much bigger with an iron nail inside. Why include the core?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **iron core makes the electromagnet much stronger** — bigger deflection, and enough force to attract clips.
>
> That is why practical electromagnets almost always have one.

---

How would you find which end of an electromagnet is its north pole?

> [!ANSWER]
> Bring a **magnetic compass** near one end and see which of the **compass's** poles is attracted.
>
> Since **unlike poles attract**, if the compass's **north** pole is drawn to that end, **that end is a south pole** — and the other end is north.

---

Name three ways to make an electromagnet stronger.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Increase the current** (use more cells).
> 2. **Increase the number of turns** in the coil.
> 3. Put an **iron core** inside.

---

Why does a battery of 2 cells make a stronger electromagnet than 1 cell?

> [!ANSWER]
> **More cells drive a larger current**, and a larger current creates a **stronger magnetic field**.
>
> The result is a bigger compass deflection and more clips attracted.

---

How do you reverse the poles of an electromagnet?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Reverse the direction of the current** — swap the connections to the cell.
>
> No permanent magnet can do this; it's one of the advantages of an electromagnet.

---

**True or false:** an electromagnet with a single cell attracts more clips than the same electromagnet with a battery of 2 cells.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
>
> More cells → more current → stronger field → **more** clips, not fewer.

---

**True or false:** a coil becomes an electromagnet only when electric current flows through it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.**
>
> Without current there is no magnetic field, and the coil is just wire.

---

What are lifting electromagnets used for, and why couldn't a permanent magnet do the job?

> [!ANSWER]
> They are hung from **cranes** in factories and scrap yards to **move, lift and sort heavy iron and steel**. The operator switches the current ON to lift and OFF to release.
>
> **A permanent magnet could never let go** — the load would stay stuck to it.

---

Sumana forgets to switch her lifting electromagnet OFF. What happens to the load, and what else might happen?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The load stays attached** — it can't be released while the current keeps the field alive.
>
> Also, current flowing continuously will **drain the cell quickly** and **heat the wire** (the heating effect), which can weaken the cell or damage the insulation.

---

Why is the Earth itself a magnet?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because deep inside it, the **movement of liquid iron in the core creates electric currents**, and those currents generate a magnetic field.
>
> It's the same magnetic effect of current — on a planetary scale.

---

Give two ways the Earth's magnetic field matters to life.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Many **migratory birds, fish and animals navigate by it** across continents and oceans.
> 2. It **shields Earth from harmful particles arriving from space**.

---

Electricity produces magnetism. Does the reverse also happen?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — a moving magnet can produce an electric current.**
>
> That second half of the link is what makes **power generators** possible, and it's studied in higher classes.

---

<!-- ===== Heating effect ===== -->

What is resistance, and what does it do to electrical energy?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Resistance is the opposition a conductor offers to the flow of current.**
>
> That opposition converts some **electrical energy into heat energy** — which is why a current-carrying wire warms up.

---

Why is nichrome used for the experiment rather than copper?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **nichrome offers much higher resistance than copper** of the same size and length.
>
> High resistance means more electrical energy is turned into heat, so the nichrome warms noticeably while copper barely does.

---

Why do appliances use nichrome for the heating element but copper for the wiring?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You want the heat exactly where the element is, and nowhere else.**
>
> Nichrome's high resistance makes it heat up; copper's low resistance lets current through with very little heating, so the connecting wires stay cool and safe.

---

List five things that decide how much heat a wire produces.

> [!ANSWER]
> - the **material** of the wire
> - its **thickness**
> - its **length**
> - the **amount of current**
> - the **duration** for which the current flows

---

For the same time, does a wire heat more with one cell or two? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **With two cells.**
>
> Two cells drive a **larger current**, and the heat generated depends on the magnitude of the current.

---

Name six household appliances that work on the heating effect, and say what they all contain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Electric room heater, electric stove, electric kettle, electric iron, water-heating immersion rod, hair dryer.**
>
> All of them contain a **heating element** — a rod or coil of wire. In some it is visible, glowing **red hot**.

---

Why does the filament of an incandescent lamp glow?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **electric current heats it** — the heating effect again.
>
> It gets so hot that it glows, giving out light as well as heat.

---

Give two problems caused by the heating effect.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Energy loss in transmission wires** — some of the electricity is wasted as heat on the way.
> 2. **Overheating in appliances** can **melt the plastic of plugs and sockets, or even start fires**.

---

How are those hazards guarded against?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Household circuits include **safety devices**.
> - You must use **wires, plugs and sockets rated for the current** they will carry — under-rated ones overheat.

---

An electric current flows through a nichrome wire and (i) the wire becomes warm, (ii) a compass below it deflects. What does this show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That both effects happen at the same time from the same current.**
>
> The heating effect (from resistance) and the magnetic effect (from the field around the conductor) are not alternatives — every current does both.

---

Give one industrial use of the heating effect.

> [!ANSWER]
> **High-temperature electric furnaces in steel plants**, used to **melt and recycle scrap steel** into usable steel.

---

<!-- ===== Cells and batteries ===== -->

Draw the parts of a Voltaic cell and say what each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Two electrodes** — rods of **two different metals**, partly dipped
> - **An electrolyte** — a liquid, usually a weak acid or salt solution
> - A glass or plastic **container**
>
> A **chemical reaction between the rods and the electrolyte produces electricity**, and current flows from the positive terminal through the circuit to the negative.

---

What does it mean when a cell is "dead"?

> [!ANSWER]
> That the **chemicals inside have been used up**, so the reaction that produced electricity can no longer happen.
>
> A dead Voltaic or dry cell cannot supply any more electricity and must be replaced.

---

Galvani saw a dead frog's leg kick when touched with copper and iron, and concluded the electricity came from the frog. How did Volta show he was wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> Volta **replaced the frog's leg with saltwater-soaked paper** and **still got a current**.
>
> **Why that settles it:** he changed **exactly one thing** — the frog — and the effect survived. So the electricity must come from **the metals plus the liquid**, not the animal.
>
> That experiment led directly to the invention of the **first battery**.

---

How do you build a lemon cell, and what plays each role?

> [!ANSWER]
> Push a **copper wire** and an **iron nail** into a lemon, a small distance apart. Chain five or six lemons — copper of one joined to the nail of the next — and connect an **LED** across the free ends.
>
> - **Electrodes:** copper wire and iron nail
> - **Electrolyte:** the **lemon juice** (a salt solution works too)

---

The LED in the lemon cell doesn't glow. What should you try first?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Reverse the LED's connections.**
>
> An LED conducts only when its **longer wire (positive terminal) goes to the positive terminal of the battery**. If it's the wrong way round, no current passes and it stays dark.

---

Why do you need **two different** metals in a cell?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the two metals behave differently in the electrolyte — one tends to act as the **positive** electrode (like copper) and the other as the **negative** (like zinc), owing to their chemical properties.
>
> **Two identical rods would have no such difference**, so there'd be nothing to push a current one way.

---

Why is a dry cell called "dry"?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because its **electrolyte is not a liquid but a thick moist paste**.
>
> (It isn't actually dry — just not runny.)

---

Name the parts of a dry cell and their polarity.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Zinc container** — the **negative** terminal
> - **Carbon rod** at the centre, topped with a metal cap — the **positive** terminal
> - **Paste-like electrolyte** surrounding the rod

---

**True or false:** dry cells are less portable than Voltaic cells.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False — they are far MORE portable.**
>
> **Why:** the paste **can't spill**, so a dry cell can be sealed, carried in a pocket and used in any orientation. A Voltaic cell is an open jar of liquid.

---

What is the advantage of a rechargeable battery, and what is its limit?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Advantage:** it can be **recharged and reused many times**, preventing waste and saving money.
>
> **Limit:** after many charge–use cycles it **slowly wears out** — which is why a phone battery needs charging more often after a year or two.

---

Name five devices that use rechargeable batteries.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Laptops, mobile phones, cameras, inverters and electric vehicles** (also watches and tablets).

---

What is the most common rechargeable battery today, and why are countries competing over it?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **lithium-ion (Li-ion)** battery.
>
> It depends on **lithium and cobalt**, which are **mined and processed in only a few parts of the world** — so countries are racing to secure supplies, **recycle old batteries** and develop new technologies.

---

What are solid-state batteries, and why do they matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> Batteries that **replace the liquid or paste electrolyte with a solid** material.
>
> They promise to be **safer, faster to charge and longer lasting** — which matters greatly as the world moves towards **environmentally friendly electrical power**.

---

Fill in: the solution used in a Voltaic cell is called the ______; a current-carrying coil behaves like a ______.

> [!ANSWER]
> **electrolyte** · **magnet** (specifically, an electromagnet)

---

Sum up the two effects of electric current in one table.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Effect | What happens | Where you meet it |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Magnetic** | current creates a magnetic field around the conductor | electromagnets, bells, motors, fans, loudspeakers |
> | **Heating** | resistance turns electrical energy into heat | heater, kettle, iron, hair dryer, lamp filament, steel furnace |
>
> **Both happen at once from the same current** — which is why the electromagnet's wire ends also got warm.
