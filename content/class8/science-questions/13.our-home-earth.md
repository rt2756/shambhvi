<!-- ===== The thin crust ===== -->

Where on Earth does all life exist, and how thick is that layer?

> [!ANSWER]
> On a **very thin layer at the surface — the CRUST** — from the tallest mountain to the deepest ocean trench.
>
> **If the Earth were the size of an apple, the crust would be as thin as the apple's skin.**

---

What is a false colour satellite image, and what can such images tell us?

> [!ANSWER]
> One where **scientists use different colours to show different kinds of information**, rather than what the eye would see.
>
> They let us study **plants on land and tiny organisms in the ocean**, and detect **ocean temperature, oil spills and wind direction**.

---

<!-- ===== Planets and the greenhouse effect ===== -->

Name the eight planets in order, and say which are rocky and which are gas.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.**
>
> - **Small and rocky:** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars
> - **Large and mostly gas:** Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune

---

Planets get colder the further they are from the Sun. What is the exception, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Venus** — it is the **hottest planet (about 450 °C)** even though **Mercury is closer** to the Sun.
>
> **Why:** Venus has a **thick atmosphere almost entirely carbon dioxide**, which **traps heat and does not let it escape** — the **greenhouse effect**.

---

Explain the greenhouse effect on Earth.

> [!ANSWER]
> Sunlight warms the Earth's surface, and the warmed Earth **gives off radiation**.
>
> **Gases like carbon dioxide in the atmosphere ABSORB that radiation**, trapping the heat instead of letting it escape into space.

---

Is the greenhouse effect a bad thing?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — it is essential.**
>
> **Without it the Earth would lose heat to space and become too cold** for water to stay liquid.
>
> **The danger is having too MUCH of it** — which is what burning fossil fuels causes.

---

How does a plant greenhouse differ from the atmospheric greenhouse effect?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Atmosphere:** gases **absorb the radiation** given off by the warmed Earth.
> - **Plant greenhouse:** it is simply a **closed space with glass walls** — the air warms during the day and **can't get out**.
>
> **Both keep things warm, but by different mechanisms.**

---

<!-- ===== Habitability ===== -->

What is the habitable zone, and why is Earth's position so important?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The range of distances from a star over which water remains LIQUID** — also called the **"Goldilocks zone"** (not too hot, not too cold, just right).
>
> - **Closer to the Sun** → too hot, all the water would **evaporate**
> - **Farther** → too cold, all the water would **freeze**
>
> **Liquid water is essential for life to evolve**, as far as we know.

---

Why is Earth called the Blue Planet?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **most of its surface is covered with water**, so from space it looks blue.

---

Could Mars ever have supported life?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Possibly, in the past.** Mars lies **at the edge** of the habitable zone. Spacecraft and rovers have found **no proof of life**, but scientists think Mars **may once have had liquid water — perhaps even lakes** — and conditions for simple life.
>
> **It's a good reminder that science doesn't always have final answers** and stays open to change as we learn more.

---

What is one major reason Mars cannot currently support life like Earth: too many volcanoes, too close to the Sun, lacking a thick atmosphere and liquid water, or too strong a magnetic field?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It lacks a thick atmosphere and liquid water.**
>
> Mars's atmosphere is about **100 times thinner** than Earth's, because Mars is small and its gravity is too weak to hold much gas.

---

Why does an almost circular orbit matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it **keeps the amount of sunlight and heat nearly steady throughout the year**.
>
> That **prevents extreme summers and winters** in most places — a very elliptical orbit would swing the planet between scorching and freezing.

---

If Earth were much smaller (with the same density), what would happen to its atmosphere?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It would escape into space**, because **gravity would be too weak to hold the gases**.
>
> **Evidence:** Mars's atmosphere is 100 times thinner than Earth's, and **Mercury has no atmosphere at all** — both are smaller.

---

What if Earth were much larger?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Gravity would be much stronger** — possibly strong enough that it would **pull us down with such force that our bones could be crushed**.
>
> **Earth is the right size in both directions:** big enough to hold an atmosphere, small enough that we can stand up.

---

What is the ozone layer, and what does it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> Some of the oxygen in the atmosphere converts to **ozone — a three-atom oxygen molecule** — forming a layer high up.
>
> **It acts as a shield, blocking harmful ultraviolet (UV) rays from the Sun** that would damage living cells.

---

What is thought to cause the Earth's magnetic field?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The movement of molten iron in the Earth's core.**
>
> (Moving charges produce a magnetic field — the same effect studied in Chapter 4, on a planetary scale.)

---

What are cosmic rays and solar wind, and why does the magnetic field matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> Tiny **high-energy particles** that constantly hit the Earth — **cosmic rays** from far across the universe, **solar wind** from the Sun.
>
> **They can damage the atmosphere, reduce the ozone layer, and let in more harmful UV.**
>
> **The Earth's magnetic field acts as a protective shield, pushing many of them away** — keeping the atmosphere and life safe.

---

List the four factors that together make Earth habitable.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Position** — in the habitable zone, so water is liquid
> 2. **Nearly circular orbit** — steady heat all year
> 3. **Size** — enough gravity to hold an atmosphere, not so much as to crush us
> 4. **Magnetic field** — shields us from high-energy particles
>
> **No single one is enough; all four are needed together.**

---

What was Mangalyaan, and why did it matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> India's **Mars Orbiter Mission**, launched by **ISRO in 2013**. It carried tools to study Mars's **atmosphere, surface and signs of past water** — helping ask whether Mars was ever suitable for life.
>
> **It showed the world that India could do space science with smart, low-cost technology.**

<!-- ===== The four spheres ===== -->

Name and define the four spheres of the Earth system.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Atmosphere** — the layer of **gases** around the Earth
> - **Hydrosphere** — all the **water**: ponds, lakes, rivers, springs, seas, oceans, groundwater (about **70%** of Earth's surface)
> - **Geosphere** — the **solid** parts: rocks, soils, minerals
> - **Biosphere** — **all living beings** and the places where they live

---

How do air, water and sunlight work together to sustain life?

> [!ANSWER]
> In the presence of **sunlight**, plants take **carbon dioxide** from the air and **water** from the soil to make food by **photosynthesis** — releasing **oxygen**.
>
> That **oxygen is used for respiration** by humans, animals and plants alike. The two processes feed each other.

---

Give three roles water plays in living things.

> [!ANSWER]
> - It is a **good solvent**, so substances can dissolve and move
> - In **plants** it **transports nutrients** from soil to leaves
> - In **animals** it **regulates body temperature, aids digestion and ensures hydration**

---

How does the water cycle affect life on land?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Water vapour forms clouds and brings rain or snow**, refilling rivers, lakes and underground water.
>
> **Rainfall decides which plants and animals can live in a place**, and moving air shapes weather and rainfall — which in turn decide **farming and water supply**.

---

Soil looks like plain dirt. Where do its nutrients come from?

> [!ANSWER]
> From the **slow breakdown of rocks** and from the **remains of plants and animals**.
>
> That's how soil comes to be rich in nutrients like **nitrogen and potassium** that plants need.

---

What is geodiversity, and why does it matter for life?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The variety of landforms, rocks and soils, along with the processes that shape and alter them.**
>
> **It creates the unique habitats where different types of life can thrive** — so the non-living parts of nature aren't just a background, they shape the story of life itself.

---

Which of these is an example of geodiversity: bird chirping in a forest, different landforms like mountains and deserts, changing monsoon weather, or the number of fish in a pond?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Different landforms like mountains, valleys and deserts.**
>
> The others are examples of **biodiversity** (birds, fish) or of **weather** — geodiversity is about the **solid Earth** and the processes shaping it.

---

Why can a single change like cutting down a forest matter so much?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **Earth is a vast system where land, air, water and living things support and affect one another**.
>
> Cutting a forest changes **rainfall, soil, air quality and the animals that live there** — the effect spreads far beyond the trees themselves.

---

<!-- ===== Reproduction ===== -->

Why is reproduction essential?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **without it, life would eventually disappear from Earth**.
>
> Reproduction **ensures each type of organism continues to exist**, maintaining the continuity of life.

---

What are genes, and where are they stored?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The genetic material — instructions telling the offspring how to develop from a single cell.** They are stored **inside every cell** of a living being.
>
> **Think of them as a detailed instruction manual:** some instructions tell the cell how to make blood, others guide bones, muscles or skin.

---

Reproduction does more than make copies. What else does it do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It allows small changes in the instructions passed from parents to offspring.**
>
> Sometimes those changes **help an organism survive better** — camels developed **humps to store fat** for deserts; some **bacteria became resistant to antibiotics**.
>
> **Over many generations such changes can produce new features — or entirely new kinds of living beings.**

---

Distinguish asexual from sexual reproduction.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Asexual** — a **single parent** produces new individuals that are **exact copies** of itself.
> - **Sexual** — instructions from **two parents combine**, so the offspring is **not exactly like either**; it shares traits with both and has differences too.

---

What is vegetative propagation? Give three examples you could try.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Reproduction in plants where a part — leaf, stem or root — planted in soil grows into a new plant.**
>
> Try a **money plant stem cutting** (in water, so you can watch the roots), the **"eyes" of a sprouted potato**, or a piece of **ginger**. Give them water, air and sunlight, and record when roots, stems and the first leaf appear.

---

How do bamboo and sugarcane grow into new plants, if you never see their seeds?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By vegetative propagation** — asexually, from parts of the parent plant rather than from seeds.

---

Give four examples of asexual reproduction in microbes and simple animals.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Bacteria and amoeba** divide into **two identical individuals**
> - **Algae** can **regrow from small cut parts**
> - **Hydra** grows **tiny buds** that break off and become new individuals
> - **Planaria**, a flatworm, can **regrow from a fragment of its body** — scientists study it to understand **regeneration**

---

If both parents pass on genetic material, why doesn't it double every generation?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **each parent makes special reproductive cells called GAMETES that carry only HALF the parent's genetic material.**
>
> When a male and a female gamete join, they form **one new cell with a complete set** — half from each parent. **No doubling.**

---

Why do brothers and sisters look different from each other?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **every gamete carries a different set of instructions** for things like eye colour and hair type.
>
> **The instructions mix differently each time** a sperm and an egg join — so each child is unique. One may inherit a nose like the mother's, another eyes like the father's.

---

Name the male and female gametes in a flower, and describe the steps from pollen to fruit.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Male gametes** — **pollen grains**, found inside the **anther**
> - **Female gametes** — **ovules**, deep inside the flower
>
> ```
> pollen carried by wind, insects or animals  = POLLINATION
>            ↓ male and female gametes combine = FERTILISATION
> ZYGOTE → becomes the SEED
> fleshy part around the ovule → becomes the FRUIT
> ```

---

How does a banyan seed end up sprouting in a crack in a wall?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A bird ate the fruit and excreted the seed** far from the parent plant.
>
> That's why fruits are fleshy and attractive — **animals eating them disperse the seeds**, which is a great advantage for a plant that cannot move.

---

Compare how fish, birds and mammals reproduce.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | Fertilisation | Embryo develops | Fed by |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Fish, frogs** | in **water** | in **water** | the egg's own store |
> | **Birds** | **inside** the female | **after the egg is laid** | food **packed into the egg**, which must last till hatching |
> | **Most mammals** | **inside** the female | **inside** the mother's body | the **mother's body**, continuously |

---

Why don't dogs lay eggs, or hens give birth to live chicks?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because these are **two different complete strategies for feeding the embryo**, and an animal's whole body is built around one of them.
>
> - **Egg-laying:** the mother must pack **all** the food in advance; she stays mobile, but the egg is exposed.
> - **Live birth:** the mother's body **supplies food and oxygen continuously**; the embryo is far safer, but the mother carries the weight and cost.
>
> You can't swap one for the other without rebuilding the animal.

---

Why are offspring different from their parents in sexual reproduction: different climates, different food, new instructions after birth, or mixed genes from both parents?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They get mixed instructions (genes) from both parents.**
>
> Each gamete carries a different half-set, and the combinations differ every time.

<!-- ===== Threats ===== -->

What is the triple planetary crisis?

> [!ANSWER]
> The three biggest environmental challenges together: **climate change, biodiversity loss and pollution**.

---

Explain how burning fossil fuels leads to global warming.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> burning coal and oil → releases greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane)
>                      → more heat trapped in the atmosphere
>                      → GLOBAL WARMING
> ```

---

Trees and ocean plankton absorb carbon dioxide. So why can't Earth cope with fossil fuels?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because burning them **releases extra carbon that has been locked underground for MILLIONS of years**.
>
> **The Earth cannot absorb it fast enough**, so the heat builds up. It isn't that the natural systems have stopped working — they've been overwhelmed.

---

Name four consequences of even a small rise in global temperature.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Ice caps melt**
> - **Sea levels rise**, flooding many coastal cities
> - **Extreme weather** becomes more common
> - **Many plants and animals disappear**

---

Define climate change.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Long-term changes in temperature, rainfall and weather patterns.**
>
> (Not the same as day-to-day weather — climate is the long-run pattern.)

---

Why does losing a few species weaken an entire ecosystem?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **every type of living thing has a role**.
>
> If grasses vanish, deer and grasshoppers struggle — and **without herbivores, predators like tigers and foxes lose their food too**. The loss spreads through the food web.

---

Name the effects of air, water and soil pollution.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Air** (factories, vehicles, burning fuels) — **breathing problems, damaged crops, smog and acid rain**
> - **Water** (factory, farm and plastic waste) — **harms aquatic life and makes water unsafe**
> - **Soil** (excess fertilisers, poor waste disposal) — **reduces crop yield and spreads harmful substances through the food chain**

---

Why is life on Earth described as balanced on narrow margins?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **a little more or less oxygen in the air, a slightly higher or lower Earth temperature, or a little less ozone could each endanger life**.
>
> The conditions that suit us span a **very narrow range** — which is exactly why small human-caused changes matter so much.

---

<!-- ===== Action ===== -->

Name four international environmental agreements and what each did.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Montreal Protocol (1987)** — reduced **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)**, letting the **ozone layer slowly recover**
> - **Earth Summit (1992)** — launched international efforts on climate change and biodiversity
> - **Kyoto Protocol (2005)** and **Paris Agreement (2015)** — committed countries to **cut greenhouse gas emissions**

---

What goal did the Paris Agreement set, and are we meeting it?

> [!ANSWER]
> To **limit global warming to below 1.5 °C**.
>
> **As of 2025 the world is NOT on track to meet it** — much more action is needed to avoid further adverse effects of climate change.

---

Why is the Montreal Protocol considered a success story?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it **worked**. Reducing CFCs allowed the **ozone layer to begin recovering** — proof that international agreement can reverse a global environmental problem when countries actually act.

---

Give three ways to protect the climate and three ways to preserve biodiversity.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Climate:** use **renewable energy** like solar and wind; **improve energy efficiency**; choose **environmentally friendly travel**.
>
> **Biodiversity:** protect habitats (national parks, sanctuaries); **local communities using natural resources sustainably**; keeping ecosystems **diverse**, since diverse ecosystems are **stronger and more balanced**.

---

What can an individual student actually do?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Reuse, repair and recycle** items like clothes and plastic
> - **Save energy and water** — small actions add up
> - **Learn more, share ideas and encourage others**
>
> Sustaining life on Earth needs action **at every scale — from local communities to global leaders**.

---

If a spaceship carried soil and water to Mars, could plants start growing there?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not without much more than soil and water.**
>
> Mars lacks a **thick atmosphere** (so there's little carbon dioxide pressure and no protection from UV), it is **very cold**, it has **no ozone layer**, and its **weak magnetic field** leaves the surface exposed to solar wind and cosmic rays.
>
> **Plants would need an enclosed, pressurised, shielded and heated environment** — essentially a small piece of Earth carried with them.

---

What would Earth look like if there were no life on it at all?

> [!ANSWER]
> Very different — because **life shapes the non-living parts too**.
>
> There would be **no oxygen in the atmosphere** (plants and plankton put it there), **no soil as we know it** (soil depends on decomposing plant and animal remains), and **no forests holding the land in place** — so far more erosion and bare rock.
