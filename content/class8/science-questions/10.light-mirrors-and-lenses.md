<!-- ===== Spherical mirrors ===== -->

Hold a shiny spoon close to your face, first one side then the other. Describe and explain what you see.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Inner curved surface** (curves inwards) → the image is **inverted**
> - **Outer surface** (bulges outwards) → the image is **erect but smaller**
>
> The spoon acts as two spherical mirrors: the inner side as a **concave** mirror, the outer side as a **convex** mirror.

---

Define concave and convex mirrors.

> [!ANSWER]
> Both are **spherical mirrors** — reflecting surfaces shaped like part of a hollow sphere.
>
> - **Concave** — the reflecting surface curves **inwards**
> - **Convex** — the reflecting surface curves **outwards**

---

Are spherical mirrors made by slicing a hollow glass sphere?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** A **flat piece of glass is ground and polished** into a curved surface, then given a reflective coating (usually thin **aluminium**).
>
> - Coating on the **outer** curved surface → **concave** mirror
> - Coating on the **inner** curved surface → **convex** mirror

---

How can you tell a concave mirror from a convex one without looking at an image?

> [!ANSWER]
> Lay both flat with reflecting surfaces facing up and **look from the side with your eye at their level**.
>
> You can then see directly whether the surface **curves inwards or outwards**.

---

<!-- ===== Images ===== -->

Describe the image in a concave mirror when the object is close, and when it is moved far away.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Close:** the image is **erect and enlarged**.
> - **Moved away:** the image becomes **inverted** — enlarged at first, then getting **smaller and smaller**.

---

Describe the image in a convex mirror at all distances.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always erect and diminished**, at every distance.
>
> Moving the object away makes the image **slightly smaller**, but it never inverts and never becomes larger than the object.

---

How does a plane mirror differ from both spherical mirrors?

> [!ANSWER]
> A plane mirror **always** gives an image that is **erect and the SAME SIZE** as the object — the distance makes no difference.
>
> In spherical mirrors the **size changes with distance**, and a concave mirror also **inverts** the image when the object is far.

---

Which type of inversion happens in all three kinds of mirror?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Lateral inversion** — left and right appearing swapped.
>
> Plane, concave and convex mirrors all show it.

---

You look into an unknown mirror. How can you identify it from the image alone?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Same size and erect, whatever the distance → **plane**
> - Always small and erect → **convex**
> - Changes size with distance, and flips over when you move back → **concave**

---

<!-- ===== Uses ===== -->

Why are torch and headlight reflectors concave?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because a **concave mirror concentrates light** — it gathers the bulb's light and sends it out as a strong directed beam instead of letting it spread in all directions.

---

Why does a dentist use a concave mirror?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because a concave mirror gives an **enlarged image when held CLOSE to the object**.
>
> Held near a tooth, it shows the dentist a magnified view.

---

Why are vehicle side-view mirrors convex rather than plane?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the outward curve gives a **much wider view of the road behind**, and the image is **always erect** so the traffic looks the right way up.
>
> A plane mirror of the same size would show a far narrower slice of road.

---

Explain the warning "Objects in mirror are closer than they appear."

> [!ANSWER]
> A convex mirror makes everything look **smaller**, and we instinctively read "small" as "**far away**".
>
> So a car that is actually close **looks distant** — hence the warning. **The wider field of view is worth this trade-off**, but drivers must be told about it.

---

Give two more places convex mirrors are used, and say why.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Road intersections and sharp bends** — drivers on both sides can see round the corner, preventing collisions.
> - **Big stores** — one mirror monitors a large area, deterring theft.
>
> Both use the convex mirror's **wide field of view**.

---

What kind of mirror is the main mirror of a modern telescope?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **large concave mirror**.
>
> Most modern telescopes are **reflecting telescopes**, and they need to **gather and converge** as much faint starlight as possible.

---

<!-- ===== Laws of reflection ===== -->

Define incident ray, reflected ray, normal, angle of incidence and angle of reflection.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Incident ray** — the ray of light falling on the mirror
> - **Reflected ray** — the ray coming back from the mirror
> - **Normal** — the line drawn at **90° to the mirror** at the point of incidence
> - **Angle of incidence (i)** — between the **normal** and the incident ray
> - **Angle of reflection (r)** — between the **normal** and the reflected ray

---

State the first law of reflection.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.**
>
> **`i = r`**

---

A ray makes 40° with the **normal**. What angle does the reflected ray make with the **mirror**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The angle of reflection is also **40° from the normal**.
>
> Since the normal is at 90° to the mirror, the angle with the **mirror** is `90 − 40 =` **50°**.
>
> **This is the trap:** angles are always measured **from the normal**, not from the mirror.

---

Light falls on a mirror along the normal. What are the angles of incidence and reflection?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both are zero.**
>
> The ray goes straight back along the same path it came.

---

Describe the bending-paper experiment and state the second law of reflection.

> [!ANSWER]
> Place a mirror on a stiff sheet with part of it overhanging the table edge. The reflected beam is visible on that extended part. **Bend the extended part down and the beam disappears; flatten it and the beam returns.**
>
> **Second law: the incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray all lie in the SAME PLANE.**
>
> **Why the experiment proves it:** bending the paper creates a **new plane**, so the reflected ray — which stays in the original plane — no longer falls on the paper.

---

Do the laws of reflection apply to curved mirrors?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — to all mirrors: plane, concave and convex.**
>
> Every individual ray obeys `i = r` and the same-plane rule, whatever the shape of the surface.

---

<!-- ===== Converging and diverging ===== -->

Multiple parallel beams fall on a plane, a concave and a convex mirror. Compare the reflected beams.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Plane** — the reflected beams remain **parallel**
> - **Concave** — they come closer: they **CONVERGE**
> - **Convex** — they spread out: they **DIVERGE**

---

If every ray obeys `i = r`, why do parallel rays converge on a concave mirror?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **the normal points in a different direction at each point of a curved surface**.
>
> So each ray meets its own normal at a different angle, and each is turned by a different amount — bringing them together for a concave mirror and spreading them for a convex one.
>
> **The law isn't broken; the shape of the surface does the work.**

---

Describe the concave-mirror burning experiment and explain it.

> [!ANSWER]
> Hold a concave mirror facing the Sun and adjust its distance from a sheet of paper until you get a **sharp bright spot**. Hold steady — **the paper smokes and burns**.
>
> **Why:** the mirror **concentrates the Sun's light into one tiny point**, producing enough heat there to ignite the paper.

---

What safety rules must you follow in that experiment?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Never look at the Sun**, or at the mirror reflecting it.
> - Focus the reflected light **only on paper** — never at anyone's face or eyes.
> - Do it **under adult supervision**.

---

What is a solar concentrator, and what is it used for?

> [!ANSWER]
> A device that uses **mirrors and lenses to concentrate sunlight into a small area**.
>
> The concentrated heat produces **steam to generate electricity**, or provides heat for **large-scale cooking** and **solar furnaces** — which are even used for **melting steel**.

---

<!-- ===== Lenses ===== -->

Describe the water-drop experiment and what it shows.

> [!ANSWER]
> Rub a thin coat of **oil or wax** on a flat glass strip (so the water beads into a round drop), place a **drop of water** on it, and hold printed text underneath.
>
> **The letters under the drop look enlarged** — the drop's **outwardly curved surface** is acting as a simple **lens**.

---

Why is the oil or wax needed?

> [!ANSWER]
> It stops the water **spreading out flat**, so it **forms a nicely rounded drop** instead.
>
> The curvature is what does the magnifying — a flat film of water would do nothing.

---

Define a lens, and distinguish convex from concave.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A piece of transparent material (glass or plastic) with curved surfaces.**
>
> - **Convex** — **thicker in the middle** than at the edges
> - **Concave** — **thicker at the edges** than in the middle

---

State the key difference between a mirror and a lens.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Light passes THROUGH a lens; it bounces OFF a mirror.**
>
> So **you see things THROUGH a lens, but IN a mirror.**

---

Describe the image seen through a convex lens as the object moves away.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Close:** erect and **enlarged**
> - **Moved away:** becomes **inverted** — enlarged at first, then **diminishing** in size

---

Describe the image seen through a concave lens.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always erect and diminished**, at every distance — its size changing slightly as the distance increases.

---

What pattern connects convex lenses with concave mirrors?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A convex lens behaves like a concave mirror, and a concave lens like a convex mirror.**
>
> Both members of each pair **converge** or both **diverge** — one by **reflection**, the other by **transmission**. Their image behaviour matches accordingly.

---

Parallel beams pass through a flat glass plate, a convex lens and a concave lens. Compare.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Flat glass plate** — the light passes straight through, **unchanged**
> - **Convex lens** — the beams **converge** → a **converging lens**
> - **Concave lens** — the beams **diverge** → a **diverging lens**

---

Can a convex lens burn paper?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes** — exactly like a concave mirror.
>
> It **concentrates sunlight to a point**, producing enough heat to ignite the paper. **Never look at the Sun through a lens** — it can permanently damage your eyes.

---

Name five uses of lenses.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Eyeglasses, cameras (including smartphone cameras), telescopes, microscopes, magnifying glasses** — and the **lens inside your own eye**.

---

What is special about the lens in a human eye?

> [!ANSWER]
> It is a **convex lens that can CHANGE ITS SHAPE**.
>
> That's what lets you switch between reading a book close up and looking at something far away — a glass lens has one fixed shape and cannot do this.

---

Why do some reading glasses have a curved line across them?

> [!ANSWER]
> They are **bifocals** — **two different lens strengths in one**, with the line marking the boundary.
>
> The **upper part** is for seeing at a distance, the **lower part** for reading.

Can we make mirrors that give enlarged or diminished images?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> - A **concave** mirror gives an **enlarged** image when the object is close (a dental or shaving mirror).
> - A **convex** mirror always gives a **diminished** image (a side-view or shop mirror).
>
> Only a **plane** mirror is stuck at exactly life-size.
