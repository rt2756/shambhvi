How did the idea of science change from Class 6 to Class 7 to Class 8?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Class 6 — wonder:** science begins with simple "Why?" and "How?" questions about the world.
> - **Class 7 — evolution:** each answer opens new questions, and scientific ideas change as we dig deeper.
> - **Class 8 — investigation:** asking **focused** questions and **designing experiments** to answer them.

---

What is the stated goal of Class 8 science, and why does it matter more than learning facts?

> [!ANSWER]
> **"We don't want you to just learn new facts — we want you to learn how to FIND new facts."**
>
> **Why it matters:** facts can be looked up. The ability to turn a puzzle into a testable question, design a fair test and read the evidence is what lets you answer things **nobody has answered yet**.

---

What do the root and the kite on the book's pages stand for?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Root** (bottom of left pages) — the **solid foundation** of careful observation, evidence, tradition and heritage.
> - **Kite** (top of right pages) — **curiosity taking flight** towards the unknown.
>
> **Why both:** observation without imagination never asks a new question; imagination without observation never checks whether the answer is true.

---

Do you need a laboratory to do science? Justify with an example from the chapter.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The chapter's whole example is a **kitchen** — investigating why a puri puffs up and why one side is thinner.
>
> **Why:** what makes something an experiment is the **method** — a controlled comparison — not the equipment.

---

Turn "cooking is interesting" into a scientific question.

> [!ANSWER]
> Something an experiment could settle, such as **"What changes the way a puri puffs up when fried?"**
>
> **Why:** a scientific question must point at something you can **change** and something you can **observe**. "Interesting" points at neither.

---

In the puri experiment, list five things you can **control**.

> [!ANSWER]
> - the **thickness** of the rolled dough
> - the **size** of the circle
> - the **type of flour** (atta, maida, …)
> - the **temperature** of the oil
> - **how you drop it in** — vertically, at an angle, slowly

---

In the same experiment, list things you can **observe or measure**.

> [!ANSWER]
> - Did it puff up? (**yes/no**)
> - **How long** did it take to puff? (seconds)
> - Does very thick dough still give a thin side?
> - Did the oil splatter, smell or smoke?

---

Why is a measurement usually more useful than a yes/no observation?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because a number tells you **how much**, not just **whether**.
>
> "It puffed" can't distinguish a slow, feeble puff from an instant one — but "3 seconds" versus "9 seconds" can, and that difference may be exactly what your question is about.

---

State the single most important rule of experimenting, and explain why it matters.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Change only ONE thing at a time.**
>
> **Why:** if you change the oil temperature *and* the dough thickness together and the result changes, you have **no way to tell which one caused it**. Only by holding everything else fixed can a difference be blamed on the thing you varied.

---

You want to test whether oil temperature affects puffing. What must you keep the same?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Everything else:** the same flour, the same dough thickness, the same size of circle, and the same way of dropping it in.
>
> Then any difference in puffing can only have come from the temperature.

---

Why should you write down things you weren't testing, like whether the oil smoked?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two reasons:
>
> 1. **Notes let you answer questions you hadn't thought of yet** — a detail you ignored may turn out to be the clue.
> 2. **They let someone else repeat exactly what you did**, which is how a result gets checked.

---

What happens after one round of experiments is finished?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You get better questions.** *Do puris puff better from fresh dough or stored dough? What if you prick a hole first?*
>
> **The cycle is:** question → experiment → observation → **better question**. Science doesn't end at an answer.

---

Is the puffing of a puri a fully understood phenomenon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — scientists still don't completely understand it.**
>
> **The lesson:** a question being **everyday** doesn't make it easy, and it doesn't mean somebody has already solved it.

---

Give the six steps of a systematic investigation.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Start from something you actually **noticed**.
> 2. **Sharpen the question** until an experiment could settle it.
> 3. Separate what you **control** from what you **measure**.
> 4. Vary **one thing at a time**.
> 5. **Record everything**, including surprises.
> 6. Expect the answer to produce a **better question**.

Name three questions the chapter offers as sparks for curiosity.

> [!ANSWER]
> - Why is one side of a puri thinner than the other?
> - Are there more grains of sand on all the world's beaches and deserts, or more stars in our galaxy?
> - Why has nature created such a vast variety of plants and animals?

How do the year's chapters connect to each other?

> [!ANSWER]
> **As a chain of questions, not a list of topics:**
>
> Electricity does work → so what are the **forces** behind it → force spread over an area is **pressure** → pressure differences move air, giving **winds and cyclones** → to understand how air pushes at all you must know what matter is **made of** → particles combine into **elements, compounds and mixtures** → and dissolve into **solutions**.

The final chapter asks what makes Earth "just right" for life. Why is it placed last?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it **puts everything together** — atmosphere (gases and pressure), liquid water (states of matter), sunlight (light and energy) and ecosystems (living things depending on one another).
>
> It also raises the urgent point that **human activity is now changing that balance**, and that the same tools — observing, measuring, experimenting — are what will let us understand and act on it.

---

A friend says "I did an experiment: I changed the flour AND the oil temperature, and the puri puffed better." What's wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two variables changed at once**, so the result is uninterpretable — the improvement could be from the flour, the temperature, or a combination.
>
> **Fix:** run two experiments. Change only the flour first, holding temperature fixed; then only the temperature, holding flour fixed.

---

Why is "keeping conditions the same" called a *fair test*?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it gives every trial the **same chance** — the only thing that differs is the thing you're investigating.
>
> Any other difference would be an unfair advantage that muddles the comparison, exactly like changing two runners' shoes *and* their track and then asking which shoe was faster.
