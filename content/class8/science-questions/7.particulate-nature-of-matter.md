<!-- ===== Constituent particles ===== -->

Chalk is broken and ground into a fine powder. Is this a physical or a chemical change?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A physical change.**
>
> **Why:** every speck of powder is **still chalk** — no new substance has formed. Only the **size** of each piece has changed.

---

What is a constituent particle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The basic unit that makes up a larger piece of a substance or material** — the point at which no further breaking down is possible.
>
> One stick of chalk is made of an enormous number of them.

---

Sugar is added to water without stirring, and the top layer isn't sweet. After stirring, it is — but no sugar can be seen. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> The sugar has **broken up into its constituent particles**, which are far too small to see, and **spread throughout the water**.
>
> Their presence can still be **sensed by taste**, which proves they're there even though they're invisible.

---

Where exactly did the dissolved sugar go?

> [!ANSWER]
> Into the **interparticle spaces** — **the gaps between the water particles**.
>
> The sugar particles separated and occupied the space that was already available between water particles.

---

How small are the constituent particles of matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **So small they cannot be seen even through an ordinary microscope.**

---

Who first proposed the idea of the atom in India, and what did he call it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Acharya Kanad**, in his work the **Vaisheshika Sutras**.
>
> He called it the **Parmanu** and held that matter is made of tiny, **indivisible, eternal** particles.

---

<!-- ===== Interparticle attraction ===== -->

What holds the constituent particles of a substance together?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Interparticle attractions** — attractive forces between the constituent particles.
>
> Their strength depends on the **nature of the substance** and the **distance between the particles**.

---

Why does interparticle distance matter so much?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **even a slight increase in distance decreases the force drastically**.
>
> That is why heating a substance — which pushes its particles slightly apart — can change its state completely.

---

What ultimately decides whether a substance is a solid, liquid or gas?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The strength of the interparticle forces** — and behind that, **the thermal energy of the particles**.
>
> More thermal energy means the particles are farther apart and less strongly held.

---

<!-- ===== Solids ===== -->

Why do solids have a definite shape and volume?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because their particles are **tightly packed** with **very strong** interparticle attractions holding them in **fixed positions**.
>
> The particles can only **vibrate about those positions** — they cannot move past one another.

---

Describe what happens, particle by particle, when a solid melts.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Heating makes the particles **vibrate more vigorously**.
> 2. The vibrations become so vigorous that particles **start leaving their fixed positions**.
> 3. The **interparticle forces weaken**.
> 4. The solid becomes a **liquid**.

---

Define melting point.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The minimum temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid at atmospheric pressure.**

---

Ice melts at 0 °C, urea at 133 °C, iron at 1538 °C. What does that tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That iron's interparticle attractions are far stronger than urea's, which are stronger than ice's.**
>
> **Melting point is a direct measure of how strongly the particles are held** — you need much more thermal energy to break strong attractions.

---

Usually a liquid's particles are farther apart than the solid's. What is the exception?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Ice.** Its particles are **farther apart than water's**, not closer.
>
> That's why ice is less dense than water — and why it **floats**.

---

<!-- ===== Liquids ===== -->

The same 200 mL of water is poured through three differently shaped containers. What stays the same and what changes?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **The shape changes** every time → **liquids have no fixed shape**
> - **The level stays at 200 mL** → **liquids have a definite volume**

---

Why do liquids have a fixed volume but no fixed shape?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because their particles are **free to move — but only within a limited space**.
>
> The freedom to move lets them take the container's shape; the attractions still being strong enough to keep them close means the total volume doesn't change.

---

Why can you move your finger through water but not through a stone?

> [!ANSWER]
> In a liquid the interparticle attractions are **slightly weaker** than in a solid, so the particles can be **temporarily displaced** and then flow back.
>
> In a solid the particles are locked in fixed positions — you'd have to break the solid permanently.

---

In the 200 mL experiment, the water level in the third container is slightly less than 200 mL. Why, and does it disprove anything?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **some water sticks to the walls** of the containers as it's poured.
>
> **It disproves nothing** — the loss is a practical one, not a change in the water's volume. That's why the containers must be clean and dry to begin with.

---

Define boiling point, and describe what happens to the particles.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The temperature at which a liquid boils and turns to vapour at atmospheric pressure.**
>
> The particles' movement becomes so vigorous that they **move apart**, the **interparticle forces decrease**, and eventually particles **escape from the liquid** into the gaseous state.

---

Give three differences between boiling and evaporation.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **Boiling** | **Evaporation** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Temperature | only at the **boiling point** | at **any** temperature |
> | Where it happens | **throughout** the liquid — bubbles form inside | only at the **surface** |
> | Speed | **fast** | **slow** |

---

Spilled water disappears even in a cool room. Which process is that?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Evaporation** — which happens at **any** temperature, slowly, and only from the surface.
>
> No heating is needed.

---

<!-- ===== Gases ===== -->

Smoke trapped in one gas jar spreads into an empty jar placed on top. What does this show?

> [!ANSWER]
> That **gases do not have a fixed volume** — they **expand to occupy all the available space**.
>
> Like liquids, they also take the shape of their container.

---

Why do gas particles behave that way?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because they **move freely in all directions** and the **interparticle attractions are negligible**.
>
> With almost nothing holding them together, there is nothing to stop them spreading out.

---

Why is smoke used in the gas-jar experiment?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **gas particles are invisible**.
>
> The tiny smoke specks are **constantly hit by invisible gas particles**, and watching the smoke move lets us **see the motion of the gas indirectly**. (Iodine vapour works the same way.)

---

What is a fluid, and why are both liquids and gases fluids?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A substance that flows and does not retain a fixed shape.**
>
> Both liquids and gases do this, which is what distinguishes them from solids.

---

<!-- ===== Interparticle spacing ===== -->

Air in a sealed syringe can be compressed; water cannot. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Gas particles have a great deal of space between them**, which external pressure can reduce by pushing them closer.
>
> **Liquid particles are already close together**, so there is almost no space to squeeze out — water is practically **incompressible**.

---

Release the syringe plunger after compressing the air. What happens, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The plunger moves back to its original position.**
>
> The gas particles **spread out again** as soon as the external pressure is removed — because their natural state is to occupy all available space.

---

Adding sugar to water raises the level, but after it dissolves the level falls again. What does this prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> That **the volume of the solution is LESS than water plus sugar taken separately**.
>
> The only way that can happen is if there are **spaces between the water particles**, into which the sugar particles fit.

---

Repeat that experiment with sand instead of sugar. What happens, and why the difference?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sand does not dissolve**, so it simply **settles at the bottom and takes up space** — the total volume **increases** and stays increased.
>
> **Why sand doesn't dissolve:** its constituent particles are held together **so strongly that the water particles cannot pull them out**.

---

Is there space between the particles of a solid? What's in it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — even closely packed solids have some space between particles.**
>
> **And that space contains nothing at all** — not air, as people often assume.

---

Compare solids, liquids and gases on spacing, attraction, movement, shape and volume.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **Solid** | **Liquid** | **Gas** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Spacing | **minimum** | a little more | **maximum** |
> | Attraction | **maximum** | slightly weaker | **negligible** |
> | Movement | only vibrations | limited space | all available space |
> | Shape | definite | container's | container's |
> | Volume | definite | definite | not definite |
> | Compressible | no | practically not | **yes** |

---

In air-pollution reports, "Suspended Particulate Matter" is mentioned. Is that the same as a constituent particle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** SPM means the tiny **dust particles suspended in air**.
>
> Those dust specks are **themselves made of an enormous number of constituent particles** — they are gigantic by comparison with atoms and molecules.
>
> **The word "particle" changes meaning with the context.**

---

<!-- ===== Motion of particles ===== -->

A grain of potassium permanganate is dropped into still water, which slowly turns uniformly pink without stirring. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **water particles are in constant motion**.
>
> They first **pull the potassium permanganate particles out of the grain**, then **hit them repeatedly**, spreading them right through the water — no stirring required.

---

The same grain is dropped into hot, room-temperature and ice-cold water. Predict and explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fastest spreading in hot water, slowest in ice-cold.**
>
> **Why:** water particles **move faster when heated**, so they knock the coloured particles about more vigorously and distribute them sooner.

---

State the general rule that experiment demonstrates.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The movement of particles increases when heat is provided.**
>
> That single fact explains melting, boiling, evaporation and diffusion all at once.

---

An incense stick is lit in one corner of a room and soon smells everywhere. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **air particles are moving constantly**. They **hit the fragrance particles and spread them** throughout the room.
>
> It's the same reason perfume reaches you from across a room.

---

Give two more everyday examples of gas particles moving.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Cooking smells** travelling from the kitchen to the rest of the house
> - **Camphor's fragrance** reaching every corner of a room when heated in one place
> - The smell of a **freshly opened bottle** filling the space around it

---

How does soap remove an oil stain?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Many soap particles surround the oil particles** on the fabric.
>
> **One end of each soap particle attaches to the oil; the other end mixes with the water** — so the oil is lifted off the fabric and washed away.

---

<!-- ===== Thermal energy ===== -->

What single idea explains all three states of matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The thermal (heat) energy of the particles.**
>
> More thermal energy → particles move faster and get farther apart → attractions weaken → the substance changes state.

---

Describe the three states in terms of thermal energy.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Solid** — **low** thermal energy; particles stay close, attractions strong, motion limited to small vibrations.
> - **At the melting point** — thermal energy is **used up overcoming the attractions**; particles leave their fixed positions, the distance increases slightly, and forces weaken enough for them to move within a limited space → **liquid**.
> - **Gas** — **enough energy to completely overcome the attractions**; particles move freely in all directions.

---

Why can you pile up sand but not water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sand is a solid** — each grain's particles are held in fixed positions by strong attractions, so a heap keeps its shape.
>
> **Water's particles are free to move**, so it flows and levels itself out.

---

Why does water take the shape of cupped hands but lose it when released?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because a **liquid has no fixed shape** — its particles move freely and adopt whatever container is offered.
>
> Released, there is no container, so it simply flows.

---

We can't see air. How can it add weight to an inflated balloon?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **air is matter** — it is made of particles, and **particles have mass**, whether or not we can see them.
>
> Invisibility is about the size of the particles, not about whether they exist.

---

Is the air we breathe today the same air that existed thousands of years ago?

> [!ANSWER]
> **In terms of its particles, largely yes.**
>
> Air particles are in **constant motion** and get mixed and recycled endlessly through breathing, photosynthesis and weather — but the matter itself is not created or destroyed, only rearranged and moved about.
