<!-- ===== Solutions ===== -->

Why does every sip of homemade ORS taste the same?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the sugar and salt form a **uniform mixture** with the water — their particles are **evenly distributed throughout**.
>
> There is no region that is saltier or sweeter than any other, so every sip is identical.

---

Define solution, solute and solvent.

> [!ANSWER]
> - A **SOLUTION** is a **uniform mixture**.
> - The **SOLUTE** is the substance that dissolves (usually the solid).
> - The **SOLVENT** is the substance it dissolves in (usually the liquid).
>
> `Solute + Solvent → Solution`

---

Two liquids are mixed. How do you decide which is the solute?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The one present in the SMALLER amount is the solute**; the one in the larger amount is the solvent.
>
> With two liquids it isn't obvious which is dissolving which, so quantity decides.

---

Gulab jamun syrup has far more sugar than water. Which is the solvent?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Water is still the solvent, and sugar the solute.**
>
> **Why the "smaller amount" rule doesn't apply here:** that rule is for **two liquids**. When a **solid** dissolves in a **liquid**, the solid is always the solute and the liquid the solvent.

---

Is air a solution?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes** — a **uniform mixture of gases**, evenly distributed, with no visibly separate components.

---

Classify: sand in water, sugar in water, sawdust in water, seawater.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Solutions (uniform):** sugar in water, seawater
> - **Non-uniform mixtures:** sand in water, sawdust in water

<!-- ===== Saturation and concentration ===== -->

Salt is added spoon by spoon to a glass of water. Describe what happens and name the stages.

> [!ANSWER]
> At first each spoonful **dissolves completely** — the solution is **UNSATURATED** (more solute can still be dissolved).
>
> Eventually the salt **stops dissolving and settles at the bottom** — the solution is now **SATURATED** at that temperature.

---

Define concentration, dilute and concentrated.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Concentration** is the amount of solute in a fixed quantity of solution (or solvent).
>
> - **Dilute** — **less** solute in that fixed quantity
> - **Concentrated** — **more** solute
>
> **Both are RELATIVE terms** — one spoon of salt is dilute *compared to* two spoons; there's no absolute cut-off.

---

Which is more concentrated: 2 spoons of salt in 100 mL, or 4 spoons in 50 mL?

> [!ANSWER]
> **4 spoons in 50 mL.**
>
> That's **4 times as much salt per unit of water** — 2 spoons per 100 mL versus 8 spoons per 100 mL equivalent.
>
> **Always compare per fixed quantity**, never the raw spoon counts.

---

Define solubility.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a fixed quantity of solvent** (at a given temperature).

---

Why must you always state a temperature when calling a solution "saturated"?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **solubility changes with temperature**.
>
> A solution saturated at 20 °C becomes **unsaturated** if you heat it to 50 °C — it can now take more solute.

---

<!-- ===== Temperature ===== -->

Describe the baking soda experiment and its conclusion.

> [!ANSWER]
> Dissolve baking soda in 50 mL of water at **20 °C** until some stays undissolved. Heat to **50 °C** — **the leftover dissolves**. Add more until it saturates again, then heat to **70 °C** — **that dissolves too**.
>
> **Conclusion:** water at 70 °C dissolves more baking soda than at 50 °C, which dissolves more than at 20 °C.

---

State the rule for how temperature affects the solubility of solids.

> [!ANSWER]
> **For most substances, solubility INCREASES with an increase in temperature.**
>
> So a saturated solution at one temperature **behaves as an unsaturated solution when heated**.

---

You added too much sugar to your tea and it won't dissolve. What can you do?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Heat the tea** (or use hotter tea) — solubility increases with temperature, so the extra sugar will go in.
>
> Alternatively, **add more tea** so there is more solvent for the same amount of sugar.

---

State the rule for gases, and say how it differs from solids.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The solubility of GASES generally DECREASES as temperature increases** — the **exact opposite** of most solids.
>
> **Cold water holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water.**

---

Why does oxygen dissolved in water matter, and why is warming water dangerous for it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The dissolved oxygen sustains all aquatic life** — plants, fish and other organisms — even though it is present only in small amounts.
>
> **As water warms, it can hold LESS dissolved oxygen**, so fish may suffocate **even though nothing has been added to the water**.

---

Is a mixture of gases in water uniform or non-uniform?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Uniform** — the gases dissolve **evenly** throughout the water.

---

What solvents do traditional Indian systems of medicine use?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Water is the main one** in Ayurveda, Siddha and other systems.
>
> Formulations also use **hydro-alcoholic extracts** of herbs, and the texts refer to **oils, ghee and milk** as solvents — chosen to bring out a drug's therapeutic benefit.

---

Who was Asima Chatterjee, and how did she use solvents?

> [!ANSWER]
> An Indian chemist who developed **anti-epileptic and anti-malarial drugs**, using solvents and solutions extensively to **extract and isolate compounds from medicinal plants**.
>
> She was the **second Indian woman to earn a Doctorate of Science** (after Janaki Ammal), the **first woman to receive the Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar Award** in chemical science, and was honoured with the **Padma Bhushan**.

<!-- ===== Density ===== -->

A wooden stick and an iron rod are the same size, but the rod feels much heavier. What property explains this?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Density** — the amount of mass packed into a given volume.
>
> "Heavier" is misleading when the sizes are the same; what differs is how much matter is packed into each cubic centimetre.

---

Define density and give the formula and SI unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The mass present in a unit volume of a substance.**
>
> **`Density = Mass ÷ Volume`**
>
> **SI unit: kilogram per cubic metre (kg/m³).** For liquids, **g/mL** or **g/cm³** are more convenient.

---

Does density depend on the size or shape of an object? What does it depend on?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — density is independent of shape and size.** Cut a block in half and both halves have the same density.
>
> **It depends on temperature and pressure.**

---

An aluminium block has mass 27 g and volume 10 cm³. Find its density and relative density.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Density** `= 27 ÷ 10 =` **2.7 g/cm³**
>
> **Relative density** `= 2.7 ÷ 1 =` **2.7** — aluminium is 2.7 times denser than water.
>
> **Relative density is a pure number with NO units**, because both densities are in the same units and they cancel.

---

A 1-litre pack of oil weighs only 910 g. What is the oil's density, and what does that tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> `910 g ÷ 1000 mL =` **0.91 g/mL**.
>
> **That is less than water's 1 g/mL** — which is exactly why **oil floats on water**.

---

A steel ship floats although steel is far denser than water. Does that break the density rule?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Density alone doesn't decide floating.
>
> The ship's **shape encloses a great deal of air**, so the **average density of the whole vessel** (steel + enclosed air) is **less than water's**.

---

Why does husk float while rice sinks when you wash rice?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **husk is less dense than water** and **rice is denser**.
>
> Husk is light and full of air spaces; the rice grain is solid and compact.

<!-- ===== Measuring mass ===== -->

Describe how to measure the mass of a stone on a digital balance.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Switch on and check it reads **zero** — press **tare/reset** if not.
> 2. Place a **dry, clean watch glass** on the pan.
> 3. **Tare again** so the display returns to zero with the watch glass on it.
> 4. Place the stone on the watch glass and read the mass.

---

Why do you tare the balance with the watch glass already on it?

> [!ANSWER]
> So the balance **subtracts the watch glass's mass automatically**, and the reading you get is **the object alone**.
>
> Otherwise you'd have to weigh the glass separately and subtract by hand.

---

Do most balances measure mass or weight?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Most measure WEIGHT**, but their scales are **marked in mass units** (grams, kilograms).
>
> **Only a two-pan balance truly compares masses**, because it balances the object against known masses — and gravity acts equally on both sides, so it cancels out.

---

<!-- ===== Measuring volume ===== -->

Give the SI unit of volume and the relationships between L, mL, dm³ and cm³.

> [!ANSWER]
> **SI unit: cubic metre (m³).**
>
> - `1 L = 1 dm³`
> - **`1 mL = 1 cm³`** (also written 1 cc)

---

A measuring cylinder marked 100 mL has 10 divisions between the 10 mL and 20 mL marks. Find its range and least count.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Range:** 0 to **100 mL**
> - Difference between big marks: 10 mL; divisions between them: 10
> - **Least count = `10 ÷ 10 =` 1 mL**

---

You need 70 mL of water. Would you use a 50 mL, 100 mL or 500 mL cylinder? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A 100 mL cylinder.**
>
> - A **50 mL** cylinder needs **two steps** (50 + 20) — inconvenient and error-prone.
> - A **500 mL** cylinder does it in one step but its least count is **5 mL**, so accuracy is poor.
> - The **100 mL** does it in one step with a **1 mL** least count.
>
> **The rule: pick the smallest cylinder that still holds the whole amount.**

---

What is a meniscus, and how do you read it correctly?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The curved surface a liquid forms in a narrow tube.**
>
> - Read the **BOTTOM** of the meniscus for **water and other colourless liquids**
> - Read the **TOP** for **coloured liquids**
> - Keep your **eye LEVEL with that point** — looking from above or below gives a wrong reading

---

Why are measuring cylinders tall and narrow rather than short and wide?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because in a **narrow** tube, a small change in volume produces a **large change in height** — so the graduations are far apart and can be read precisely.
>
> In a wide beaker the same volume change barely moves the level, making accurate reading impossible.

---

Why are water bottles usually tall cylinders rather than spheres?

> [!ANSWER]
> Partly the same reason — a **tall narrow shape** makes the level easy to see and judge — and partly practical: cylinders **stand upright, pack together, and are easy to hold**, none of which a sphere manages.

---

Find the volume of a notebook 25 cm × 18 cm × 2 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Volume = l × w × h = 25 × 18 × 2 =` **900 cm³**

---

Describe how to find the volume of an irregular stone.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The displacement method:**
>
> 1. Fill a measuring cylinder to a known level — say **50 mL** (initial, A).
> 2. Tie the stone with a thread and **lower it in slowly**.
> 3. Read the new level — say **55 mL** (final, B).
> 4. **Volume of the stone = B − A =** `55 − 50 =` **5 mL = 5 cm³**

---

Why does the displacement method work?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the object **pushes aside exactly its own volume of water**.
>
> The rise in the water level *is* the volume of water displaced — and that equals the volume of the object.

---

Why lower the object slowly on a thread rather than dropping it in?

> [!ANSWER]
> To avoid **splashing water out** (which would make the final reading too low) and to avoid **cracking the cylinder**.

---

A stone has mass 16.400 g and volume 5 cm³. Find its density, and say whether it floats.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Density = 16.400 ÷ 5 =` **3.28 g/cm³**
>
> That is **more than water's 1 g/cm³**, so the stone **sinks**.

<!-- ===== Density in the world ===== -->

Name the Earth's layers in order and say how density varies.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Crust → upper mantle → lower mantle → outer core (liquid) → inner core (solid).**
>
> **The crust is the lightest, and density increases as you move towards the centre.**
>
> **Why:** deeper down, **both pressure and temperature rise sharply**, making the materials **heavier and more compact**.

---

What happens to density when a substance is heated, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Density generally DECREASES on heating** (and increases on cooling).
>
> **Why:** heating makes the particles move faster and **spread farther apart**, so the **same mass now occupies a larger volume** — and `mass ÷ larger volume` is a smaller density.

---

Does pressure affect the density of solids, liquids and gases equally?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — pressure mainly affects GASES.** Its effect on solids and liquids is **negligible**.
>
> **Why:** gas particles have **large spaces between them** that pressure can squeeze out. Solid and liquid particles are already close together, with almost nothing to compress.

---

Why did people use bamboo for rafts before large ships existed?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **bamboo is light and hollow**, so it is **much less dense than water** and floats easily.
>
> Poles were tied together into rafts and small boats for fishing, trading and crossing rivers. Strong wooden logs were also hollowed out into boats.

---

Why do sugar and salt dissolve in water but not in oil?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **dissolving depends on the match between the solute and the solvent**, not just on being a liquid.
>
> Water's particles can surround and pull apart sugar and salt particles; oil's cannot. That's also why **water is called a good solvent** — it dissolves an unusually wide range of substances, which is why it's the solvent of life, of cooking and of medicine.
