<!-- ===== What health is ===== -->

Give the WHO definition of health and name its three aspects.

> [!ANSWER]
> *"A state of complete **physical, mental and social** well-being, and not merely the absence of disease."*
>
> - **Physical** — the body works well
> - **Mental** — a positive mindset, coping in difficult situations
> - **Social** — strong relationships, adjusting with peers and society

---

A student moves to a new city, has no friends, spends hours on his phone, and develops headaches, weight loss and sleeplessness. What caused his health problems?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not an infection.** His health failed on the **social and mental** sides — loneliness, no friends, excessive screen time — **and the body followed** with real physical symptoms.
>
> This is exactly why the WHO definition includes all three aspects: neglect one and the others suffer.

---

What does Ayurveda say about health, and what are dinacharya and ritucharya?

> [!ANSWER]
> That true health is a **balance of body, mind and surroundings** — the same three-part idea.
>
> - **dinacharya** — a **daily** routine
> - **ritucharya** — a **seasonal** routine
>
> It also prescribes fresh food suited to one's **prakriti** (body constitution), exercise, cleanliness, restful sleep and a calm mind, supported by yoga and meditation.

---

Name the two big factors that decide a person's health.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Lifestyle** (how you live) and **environment** (your surroundings).
>
> **Why both matter:** the best diet won't protect you in polluted air, and clean air won't help someone who never sleeps.

---

List four habits that harm health and say why each does.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Too much screen time** — cuts physical activity, sleep and real social contact
> - **Daily fast food and junk food** — too much fat and sugar, too few nutrients
> - **Sleeping very late / too little sleep** — the body and mind never fully recover
> - **Skipping meals, especially breakfast** — no fuel for the day's work

---

What is the Air Quality Index, and why does clean air matter for health?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **AQI** tells us **how clean the air is**.
>
> Air pollution from vehicles and factories causes **coughing and asthma** — the lungs take in whatever is in the air, so polluted air directly damages them.

---

Two neighbourhoods are equally well-off, but one has an unclean, fly-and-mosquito-ridden playground. Which residents fall sick more often, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Those near the unclean playground.**
>
> Flies and mosquitoes are **vectors** — they carry pathogens. Dirty stagnant surroundings also breed them, so people there are exposed far more often.

---

<!-- ===== Signs and symptoms ===== -->

What is the difference between a sign and a symptom?

> [!ANSWER]
> - A **symptom** is what you **FEEL** — pain, tiredness, dizziness.
> - A **sign** is what can be **SEEN or MEASURED** — fever, rash, high blood pressure, swelling.
>
> **Why the distinction matters:** a sign can be checked by someone else; a symptom has to be reported by you. Doctors use both together.

---

Classify each as sign or symptom: fever, headache, rash, dizziness, swelling.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Signs** (measurable): fever, rash, swelling
> - **Symptoms** (felt): headache, dizziness

---

<!-- ===== Types of disease ===== -->

Define a disease, and name the two major types.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A condition that affects the normal working of the body or mind**, usually because one or more organs or organ systems stop functioning properly.
>
> The two types are **communicable** (spread from person to person, caused by pathogens) and **non-communicable** (not caused by pathogens and not spread).

---

What is a pathogen? Name five kinds.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A disease-causing organism.**
>
> **Bacteria, viruses, fungi, worms and protozoa.**

---

Sort these: cancer, typhoid, diabetes, dengue, asthma, chickenpox, COVID-19, obesity.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Communicable:** typhoid, dengue, chickenpox, COVID-19
> - **Non-communicable:** cancer, diabetes, asthma, obesity

---

Why have non-communicable diseases become more common in India?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because of **changes in how people live** — more **processed food**, **less exercise**, and **longer lives**.
>
> **Today most deaths in India are caused by NCDs**, and India has one of the highest numbers of people with diabetes in the world.

---

What is a chronic disease? Give three examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> One that **persists for a long time — more than about 3 months** — and needs regular treatment or care.
>
> **Cancer, diabetes and asthma.**

---

What are deficiency diseases, and are they communicable?

> [!ANSWER]
> Diseases caused by a **lack of specific nutrients** in the diet — **scurvy, anaemia, goitre**.
>
> They are **non-communicable**: no pathogen is involved and they cannot spread from person to person.

---

What causes diabetes to develop?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **combination** of factors: hormonal imbalances, unhealthy eating habits, lack of physical activity, and being overweight or obese.
>
> **Not one single cause** — which is why lifestyle change (rather than a single pill) is central to managing it.

---

<!-- ===== Spread ===== -->

Name the main routes by which communicable diseases spread, with an example each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Air** (coughing, sneezing) — common cold, TB
> - **Direct contact** — shaking hands
> - **Indirect contact** — sharing towels or handkerchiefs
> - **Contaminated food and water** — cholera, typhoid
> - **Vectors** (insects) — malaria, dengue
> - **Animal bite** — rabies

---

What is a vector? Does a mosquito cause malaria?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **vector** is an insect or animal that **carries a pathogen** from one person to another.
>
> **The mosquito does not cause malaria** — it **delivers** the protozoan that does. That distinction matters: killing the mosquito is a way of cutting the delivery route.

---

Which pathogen causes each: malaria, dengue, tuberculosis, cholera, ascariasis, hepatitis A?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Malaria** — **protozoa**
> - **Dengue** — **virus**
> - **Tuberculosis** — **bacteria**
> - **Cholera** — **bacteria**
> - **Ascariasis** — **worms**
> - **Hepatitis A** — **virus**

---

Give the symptoms and the site of infection for tuberculosis.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Site:** the **lungs**.
>
> **Symptoms:** cough, fever, fatigue, loss of appetite and **night sweats**.
>
> **Prevention:** avoiding close contact with infected people, covering the mouth and nose, good hygiene, and **vaccination**.

---

Why is dengue nicknamed "break bone fever"?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because of its severe **muscle and joint pain** — patients feel as though their bones are breaking.
>
> Other symptoms are fever, headache and nausea.

---

Malaria and dengue prevention both mention removing still water. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **mosquitoes breed in still water**.
>
> Removing it attacks the **vector's life cycle** — far more effective than trying to avoid bites one by one.

---

Look at the prevention column for air-borne, water-borne and vector-borne diseases. What pattern do you see?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The prevention always attacks the ROUTE, not the germ.**
>
> - Air-borne → **cover the mouth and nose**
> - Water- and food-borne → **boil water, cook food properly, keep sanitation**
> - Vector-borne → **nets, repellents, long sleeves, remove breeding water**

---

What is a parasite, and how do intestinal worms fit that description?

> [!ANSWER]
> **An organism that lives in or on another living being** and feeds off it.
>
> Intestinal worms like roundworm live in the **digestive system**, **feeding on the nutrients** meant for their host — which is why the symptoms include poor growth, weight loss and anaemia.

---

Why does washing hands with soap and water reduce infection so much?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it **physically removes pathogens** from the hands before they reach the mouth, nose or eyes, or get passed to someone else.
>
> Hands touch surfaces, faces and food constantly — they're the busiest transmission route there is.

---

Why does staying home when unwell help twice over?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Rest lets your body recover.**
> 2. **You don't pass the disease on** — cutting the chain of transmission.

---

What did the Bhadrak sanitation campaign show?

> [!ANSWER]
> A community-led campaign in Bhadrak district, Odisha got far more people to **build and use toilets**. **Open defecation dropped sharply, and child health improved with fewer cases of diarrhoea and infection.**
>
> **The lesson:** simple sanitation at **community scale** prevents disease more effectively than treating cases one by one.

---

<!-- ===== Immunity and vaccines ===== -->

What is immunity, and why do some people fall sick more often than others in the same environment?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Immunity is the body's natural ability to fight disease**, carried out by the **immune system**.
>
> People in the same surroundings differ because **their immune systems differ** — in strength, in nutrition, and in what they have been exposed to before.

---

What is acquired immunity?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Protection developed AFTER exposure** — either to the pathogen itself, or to a **vaccine**.
>
> It is "acquired" because you weren't born with it; your body built it in response to an encounter.

---

How does a vaccine work?

> [!ANSWER]
> It **trains the immune system to recognise and attack a particular germ** — without you having to catch the disease.
>
> When the real germ arrives later, the immune system already knows how to fight it.

---

Name three ways vaccines can be made.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. From **weakened or dead** pathogens
> 2. From **inactive or harmless parts** of the pathogen (a tetanus shot uses an **inactivated bacterial toxin**)
> 3. Newer ones **instruct your own cells** to make a harmless piece of the germ for the immune system to practise on

---

Are vaccines preventive or curative? Why does the difference matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Preventive, not curative.**
>
> They stop a disease **before** it happens; they do **not** treat someone already ill. That's why vaccination campaigns are run on healthy populations, not in hospitals — waiting until you're sick is too late.

---

Getting vaccinated protects more than yourself. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Fewer infected people means fewer chances for the germ to spread.**
>
> So each vaccinated person also shields the people around them — including babies and the ill, who may not be able to be vaccinated themselves.

---

Trace Jenner's discovery through the five steps of scientific investigation.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Observation** — milkmaids who had caught the mild disease **cowpox** never got **smallpox**.
> 2. **Hypothesis** — something in cowpox blisters protects against smallpox.
> 3. **Experiment** — he injected cowpox material into a boy, who then showed no illness when exposed to smallpox.
> 4. **Result** — people infected with cowpox were **resistant to smallpox**.
> 5. **Application** — mass vaccination eventually **eradicated smallpox worldwide in 1979**.
>
> That's the investigative method of Chapter 1, applied to a real problem.

---

What was variolation, and who were teekedaars?

> [!ANSWER]
> **An Indian method used long before modern vaccines**: material from a smallpox sore was used to **scratch the skin**, creating a **mild infection** that built immunity.
>
> **Teekedaars** were the people who performed it.

---

What role does India play in vaccines today?

> [!ANSWER]
> India is **one of the world's largest vaccine producers**, manufacturing on a massive scale and supplying many countries.
>
> Indian vaccine companies played a **key role during the COVID-19 pandemic** and continue to support global health.

---

Who were Kamal Ranadive and Maharaj Kishan Bhan?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Kamal Ranadive** (1917–2001) — biomedical researcher who studied how **hormones and certain viruses link to cancer**, and showed how tobacco, diet and pollution raise the risk.
> - **Maharaj Kishan Bhan** — doctor and scientist who helped develop the **Rotavirus vaccine** protecting children from diarrhoea, and championed **affordable healthcare**.

---

<!-- ===== Antibiotics ===== -->

What do antibiotics do, and what do they NOT work against?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They kill bacteria.**
>
> They do **not** work against **viruses or protozoa**.
>
> **Why:** antibiotics target parts of **bacterial cells** that differ from human cells. A virus isn't a cell at all — so there's simply nothing for the antibiotic to attack.

---

Someone takes an antibiotic for a common cold. Is that useful?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — the common cold is caused by a virus**, and antibiotics have no effect on viruses.
>
> Worse, the unnecessary dose **kills useful bacteria** in the body and **helps resistant bacteria spread**.

---

How was penicillin discovered?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By accident, in 1928, by Alexander Fleming** in London.
>
> While studying harmful bacteria he noticed a **mould** on a discarded petri dish had **stopped the bacteria growing around it**. He realised the mould released a bacteria-killing substance — the first **antibiotic**.
>
> **The accident only counted because he noticed and investigated instead of throwing the dish away.**

---

Explain, step by step, how antibiotic resistance develops.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. A **few bacteria** happen to be **resistant** to the antibiotic.
> 2. The antibiotic kills the vulnerable ones — **including the good bacteria** that protect the body.
> 3. The resistant ones now face **no competition**, so they **grow and take over**.
> 4. Some bacteria even **transfer resistance to other bacteria**.

---

Name two behaviours that cause antibiotic resistance.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Taking antibiotics when they aren't needed** (e.g. for a viral infection).
> 2. **Not finishing the prescribed course.**
>
> **Why stopping early is dangerous:** it leaves the **toughest bacteria alive** to multiply. "I felt better so I stopped" breeds exactly the resistant strain you didn't want.

---

How does resistance spread beyond one patient?

> [!ANSWER]
> Resistant bacteria pass **from person to person**, through **hospitals** (dirty surfaces, healthcare workers), through **animals given antibiotics**, through **animal food products**, and through **soil in crop fields** via manure — eventually reaching people through crop produce.

---

Why should farmers not give antibiotics to healthy cattle?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it **breeds resistant bacteria in the animals**, which then reach humans through **meat, milk and manure-treated crops**.
>
> Resistance developed anywhere eventually turns up everywhere.

---

What is the correct way to use antibiotics?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only when prescribed by a doctor, at the correct dose, and for the full duration.**
>
> Avoiding unnecessary use keeps antibiotics working for future generations — resistance, once widespread, is very hard to undo.

---

Why does antibiotic resistance make common infections dangerous again?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because infections that were **easily cured** become **hard to treat**, raising the risk of **complications, prolonged illness and even death**.
>
> The medicines still exist — they just stop working.

---

<!-- ===== Traditional medicine ===== -->

What are Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani, and what are their limits?

> [!ANSWER]
> Traditional Indian medicine systems using **herbs, oils and minerals**, with a focus on a **healthy lifestyle and balanced diet**.
>
> **They help with some conditions and with everyday well-being, but are not effective for all diseases at all stages** — which is why they complement rather than replace modern treatment for serious illness.

---

How are non-communicable diseases treated?

> [!ANSWER]
> By **managing symptoms and improving quality of life** — through medication, **lifestyle changes** and rehabilitation.
>
> **Early diagnosis and continuous care** are the key, because they stop the disease progressing and prevent complications.

---

Why is "prevention is better than cure" a conclusion rather than a slogan?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because nearly every disease in this chapter is **far cheaper and easier to avoid than to treat**.
>
> Clean water, a mask, a mosquito net, a vaccine, a balanced diet — each costs a fraction of the treatment it makes unnecessary, and none of them carries the risk of the disease itself.
