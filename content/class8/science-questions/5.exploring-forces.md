<!-- ===== What a force is ===== -->

Define force and give its SI unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A push or a pull on an object, resulting from the object's interaction with another object.**
>
> SI unit: the **newton**, symbol **N**.

---

Why must at least two objects be involved for a force to exist?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **a force is an interaction**. There must be something applying it and something receiving it.
>
> When you push a table, your hand and the table are the two objects — and **your hand feels a force too**. Stop pushing and both forces vanish together.

---

List the four things a force can do to an object, with an example of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Make it move from rest** — pushing a stationary box
> 2. **Change its speed** — a friend holding your bicycle from behind
> 3. **Change its direction of motion** — hitting a moving ball with a bat
> 4. **Change its shape** — pressing a balloon, rolling a chapati

---

If an object's speed or direction changes, what can you conclude?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That a force must be acting on it.**
>
> None of these changes happen on their own — the rule works in both directions, which is what makes force a useful idea.

---

An object is at rest. Does that mean no force acts on it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** It means the forces on it are **balancing one another**.
>
> A book on a table is pulled **down** by gravity and pushed **up** by the table — two forces, cancelling out, so nothing moves.

---

<!-- ===== Contact forces ===== -->

What is a contact force? Give two examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A force that acts only when the objects are physically touching** — directly (a hand) or indirectly (a stick, a rope).
>
> **Muscular force** and **friction**.

---

What is muscular force, and where does it act inside your body?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The force produced by muscles contracting and elongating** during any physical activity.
>
> **Inside the body:** it chews food and pushes it along the alimentary canal, and the **contraction and expansion of heart muscles circulates the blood** — essential for survival.

---

A rolling ball stops by itself, though nobody touches it. What does this tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> That **a force must be acting** — because speed cannot change without one.
>
> The force is **friction**, acting between the ball and the ground.

---

Define friction, and state the one rule about its direction.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The force that comes into play when an object moves, or tries to move, over another surface.**
>
> **It always acts OPPOSITE to the direction of motion (or attempted motion)** — which is exactly why it slows things down.

---

Why does friction exist at all, even between surfaces that look smooth?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **every surface has minute irregularities**, however smooth it looks.
>
> When two surfaces touch, **the irregularities of one lock into those of the other**, opposing any attempt to slide one over the other.

---

The same object is pushed with the same force across glass, cloth, wood, tile and sand. What do you expect?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It stops after a different distance on each** — shortest on the roughest surface.
>
> **Why:** friction depends on the **nature of the surfaces in contact**, and is **greater on rough surfaces** because there are more and deeper irregularities to lock together.

---

Is friction a contact or non-contact force? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A contact force** — it arises **only because two surfaces are touching**.
>
> Lift the object off the surface and the friction vanishes instantly.

---

Do liquids and gases exert friction? What do engineers do about it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Air, water and other liquids all exert friction on objects moving through them.
>
> That's why **aeroplanes, ships, boats and high-speed trains are given streamlined shapes** — to cut the friction from the air or water around them.

---

Why is it easier to slip on a wet floor?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **water fills the tiny irregularities** in the two surfaces, so they can no longer lock together.
>
> **Less locking means less friction** — and less friction means less grip.

---

<!-- ===== Non-contact forces ===== -->

What is a non-contact force? Name three.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A force whose effect is felt even when the objects are NOT touching.**
>
> **Magnetic force, electrostatic force and gravitational force.**

---

Two ring magnets are slid onto a vertical stick with like poles facing. What happens, and what does it prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The upper magnet floats above the lower one** — and pushing it down, you can feel the force resisting.
>
> **It proves a magnet can exert force on another magnet without any contact.** Reverse one magnet and it drops instead, since unlike poles attract.

---

A plastic scale rubbed with polythene picks up small pieces of paper. Explain.

> [!ANSWER]
> Rubbing makes **electrical charges build up** on the surfaces. Because they don't move by themselves they're called **static charges**, and the scale is now a **charged object**.
>
> **A charged object exerts a force on an uncharged one** — attracting the paper — and it does so **without touching it**.

---

Two balloons rubbed with the same woollen cloth move apart. What does that show?

> [!ANSWER]
> That they carry **the same kind of charge**, and **like charges repel each other**.
>
> They were charged identically, so their charges must be identical.

---

Bring that woollen cloth near one of the rubbed balloons and they attract. What does that show?

> [!ANSWER]
> That the **cloth and the balloon carry OPPOSITE kinds of charge**, and **unlike charges attract**.
>
> **Why they're opposite:** in rubbing, **both** objects get charged — but with **opposite** kinds.

---

How many kinds of static charge are there, and what are they called?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two — positive and negative.**
>
> **Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.**

---

What is electrostatic force?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The force exerted by a charged body on another charged body or on an uncharged body.**
>
> It is a **non-contact** force.

---

How is static electricity related to the current in a circuit?

> [!ANSWER]
> **When these charges MOVE, they constitute an electric current.**
>
> It is the same current that makes a lamp glow and produces the heating and magnetic effects of Chapter 4. Static and current electricity are the same charges — still or moving.

---

A ball thrown up always comes down, however hard you throw it. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **Earth attracts it** — the **gravitational force**, also called the force of gravity.
>
> It is a **non-contact** force: the Earth pulls without touching.

---

How is gravitational force different from magnetic and electrostatic forces?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Gravitational force is ALWAYS attractive.**
>
> Magnetic and electrostatic forces can be **either attractive or repulsive**, depending on the poles or charges involved.

---

Describe what happens to an object thrown vertically upwards.

> [!ANSWER]
> It **moves up straight, slows down, stops momentarily at the top, changes direction, then speeds up on the way down**.
>
> Gravity acts downwards the whole time — slowing it on the way up, speeding it on the way down.

---

Why do you feel "light" at the top of a swing as it starts to come down?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because that is the point where **your motion reverses** — you were rising, and now you begin to fall.
>
> It's the same momentary stop-and-turn as a ball at the top of its throw.

---

<!-- ===== Weight and mass ===== -->

Define weight and give its unit.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The force with which the Earth pulls an object towards itself.**
>
> Since weight is a **force**, its SI unit is the **newton (N)**.

---

Different objects hung on the same spring stretch it by different amounts. What does that show?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That the Earth pulls different objects with different forces** — i.e. different objects have **different weights**.
>
> It also shows the spring can be used to **measure** weight, since the stretch varies with the pull.

---

How does a spring balance work?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **spring is fixed at one end with a hook at the other**. Hanging an object stretches the spring, and **the amount of stretch gives the weight**, read off a scale marked in newtons.
>
> Most balances have a second scale showing the corresponding mass in grams.

---

A spring balance reads up to 10 N, with 5 small divisions between each 1 N mark. What is the smallest weight it can measure?

> [!ANSWER]
> `1 N ÷ 5 =` **0.2 N**.
>
> **The two things to check on any instrument** are its **range** (here 0 to 10 N) and its **least count** (here 0.2 N).

---

Why must you never hang something heavier than the balance's maximum?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it can **damage the spring permanently** — stretched too far, a spring does not return to its original length, and every reading afterwards would be wrong.

---

Give three differences between mass and weight.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **Mass** | **Weight** |
> |---|---|---|
> | What it is | the **amount of matter** | the **gravitational force** on it |
> | Unit | gram, kilogram | **newton** |
> | Varies with place? | **No** | **Yes** |

---

A 1 kg object is taken to the Moon. What happens to its mass and its weight?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Mass stays 1 kg** — the amount of matter hasn't changed.
> - **Weight drops from about 10 N to about 1.6 N** — the Moon's gravitational pull is much weaker.

---

On which of Earth, Moon, Mars, Venus, Jupiter would a 1 kg object weigh the most, and the least?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Most on Jupiter — 25.4 N**
> - **Least on the Moon — 1.6 N**
>
> (Earth 10 N, Venus 9 N, Mars 3.8 N.) The mass is 1 kg in every column.

---

"The weight of this wheat bag is 10 kg." What's wrong with that sentence?

> [!ANSWER]
> **kg is a unit of MASS, not weight.** Weight is a force, measured in newtons.
>
> **Why the mistake is so common:** in daily life we genuinely care about the **amount of matter** we're buying, not the Earth's pull — so shops measure mass and casually call it weight.

---

Why is it acceptable in practice to find an object's mass by weighing it?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **Earth's gravitational force varies only very slightly from place to place**.
>
> So the weight of an object is essentially the same everywhere on Earth, making it a reliable stand-in for mass — using a spring balance, or a beam balance comparing against a known mass.

---

<!-- ===== Floating and sinking ===== -->

Push a capped empty bottle underwater and release it. What happens, and what does it prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> You **feel an upward push**, and the bottle **bounces back to the surface**.
>
> **It proves water applies an upward force on the object.** All liquids do — this is called **upthrust** or **buoyant force**.

---

Name the two forces acting on an object placed in a liquid, and state when it sinks and when it floats.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Gravitational force** — downwards
> - **Buoyant force** — upwards
>
> - Gravity **greater** than buoyancy → the object **sinks**
> - The two **equal** → the object **floats**

---

Why does a mug feel lighter when you lift it inside a bucket of water?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the water's **buoyant force pushes upward on the mug**, partly cancelling the downward pull of gravity.
>
> You only have to supply the difference — so it feels lighter.

---

State Archimedes' Principle and use it to explain sinking and floating.

> [!ANSWER]
> **When an object is fully or partly immersed in a liquid, it experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the liquid it displaces.**
>
> - Weight of liquid displaced **less than** the object's weight → the upward force is too small → it **sinks**
> - Weight of liquid displaced **equal to** the object's weight → the forces balance → it **floats**

---

How can a rock float on water?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Pumice** does. It forms when lava full of gas and water vapour **cools quickly**, trapping tiny gas bubbles inside.
>
> The result is a **light, porous rock full of air pockets — less dense than water**, so it floats.

---

<!-- ===== Applying it ===== -->

Why is pedalling uphill harder than on flat ground?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because you must work against the **gravitational force** pulling you back down the slope, in addition to the usual **friction**.
>
> On flat ground gravity acts straight down and doesn't oppose your forward motion.

---

Sonali and Ragini's bicycles speed up going downhill without pedalling. What force is responsible?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Gravity** — the Earth pulling them down the slope.
>
> Their speed increases because a force is acting in the same direction as their motion.

---

Riding against the wind feels much harder. Which force is at work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Friction from the air** — air exerts a force opposing motion through it.
>
> Riding **against** the wind increases the relative speed of air past the rider, and so increases that opposing force.

---

Classify each as contact or non-contact: kicking a football, a magnet lifting a pin, a falling apple, applying bicycle brakes, a charged comb attracting hair.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Contact:** kicking a football (muscular), applying brakes (friction)
> - **Non-contact:** magnet lifting a pin (magnetic), falling apple (gravitational), charged comb attracting hair (electrostatic)

---

**True or false:** a force is always required to keep an object moving.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
>
> A force is required to **change** motion — to start it, speed it up, slow it down or turn it. An object slows on its own only because **friction** is acting; remove friction and it would keep going.
