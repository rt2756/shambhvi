<!-- ===== Mixtures ===== -->

Define a mixture, and state the two conditions it must satisfy.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two or more substances mixed together where each substance RETAINS ITS OWN PROPERTIES.**
>
> Two conditions:
> 1. The individual substances (**components**) keep their properties.
> 2. They **do not react chemically** with each other.

---

Distinguish uniform from non-uniform mixtures, with two examples of each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Non-uniform** — components are **visible** with the eye or a magnifier: **sprout salad**, **sand in water**.
> - **Uniform** — components are **evenly distributed and cannot be seen separately even under a microscope**: **sugar in water**, **air**.

---

Sugar dissolved in water looks like plain water. Is it a mixture? How do you know?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — a uniform mixture.**
>
> **How you know:** the sugar has **kept its own property of sweetness** (you can taste it), and no chemical reaction has occurred — it can be recovered unchanged by **evaporating the water**.

---

What is an alloy? Give three examples with their components.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A uniform mixture of metals**, mixed so evenly that the individual substances cannot be seen.
>
> - **Stainless steel** — iron + nickel + chromium + a little carbon
> - **Brass** — copper + zinc
> - **Bronze** — copper + tin

---

What was Mishraloha, and what does the Charaka Samhita say about alloys?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Mishraloha** was the ancient Indian name for a **mixture of two or more metals with properties distinct from its constituents**.
>
> Texts such as the **Charaka Samhita, Susruta Samhita, Rasaratna Samucchaya** and **Rasa Jala Nidhi** describe alloys used **medicinally** — for instance **bronze (Kamsya)**, copper (**Tamra**) 4 parts to tin (**Vanga**) 1 part, used to improve digestion and boost immunity.

---

Classify as uniform or non-uniform: soda water, oil and water, seawater, sand and water, baking powder.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Uniform:** soda water, seawater
> - **Non-uniform:** oil and water, sand and water, baking powder (baking soda + tartaric acid)

---

In everyday speech poha is called a mixture. Why isn't that quite what a scientist means?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **in science all the components of a mixture must be PURE SUBSTANCES.**
>
> Poha's "components" — rice flakes, onion, oil — are **themselves mixtures**, not pure substances.

<!-- ===== Air ===== -->

What is air composed of, and what kind of mixture is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **uniform mixture** of mainly **nitrogen (~78%), oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour**.

---

Why is nitrogen useful in air even though it doesn't support combustion?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Precisely because it doesn't.** Making up 78% of air, it **dilutes the oxygen**.
>
> If air were mostly oxygen, fires would burn uncontrollably. Nitrogen keeps combustion manageable.

---

Describe the experiment that proves carbon dioxide is present in air.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Add **calcium oxide (quick lime)** to water — it reacts vigorously to form **calcium hydroxide** and releases heat.
> 2. Stir and filter to get **lime water**, a colourless solution.
> 3. Leave the lime water in an open petri dish for a few hours, stirring occasionally.
> 4. **It turns milky.**
>
> **Why:** `calcium hydroxide + carbon dioxide → calcium carbonate + water`, and calcium carbonate is an **insoluble white solid** — hence the milkiness. Since the only source of the gas was the air, **carbon dioxide must be present in air**.

---

A clean black sheet left near a window collects tiny particles. Is dust part of air?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Dust particles are **suspended** in air but are **not an integral part of it** — they are **pollutants**.
>
> Their **nature and number vary from time to time and place to place**, whereas air's actual gas composition is fairly constant.

---

Name the major air pollutants and the tool used to describe air quality.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Particulate matter** (dust, soot) and gases such as **carbon monoxide, ozone, nitrogen dioxide and sulfur dioxide**.
>
> The **Air Quality Index (AQI)**.

---

<!-- ===== Pure substances ===== -->

What does "pure" mean in everyday use, and what is adulteration?

> [!ANSWER]
> In everyday use, **pure means unadulterated**.
>
> **Adulteration** is the **illegal** addition of **cheaper or poorer-quality substances** to a product, usually to increase quantity or cut cost. It **deteriorates quality** and can make the product **hazardous to health**.

---

What does "pure" mean in science?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A pure substance contains NO other substance — it consists of the SAME TYPE OF PARTICLES throughout, and cannot be separated into other kinds of matter by any physical process.**

---

A packet of milk says "pure". Would a scientist agree?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Scientifically, milk is a **mixture** — of water, fats, proteins and other substances.
>
> The label means "not adulterated", which is a **different claim** from "made of one kind of particle".

---

Classify as mixture or pure substance: milk, packed fruit juice, baking soda, sugar, soil.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Mixtures:** milk, packed fruit juice, soil
> - **Pure substances:** baking soda, sugar

---

<!-- ===== Splitting water ===== -->

Describe the experiment that splits water, and what is collected.

> [!ANSWER]
> Fill two test tubes with water (with a few drops of **dilute sulfuric acid** added), invert them over the two terminals of a **9 V battery**, and wait.
>
> **Gas bubbles collect in both** — and **one collects about twice as much as the other**.

---

How do you identify the two gases?

> [!ANSWER]
> Bring a **burning candle** near the mouth of each test tube.
>
> - A **"pop" sound** → **hydrogen** (the one with more gas)
> - The flame **glows brighter** → **oxygen**

---

How do you know these gases are not just water vapour?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **water vapour would have condensed back into liquid water** inside the test tube.
>
> These stayed as gases — so they are something else.

---

What does the water-splitting experiment prove, and is it a physical or chemical change?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That water is composed of two different constituents — hydrogen and oxygen.**
>
> It is a **CHEMICAL change**: entirely new substances with different properties are formed, and it cannot be reversed by simply cooling.

---

<!-- ===== Elements ===== -->

Define an element and say what its particles are called.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances** — the building block of all matter.
>
> Each element is made of identical particles called **ATOMS**, and its atoms are **different from those of every other element**.

---

What is a molecule, and why do most elements form them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A stable particle formed when two or more atoms combine.**
>
> **Because the atoms of most elements cannot exist independently** — two hydrogen atoms combine into one hydrogen molecule, two oxygen atoms into one oxygen molecule.

---

Into what three groups are elements classified? Give examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Metals** — gold, silver, magnesium, iron, aluminium
> - **Non-metals** — carbon, sulfur, hydrogen, oxygen
> - **Metalloids** — silicon, boron; they have **intermediate properties** between metals and non-metals

---

How many elements are known, and what state are most of them in?

> [!ANSWER]
> **118**, and **most exist as solids**.
>
> Only **11 are gases** at room temperature (all non-metals), and only **2 are liquids**.

---

Which two elements are liquid at room temperature, and which two nearly are?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Liquid:** **mercury** (a metal) and **bromine** (a non-metal).
>
> **Nearly:** **gallium** and **caesium** are solid but melt at about **30 °C** — they would melt in a warm hand.

---

How many different elements go into a mobile phone?

> [!ANSWER]
> **More than 45** — including aluminium, copper, silicon, cobalt, lithium, gold and silver — across the screen, battery and other components.

---

<!-- ===== Compounds ===== -->

Define a compound and give its three defining features.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Formed when different elements combine CHEMICALLY in a FIXED RATIO to form something entirely new.**
>
> 1. Combination is **chemical**, not just mixing
> 2. The ratio is **fixed**
> 3. Properties are **entirely different** from the elements', and the elements **cannot be separated by physical methods**

---

Hydrogen is a fuel and oxygen supports combustion, yet water puts out fire. What does this illustrate?

> [!ANSWER]
> **That a compound's properties bear no relation to those of its elements.**
>
> It is the clearest possible demonstration that a compound is a genuinely **new substance**, not a blend of its ingredients' behaviour.

---

In what ratio are hydrogen and oxygen present in water? How does the electrolysis experiment show this?

> [!ANSWER]
> **2 hydrogen atoms to 1 oxygen atom (2 : 1).**
>
> The experiment shows it because **about twice as much hydrogen gas is collected as oxygen**.

---

Sodium is a soft metal and chlorine a hazardous gas. What do they form, and in what ratio?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sodium chloride — common salt** — in a **1 : 1** ratio.
>
> A harmless, taste-enhancing substance **essential for life**, made from two dangerous elements.

---

Salt dissolved in water can be recovered by evaporation, but salt cannot be split into sodium and chlorine physically. Why the difference?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because they are **two different situations**:
>
> - **Salt + water is a MIXTURE** — physical separation works.
> - **Salt itself is a COMPOUND** — its elements are chemically combined, and **no physical method can separate them**.

---

Heating sugar gives a black residue and water droplets. Where did the water come from, and what does the experiment prove?

> [!ANSWER]
> **From the sugar itself** — the tube was being heated, so nothing could have condensed onto it from the air.
>
> `Sugar → carbon (charcoal) + water`, and water is hydrogen + oxygen. **So sugar contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen — it cannot be an element. It is a compound.**

<!-- ===== Iron and sulfur ===== -->

Iron filings and sulfur powder are mixed (Sample A). Heating half of it gives a black mass (Sample B). Compare their appearance.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Sample A** — **black and yellow particles visible separately**; non-uniform.
> - **Sample B** — **the same colour and texture throughout**; a uniform black mass.

---

What happens when a magnet is moved over each sample, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Sample A:** the **iron filings are attracted** and can be separated out — the iron has kept its magnetic property.
> - **Sample B:** **nothing is attracted.**
>
> **Why:** in Sample B the iron **no longer exists as iron**. It has chemically combined into an entirely new substance, **iron sulfide**, with different properties.

---

Dilute hydrochloric acid is added to each sample. Compare what happens.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Sample A:** `Iron + HCl → iron chloride + HYDROGEN gas` — colourless, **odourless**, burns with a **"pop"**. The **sulfur is left as a yellow solid** at the bottom, unreacted.
> - **Sample B:** `Iron sulfide + HCl → iron chloride + HYDROGEN SULFIDE gas` — colourless, with a **rotten-egg smell**.

---

What is the correct way to smell an evolved gas, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Waft it gently towards your nose with your hand — never smell it directly.**
>
> Some gases are irritating or harmful, and a direct deep sniff delivers a full dose straight into your lungs.

---

Which sample is a mixture and which is a compound? Give your reasons.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Sample A is a MIXTURE.** Its components **retain their own properties** (iron still magnetic, sulfur still yellow and unreactive) and can be **separated physically**.
> - **Sample B is a COMPOUND — iron sulfide.** It is a **new substance** with completely different properties: **not magnetic**, gives a different gas with acid, and its elements **cannot be separated**.

---

Tabulate the differences between mixtures and compounds.

> [!ANSWER]
> | | **Mixture** | **Compound** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Formation | components simply mixed | elements **chemically combined** |
> | Ratio | **any** ratio | **fixed** ratio |
> | Properties | components **keep** their own | **entirely new** |
> | Separation | **physical** methods work | physical methods **don't work** |
> | Type of change | physical | **chemical** |
> | Example | iron + sulfur powder | iron sulfide |

<!-- ===== Uses ===== -->

Give two ways understanding elements and compounds helps chemists.

> [!ANSWER]
> - Inventing **life-saving medicines and vaccines** by studying how elements combine.
> - Creating **fertilisers** that raise crop production to feed a growing population.

---

Why did material scientists develop stainless steel rather than using pure iron?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the alloy is **stronger and more durable** than pure iron — and resists rust.
>
> **Mixing gives properties no single component has**, which is the whole point of designing alloys.

---

What is graphene aerogel and why is it useful?

> [!ANSWER]
> A material made from **carbon**, said to be the **lightest material on Earth** — light enough for a blade of grass to hold.
>
> Being **highly porous**, it has a **very high absorbing capacity**, so it could clean up **oil spills** at sea and on land, and is useful in **energy-saving devices** and special building coatings.

<!-- ===== Minerals ===== -->

What are minerals, and what is a native mineral?

> [!ANSWER]
> Most rocks are **mixtures of minerals**, visible by eye, magnifier or microscope.
>
> A **native mineral** is one that is a **pure element rather than a compound** — metals like **gold, silver, copper**, or non-metals like **sulfur, carbon**.

---

Name five common minerals and two everyday products made from them.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Quartz, calcite, mica, pyroxene, olivine** (and talc).
>
> - **Cement** — from calcite, quartz, alumina and iron oxide
> - **Talcum powder** — from the mineral **talc**

---

Describe Dhokra art and identify where elements, compounds and mixtures appear in it.

> [!ANSWER]
> An old craft from **Bihar and Odisha**: a design is shaped in **beeswax**, covered with **clay** to make a mould; when the clay hardens the **wax is melted out**, leaving a hollow space which is filled with **molten brass or bronze**.
>
> - **Mixture** — the brass or bronze **alloy**, which gives the figure its strength and golden shine
> - **Compounds** — the minerals in the clay
> - **Elements** — the copper, zinc and tin the alloy is made from

---

Sort into element, compound and mixture: air, water, gold, iron sulfide, brass, common salt, soil, oxygen.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Elements:** gold, oxygen
> - **Compounds:** water, iron sulfide, common salt
> - **Mixtures:** air, brass, soil
