## The big idea of this chapter

You already **notice** number patterns. This chapter is about **proving** them — turning "it worked for the five I tried" into "it must work for every number there is".

The tool is **algebra**: write the number in a general form (`2n`, `6k + 2`, `10b + a`) and the pattern either falls out or doesn't.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A pattern that holds for 100 examples is a **conjecture** — a good guess. Only a proof makes it a **fact**. And a single **counterexample** kills a conjecture stone dead.

## Sums of consecutive numbers

`7 = 3 + 4` · `12 = 3 + 4 + 5` · `15 = 7 + 8 = 4 + 5 + 6 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5`

- **Every odd number** is a sum of two consecutive numbers: `2k + 1 = k + (k + 1)`.
- **Powers of 2 (2, 4, 8, 16, …) cannot be written this way at all.** Every other number can.

> [!TIP]
> **Why powers of 2 fail.** A run of n consecutive numbers starting at a has sum `n × (middle value)`. If n is **odd**, the sum has n as a factor — an odd factor bigger than 1. If n is **even**, the sum is n/2 times an odd number. Either way **a sum of ≥ 2 consecutive numbers always has an odd factor bigger than 1** — and a power of 2 has none.

**Useful shortcut:** the sum of consecutive numbers is `(count) × (average)`. Four consecutive numbers summing to 34 → average `34 ÷ 4 = 8.5` → the numbers are **7, 8, 9, 10**.

If p is the greatest of five consecutive numbers, the others are **p−1, p−2, p−3, p−4**.

## Parity: `a ± b ± c ± d` always gives the same answer's parity

Take 3, 4, 5, 6 and put `+` or `−` between them in all **8** ways:

```
3+4+5+6 = 18      3−4+5+6 = 10
3+4+5−6 =  6      3−4+5−6 = −2
3+4−5+6 =  8      3−4−5+6 =  0
3+4−5−6 = −4      3−4−5−6 = −12
```

**Every result is even.** Try 5, 6, 7, 8 — every result is even again.

**Why it works.** Flip **one** sign, say `+b` to `−b`. The value changes by

`(a + b − c − d) − (a − b − c − d) = 2b`

— always an **even** change. Two numbers differing by an even amount have the **same parity**. Since any of the 8 expressions can be reached from any other by flipping signs one at a time, **all 8 have the same parity**.

> [!TIP]
> **Shorter version:** `a + b` and `a − b` always have the same parity (they differ by 2b). So the ± signs are irrelevant to parity — only the numbers matter.

**This works for any four numbers, not just consecutive ones**, and for any count of numbers.

## Spotting even expressions without computing

| Expression | Even? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `43 + 37` | Yes | odd + odd = even |
| `672 − 348` | Yes | even − even = even |
| `4 × 347 × 3` | Yes | one factor is even ⇒ product even |
| `543 − 479` | Yes | odd − odd = even |
| `119 × 303` | No | odd × odd = odd |
| `2a + 2b` | Always | `= 2(a + b)`, has 2 as a factor |
| `4m + 2n` | Always | `= 2(2m + n)` |
| `2u − 4v` | Always | `= 2(u − 2v)` |
| `13k − 5k` | Always | `= 8k` |
| `3g + 5h` | **No** | `g = h = 1` gives 8 (even), `g = 1, h = 0` gives 3 (odd) |
| `6m − 3n` | **No** | `= 3(2m − n)` — parity depends on n |
| `x² + 2` | **No** | `x = 6` → 38 ✓, `x = 3` → 11 ✗ |
| `b² + 1` | **No** | odd b gives even, even b gives odd |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The reliable test: can you pull a 2 out as a factor?** If the expression can be written as `2 × (something whole)`, it is even for *every* integer. If not, hunt for a counterexample.

## When do two even numbers add to a multiple of 4?

Every even number is one of two kinds: `4p` (multiple of 4) or `4p + 2` (leaves remainder 2).

| Adding | Algebra | Multiple of 4? |
|---|---|---|
| `4p + 4q` | `= 4(p + q)` | **Always** |
| `(4p + 2) + (4q + 2)` | `= 4p + 4q + 4 = 4(p + q + 1)` | **Always** — the two 2s join into a 4 |
| `4p + (4q + 2)` | `= 4(p + q) + 2` | **Never** — a lonely 2 is left over |

> [!TIP]
> This is exactly the **odd/even** story one level up. Remainder-2 plays the role of "odd": two of them combine to nothing, one on its own survives.

## Four divisibility laws worth knowing by heart

**Law 1 — Sums and differences.** If `a` divides M and `a` divides N, then `a` divides `M + N` **and** `M − N`.
**Why:** `M = a×j`, `N = a×k`, so `M ± N = a(j ± k)` — the a factors right out.

**Law 2 — Multiples inherit.** If A is divisible by k, then **every multiple of A** is divisible by k.
**Why:** `A = kj`, so `A × m = k(jm)`.

**Law 3 — Factors inherit.** If A is divisible by k, then A is divisible by **every factor of k**.
**Why:** `12m = 2 × 6 × m = 3 × 4 × m`, so 2, 3, 4, 6 all divide it.

**Law 4 — Two divisors give the LCM.** If A is divisible by k **and** by m, then A is divisible by **LCM(k, m)**.
**Why:** A's prime factorisation must contain everything in k's and everything in m's; the smallest number containing both is exactly the LCM.

> [!WARNING]
> **Law 4 is NOT "divisible by k × m".** 12 is divisible by 4 and by 6, but **not** by 24 — because `LCM(4, 6) = 12`, not 24. The 4 and the 6 **share** a factor of 2, so it gets counted twice.
>
> Divisible by 9 and by 4 ⇒ divisible by 36 ✓ (9 and 4 share nothing).

### Always, sometimes or never?

| Statement | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 8 divides two numbers ⇒ 8 divides their sum | **Always** | Law 1 |
| A number divisible by 8 splits into two numbers each divisible by 8 | **Sometimes** | `72 = 48 + 24` ✓ but `72 = 50 + 22` ✗ |
| Divisible by 7 ⇒ all its multiples divisible by 7 | **Always** | Law 2 |
| Divisible by 12 ⇒ divisible by every factor of 12 | **Always** | Law 3 |
| Divisible by 7 ⇒ divisible by every **multiple** of 7 | **Sometimes** | 42 is divisible by 14 but not by 28 |
| Divisible by 9 and 4 ⇒ divisible by 36 | **Always** | LCM(9, 4) = 36 |
| Divisible by 6 and 4 ⇒ divisible by 24 | **Sometimes** | LCM(6, 4) = 12; 12 itself is a counterexample |
| odd + even is a multiple of 6 | **Never** | odd + even = odd, and every multiple of 6 is even |

**The algebraic version of the last one:** suppose `2n + (2m + 1) = 6j`. Then `2(n + m) = 6j − 1` — an even number equal to an odd number. Impossible.

## Remainders in algebra

**Numbers leaving remainder 3 on division by 5** are `5k + 3`: 3, 8, 13, 18, 23, …

The same set can be written **`5k − 2`** (for k ≥ 1) — "2 short of a multiple of 5" says the same thing as "3 more than a multiple of 5".

> [!TIP]
> **Reading a remainder off an expression.** Rewrite until the multiple part is obvious.
> `4779 + 661` where `4779 = 7p + 5` and `661 = 7q + 3`:
> `= 7p + 7q + 8 = 7(p + q + 1) + 1` → **remainder 1**.
> `4779 − 661 = 7(p − q) + 2` → **remainder 2**.

**Two conditions at once.** A number leaving remainder 2 on division by 3 **and** remainder 2 on division by 4: then `x − 2` is a multiple of both 3 and 4, so a multiple of **LCM(3,4) = 12**. All such numbers are **12n + 2**: 14, 26, 38, …

**The "one short" trick.** Remainder 2 on ÷3, 3 on ÷4, 4 on ÷5 — each remainder is **one less than its divisor**, so `x + 1` is a multiple of 3, 4 and 5. Smallest is `LCM = 60`, so `x =` **59**.

**Worked riddle:** pebbles that leave 1 when grouped by 2, 3 or 5, and 0 when grouped by 7, under 100.
`LCM(2,3,5) = 30`, so the number is `30k + 1`: 31, 61, 91. Of these only **91** is a multiple of 7. → **91 pebbles**.

## Divisibility shortcuts — and why they work

Write any number by **place value**: `… + 1000d + 100c + 10b + a`.

### By 10, 5, 2 (and 4, 8)

Every place value above the units is a multiple of 10, hence of **5 and 2** as well. So those places can be ignored entirely, and **only the units digit decides** divisibility by 10, 5 and 2.

For **4**: every place from **hundreds up** is a multiple of 100 = 4 × 25, so only the **last two digits** matter.
For **8**: every place from **thousands up** is a multiple of 1000 = 8 × 125, so only the **last three digits** matter.

### By 9 — add the digits

**Rule: a number is divisible by 9 exactly when the sum of its digits is.**

**Why it works.** Each place value is **one more than a multiple of 9**:

```
1     = 0    + 1
10    = 9    + 1
100   = 99   + 1
1000  = 999  + 1
```

So for 7309:

```
7×1000 + 3×100 + 0×10 + 9×1
= 7(999+1) + 3(99+1) + 0(9+1) + 9(0+1)
= (7×999 + 3×99 + 0×9)  +  (7 + 3 + 0 + 9)
   └── multiple of 9 ──┘     └─ the digit sum ─┘
```

The first bracket is always a multiple of 9, so **the whole number's remainder equals the digit sum's remainder**. Repeat until one digit is left.

`7 + 3 + 0 + 9 = 19 → 1 + 9 = 10 → 1 + 0 = 1`, so **7309 ÷ 9 leaves remainder 1**.

> [!TIP]
> The rule also runs backwards: if the digit sum is divisible by 9 the number is, and if it isn't, it isn't. **All four "if–then" versions of this statement are true** — because the remainders are literally equal, not just related.

### By 3 — same trick

Each place value is also **one more than a multiple of 3** (`10 = 9+1`, `100 = 99+1`, and 9, 99, 999 are all multiples of 3). So **a number is divisible by 3 exactly when its digit sum is.**

### By 11 — alternate the signs

**Why the shortcut looks different:** place values alternate around multiples of 11.

```
1    = 11×0  + 1     ← 1 MORE
10   = 11×1  − 1     ← 1 LESS
100  = 11×9  + 1     ← 1 MORE
1000 = 11×91 − 1     ← 1 LESS
```

So each digit contributes `+digit` or `−digit`, alternating from the units place.

**Rule: put alternating + and − signs on the digits starting from the units digit, add them up; the result is the remainder (mod 11).**

For **328105**: `−3 + 2 − 8 + 1 − 0 + 5 = −3`.
So 328105 is **3 short of** a multiple of 11 — i.e. it leaves remainder `11 − 3 =` **8**. Not divisible.

> [!TIP]
> Equivalently: **sum of digits in the "+1" places minus sum in the "−1" places**. Here `(2 + 1 + 5) − (3 + 0 + 8) = 8 − 11 = −3`. Same answer.

### By composite numbers — use COPRIME factors

To test divisibility by 6, check **2 and 3**. ✓ works.

> [!WARNING]
> To test by 24, checking **4 and 6 does not work** — 12 passes both but isn't a multiple of 24.
>
> **Why:** `24 = 2³ × 3`. The pair 4 and 6 supplies `2²` and `2 × 3` — but their overlap in the 2s means together they only guarantee `LCM(4,6) = 12`. Use **8 and 3** instead: `8 × 3 = 24` with **nothing shared**.
>
> **The general rule: split the divisor into factors with no common factor (coprime).** Then their product really is guaranteed.

## Digital roots

**Add a number's digits repeatedly until one digit is left.**

`489710 → 4+8+9+7+1+0 = 29 → 2+9 = 11 → 1+1 =` **2**

**Why it matters:** by the divisibility-by-9 proof, **the digital root is the remainder on division by 9** — except that multiples of 9 give a digital root of **9** rather than 0.

- Digital roots of consecutive numbers cycle **1, 2, 3, …, 9, 1, 2, 3, …**
- Multiples of 9 always have digital root **9**.
- Adding **11** repeatedly moves the digital root on by `11 → 2` each time: 2, 4, 6, 8, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, …
- `9a + 36b + 13`: both `9a` and `36b` are multiples of 9, so the digital root is that of **13**, which is **4**.
- Adding **10** to a number raises its digital root by 1 (10 leaves remainder 1 on ÷9). A digital root of 5 becomes **6**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Aryabhata II** (c. 950 CE) describes digital roots in the *Mahāsiddhānta* — they were used as a **check on arithmetic**. If two numbers' digital roots don't match what your answer's digital root should be, you've made a mistake somewhere.

## Cryptarithms — letters standing for digits

**Rules:** each letter is one digit, different letters are different digits, and a number never starts with 0.

**Worked example: `PQ × 8 = RS`.**
A 2-digit number times 8 must stay 2-digit, so `PQ ≤ 12`. `PQ = 10` gives 80 — but then Q and S are both 0, not allowed. `PQ = 11` repeats a digit. **`12 × 8 = 96`** ✓

**Worked example: `GH × H = 9K`.**
The multiplier must be **the same digit as the units digit** of the 2-digit number, and the product must land in the 90s.

- `16 × 6 = 96` — the multiplier matches (H = 6) ✓, **but** then K = 6 = H, and two letters can't share a digit. ✗
- `31 × 3 = 93` — H = 1 but the multiplier is 3. ✗
- **`24 × 4 = 96`** — H = 4 matches the multiplier, and G = 2, H = 4, K = 6 are **all different**. ✓

**Worked example: `BYE × 6 = RAY`.**
- The product is 3-digit, so `BYE < 167`. Hence **B = 1**.
- If Y were 7 or more, `170 × 6 = 1020` — too big. So **Y ≤ 6**.
- The product ends in Y, and `6 × E` is always even, so **Y is even**.

> [!TIP]
> **The method:** attack the **first digit** (size limits it) and the **last digit** (multiplication tables limit it). Then squeeze the middle.

## Handy results to carry away

- The product of **2 consecutive integers** is always a multiple of **2** — one of them must be even.
- The product of **3 consecutive integers** is always a multiple of **6** — one is even, one is a multiple of 3.
- The product of **n consecutive integers** is always a multiple of **n!** — among any n in a row there is a multiple of n, a multiple of n−1, and so on.
- The sum of **three consecutive even numbers** is `2n + (2n+2) + (2n+4) = 6(n+1)` — **always a multiple of 6**.
- **Reversing the digits of a multiple of 9 keeps it a multiple of 9** — indeed **any shuffle** does, since the digit sum never changes.
- Doubling a multiple of 11 always gives a multiple of 11 (Law 2) — so a claim that "some do and some don't" is **false**.
