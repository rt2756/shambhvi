## What this chapter is about

You already **notice** patterns in numbers: odd + odd is even, multiples of 9 have digits adding up to 9, and so on. This chapter is about **proving** them, so that "it worked for the five numbers I tried" becomes "it must work for **every** number there is".

- Why is every answer even when you put + and − signs between **any** four consecutive numbers?
- When do two even numbers add up to a multiple of 4?
- Why does "add the digits" test divisibility by 9 and by 3, and what is the trick for 11?
- How can letters standing for digits (**cryptarithms**) be cracked by reasoning instead of guessing?

The main tool is **algebra**: write a number in a general form (2n, 4p + 2, 7q + 3, 1000d + 100c + 10b + a), and the pattern either falls out or it doesn't. A second tool is **pictures**: dots arranged in rows show at a glance why a sum is, or is not, a multiple of something.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** **Parity** means whether a number is even or odd. A **multiple** of 4 is 4 × (a whole number): 0, 4, 8, 12, … and also −4, −8, … The **remainder** is what is left after making as many full groups as possible. A **conjecture** is a guess that seems true. A **counterexample** is one case that shows a statement is false. **LCM** is the lowest common multiple.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A pattern that holds for a hundred examples is still only a **conjecture**. A **proof** shows it holds for every case. And a single **counterexample** is enough to kill a conjecture.

## 5.1 Sums of consecutive numbers

Anshu writes numbers as sums of **consecutive numbers** (numbers that follow one after another):

- 7 = 3 + 4
- 10 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4
- 12 = 3 + 4 + 5
- 15 = 7 + 8 = 4 + 5 + 6 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5

**Example 1: four consecutive numbers that add to 34.**
Call the smallest number n. The four numbers are n, n + 1, n + 2, n + 3, and their sum is 4n + 6.
- 4n + 6 = 34, so 4n = 28 and n = 7.
- The numbers are **7, 8, 9, 10**. Check: 7 + 8 + 9 + 10 = 34 ✓

**Why this works:** writing the numbers with one letter turns "four numbers I don't know" into "one number I don't know", and one unknown can be found from one equation.

> [!TIP]
> **The middle trick.** A run of consecutive numbers adds up to **(how many numbers) × (the middle value)**. The numbers balance around the middle: in 4 + 5 + 6, the 4 is one below 5 and the 6 is one above, so the sum is 3 × 5. For 34 as four numbers, the middle is 34 ÷ 4 = 8.5, so the numbers sit around 8.5: 7, 8, 9, 10.

### Check yourself

**1.** Anshu wonders: (a) Can every natural number be written as a sum of consecutive numbers (at least two of them)? (b) Which numbers can be written that way in more than one way? (c) Every odd number is a sum of two consecutive numbers; can every even number be written as a sum of consecutive numbers? (d) Can 0 be written as a sum of consecutive numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a) No.** Try the small numbers: 3 = 1 + 2, 5 = 2 + 3, 6 = 1 + 2 + 3, 7 = 3 + 4, 9 = 4 + 5 = 2 + 3 + 4. But **1, 2, 4, 8, 16, …** (the powers of 2) cannot be written this way.
>
> **Why the powers of 2 fail:**
> 1. If the run has an **odd** count of numbers, say 3, the sum is 3 × (middle number), so it has an **odd factor** bigger than 1.
> 2. If the run has an **even** count, say 4, the two middle numbers are next to each other, so one is odd and one is even and their sum is odd. The total is 2 × (that odd sum), which again has an **odd factor** bigger than 1.
> 3. A power of 2 (like 16 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2) has **no odd factor** except 1. So it can never be such a sum.
>
> Every other number has an odd factor bigger than 1 and **can** be written this way.
>
> **(b)** Numbers with **more than one odd factor bigger than 1** have more than one way. For instance 15 (odd factors 3, 5, 15) has three ways, and 9 (odd factors 3, 9) has two: 4 + 5 and 2 + 3 + 4. A prime like 7 has only one way (3 + 4), because its only odd factor above 1 is 7 itself.
>
> **(c)** Most even numbers, yes: 6 = 1 + 2 + 3, 10 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4, 12 = 3 + 4 + 5, 14 = 2 + 3 + 4 + 5. But **not** the even numbers that are powers of 2 (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, …), as shown in (a).
>
> **(d)** Using only positive numbers, no. With negative numbers, yes: **−1 + 0 + 1 = 0**, or −3 + −2 + −1 + 0 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 0. Each negative cancels the matching positive. (Negative numbers even rescue the powers of 2: 8 = −7 + −6 + … + 7 + 8, because everything from −7 to 7 cancels, leaving 8.)

## 5.2 Plus and minus signs between four numbers

Take four consecutive numbers, say 3, 4, 5 and 6. Put a + or a − sign in each of the three gaps. Each gap has 2 choices, so there are 2 × 2 × 2 = **8** expressions. A tree lists them all without missing any:

![A tree of + and − choices for 3, 4, 5, 6 giving the eight expressions and their values 18, 6, 8, −4, 10, −2, 0, −12](assets/img/class8/math/5/sign_tree.svg)

**All eight answers are even.** Try 5, 6, 7, 8: 5 + 6 − 7 + 8 = 12, 5 − 6 − 7 − 8 = −16, … again all even. Is that luck, or must it happen?

**Try this.** Pick any four numbers at all, not consecutive (say 2, 9, 4, 13). Work out all eight expressions. Look at their parities. Now do it again with four other numbers.

You will find that, for each set of four numbers, **all eight results have the same parity** (all even, or all odd). Here are three different proofs.

**Explanation 1: switching one sign.** Take a + b − c − d and switch the + before b to −:

(a + b − c − d) − (a − b − c − d) = a + b − c − d − a + b + c + d = **2b**.

So switching one sign changes the value by 2b, which is **even**. Two numbers that differ by an even amount have the same parity. Every expression can be reached from every other by switching signs one at a time, so all eight have the same parity.

**Explanation 2: parity rules.** odd ± odd = even, even ± even = even, odd ± even = odd. Notice that + and − give the **same** parity in every rule. So a + b and a − b always have the same parity; then a ± b + c and a ± b − c do; and so on to a ± b ± c ± d.

**Explanation 3: the token picture.** Think of each number as a pile of tokens. Subtracting a number is the same as adding it and then taking it away **twice**:

a − b + c − d = (a + b + c + d) − 2b − 2d.

Every expression is **(a + b + c + d) minus twice something**. Taking away an even amount never changes parity, so every expression has the same parity as a + b + c + d.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Rule:** however you place + and − signs between some numbers, every result has the **same parity** as the plain sum.
> **Why:** a − b = (a + b) − 2b, so changing a + to a − always changes the answer by an even number.

For four **consecutive** numbers, the plain sum is n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) + (n + 3) = 4n + 6 = 2(2n + 3), which is even. That is why every result came out even.

> [!TIP]
> In maths there is often more than one way to see why something is true. Listening to how a friend thought about it can teach you a new tool, so share your method and listen to theirs.

## 5.3 Breaking even: parity without calculating

You can tell whether an answer is even **without working it out**, using the parity rules:

| Operation | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| even ± even | even | 2a ± 2b = 2(a ± b) |
| odd ± odd | even | (2a + 1) ± (2b + 1) is 2(a + b + 1) or 2(a − b) |
| even ± odd | odd | 2a ± (2b + 1) = 2(a ± b) ± 1 |
| even × anything | even | the factor 2 stays in the product |
| odd × odd | odd | (2a + 1)(2b + 1) = 2(2ab + a + b) + 1 |

For a **letter** expression, "always even" means even for **every** whole-number value of the letters.

**Example 2: is 4m + 2q always even?** Yes, and here are two reasons.
- 4m is even and 2q is even for any integers m and q, and even + even is even.
- 4m + 2q = 2(2m + q). It has 2 as a factor, so it is 2 times a whole number.

For instance, m = 4 and q = −9 give 16 − 18 = −2, which is even.

**Example 3: is x² + 2 always even?** No. If x is even, x² is even and x² + 2 is even (x = 6 gives 38). If x is odd, x² is odd and x² + 2 is odd (x = 3 gives 11). One example where it works and one where it fails: so **not always**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Test for "always even":** can you pull out a factor of 2, so the expression is 2 × (a whole number)? If yes, it is always even. If you can't, look for a **counterexample**: one value of the letters that gives an odd number.

## 5.4 Pairs to make fours

Two even numbers always add to an even number. But when does the sum of two even numbers make a **multiple of 4**?

Even numbers come in **two kinds**, depending on the remainder when divided by 4:
- multiples of 4 (remainder 0): 4, 8, 12, 16, … written **4p**;
- even numbers that are not multiples of 4 (remainder 2): 2, 6, 10, 14, … written **4p + 2**.

**Try this.** Take 30 counters. Pick out pairs of even numbers (say 6 and 10, 8 and 12, 6 and 8), lay each number out in rows of 4 and push the two piles together. Which sums fill their rows exactly?

Draw a number as dots in **rows of 4**. A multiple of 4 fills its rows exactly; a "4p + 2" number has a last row with only 2 dots.

**Case 1: both are multiples of 4.** 4p + 4q = 4(p + q). The rows just stack up, so the sum is **always** a multiple of 4. For instance 12 + 16 = 4(3 + 4) = 28.

![12 as 3 rows of 4 plus 16 as 4 rows of 4 stack into 28 = 7 rows of 4](assets/img/class8/math/5/fours_both.svg)

**Case 2: both leave remainder 2.** (4p + 2) + (4q + 2) = 4p + 4q + 4 = 4(p + q + 1). The two half-rows of 2 join into one full row, so the sum is **always** a multiple of 4. For instance 6 + 10 = 16 and 22 + 6 = 28.

![6 = 4 + 2 and 10 = 8 + 2: the two leftover pairs make one more full row, so 16 = 4 rows of 4](assets/img/class8/math/5/fours_both_rem2.svg)

**Case 3: one of each.** 4p + (4q + 2) = 4(p + q) + 2. Only **one** half-row of 2 is left over, so the sum is **never** a multiple of 4.

![8 + 6 = 14: three full rows of 4 and a lonely 2 left over](assets/img/class8/math/5/fours_mixed.svg)

> [!TIP]
> This is the odd-and-even story one level up. "Remainder 2" behaves like "odd": two of them add up to a full group (like odd + odd = even), and one alone leaves a remainder (like odd + even = odd).

### Check yourself

**1.** Write the explanation and three examples for case 3: adding a multiple of 4 to an even number that is not a multiple of 4.

> [!ANSWER]

That is (p + q) full rows of 4 with 2 dots left over, so it leaves remainder 2 on division by 4 and is **never** a multiple of 4. It is even, though, because 4(p + q) + 2 = 2(2p + 2q + 1).
>
> **Examples:** 8 + 6 = 14 = 4 × 3 + 2; 12 + 10 = 22 = 4 × 5 + 2; 20 + 2 = 22 = 4 × 5 + 2. None is a multiple of 4.

## 5.5 Always, sometimes or never?

Some statements about multiples are **always true**, some are **sometimes true** (true for some numbers, false for others) and some are **never true**. To decide, use algebra for "always" and "never", and an example plus a counterexample for "sometimes".

**Statement 1. If 8 divides each of two numbers, it divides their sum.** The numbers are 8a and 8b, and 8a + 8b = 8(a + b). In dots: a rows of 8 plus b rows of 8 make (a + b) rows of 8. **Always true.** For instance 16 + 56 = 72 = 8 × 9.

**Statement 2. If 8 divides a number, then 8 divides any two numbers that add up to it.** 72 = 48 + 24 splits into multiples of 8, but 72 = 50 + 22 doesn't. **Sometimes true.**

![72 drawn as 9 rows of 8: cutting it into 48 + 24 takes whole rows, cutting it into 50 + 22 cuts through a row](assets/img/class8/math/5/eight_split.svg)

**Statement 3. If 7 divides a number, it divides every multiple of that number.** The number is 7j, and any multiple of it is 7j × m = 7(jm), which has the factor 7. **Always true.** For instance 14 is divisible by 7, so 28, 70 and 154 (= 14 × 11) are too.

**Statement 4. If 12 divides a number, so does every factor of 12.** The number is 12m = 2 × 6 × m = 3 × 4 × m, so 2, 3, 4 and 6 all divide it. In dots: one row of 12 splits exactly into groups of 2, 3, 4 or 6, so every row of the number does. **Always true.**

![A row of 12 dots split into 6 groups of 2, 4 groups of 3, 3 groups of 4 and 2 groups of 6](assets/img/class8/math/5/twelve_rows.svg)

**Statement 5. If 7 divides a number, so does every multiple of 7.** 42 is divisible by 7 and by 14, but not by 28. **Sometimes true.** In general 7k is divisible by 7m only when m is a factor of k.

**Statement 8. Odd + even is a multiple of 6.** Every multiple of 6 is even, but odd + even is always odd. **Never true.** With algebra: if 2n + (2m + 1) = 6j, then 2(n + m) = 6j − 1, an even number equal to an odd number, which is impossible.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Four laws of divisibility**
> 1. If a divides M and a divides N, then a divides **M + N** and **M − N**. **Why:** M = aj and N = ak, so M ± N = a(j ± k).
> 2. If k divides A, then k divides **every multiple of A**. **Why:** A = kj, so A × m = k(jm).
> 3. If k divides A, then **every factor of k** divides A. **Why:** if k = f × g, then A = k × j = f × (g × j).
> 4. If A is divisible by k **and** by m, then A is divisible by **LCM(k, m)**. **Why:** A's prime factorisation must contain all the primes of k and all those of m, and the smallest number that contains both is the LCM.

> [!WARNING]
> Law 4 says **LCM**, not "k × m". 12 is divisible by 4 and by 6, but not by 4 × 6 = 24, because LCM(4, 6) = 12. The 4 and the 6 share a factor 2, and it gets counted twice in 24.

**1.** Is statement 1 also true with **subtraction**: if 8 divides two numbers, does 8 divide their difference?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always true.**
> 1. 9 = 3 × 3 and 4 = 2 × 2 have **no common prime factor**.
> 2. A number divisible by 9 has 3 × 3 in its prime factorisation; one divisible by 4 has 2 × 2.
> 3. So it has 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = **36** in it, which is LCM(9, 4).
>
> **Examples:** 36, 72, 180 = 36 × 5. You cannot find a counterexample.

**3.** Always, sometimes or never: "If a number is divisible by both 6 and 4, it must be divisible by 24."

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes true.**
> - **Counterexample:** 12 is divisible by 6 and by 4, but not by 24. So is 36.
> - **Example:** 48 is divisible by 6, by 4 and by 24.
>
> **Why:** 6 = 2 × 3 and 4 = 2 × 2 share a factor 2. A number divisible by both is only sure to contain 2 × 2 × 3 = 12 = LCM(6, 4), not 24 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3.

## 5.6 What remains? Remainders in algebra

Which numbers leave a **remainder of 3** when divided by 5? They are 3 more than a multiple of 5: 3, 8, 13, 18, 23, … Multiples of 5 are 5k, so these numbers are **5k + 3** (k = 0, 1, 2, …).

![5k + 3 drawn as k full rows of 5 dots plus 3 more, for k = 0, 1, 2, 3: 3, 8, 13, 18](assets/img/class8/math/5/five_k_plus_3.svg)

The same numbers are also **2 less** than a multiple of 5: 3 = 5 − 2, 8 = 10 − 2, … so they can be written **5k − 2** (k = 1, 2, 3, …).

**Example 4: two remainders at once.** Find numbers that leave remainder 2 when divided by 3 **and** remainder 2 when divided by 4.
1. Such a number x is 2 more than a multiple of 3, so x − 2 is a multiple of 3.
2. It is also 2 more than a multiple of 4, so x − 2 is a multiple of 4.
3. A multiple of 3 and of 4 is a multiple of LCM(3, 4) = 12 (law 4). So x − 2 = 12n, and **x = 12n + 2**: 2, 14, 26, 38, …

Check 26: 26 = 3 × 8 + 2 ✓ and 26 = 4 × 6 + 2 ✓.

**Example 5: remainders of a sum.** 661 leaves remainder 3 on division by 7, and 4779 leaves remainder 5. What remainder does 4779 + 661 leave?
1. Write 4779 = 7p + 5 and 661 = 7q + 3.
2. The sum is 7p + 7q + 8 = 7p + 7q + 7 + 1 = **7(p + q + 1) + 1**.
3. So the remainder is **1**. The two leftovers, 5 + 3 = 8, make one more group of 7 with 1 left.

![7p + 5 and 7q + 3 as rows of 7 dots with leftovers; the leftovers 5 + 3 = 8 make one more row of 7 and 1 spare](assets/img/class8/math/5/sevens_add.svg)

> [!TIP]
> **To find the remainder of a sum or difference, add or subtract the remainders**, then take away whole groups. You never need the big numbers themselves.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes true.** Two even numbers are 2a and 2b; their sum is 2(a + b). It is a multiple of 3 only when a + b is a multiple of 3.
> - Example: 2 + 4 = 6 ✓, 4 + 8 = 12 ✓
> - Non-example: 2 + 6 = 8 ✗, 6 + 8 = 14 ✗

**(ii)** If a number is not divisible by 18, then it is also not divisible by 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes true.**
> - Example: 30 is not divisible by 18, and not by 9 either ✓.
> - Non-example: **27** is not divisible by 18, but it **is** divisible by 9 ✗.
>
> **Why:** 18 = 2 × 9. An odd multiple of 9 (9, 27, 45, …) is divisible by 9 but not by 18.

**(iii)** If two numbers are not divisible by 6, their sum is not divisible by 6.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes true.** Write the numbers as 6a + r and 6b + s, where the remainders r and s are between 1 and 5. The sum is 6(a + b) + (r + s), which is divisible by 6 exactly when r + s = 6.
> - Example: 9 + 11 = 20, not divisible by 6 ✓ (remainders 3 + 5 = 8)
> - Non-example: 8 + 10 = 18, divisible by 6 ✗ (remainders 2 + 4 = 6)

**(iv)** The sum of a multiple of 6 and a multiple of 9 is a multiple of 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always true.** 6x + 9y = **3(2x + 3y)**, which has the factor 3. (Both 6 and 9 are multiples of 3, so this is law 1.) For instance 12 + 18 = 30 and 6 + 27 = 33, both multiples of 3.

**(v)** The sum of a multiple of 6 and a multiple of 3 is a multiple of 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes true.** 6x + 3y = 3(2x + y). It is a multiple of 9 only when 2x + y is a multiple of 3.
> - Example: 18 + 9 = 27 ✓
> - Non-example: 12 + 9 = 21 ✗

**4.** Find a few numbers that leave remainder 2 when divided by 3 and remainder 2 when divided by 4. Write an expression for all such numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. If x leaves remainder 2 on division by both, then x − 2 is a multiple of 3 and of 4.
> 2. So x − 2 is a multiple of LCM(3, 4) = 12: x − 2 = 12n.
> 3. **x = 12n + 2**: 2, 14, 26, 38, 50, …
>
> Check 38: 38 = 3 × 12 + 2 ✓, 38 = 4 × 9 + 2 ✓. (2 itself works too: 2 = 3 × 0 + 2 = 4 × 0 + 2.)

**5.** A riddle: "I hold some pebbles, fewer than a hundred. Grouped in 3s, one is left over. Paired up, one is left over. Grouped in 5s, one is left over. Grouped in 7s, none is left over. How many pebbles?"

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The number leaves remainder 1 on division by 3, by 2 and by 5. So (number − 1) is a multiple of 3, 2 and 5, hence of LCM = 30.
> 2. So the number is 30k + 1: **31, 61, 91** (the next, 121, is too many).
> 3. It must be a multiple of 7: 31 = 7 × 4 + 3 ✗, 61 = 7 × 8 + 5 ✗, 91 = 7 × 13 ✓.
>
> **91 pebbles.**

**6.** Tathagat writes numbers that leave remainder 2 on division by 6. He claims, "If you add any three of them, the sum is always a multiple of 6." Is he right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Write the three numbers as 6a + 2, 6b + 2 and 6c + 2.
> 1. Sum = 6a + 6b + 6c + 6
> 2. = **6(a + b + c + 1)**, a multiple of 6.
>

**8.** Find a number that leaves remainder 2 on division by 3, remainder 3 on division by 4 and remainder 4 on division by 5. What is the smallest such number, and why is it the smallest?

## 5.7 Checking divisibility quickly

Write a number by its place values. A number with digits d c b a (thousands, hundreds, tens, units) is

**1000d + 100c + 10b + a.** For instance, 4075 = 1000 × 4 + 100 × 0 + 10 × 7 + 5.

**Example 10: why the units digit decides divisibility by 10.** Every place value except the units (10, 100, 1000, …) is a multiple of 10. So 1000d + 100c + 10b is a multiple of 10, and the number is divisible by 10 **exactly when the units digit a is 0**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The idea behind every shortcut:** split the number into a part that is surely a multiple of the divisor, and a small part left over. Only the left-over part decides.

### A shortcut for 9

Is 10 divisible by 9? No: 10 = 9 + 1, remainder 1. And 20 = 18 + 2, 30 = 27 + 3: a multiple of 10 leaves remainder equal to its **number of tens**. Likewise 100 = 99 + 1, so 400 = 4 × 99 + 4 leaves remainder 4.

**Example 6: the remainder of 427 on division by 9.** 427 = 4 hundreds + 2 tens + 7 ones.
- Each hundred is 99 + 1, each ten is 9 + 1. The 99s and 9s are multiples of 9.
- What is left is 4 + 2 + 7 = 13 ones. 13 = 9 + 4.
- So 427 leaves **remainder 4**. Check: 427 = 9 × 47 + 4 ✓

![427 as four 99-blocks, two 9-blocks and 13 single ones; 13 = 9 + 4, so the remainder is 4](assets/img/class8/math/5/nines_427.svg)

This works for every place value: 1 = 0 + 1, 10 = 9 + 1, 100 = 99 + 1, 1000 = 999 + 1, 10000 = 9999 + 1, … Each is **one more than a multiple of 9**. So for 7309:

7309 = 7 × (999 + 1) + 3 × (99 + 1) + 0 × (9 + 1) + 9 × 1
= (7 × 999 + 3 × 99 + 0 × 9) + (7 + 3 + 0 + 9)

The first bracket is a multiple of 9. So 7309 has the **same remainder as its digit sum**, 19. Again, 19 has the same remainder as 1 + 9 = 10, which has the same remainder as 1 + 0 = 1. So **7309 ÷ 9 leaves remainder 1**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Divisibility by 9:** a number is divisible by 9 **exactly when** the sum of its digits is. Adding the digits again and again until one digit is left gives the remainder on division by 9 (a final 9 means remainder 0).
> **Why:** every place value is 1 more than a multiple of 9 (999…9 + 1), so each digit leaves behind exactly itself.

**Try this.** Write five numbers using only the digits 0 and 9, like 909 or 99009. Divide each by 9. Then write 99 and 109 and divide those by 9.

A number made only of the digits 0 and 9 (like 99009) is always divisible by 9: each part is 9 × (place value) or 0. But you can't spot multiples of 9 from the last digit alone: 99 and 109 both end in 9, yet only 99 is a multiple of 9.

> - **By 4:** 100 = 4 × 25, so 100, 1000, 10000, … are all multiples of 4. Only the **last two digits** (10b + a) are left over. So a number is divisible by 4 when its last two digits make a multiple of 4. For instance 7316: 16 = 4 × 4 ✓.
> - **By 8:** 1000 = 8 × 125, so 1000, 10000, … are multiples of 8. Only the **last three digits** are left over. For instance 25,104: 104 = 8 × 13 ✓.

### A shortcut for 11

Place values sit **alternately one above and one below** a multiple of 11:

![1 = 11×0 + 1, 10 = 11×1 − 1, 100 = 11×9 + 1, 1000 = 11×91 − 1, 10,000 = 11×909 + 1, 1,00,000 = 11×9,091 − 1](assets/img/class8/math/5/elevens_places.svg)

So 400 = 4 × 100 is 4 **more** than a multiple of 11 (400 = 396 + 4), 60 = 6 × 10 is 6 **less** than one (60 = 66 − 6), and 2 is 2 more.

**Example 7: the remainder of 320185 on division by 11.**
1. Digits in the "one more" places (units, hundreds, ten-thousands): 5, 1, 2. Excess = 5 + 1 + 2 = 8.
2. Digits in the "one less" places (tens, thousands, lakhs): 8, 0, 3. Shortfall = 8 + 0 + 3 = 11.
3. Excess − shortfall = 8 − 11 = −3. So 320185 is **3 short of** a multiple of 11, which is the same as 11 − 3 = **8 more** than one. Remainder 8.

A quicker way to write the same thing: put + and − signs **alternately before the digits, starting with + at the units digit**, and add.

![320185 with signs −, +, −, +, −, + over its digits from the left: +5 − 8 + 1 − 0 + 2 − 3 = −3](assets/img/class8/math/5/elevens_signs.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Divisibility by 11:** starting from the units digit, add and subtract the digits alternately. The number is divisible by 11 **exactly when** this result is 0 or a multiple of 11 (like 11, −11, 22). Otherwise the result tells you how far above (or below) a multiple of 11 the number is.
> **Why:** the places alternate between "1 more" and "1 less" than a multiple of 11, so each digit leaves behind +digit or −digit.

## 5.8 Digital roots

Add the digits of a number again and again until one digit is left. That digit is the **digital root**. For 489710: 4 + 8 + 9 + 7 + 1 + 0 = 29, then 2 + 9 = 11, then 1 + 1 = **2**.

From the shortcut for 9, each step keeps the remainder on division by 9 the same. So **the digital root is the remainder on division by 9**, except that a multiple of 9 has digital root 9 (not 0).

As you count 1, 2, 3, …, the digital roots go 1, 2, …, 9, 1, 2, …, 9, round and round like a clock with 9 numbers. Adding 4 moves 4 steps round the clock; adding 3 moves 3 steps:

![Two 9-clocks: jumping by 4 visits 4, 8, 3, 7, 2, 6, 1, 5, 9 (all nine); jumping by 3 visits only 3, 6, 9](assets/img/class8/math/5/root_clock.svg)

> [!NOTE]
> **Aryabhata II** (about 950 CE) describes repeatedly adding digits in his work *Mahāsiddhānta*. Digital roots were used to **check calculations**: the digital root of a product must match the digital root of (digital root × digital root). If it doesn't, there is a mistake.

**Try this.** Check 23 × 47 = 1081 with digital roots. Roots of 23 and 47 are 5 and 2; 5 × 2 = 10 → 1. Root of 1081 is 1 + 0 + 8 + 1 = 10 → 1. They match ✓. Now try a wrong answer, 23 × 47 = 1071: its root is 9, which doesn't match, so it must be wrong.

>
> **(ii) Division by 9:** the remainder **equals** the root, except root 9 means remainder 0.
> **Division by 3:** roots 3, 6, 9 → remainder 0; roots 1, 4, 7 → remainder 1; roots 2, 5, 8 → remainder 2. **Why:** the root has the same remainder on division by 9 as the number, and therefore also on division by 3 (since 9 is a multiple of 3).

## 5.9 Digits in disguise: cryptarithms

In a **cryptarithm**, each letter stands for a digit, different letters stand for different digits, and a number never starts with 0.

**Example 8: PQ × 8 = RS.** A two-digit number times 8 gives a two-digit number.
1. 13 × 8 = 104 has three digits, so PQ is at most 12.
2. PQ = 10 gives 80: Q and S would both be 0, but Q and S are different letters. ✗
3. PQ = 11 has P = Q. ✗
4. **12 × 8 = 96** ✓ (P = 1, Q = 2, R = 9, S = 6).

> [!TIP]
> **How to crack a cryptarithm:** look at the **first digits** (size limits them: a product can't have too many digits) and the **last digits** (the multiplication table limits them). Then squeeze the middle. Check your answer, and check whether another answer also works.

### Check yourself

**1.** Solve A1 + 1B = B0 (a two-digit sum).

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Units:** 1 + B ends in 0, so 1 + B = 10, **B = 9**, carry 1.
> 2. **Tens:** A + 1 + (carry 1) = B = 9, so **A = 7**.
>
> **71 + 19 = 90** ✓

**2.** Solve AB + 37 = 6A.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Tens:** A + 3 (+ a carry of 0 or 1) = 6, so A = 3 or A = 2.
> 2. If A = 3 there's no carry, so B + 7 = 3, impossible.
> 3. So **A = 2** with a carry: B + 7 = 10 + A = 12, **B = 5**.
>
> **25 + 37 = 62** ✓

**3.** Solve ON + ON + ON = PO.

> [!ANSWER]
> This is ON × 3 = PO, a two-digit answer, so ON is at most 33.
> 1. **Units:** 3 × N ends in O.
> 2. Try each possible O (it starts a number, so O ≠ 0): O = 1, 2, 3.
>    - O = 1: N with 3N ending in 1 is N = 7: 17 × 3 = **51** ✓ (P = 5)
>    - O = 2: 3N ends in 2 → N = 4: 24 × 3 = **72** ✓ (P = 7)
>    - O = 3: 3N ends in 3 → N = 1: 31 × 3 = **93** ✓ (P = 9)
>
> So there are **three answers**: 17 + 17 + 17 = 51, 24 + 24 + 24 = 72 and 31 + 31 + 31 = 93. (Trying every digit in a script confirms there are no others.)

**4.** Solve QR + QR + QR = PRR.

> [!ANSWER]
> This is QR × 3 = PRR.
> 1. **Units:** 3R ends in R, so 2R ends in 0: R = 0 or R = 5.
> 2. R = 0 would need QR × 3 = 30Q = P00, so 3Q = 10P: impossible for digits. So **R = 5**, and 3 × 5 = 15 gives carry 1.
> 3. **Tens:** 3Q + 1 ends in R = 5, so 3Q ends in 4: **Q = 8** (24).
> 4. 85 × 3 = 255, so **P = 2**.
>
> **85 + 85 + 85 = 255** ✓

**5.** Solve GH × H = 9K. Which of these fits: 11 × 9 = 99, 12 × 8 = 96, 46 × 2 = 92, 24 × 4 = 96, 47 × 2 = 94, 31 × 3 = 93, 16 × 6 = 96?

> [!ANSWER]
> The multiplier must be the **same digit as the units digit** of GH (both are H), and K must be different from G and H.
> - 11 × 9, 12 × 8, 46 × 2, 47 × 2, 31 × 3: the multiplier is not the units digit. ✗
> - 16 × 6 = 96: multiplier matches (H = 6), but then K = 6 = H. ✗
> - **24 × 4 = 96**: G = 2, H = 4, K = 6, all different ✓

**6.** Solve BYE × 6 = RAY.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **B:** BYE × 6 has three digits, so BYE < 167 (167 × 6 = 1002). So **B = 1**.
> 2. **Y:** if Y were 7 or more, BYE ≥ 170 and 170 × 6 = 1020 has four digits. So Y ≤ 6. Also RAY ends in Y = last digit of 6 × E, which is even. So Y is 0, 2, 4 or 6.
> 3. Units: 6E ends in Y. Try each Y:
>    - Y = 0: 6E ends in 0 → E = 5 (E = 0 would repeat Y). BYE = 105, 105 × 6 = **630**, so R = 6, A = 3, Y = 0 ✓.
>    - Y = 2: E = 2 or 7; E = 2 repeats Y; 127 × 6 = 762 gives R = 7 = E ✗.
>    - Y = 4: E = 4 or 9; 149 × 6 = 894, ends in 4 but A = 9 = E ✗.
>    - Y = 6: E = 1 (= B ✗) or 6 (= Y ✗).
>
> **105 × 6 = 630** (B = 1, Y = 0, E = 5, R = 6, A = 3), the only answer.

**7.** Solve UT × 3 = PUT.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The answer ends in the **same two digits** UT. So UT × 3 − UT = UT × 2 must be the hundreds part, P00.
> 2. UT × 2 = 100 × P. A two-digit UT gives UT × 2 under 200, so P = 1 and UT = 50.
>
> **50 × 3 = 150** (U = 5, T = 0, P = 1) ✓

**8.** Solve AB × 5 = BC.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The answer is two-digit, so AB < 20 (20 × 5 = 100). So **A = 1**.
> 2. The answer BC must have **B as its tens digit**. Try each B: 12 × 5 = 60, 13 × 5 = 65, 14 × 5 = 70, 15 × 5 = 75, 16 × 5 = 80, 17 × 5 = 85, 18 × 5 = 90: the tens digit never equals B ✗.
> 3. **19 × 5 = 95**: tens digit 9 = B ✓.
>
> **19 × 5 = 95** (A = 1, B = 9, C = 5). The C is allowed to equal the printed 5, since 5 isn't a letter.

**9.** Solve L2N × 2 = 2NP.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The answer starts with 2, so L2N is between 100 and 149: **L = 1**.
> 2. 12N × 2 = 240 + 2N, and its tens digit must be N.
>    - N = 4: 124 × 2 = **248** → tens digit 4 = N ✓, P = 8
>    - N = 5: 125 × 2 = **250** → tens digit 5 = N ✓, P = 0
>    - other N fail (e.g. N = 3: 246, tens 4 ≠ 3).
>
> **Two answers:** 124 × 2 = 248 and 125 × 2 = 250.

**10.** Solve XY × 4 = ZX.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The answer has two digits, so XY < 25 and **X is 1 or 2**.
> 2. The answer ends in X, and 4 × Y is even, so X is even: **X = 2**.
> 3. 4Y ends in 2: Y = 3 or 8. XY = 28 gives 112 (three digits) ✗. **Y = 3:** 23 × 4 = 92.
>
> **23 × 4 = 92** (X = 2, Y = 3, Z = 9) ✓

**11.** Solve PP × QQ = PRP.

> [!ANSWER]
> PP = 11P and QQ = 11Q, so PP × QQ = 121 × P × Q. For a three-digit answer, P × Q ≤ 8, and the first and last digits must both be P.
> - P × Q = 2: 242 → P = 2, Q = 1, R = 4: **22 × 11 = 242** ✓
> - P × Q = 3: 363 → P = 3, Q = 1: **33 × 11 = 363** ✓
> - P × Q = 4: 484 → P = 4, Q = 1: **44 × 11 = 484** ✓
> - P × Q = 6 or 8 gives 726 or 968, whose first and last digits differ ✗; P = Q is not allowed.
>
> So there are **three answers**; the simplest is 22 × 11 = 242. (Checked by trying every digit.)

**12.** Solve JK × 6 = KKK.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. KKK = K × 111 = K × 3 × 37. So JK × 6 = 111K, and JK = 111K ÷ 6 = 37K ÷ 2.
> 2. For JK to be whole, K must be even. K = 2: JK = 37, but then K would be 7 ✗. **K = 4:** JK = 74 ✓ (its last digit is 4 = K). K = 6: 111, three digits ✗.
>
> **74 × 6 = 444** (J = 7, K = 4) ✓

## Exercises — try first

### 1. If 31z5 is a multiple of 9 (z a digit), what is z? Explain why there are two answers.

> [!ANSWER]
> Digit sum = 3 + 1 + z + 5 = 9 + z. It must be a multiple of 9.
> - z is a digit (0 to 9), so 9 + z is between 9 and 18.
> - The multiples of 9 in that range are **9 and 18**, giving **z = 0** or **z = 9**.
>
> Two answers because there are **two** multiples of 9 the digit sum can reach: 3105 = 9 × 345 and 3195 = 9 × 355.

### 2. Snehal says: "I take a number that leaves remainder 8 on division by 12, and another that is 4 short of a multiple of 12. Their sum is always a multiple of 8." Is he right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not always.** Write the numbers as 12n + 8 and 12m − 4.
> 1. Sum = 12n + 12m + 4 = **12(n + m) + 4**.
> 2. If n + m = 1: 12 + 4 = 16 = 8 × 2 ✓ (e.g. 8 + 8 = 16, since 8 = 12 − 4).
> 3. If n + m = 2: 24 + 4 = 28, not a multiple of 8 ✗ (e.g. 20 + 8 = 28).
>
> So the sum is **sometimes** a multiple of 8 (when n + m is odd) and Snehal's "always" is false. The sum is always a multiple of **4**, though: 12(n + m) + 4 = 4(3n + 3m + 1).

### 3. When is the sum of two multiples of 3 a multiple of 6, and when is it not? Explain all cases and generalise.

> [!ANSWER]
> Write them as 3m and 3n. Their sum is 3(m + n). It is a multiple of 6 exactly when m + n is even.
> - **m, n both even** (both numbers are multiples of 6): sum is a multiple of 6. e.g. 6 + 12 = 18 ✓
> - **m, n both odd** (both are odd multiples of 3): odd + odd is even, so ✓. e.g. 3 + 9 = 12 ✓
> - **one even, one odd**: m + n is odd, so ✗. e.g. 3 + 6 = 9 ✗
>
> **Generalisation:** the sum of two multiples of 3 is a multiple of 6 exactly when the two numbers have the **same parity** (both even or both odd).

### 4. Sreelatha says, "My number is divisible by 9. If I reverse its digits, it is still divisible by 9."

**(i)** Is her conjecture true for every multiple of 9?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Reversing the digits doesn't change the **digit sum**. A multiple of 9 has digit sum divisible by 9, so the reversed number also has digit sum divisible by 9, and so it is a multiple of 9. For instance 1863 = 9 × 207, and 3681 = 9 × 409.

**(ii)** Do other shuffles of the digits also give multiples of 9?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, every shuffle does**, for the same reason: any rearrangement keeps the same digits, so the same digit sum. For instance 1863 → 8613 = 9 × 957 and 6381 = 9 × 709. (If a shuffle puts 0 first, it simply becomes a number with fewer digits, still a multiple of 9.)

### 5. If 48a23b is a multiple of 18, list all possible pairs (a, b).

> [!ANSWER]
> 18 = 2 × 9 (no common factor), so the number must be divisible by **2** and by **9**.
> 1. **By 2:** b is even: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8.
> 2. **By 9:** 4 + 8 + a + 2 + 3 + b = 17 + a + b is a multiple of 9. a + b is between 0 and 17, so 17 + a + b is 18 or 27: **a + b = 1 or 10**.
> 3. Combine: b = 0 → a = 1 (or 10, not a digit); b = 2 → a = 8; b = 4 → a = 6; b = 6 → a = 4; b = 8 → a = 2.
>
> **(a, b) = (1, 0), (8, 2), (6, 4), (4, 6), (2, 8).** For instance 481230 = 18 × 26735.

### 6. If 3p7q8 is divisible by 44, list all possible pairs (p, q).

> [!ANSWER]
> 44 = 4 × 11 (no common factor), so test **4** and **11**.
> 1. **By 4:** the last two digits q8 must be a multiple of 4. 08, 28, 48, 68, 88 are; 18, 38, … are not. So **q is even**.
> 2. **By 11:** alternate signs from the units: 8 − q + 7 − p + 3 = 18 − (p + q). This must be 0 or a multiple of 11, so p + q = 18 or p + q = 7.
> 3. p + q = 18 needs p = q = 9, but q is even ✗. So **p + q = 7** with q even.
>
> **(p, q) = (7, 0), (5, 2), (3, 4), (1, 6).** For instance 37708 = 44 × 857.

### 7. Find three consecutive numbers where the first is a multiple of 2, the second a multiple of 3 and the third a multiple of 4. Are there more? How often do they occur?

> [!ANSWER]
> Call them n, n + 1, n + 2.
> 1. n + 2 is a multiple of 4, so n = 4k − 2: n leaves remainder 2 on division by 4 (and is then even, as needed).
> 2. n + 1 is a multiple of 3, so n leaves remainder 2 on division by 3.
> 3. n leaves remainder 2 on division by both 3 and 4, so n − 2 is a multiple of 12: **n = 12k + 2**.
>
> The triples are **2, 3, 4**; **14, 15, 16**; **26, 27, 28**; … There are infinitely many, and they come **every 12 numbers**.

### 8. Write five multiples of 36 between 45,000 and 47,000.

> [!ANSWER]
> 36 = 4 × 9 (no common factor), so a multiple of 36 must pass the tests for **4** and **9**.
> 1. 45,000: last two digits 00 (divisible by 4), digit sum 9. So **45,000 is itself a multiple of 36** (= 36 × 1250).
> 2. Add 36 again and again: **45,036, 45,072, 45,108, 45,144, 45,180**.
>
> Check 45,108: last two digits 08 ✓ (by 4), digit sum 4 + 5 + 1 + 0 + 8 = 18 ✓ (by 9).

### 9. The middle number of five consecutive even numbers is 5p. Write the other four in terms of p.

> [!ANSWER]
> Consecutive even numbers differ by **2**. Going down and up from 5p: **5p − 4, 5p − 2**, 5p, **5p + 2, 5p + 4**.
>
> Check with p = 2: 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 ✓

### 10. Write a 6-digit number that is divisible by 15 and whose reverse is divisible by 6.

> [!ANSWER]
> Call it abcdef.
> 1. **Divisible by 15** = by 3 and by 5. So f = 0 or 5, and the digit sum is a multiple of 3.
> 2. If f = 0, the reverse starts with 0 and isn't a 6-digit number, so **f = 5**.
> 3. **Reverse fedcba divisible by 6** = by 2 and by 3. Its last digit a must be even (a ≠ 0, since it leads the original): a = 2, 4, 6 or 8. Its digit sum is the same as the original's, so the "by 3" test is already covered.
> 4. Choose a = 2, f = 5 and fill the middle so the digit sum is a multiple of 3: 2 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 2 + 5 = 9 ✓.
>
> **200025** works: 200025 = 15 × 13335, and 520002 = 6 × 86667. (Others: 200055, 211125, 400035, …)

### 11. Deepak says: "Some multiples of 11 are still multiples of 11 when doubled, but others aren't." Is he right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No, his conjecture is false.** Every multiple of 11 stays a multiple of 11 when doubled.
>
> **Proof:** a multiple of 11 is 11k. Doubled, it is 22k = **11 × (2k)**, which is a multiple of 11. (This is law 2: every multiple of a multiple of 11 is a multiple of 11.) So there is no multiple of 11 that fails.

### 12. Always, sometimes or never?

**(i)** The product of a multiple of 6 and a multiple of 3 is a multiple of 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always true.** 6a × 3b = 18ab = **9 × (2ab)**. The 6 brings one 3 and the other number brings another 3, and 3 × 3 = 9. For instance 6 × 3 = 18, 12 × 15 = 180 = 9 × 20.

**(ii)** The sum of three consecutive even numbers is divisible by 6.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always true.** Write them as 2n − 2, 2n, 2n + 2. Their sum is **6n**. For instance 4 + 6 + 8 = 18 = 6 × 3.

**(iii)** If abcdef is a multiple of 6, then badcef is a multiple of 6.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always true.** Divisible by 6 = divisible by 2 and 3.
> 1. **By 2** depends only on the units digit, f, which is unchanged.
> 2. **By 3** depends on the digit sum, which is unchanged when digits are swapped.
>
> So badcef passes both tests. For instance 123456 = 6 × 20576, and 214356 = 6 × 35726.

**(iv)** 8(7b − 3) − 4(11b + 1) is a multiple of 12.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never true.**
> 1. Expand: 56b − 24 − 44b − 4 = **12b − 28**.
> 2. 12b − 28 = 12b − 36 + 8 = **12(b − 3) + 8**.
>
> So it always leaves remainder 8 on division by 12 and is never a multiple of 12. (b = 0 gives −28, b = 3 gives 8, b = 5 gives 32.)

### 13. Choose any three numbers. When is their sum divisible by 3? Explore all cases and generalise.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each number leaves remainder 0, 1 or 2 on division by 3. The sum leaves the same remainder as the **sum of the three remainders**. So we look at the remainders:
>
> | Remainders | Sum of remainders | Sum divisible by 3? |
> |---|---|---|
> | 0, 0, 0 | 0 | **Yes** |
> | 1, 1, 1 | 3 | **Yes** |
> | 2, 2, 2 | 6 | **Yes** |
> | 0, 1, 2 | 3 | **Yes** |
> | any other mix (e.g. 0, 0, 1 or 1, 1, 2) | 1, 2, 4 or 5 | No |
>
> **Generalisation:** the sum of three numbers is divisible by 3 exactly when **all three leave the same remainder** or **all three leave different remainders**. For instance 4 + 7 + 10 = 21 (all remainder 1) ✓, and 3 + 4 + 5 = 12 (remainders 0, 1, 2) ✓.

### 14. Products of consecutive integers.

**(a)** Is the product of two consecutive integers always a multiple of 2? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Of two consecutive integers, one is even and one is odd (they alternate). The even one brings a factor 2 into the product. For instance 7 × 8 = 56, −3 × −2 = 6.

**(b)** Is the product of three consecutive integers always a multiple of 6? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Among any three consecutive integers:
> 1. at least one is **even** (every second number is), giving a factor 2;
> 2. exactly one is a **multiple of 3** (every third number is), giving a factor 3.
>
> 2 and 3 share no factor, so the product is a multiple of 2 × 3 = **6**. For instance 4 × 5 × 6 = 120 = 6 × 20.

**(c)** What about the product of four consecutive integers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always a multiple of 24.**
> 1. Among four consecutive integers, one is a **multiple of 4**, and another one (two places away) is **even**, so together they give 4 × 2 = 8.
> 2. At least one is a **multiple of 3**.
> 3. 8 and 3 share no factor, so the product is a multiple of 8 × 3 = **24**.
>
> For instance 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 = 24 and 5 × 6 × 7 × 8 = 1680 = 24 × 70.

**(d)** What about five consecutive integers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always a multiple of 120** (= 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 × 5).
> 1. As in (c), the product of any four of them in a row gives a multiple of 24 = 8 × 3.
> 2. One of any five consecutive integers is a **multiple of 5**.
> 3. 24 and 5 share no factor, so the product is a multiple of 24 × 5 = **120**.
>
> For instance 2 × 3 × 4 × 5 × 6 = 720 = 120 × 6.

### 15. Solve the cryptarithms.

**(i)** EF × E = GGG

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. GGG = G × 111 = G × 3 × 37. So EF × E = 3 × 37 × G.
> 2. 37 is prime, so EF must be a multiple of 37 (it can't come from E, a single digit): EF = 37 or 74.
> 3. **EF = 37:** E = 3, and 37 × 3 = **111** ✓ (G = 1). EF = 74: 74 × 7 = 518 ✗.
>
> **37 × 3 = 111** (E = 3, F = 7, G = 1).

**(ii)** WOW × 5 = MEOW

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **Units:** 5 × W ends in W. 5 × W ends in 0 or 5, and W ≠ 0 (it leads), so **W = 5**, carry 2.
> 2. **Tens:** 5 × O + 2 ends in O. Try O: 5O + 2 − O = 4O + 2 must end in 0, so 4O ends in 8: O = 2 or 7.
>    - O = 2: 525 × 5 = 2625, which should be M E O W = M E 2 5 ✓ in form, but then M = 2 = O ✗.
>    - **O = 7:** 575 × 5 = **2875** = M E O W with M = 2, E = 8, O = 7, W = 5 ✓.

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - Whatever + and − signs you place between some numbers, every result has the **same parity** as the plain sum, because a − b = (a + b) − 2b.
> - An expression is always even when you can write it as **2 × (whole number)**; otherwise find a counterexample.
> - Even numbers are 4p or 4p + 2. **Two of the same kind add to a multiple of 4**; one of each never does.
> - **Divisibility laws:** a divides M and N ⇒ a divides M ± N; k divides A ⇒ k divides every multiple of A; k divides A ⇒ every factor of k divides A; k and m divide A ⇒ **LCM(k, m)** divides A.
> - Remainders behave like numbers: **add or subtract the remainders**, then remove whole groups.
> - **9 and 3:** add the digits (each place value is 99…9 + 1). **11:** add and subtract digits alternately from the units (place values are alternately 1 more and 1 less than a multiple of 11). **4:** last two digits. **8:** last three digits.
> - To test a composite number, use factors that **share no prime** (3 and 8 for 24, not 4 and 6).
> - The **digital root** is the remainder on division by 9 (9 for multiples of 9).
> - In cryptarithms, attack the **first and last digits** first, then check every case: some have more than one answer.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** divisible by 4 and 6 does **not** mean divisible by 24 (12!) • "sometimes true" needs **both** an example and a counterexample • 5k − 3 leaves remainder **2**, not 3 • for 11, start the + sign at the **units** digit • a negative alternating sum −3 means remainder **8**, not 3 • the digital root of a multiple of 9 is **9**, not 0 • a cryptarithm answer must use **different digits for different letters**.
