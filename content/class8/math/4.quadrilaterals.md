## What a quadrilateral is

**A closed figure made of four straight sides.** The name is Latin — *quadri* (four) + *latus* (side).

Its **angles** are the angles between the sides at each corner.

> [!WARNING]
> A figure with four line segments is **not** automatically a quadrilateral — it must be **closed** and the sides must **not cross**. An open zig-zag of four segments, or two segments cutting across each other, doesn't count.

## The one fact everything rests on: angles add to 360°

**The angle sum of any quadrilateral is 360°.**

**Why it works:** draw one diagonal. It splits the quadrilateral into **two triangles**.

```
S ------- O
|  \      |
|    \    |     diagonal SM  →  ∆SEM  and  ∆SOM
|      \  |
E ------- M
```

Each triangle's angles add to 180°, and **together those six angles are exactly the four corner angles of the quadrilateral** (two corners get split in half by the diagonal, and the halves add back up).

`180° + 180° = 360°` ✓

> [!TIP]
> This instantly answers a puzzle: **can a quadrilateral have three right angles and a fourth angle that is not 90°?** No. Three 90°s use up 270°, so the fourth **must** be 360 − 270 = **90°**. The shape has no choice.

## Rectangles

### Three definitions that all describe the same shape

**Definition A (the familiar one):** all four angles are 90° **and** opposite sides are equal.

**Definition B (the short one):** **all four angles are 90°.** That's it.

**Definition C (the diagonal one):** the **diagonals are equal and bisect each other**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Different-looking definitions can pick out **exactly the same** set of shapes. Proving that is real mathematics — and each definition below is *proved* to give the same shape, not just asserted.

### Why B is enough — the "opposite sides equal" part comes free

Take a quadrilateral ABCD with **all four angles 90°**. Join BD.

```
B --------- C
| \   3     |
|1  \       |
|      \  2 |
A --------- D
```

- In ∆ABD: `∠3 + ∠1 = 90°` (they make up the right angle at B).
- In ∆BCD: `∠3 + ∠2 + 90° = 180°`, so `∠3 + ∠2 = 90°`.
- Both equal `90° − ∠3`, so **∠1 = ∠2**.

Now ∆BAD and ∆DCB have two equal angles and the **common side BD** → congruent by **AAS**.

Therefore `AD = CB` and `AB = CD`. **The opposite sides had to be equal all along** — saying so in the definition was repeating yourself.

### The Carpenter's Problem — why C works

A carpenter joins two thin strips of wood; a thread round their four endpoints must form a rectangle. She has one strip 8 cm long. **How long is the other, and where do they cross?**

The strips are the **diagonals**. Three questions:

**1. How long is the other diagonal?**
In rectangle ABCD, ∆ADC and ∆DAB have `AB = CD`, `∠BAD = ∠CDA = 90°`, and the common side AD → congruent by **SAS** → `AC = BD`.
**Diagonals of a rectangle are equal.** So the second strip is also **8 cm**.

**2. Where do they cross?**
Using the same ∠1 = ∠2 argument as above, `∆AOB ≅ ∆COD` (AAS) → `OA = OC` and `OB = OD`.
**The diagonals bisect each other** — they cross at their **midpoints**, so join the strips at their centres.

**3. What angle should they cross at?**
**Any angle at all.** Here's the proof. Let one angle at O be x, so the four angles round O are x, x, 180 − x, 180 − x.

- ∆AOB is **isosceles** (OA = OB, both being half of equal diagonals), so its base angles are equal — call them a.
  `a + a + x = 180` → **a = 90 − x/2**
- ∆AOD is isosceles too, base angles b.
  `b + b + (180 − x) = 180` → `2b = x` → **b = x/2**

Each corner of the quadrilateral is `a + b = (90 − x/2) + x/2 = ` **90°**, whatever x is. The x's cancel!

> [!TIP]
> **This is why the method is used for real.** Carpenters in Europe and house-builders in Mozambique lay out a rectangular base by making two strings **equal** and pegging them at their **midpoints** — no protractor needed, and the angle they happen to cross at doesn't matter.

### Properties of a rectangle

1. All angles are 90°.
2. Opposite sides are equal.
3. Opposite sides are **parallel**.
4. Diagonals are **equal** and **bisect each other**.

**Why property 3:** AB is a transversal cutting AD and BC, and `∠A + ∠B = 90° + 90° = 180°`. Co-interior angles adding to 180° ⇒ **the lines are parallel**.

## Squares

**A square is a quadrilateral with all angles 90° and all sides equal** — a rectangle with the extra condition that the sides match.

**Every square is a rectangle; not every rectangle is a square.** (Like: every Malayali is an Indian, not every Indian is a Malayali.)

### The angle between a square's diagonals

Back to the carpenter — what makes the thread trace a **square** rather than just a rectangle?

In square ABCD, ∆BOA and ∆BOC have `BA = BC` (all sides equal), `OA = OC` (diagonals bisect), and the common side OB → congruent by **SSS**.
So `∠BOA = ∠BOC`, and since they form a straight line they add to 180° → **each is 90°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Diagonals equal + bisecting + crossing at 90° ⇒ square.** For a rectangle the crossing angle was free; pinning it to 90° is exactly what forces the sides equal.
>
> **To construct a square of diagonal 8 cm without a protractor:** draw two 8 cm segments crossing at right angles at their midpoints, and join the four ends.

### Diagonals bisect the angles

In square ABCD, look at ∆ADC: `∠1 + ∠3 + 90° = 180°`, and since `AD = DC` the triangle is isosceles so `∠1 = ∠3`. Hence **∠1 = ∠3 = 45°** — the diagonal splits the 90° corner into two 45°s.

**Properties of a square:** all sides equal · opposite sides parallel · all angles 90° · diagonals equal, bisecting each other **at 90°** · diagonals **bisect the angles**.

## Parallelograms

**A quadrilateral whose opposite sides are parallel.**

Draw two pairs of parallel lines that *don't* meet at right angles and you get a parallelogram that is **not** a rectangle — so parallelograms are a **bigger** family than rectangles.

### Angles

**Adjacent angles add to 180°; opposite angles are equal.**

**Why (adjacent):** `AB || CD` with AD as transversal, so `∠A + ∠D = 180°` — they are co-interior angles.

**Why (opposite):** let `∠P = x`. Then `∠R = 180 − x` (adjacent), and `∠A = 180 − ∠R = 180 − (180 − x) =` **x**. So `∠P = ∠A`. The two subtractions undo each other.

> [!TIP]
> One angle tells you all four. A parallelogram with a 30° corner has angles **30°, 150°, 30°, 150°**.

### Sides

**Opposite sides are equal.**

**Why:** in ∆ABD and ∆CDB — the opposite angles of the parallelogram are equal (one arc), the alternate angles across the transversal BD are equal (double arc), and BD is common → **AAS** → `∆ABD ≅ ∆CDB` → `AD = CB` and `AB = CD`.

### Diagonals

**They bisect each other — but they are NOT equal.**

**Why they bisect:** in parallelogram EASY, `AE = YS` (opposite sides) and the two pairs of alternate angles are equal → **ASA** → `∆AOE ≅ ∆YOS` → `OA = OY`, `OE = OS`.

> [!WARNING]
> **Equal diagonals is the rectangle's private property.** A tilted parallelogram has one long diagonal and one short one. Squash a rectangle sideways and watch one diagonal stretch while the other shrinks.

**Properties:** opposite sides equal · opposite sides parallel · adjacent angles sum 180°, opposite angles equal · diagonals bisect each other.

## Rhombus

**A quadrilateral with all four sides equal.**

Construct one: draw two equal sides AD and AB at *any* angle (not 90°), then cut arcs of that same length from B and D to find C.

### Every rhombus is a parallelogram

In rhombus GAME, a diagonal creates **four equal angles**: ∆GAE is isosceles so a = d; ∆MAE is isosceles so b = c; and `∆GAE ≅ ∆MAE` (SSS) gives a = b. So **a = b = c = d**.

Those equal angles are **alternate angles** across the diagonal — so `EM || GA` and `GE || AM`. **Opposite sides are parallel ⇒ it's a parallelogram**, and every parallelogram property comes along free.

**Worked example:** rhombus ABCD with `∠A = 50°`. In ∆ADB, `a + a + 50 = 180` → **a = 65°**. So each corner angle is 65 + 65 = 130° or 50°, giving **50°, 130°, 50°, 130°**.

### Diagonals meet at 90°

`∆GEO ≅ ∆MEO` (SSS: GE = ME, EO common, GO = MO) → `∠GOE = ∠MOE`, and they add to 180° → **90° each**.

**Properties of a rhombus:** all sides equal · opposite sides parallel · adjacent angles sum 180°, opposite angles equal · diagonals **bisect each other at 90°** · diagonals **bisect the angles**.

> [!TIP]
> **Rhombus vs square.** A rhombus has equal sides but tilted angles; a square has equal sides *and* 90° corners. So **a square is a rhombus that is also a rectangle** — it sits in the overlap.

## Kite

**A quadrilateral labelled ABCD with `AB = BC` and `CD = DA`** — two *adjacent* pairs of equal sides (not opposite pairs, which would make it a parallelogram).

**Property:** the diagonal **BD** (the one joining the two "corners between equal sides")

- **bisects ∠ABC and ∠ADC**, and
- **bisects AC at right angles.**

**Why:** `∆ABD ≅ ∆CBD` by SSS (AB = CB, AD = CD, BD common), giving the bisected angles. Then `∆AOB ≅ ∆COB` (SAS) gives `AO = OC` and two equal angles at O on a straight line → 90° each.

> [!NOTE]
> **A rhombus is a kite** whose two pairs happen to be equal to each other. That's why a rhombus's diagonals also cross at 90° — it inherits it from the kite.

## Trapezium

**A quadrilateral with at least one pair of parallel opposite sides.**

**Property 1:** in trapezium PQRS with `PQ || SR`, `∠S + ∠P = 180°` and `∠R + ∠Q = 180°` — co-interior angles on each slanted side.

> [!TIP]
> Given one base angle you get the one directly above/below it by **subtracting from 180**.

### Isosceles trapezium

**The two non-parallel sides are equal.** Then **the base angles are equal**: `∠U = ∠V`.

**Why:** drop perpendiculars XY and WZ from the top corners to the base UV.

```
X ------- W
|         |
|         |
U -- Y -- Z -- V
```

XWZY has two 90° angles at Y and Z, and since `XW || UV` the angles at X and W are also 90° — so **XWZY is a rectangle**, giving `XY = WZ`. Then `∆UXY ≅ ∆VWZ` (RHS: equal slant sides, equal heights) → **∠U = ∠V**.

## Building quadrilaterals out of two triangles

Take two identical cardboard triangles and join them along a matching side:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> There are only **two ways** to attach the second triangle to a chosen side:
>
> - **Turn it 180°** about the midpoint of that side → you always get a **parallelogram**, and its two side lengths are the triangle's **other two sides**.
> - **Flip it over** (mirror it across that side) → you always get a **kite**, made of two matching adjacent pairs.

| Triangles joined | Turned 180° | Flipped over |
|---|---|---|
| Two **equilateral**, side 8 | **Rhombus** 8,8,8,8 (60°,120°,60°,120°) | same **rhombus** |
| Two **isosceles** 8,8,6 — on the **6 cm** side | **Rhombus** 8,8,8,8 | same **rhombus** |
| Two **isosceles** 8,8,6 — on an **8 cm** side | **Parallelogram** 8,6,8,6 | **Kite** 8,6,6,8 |
| Two **scalene** 6,9,12 — on the **12 cm** side | **Parallelogram** 6,9,6,9 | **Kite** 6,6,9,9 |
| Two **scalene** 6,9,12 — on the 9 or 6 cm side | **Parallelogram** (other two sides) | **Kite** |

**Why the rhombus in row 2:** joining on the 6 cm base leaves the other two sides — 8 and 8 — as the parallelogram's side lengths, so all four sides are 8.

**Why the scalene kite is a kite, not a parallelogram:** the mirror image places 6 next to 6 and 9 next to 9 — **adjacent** equal pairs, which is exactly the kite definition. The 12 cm join becomes the kite's short diagonal.

## The family tree

```
            Trapezium  (≥ 1 pair of parallel sides)
                 └── Parallelogram  (both pairs parallel)
                          ├── Rectangle  (all angles 90°)
                          ├── Rhombus    (all sides equal)  ── also a Kite
                          └── Square     = Rectangle ∩ Rhombus
```

- **Square = rectangle AND rhombus.** It is in every family.
- **Rhombus = parallelogram AND kite.** The only kite that is a parallelogram.
- **A kite is not usually a rhombus** — but **every rhombus is a kite**.
- **A kite that is also a rectangle must be a square.**

## Quick true/false drill

| Claim | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Diagonals equal **and** bisecting ⇒ square | **False** | that's a **rectangle**; you also need them at 90° |
| Three right angles ⇒ rectangle | **True** | the fourth is forced to 90° by the 360° sum |
| Diagonals bisect each other ⇒ parallelogram | **True** | congruent triangles give both pairs of opposite sides equal |
| Diagonals perpendicular ⇒ rhombus | **False** | a **kite** also has perpendicular diagonals |
| Opposite angles equal ⇒ parallelogram | **True** | angles become x, y, x, y with 2x + 2y = 360 ⇒ x + y = 180, so co-interior ⇒ parallel |
| All angles equal ⇒ rectangle | **True** | four equal angles summing to 360° are 90° each |
| Isosceles trapezium is a parallelogram | **False** | only **one** pair of its sides is parallel |
