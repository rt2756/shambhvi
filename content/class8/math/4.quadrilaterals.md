## What this chapter is about

A **quadrilateral** is a closed shape with **four straight sides**. The word comes from Latin: *quadri* means four and *latus* means side. You already know some of them by name: squares, rectangles, the slanted "pushed-over rectangle" called a parallelogram, the diamond-like rhombus, the kite, the trapezium.

In this chapter we stop just *recognising* these shapes and start **reasoning** about them:

- What exactly makes a shape a rectangle? Is there more than one way to say it?
- Why do the angles of every quadrilateral add up to **360°**?
- What can we **prove** about the sides, angles and diagonals of each special quadrilateral?
- How do the families fit inside each other: is a square a rectangle? Is a rhombus a kite?

> [!TIP]
> **The one move to learn.** Almost every fact in this chapter is proved the same way: **draw a diagonal, find two triangles, show they are congruent, then read off the equal sides or angles.** The congruence rules from class 7 are your tools: **SSS** (three sides), **SAS** (two sides and the angle *between* them), **ASA / AAS** (two angles and a side), **RHS** (right angle, hypotenuse and one side).

> [!NOTE]
> **Two facts about parallel lines we use again and again.** When a line (a **transversal**) cuts two parallel lines:
> - **alternate angles** (the Z-shape) are equal, and if alternate angles are equal, the lines are parallel;
> - **co-interior angles** (inside the pair, on the same side of the transversal, the C-shape) add up to **180°**, and if they add up to 180°, the lines are parallel.

## 4.1 What counts as a quadrilateral?

Look at these five shapes.

![Five shapes: (i), (ii), (iii) have four straight sides with angles marked; (iv) has three straight sides and one curved side; (v) has two straight sides and two curved sides](assets/img/class8/math/4/intro_shapes.svg)

A quadrilateral must be:

1. **closed** (no gaps),
2. made of **four straight sides** (line segments),
3. with sides that meet only at their ends (they do not cross each other).

The **angles** of a quadrilateral are the angles between its sides at the four corners (the **vertices**). A **diagonal** joins two corners that are not next to each other; every quadrilateral has two.

### Check yourself

**1.** Which of the shapes (i) to (v) above are quadrilaterals, and why are the others not?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i), (ii) and (iii) are quadrilaterals; (iv) and (v) are not.**
> - (i) and (ii) are closed, with four straight sides meeting only at their ends.
> - (iii) looks odd because one corner is "pushed in" (its inside angle is more than 180°), but it is still closed with four straight sides, so it **is** a quadrilateral. A quadrilateral with a dent like this is called **concave**.
> - (iv) has a **curved** side, and (v) has **two** curved sides. A side of a quadrilateral must be a straight line segment, so these are not quadrilaterals, even though each has four "edges".

## 4.2 Rectangles and the carpenter's problem

Here is the definition you probably already use.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Rectangle (first definition):** a quadrilateral in which **(i) all the angles are 90°** and **(ii) the opposite sides are equal**.

A definition states exactly what a shape must satisfy to get its name. Is this the only way to say what a rectangle is? A practical puzzle helps us find another.

### The carpenter's problem

A carpenter wants to fix two thin wooden strips together so that a thread passed through their four ends makes a **rectangle**. One strip is 8 cm long. **How long must the other strip be, and where should the two be joined?**

![Two wooden strips AC and BD crossing at O; a dashed thread joins their ends A, B, C, D into a rectangle; AC is 8 cm and BD is unknown](assets/img/class8/math/4/carpenter.svg)

Think of the strips as line segments. They are the **diagonals** of the quadrilateral ABCD formed by their ends. So we need to know three things about the diagonals of a rectangle:

1. How long is the other diagonal?
2. Where do the diagonals cross?
3. What angle must there be between them?

Let's **suppose** ABCD already is a rectangle and reason out what its diagonals must be like.

### Deduction 1: the diagonals of a rectangle are equal

Compare ∆ADC (made with diagonal AC) and ∆DAB (made with diagonal BD).

![Rectangle ABCD drawn twice: once with triangle ADC shaded, once with triangle DAB shaded; AB and DC are ticked equal, and the right angles at A and D are marked](assets/img/class8/math/4/ded1.svg)

- AB = DC (opposite sides of a rectangle).
- ∠DAB = ∠ADC = 90° (angles of a rectangle).
- AD is a side of **both** triangles.

So ∆DAB ≅ ∆ADC by **SAS** (two sides and the angle between them). Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal, so **AC = BD**.

**Why it works:** the two triangles are the same triangle placed two ways, and in each one the diagonal is the side opposite the right angle. So the carpenter's second strip must also be **8 cm**.

### Deduction 2: the diagonals of a rectangle bisect each other

Let O be the point where the diagonals cross. To compare OA with OC and OB with OD, look at ∆AOB and ∆COD.

![Rectangle ABCD with diagonals meeting at O; angle 1 is between BA and BD, angle 3 between BD and BC, angle 2 between DB and DC; the vertically opposite angles at O are marked](assets/img/class8/math/4/ded2.svg)

1. ∠AOB = ∠COD, because they are **vertically opposite** angles.
2. AB = CD (opposite sides of the rectangle).
3. Now ∠1 and ∠2. At B the corner is 90°, so ∠1 + ∠3 = 90°. In ∆BCD the angles add up to 180° and ∠C = 90°, so ∠3 + ∠2 = 90°. Both ∠1 and ∠2 are "90° minus ∠3", so **∠1 = ∠2**.

So ∆AOB ≅ ∆COD by **AAS** (two angles and a side not between them). Therefore **OA = OC** and **OB = OD**: O is the **midpoint of both diagonals**.

When two segments cross at each other's midpoints we say they **bisect each other** (to bisect is to cut into two equal parts).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The diagonals of a rectangle are equal and bisect each other.** So the carpenter must use two **equal** strips joined at their **midpoints**.

### Check yourself: a tempting wrong proof

**1.** In the rectangle above, someone tries to prove ∆AOD ≅ ∆COB using these three facts: AO = CO (proved above), ∠AOB = ∠COD (vertically opposite), and AD = CB. Does this work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Look at which triangles the facts belong to.
> - AO = CO: AO is a side of ∆AOD and CO is a side of ∆COB. Fine.
> - AD = CB: sides of ∆AOD and ∆COB. Fine.
> - **∠AOB and ∠COD are not angles of ∆AOD or ∆COB at all.** They are the angles of the *other* two triangles, so they tell us nothing here.
>
> With just two sides and no angle, there is no congruence rule we can use. A correct proof: AO = CO and DO = BO (both from Deduction 2), and AD = CB (opposite sides), so ∆AOD ≅ ∆COB by **SSS**. (Or use AO = CO, DO = BO and ∠AOD = ∠COB, which *are* vertically opposite angles of these two triangles, for **SAS**.)

### Deduction 3: what angle between the strips?

Now go the other way. Take two **equal** diagonals that **bisect each other**, and choose some angle between them. Do we always get a rectangle?

**Example 1: diagonals at 60°.** The diagonals AC and BD are equal and bisect each other at O, and ∠AOB = 60°. Find every angle in the figure.

![Equal diagonals bisecting each other at 60 degrees: angles at O are 60, 120, 60, 120, and each corner is split into 60 and 30](assets/img/class8/math/4/ded3_60.svg)

1. **Angles at O.** ∠AOB = 60°. ∠BOC = 180° − 60° = **120°** (a linear pair with ∠AOB). ∠COD = 60° and ∠DOA = 120° (vertically opposite).
2. **∆AOB.** The diagonals are equal and cut in half, so OA = OB = OC = OD. In ∆AOB, OA = OB, so the angles opposite them are equal; call each a. Then a + a + 60° = 180°, so 2a = 120° and **a = 60°**.
3. **∆AOD.** OA = OD, so its base angles are equal; call each b. Then b + b + 120° = 180°, so **b = 30°**.
4. The same works in ∆BOC (30°, 30°) and ∆COD (60°, 60°).
5. **Corners.** At A: 60° + 30° = **90°**. The same at B, C and D. All four corners are right angles!
6. **Sides.** ∆AOB ≅ ∆COD (SAS: OA = OC, OB = OD, ∠AOB = ∠COD), so AB = CD. In the same way ∆AOD ≅ ∆COB, so AD = CB.

All angles 90° and opposite sides equal: ABCD is a **rectangle**.

**Example 2: any angle x.** Does this depend on choosing 60°? Let the angle between the diagonals be x.

![Equal diagonals bisecting each other at angle x: at O the angles are x and 180° − x; the corner angles are 90° − x/2 and x/2](assets/img/class8/math/4/ded3_x.svg)

1. At O the four angles are x, 180° − x, x, 180° − x (linear pairs and vertically opposite angles).
2. ∆AOB is isosceles (OA = OB). Its base angles a satisfy a + a + x = 180°, so 2a = 180° − x and **a = 90° − x/2**.
3. ∆AOD is isosceles (OA = OD). Its base angles b satisfy b + b + (180° − x) = 180°, so 2b = x and **b = x/2**.
4. Each corner is a + b = (90° − x/2) + x/2 = **90°**.
5. As before, ∆AOB ≅ ∆COD and ∆AOD ≅ ∆COB (SAS), so the opposite sides are equal.

**Why it works:** the x/2 bits cancel. Whatever the angle between the strips, each corner is made of one base angle from a "wide" isosceles triangle and one from a "narrow" one, and together they always make 90°.

![Three rectangles made from the same two 8 cm strips crossed at 40°, 75° and 125°; every corner is a right angle](assets/img/class8/math/4/strips_angles.svg)

So the carpenter's answer is: **two equal strips, joined at their midpoints, at any angle**. This is really used: carpenters set out rectangular frames this way, and farmers in Mozambique mark out the rectangular base of a house with two equal ropes crossed at their middles.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Rectangle (second definition):** a quadrilateral whose **diagonals are equal and bisect each other**.
> **Why this is the same family of shapes:** Deductions 1 and 2 showed every rectangle has such diagonals, and Deduction 3 showed every pair of such diagonals gives a rectangle.

> [!NOTE]
> **Conjecture or proof?** You could draw ten rectangles, measure their diagonals and see that they always bisect each other. That gives a **conjecture**: something we strongly believe. But can we be sure the 1000th rectangle will behave the same? Only a **proof**, like Deduction 2, makes us sure, because it works for every rectangle at once. Measuring is still useful: it gives ideas, and it checks a proof against reality.

### Deduction 4: four right angles are enough

The first definition asked for right angles **and** equal opposite sides. Could we drop the second condition? Try to draw a quadrilateral with four right angles whose opposite sides are **not** equal. You can't. Here is why.

![Quadrilateral ABCD with four right angles and diagonal BD; angle 1 is between BA and BD, angle 3 between BD and BC, angle 2 between DB and DC](assets/img/class8/math/4/ded4.svg)

Join BD and compare ∆BAD with ∆DCB.

1. ∠BAD = ∠DCB = 90°.
2. BD is a side of both triangles.
3. At B: ∠1 + ∠3 = 90°. In ∆BCD: ∠3 + ∠2 + 90° = 180°, so ∠3 + ∠2 = 90°. Hence **∠1 = ∠2**.

So ∆BAD ≅ ∆DCB by **AAS**, and therefore AD = CB and BA = DC.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Rectangle (simplest definition):** a quadrilateral in which **all the angles are 90°**. The equal opposite sides come free.

### Check yourself

**1.** In Deduction 4, is it wrong to write ∆BAD ≅ ∆CDB? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, it is wrong.** The order of the letters tells us which corners match. ∆BAD ≅ ∆CDB would match A with D, which claims ∠BAD = ∠CDB. But ∠BAD = 90° while ∠CDB is the angle ∠2, which is not 90°.
>
> The corners that really match are: the right angles A ↔ C, and the ends of the shared side B ↔ D, D ↔ B. So the correct statement is **∆BAD ≅ ∆DCB**.

**2.** In rectangle ABCD (A bottom-left, B top-left, C top-right, D bottom-right), show that AB ∥ DC.

![Rectangle ABCD with the left side AB extended as a transversal; right angles at A and B; AD and BC marked parallel](assets/img/class8/math/4/rect_parallel.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Use the bottom side AD as a **transversal** cutting AB and DC.
> 1. ∠A = 90° and ∠D = 90° (angles of a rectangle).
> 2. These are **co-interior angles** (inside the pair AB, DC, on the same side of AD), and they add up to 90° + 90° = 180°.
> 3. When co-interior angles add up to 180°, the two lines are parallel. So **AB ∥ DC**.
>
> (The figure shows the other pair: with AB as the transversal, ∠A + ∠B = 180°, so AD ∥ BC.)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Properties of a rectangle**
> 1. All the angles are 90°.
> 2. Opposite sides are equal.
> 3. Opposite sides are parallel.
> 4. The diagonals are equal and bisect each other.

### Squares: a special rectangle

**Example 3: which are rectangles?** All four of these shapes have four right angles (the side lengths are marked). Are any of them not rectangles?

![Four shapes with four right angles each: (i) 5 cm by 2 cm, (ii) 6 cm by 3.6 cm tilted, (iii) 1 cm by 5 cm, (iv) 4 cm on every side, tilted](assets/img/class8/math/4/special_rects.svg)

**All four are rectangles**, because all their angles are 90° (that is the simplest definition). Tilting a shape does not change it, and a tall thin rectangle is still a rectangle. Shape (iv) is special: its sides are **all equal**. We call it a **square**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Square:** a quadrilateral in which **all the angles are 90° and all the sides are equal**. So **every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.**

This "one family inside another" idea can seem strange at first. It is like saying "I am an Indian and I am a Malayali": both are true at once, because every Malayali is an Indian. A **Venn diagram** shows it: a closed curve stands for a set, every point inside it stands for one shape, and the set of squares sits **inside** the set of rectangles.

![Venn diagram: an oval labelled Rectangles containing a smaller oval labelled Squares](assets/img/class8/math/4/venn_sq_rect.svg)

### Deduction 5: the carpenter's square

What must the carpenter do so that the thread makes a **square**? We already know the diagonals must be equal and bisect each other (that gives a rectangle). Can the **angle** between them make the sides equal too?

Suppose ABCD is a square, with diagonals meeting at O.

![Square ABCD with diagonals meeting at O; triangles BOA and BOC shaded; BA and BC ticked equal, OA and OC ticked equal; BO is shared; the angles BOA and BOC are marked with question marks](assets/img/class8/math/4/square_ded5.svg)

1. BA = BC (sides of a square).
2. OA = OC (a square is a rectangle, so its diagonals bisect each other).
3. BO is a side of both ∆BOA and ∆BOC.

So ∆BOA ≅ ∆BOC by **SSS**, and ∠BOA = ∠BOC. But these two angles sit side by side on the straight line AC, so they add up to 180°. Two equal angles adding to 180° must each be **90°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The diagonals of a square are equal and bisect each other at right angles.** And since the four ends of the diagonals fix the four corners, diagonals drawn this way always give a square.

**One more square fact.** In square ABCD, draw diagonal AC. What are the angles ∠1, ∠2, ∠3, ∠4 it makes?

![Square ABCD with diagonal AC; angles 1 and 2 at A, angles 3 and 4 at C](assets/img/class8/math/4/square_45.svg)

In ∆ADC: ∠1 + ∠3 + 90° = 180°, so ∠1 + ∠3 = 90°. Also AD = DC, so the angles opposite them are equal: ∠1 = ∠3. So **∠1 = ∠3 = 45°**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Properties of a square** (a square is a rectangle, so it has every rectangle property, plus more):
> 1. All sides are equal.
> 2. Opposite sides are parallel.
> 3. All angles are 90°.
> 4. The diagonals are equal and bisect each other at 90°.
> 5. The diagonals **bisect the angles** of the square (each 90° corner is split into 45° + 45°).

### Check yourself

**1.** Construct a square whose diagonal is 8 cm. Say why your method must give a square.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw AC = 8 cm.
> 2. Find its midpoint O and draw the line through O perpendicular to AC. (With a compass: open it more than 4 cm, draw arcs above and below AC from A and from C, and join the two crossing points. This line cuts AC at its midpoint, at 90°.)
> 3. On this line mark B and D, each **4 cm** from O, one on each side.
> 4. Join A, B, C, D.
>
> **Why it is a square:** AC = BD = 8 cm (equal diagonals), O is the midpoint of both (they bisect each other), and they meet at 90°. Equal, bisecting diagonals give a rectangle; meeting at right angles makes the sides equal (Deduction 5). Check by measuring: each side is about 5.7 cm and each corner is 90°.

**2.** In the square above, find ∠2 and ∠4.

> [!ANSWER]
> Look at ∆ABC.
> 1. ∠B = 90°, so ∠2 + ∠4 + 90° = 180°, giving ∠2 + ∠4 = 90°.
> 2. AB = BC (sides of the square), so the angles opposite them are equal: ∠4 = ∠2.
> 3. So **∠2 = ∠4 = 45°**.
>
> Together with ∠1 = ∠3 = 45°, this shows diagonal AC cuts both corner angles at A and C exactly in half.

> 3. They are **perpendicular** (given).

**4.** You have no paper to fold, only two sticks of equal length and a thread. How can you make an exact 90° angle?

## 4.3 The angles of a quadrilateral add up to 360°

Can a quadrilateral have **three** right angles and a fourth angle that is not 90°? Try drawing one: you will find you can't. The reason is a rule about **every** quadrilateral.

Take any quadrilateral SEMO and draw the diagonal SM. It cuts the quadrilateral into two triangles.

![Quadrilateral SEMO with diagonal SM; triangle SEM has angles 1 (at S), 2 (at E), 3 (at M); triangle SMO has angles 4 (at S), 6 (at M), 5 (at O)](assets/img/class8/math/4/angle_sum.svg)

- In ∆SEM: ∠1 + ∠2 + ∠3 = 180°.
- In ∆SMO: ∠4 + ∠5 + ∠6 = 180°.

Add them: ∠1 + ∠2 + ∠3 + ∠4 + ∠5 + ∠6 = 360°. Regroup: (∠1 + ∠4) + ∠2 + (∠3 + ∠6) + ∠5 = 360°. But ∠1 + ∠4 is the whole angle at S, ∠3 + ∠6 is the whole angle at M, ∠2 is the angle at E and ∠5 the angle at O.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The angles of any quadrilateral add up to 360°.**
> **Why it works:** a diagonal splits it into two triangles, each with 180°, and the triangles' angles exactly fill the four corners.

This is why three right angles force the fourth: 360° − 3 × 90° = 90°.

**Example 4.** Three angles of a quadrilateral are 80°, 95° and 110°. Find the fourth.
1. The three add up to 80° + 95° + 110° = 285°.
2. All four add up to 360°, so the fourth is 360° − 285° = **75°**.

> [!TIP]
> For a quadrilateral with a dent (concave), pick the diagonal that lies **inside** it: the other diagonal runs outside and does not split it into two triangles. End question 10 shows this.

## 4.4 Parallelograms

Rectangles have both pairs of opposite sides parallel. Are there other quadrilaterals like that? Yes: draw two pairs of parallel lines that do **not** meet at right angles.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Parallelogram:** a quadrilateral in which **both pairs of opposite sides are parallel**.

A rectangle has its opposite sides parallel, so **every rectangle is a parallelogram**: a special one, with right angles. And every square is a rectangle. So the families nest:

![Venn diagram: Parallelograms contains Rectangles, which contains Squares](assets/img/class8/math/4/venn_para.svg)

**Try this: build a parallelogram.** Make one with sides 4 cm and 5 cm and a 30° angle between them.

![Step 1: AB = 4 cm and AD = 5 cm with a 30° angle at A. Step 2: a line through D parallel to AB and a line through B parallel to AD meet at C](assets/img/class8/math/4/para_construct.svg)

1. Draw AB = 4 cm. At A draw a 30° angle and mark AD = 5 cm along it.
2. Through D draw a line parallel to AB; through B draw a line parallel to AD (with a set-square sliding along a ruler, or with a compass by copying angles). They meet at C.

**What you will see:** measure the angles and sides of ABCD. You should find angles 30°, 150°, 30°, 150° and sides 4, 5, 4, 5 cm. Now let's prove that this always happens.

### Deduction 6: the angles of a parallelogram

In parallelogram ABCD, AB ∥ DC and AD is a transversal, so ∠A and ∠D are co-interior angles: **∠A + ∠D = 180°**. In the same way (using the other transversals) ∠A + ∠B = 180°, ∠B + ∠C = 180° and ∠C + ∠D = 180°.

**Example 5.** In the parallelogram we built, ∠A = 30°. Then ∠D = 180° − 30° = **150°**, ∠B = 180° − 30° = **150°**, and ∠C = 180° − ∠B = **30°**.

![Parallelogram ABCD with angles 30°, 150°, 30°, 150° and sides 4 cm, 5 cm, 4 cm, 5 cm](assets/img/class8/math/4/para_angles.svg)

Are opposite angles **always** equal? Take a parallelogram PEAR with ∠P = x.

![Parallelogram PEAR with x at P and A, and 180° − x at E and R](assets/img/class8/math/4/para_x.svg)

1. ∠P + ∠R = 180° (co-interior, PE ∥ RA), so ∠R = 180° − x.
2. ∠R + ∠A = 180° (co-interior, PR ∥ EA), so ∠A = 180° − (180° − x) = **x**.
3. In the same way ∠E = 180° − x = ∠R.

**Why it works:** each angle is "180° minus its neighbour", and doing that twice brings you back to where you started. So the angles across from each other must be equal.

### Deduction 7: the sides of a parallelogram

Opposite sides *look* equal. To prove it, draw diagonal BD and compare ∆ABD and ∆CDB.

![Parallelogram ABCD with diagonal BD; equal opposite angles at A and C marked with single arcs; equal alternate angles ADB and CBD marked with double arcs](assets/img/class8/math/4/para_sides.svg)

1. ∠A = ∠C (opposite angles, Deduction 6). Single arcs.
2. AD ∥ BC with BD as a transversal, so the **alternate angles** ∠ADB and ∠CBD are equal. Double arcs.
3. BD is a side of both triangles.

So ∆ABD ≅ ∆CDB by **AAS**, and therefore **AD = CB and AB = CD**: opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal.

### Deduction 8: the diagonals of a parallelogram

Are the diagonals equal? Measure them in the 30° parallelogram: one is much longer than the other. So **no**, not in general. Do they at least bisect each other? Take parallelogram EASY with diagonals meeting at O.

![Parallelogram EASY with diagonals AY and ES meeting at O; triangles AOE and YOS shaded; AE and YS ticked equal; single arcs mark equal alternate angles at A and Y, double arcs at E and S](assets/img/class8/math/4/para_diags.svg)

1. AE = YS (opposite sides, Deduction 7).
2. AE ∥ SY, and AY is a transversal, so ∠OAE = ∠OYS (alternate angles, single arcs).
3. ES is also a transversal, so ∠OEA = ∠OSY (alternate angles, double arcs).

So ∆AOE ≅ ∆YOS by **ASA** (two angles and the side between them). Hence **OA = OY and OE = OS**: O is the midpoint of both diagonals.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Properties of a parallelogram**
> 1. Opposite sides are equal.
> 2. Opposite sides are parallel.
> 3. Adjacent (neighbouring) angles add up to 180°, and opposite angles are equal.
> 4. The diagonals bisect each other (but are usually not equal).

**Going backwards: diagonals that bisect each other always make a parallelogram.** Suppose the diagonals of ABCD cross at O with OA = OC and OB = OD. Then ∆AOB ≅ ∆COD by SAS (OA = OC, OB = OD, and ∠AOB = ∠COD as vertically opposite angles). So ∠OAB = ∠OCD. These are alternate angles for AB and CD with transversal AC, so **AB ∥ CD**. The same argument with ∆AOD and ∆COB gives **AD ∥ BC**. We will use this to construct parallelograms from their diagonals.

### Check yourself

**1.** In Deduction 7, is it wrong to write ∆ABD ≅ ∆CBD? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, it is wrong.** The letters must be in matching order. The matching corners are A ↔ C (the equal opposite angles), B ↔ D and D ↔ B (the ends of the shared diagonal, swapped). So the correct statement is **∆ABD ≅ ∆CDB**.
>
> Writing ∆ABD ≅ ∆CBD would match B with B and D with D. That claims, for instance, that AB = CB, two neighbouring sides, which is false in most parallelograms (in ours AB = 5.5 cm and CB = 3.6 cm).

**2.** In Deduction 8, is it wrong to write ∆AOE ≅ ∆SOY? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** The matching corners are A ↔ Y (single-arc angles), O ↔ O and E ↔ S (double-arc angles), so the correct statement is **∆AOE ≅ ∆YOS**.
>
> ∆AOE ≅ ∆SOY would match A with S, claiming OA = OS and ∠OAE = ∠OSY. Neither is true in general: OA is half of diagonal AY, OS is half of the other diagonal ES, and these diagonals usually have different lengths.

**3.** Do the diagonals of a parallelogram always meet at some particular angle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Rectangles are parallelograms, and we saw rectangles whose diagonals meet at 40°, 75°, 125° or any other angle (Deduction 3). In a square they meet at 90°, in a long thin rectangle at a small angle. Since the angle changes from one parallelogram to another, there is no fixed angle.
>
> (We will see that one special kind of parallelogram, the rhombus, always has diagonals meeting at 90°.)

## 4.5 Rhombuses: four equal sides

Are squares the only quadrilaterals with four equal sides? Let's construct one that is not a square.

**Try this.** Draw AB = 4 cm and AD = 4 cm with a 50° angle between them (not 90°). Open your compass to 4 cm and draw arcs from B and from D. They cross at C. Join BC and DC.

![Rhombus ABCD with all sides 4 cm and a 50° angle at A; compass arcs from B and D meet at C](assets/img/class8/math/4/rhombus_construct.svg)

**What you will see:** a slanted shape with all four sides equal and one angle of 50°. Any angle less than 180° would have worked instead of 50°.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Rhombus:** a quadrilateral in which **all four sides are equal**.

### Deduction 9: the angles of a rhombus

Take any rhombus GAME and draw the diagonal AE. Call the angles it makes a, b, c, d.

![Rhombus GAME with diagonal AE; at E the angles a (towards G) and b (towards M); at A the angles c (towards M) and d (towards G)](assets/img/class8/math/4/rhombus_game.svg)

1. In ∆GAE, GE = GA, so the angles opposite them are equal: **a = d**.
2. In ∆MAE, ME = MA, so **b = c**.
3. ∆GAE ≅ ∆MAE (see Check yourself below), so **a = b** and **c = d**, and also ∠G = ∠M.

So **a = b = c = d**: the diagonal cuts the angles at E and A exactly in half, and all four pieces are equal.

**Example 6.** Find the angles of our rhombus ABCD with ∠A = 50°.

![Rhombus ABCD with diagonal BD: 50° at A and C, and 65° in each of the four angles the diagonal makes](assets/img/class8/math/4/rhombus_65.svg)

1. Diagonal BD makes four equal angles; call each a.
2. In ∆ABD: a + a + 50° = 180°, so 2a = 130° and **a = 65°**.
3. So ∠B = ∠D = 65° + 65° = **130°**.
4. ∠C = ∠A = **50°** (Deduction 9 showed the angles at the two ends of a diagonal's opposite corners are equal, like ∠G = ∠M). Check: 50 + 130 + 50 + 130 = 360 ✓.

The angles are 50°, 130°, 50°, 130°: opposite angles equal, neighbours adding to 180°, just like a parallelogram. That is no accident.

**Every rhombus is a parallelogram.** Look at rhombus GAME again, with the transversal AE.
- a (= ∠GEA) and c (= ∠MAE) are **alternate angles** for the lines GE and AM. They are equal, so **GE ∥ AM**.
- b (= ∠MEA) and d (= ∠GAE) are alternate angles for the lines EM and GA. They are equal, so **EM ∥ GA**.

Both pairs of opposite sides are parallel, so **every rhombus is a parallelogram, and all the parallelogram properties hold for it**.

A square has four equal sides, so it is a rhombus; it also is a rectangle. The family picture grows:

![Venn diagram: inside Parallelograms, the ovals Rectangles and Rhombuses overlap; the overlap is Squares](assets/img/class8/math/4/venn_rhombus.svg)

**Squares are exactly the shapes that are both rectangles and rhombuses**: right angles and equal sides.

### Deduction 10: the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular

![Rhombus GAME with both diagonals meeting at O; GO and OM ticked equal; triangles GEO and MEO shaded; the angle at O marked as a right angle](assets/img/class8/math/4/rhombus_perp.svg)

∆GEO ≅ ∆MEO (Check yourself below), so ∠GOE = ∠MOE. These two angles lie side by side on the straight line GM, so they add up to 180°. Two equal angles making 180° are **90° each**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Properties of a rhombus**
> 1. All sides are equal.
> 2. Opposite sides are parallel.
> 3. Adjacent angles add up to 180°, opposite angles are equal.
> 4. The diagonals bisect each other (a rhombus is a parallelogram).
> 5. The diagonals bisect the angles of the rhombus.
> 6. The diagonals meet at **90°**.

**Going backwards again.** If two diagonals **bisect each other at right angles**, the four triangles around the crossing point have equal legs in pairs with a 90° angle between them, so they are all congruent (SAS) and the four sides are equal: the shape is a **rhombus**. We use this to draw a rhombus from its diagonals.

### Check yourself

**1.** In Deduction 9, how do we know ∆GAE ≅ ∆MAE?

> [!ANSWER]
> By **SSS**:
> - GA = MA (sides of the rhombus),
> - GE = ME (sides of the rhombus),
> - AE is a side of both triangles.
>
> Three pairs of equal sides, so the triangles are congruent.

**2.** Are the diagonals of a rhombus equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not in general.** In the 50° rhombus with 4 cm sides, measure them: BD is about 3.4 cm and AC is about 7.3 cm.
>
> **When can they be equal?** A rhombus is a parallelogram, so its diagonals bisect each other. If they were also equal, the rhombus would be a rectangle (equal diagonals that bisect each other), so all its angles would be 90°. A rhombus with right angles is a **square**. So the only rhombuses with equal diagonals are squares.

**3.** In Deduction 10, why is ∆GEO ≅ ∆MEO?

> [!ANSWER]
> By **SSS**:
> - GE = ME (sides of the rhombus),
> - GO = MO (a rhombus is a parallelogram, so its diagonals bisect each other),
> - EO is a side of both triangles.

## 4.6 Playing with quadrilaterals

**Try this: geoboard.** You need a geoboard and rubber bands, or dot paper. Put two rubber bands across each other at right angles so that they are **equal** in length and cross at their middles. Stretch a third band round their four ends. Then stretch one of the first two bands 2 cm further at **each** end and look again.

![Dot grid: left, equal perpendicular diagonals of 4 units crossing at their middles, with a square round their ends; right, one diagonal stretched to 8 units, giving a rhombus](assets/img/class8/math/4/geoboard.svg)

**Try this: joining triangles.** Cut two identical triangles from card and put them together, edge to matching edge, in every way you can. Each time, name the quadrilateral and say why. Do it for: two equilateral triangles with 8 cm sides; two isosceles triangles with sides 8 cm, 8 cm, 6 cm; two scalene triangles with sides 6 cm, 9 cm, 12 cm.

> [!TIP]
> When you put two equal edges together there are two ways to place the second triangle: **flip** it over the shared edge (a mirror image), or **turn** it half way round the middle of the shared edge. A flip makes neighbouring sides equal (a kite); a half-turn makes opposite sides equal (a parallelogram).

### Check yourself

**1.** On the geoboard, the two equal rubber bands cross at right angles at their middles. What quadrilateral do you get? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A square.**
> 1. The diagonals are **equal** and **bisect each other**, so the shape is a rectangle: all angles 90° (Deduction 3).
> 2. They also cross at **right angles**, so the four triangles round the middle are congruent (each has two half-diagonals with 90° between them: SAS). So all four sides are equal.
>
> All angles 90° and all sides equal: a square.

**2.** Now one diagonal is stretched by 2 cm at each end. What quadrilateral do you get? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A rhombus (not a square).**
> 1. Both diagonals still cross at their middles: the stretched one grew by the same 2 cm at each end, so its middle has not moved. They still cross at 90°.
> 2. Diagonals that bisect each other at right angles give four congruent right triangles (SAS), so all four sides are equal: a **rhombus**.
> 3. The diagonals are now unequal (8 and 4 on our board), so it is not a rectangle, and so not a square. You can see the corners are no longer right angles.

**3.** Join two equilateral triangles with 8 cm sides. What quadrilateral do you get? Justify.

![Two equilateral triangles of side 8 cm joined along one edge, making a four-sided shape with every side 8 cm](assets/img/class8/math/4/join_equilateral.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **A rhombus.** The shared edge disappears inside, and the four outer edges are all sides of equilateral triangles, so all four are **8 cm**. Four equal sides make a rhombus. Its angles are 60° (one triangle's corner) at two ends and 60° + 60° = **120°** at the other two, so it is **not** a square. (However you join two equilateral triangles, you get this same rhombus, because all their edges and angles are alike.)

**4.** Join two isosceles triangles with sides 8 cm, 8 cm, 6 cm. In what ways can they be joined, and what quadrilaterals do you get? Justify.

![Three ways to join two 8-8-6 triangles: along the 6 cm side gives a rhombus; along an 8 cm side, flipped, gives a kite; along an 8 cm side, half-turned, gives a parallelogram](assets/img/class8/math/4/join_isosceles.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> There are **three** different quadrilaterals.
> 1. **Along the 6 cm sides:** the outside edges are 8, 8, 8, 8 cm, so it is a **rhombus**. (Flipping or turning gives the same shape here, because the triangle is symmetric about the middle of its 6 cm side.)
> 2. **Along 8 cm sides, flipped:** going round, the outside edges are 6, 8, 8, 6 cm. Two pairs of **neighbouring** sides are equal, so it is a **kite** (see 4.7).
> 3. **Along 8 cm sides, half-turned:** the outside edges are 6, 8, 6, 8 cm, with equal sides **opposite** each other. The half-turn puts equal angles in alternate positions along the shared edge, so both pairs of opposite sides are parallel: a **parallelogram**.

**5.** Join two scalene triangles with sides 6 cm, 9 cm, 12 cm. In what ways can they be joined, and what quadrilaterals do you get? Justify.

![Six ways to join two 6-9-12 triangles: sharing 12, 9 or 6 cm, either flipped or half-turned; the flips give a kite and two darts, the turns give three different parallelograms](assets/img/class8/math/4/join_scalene.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> The shared edge can be 12, 9 or 6 cm, and each can be **flipped** or **turned**: **six** quadrilaterals.
> - **Turned** (half-turn): opposite sides equal and parallel, so three **parallelograms**: sides 6 and 9 (sharing 12), sides 6 and 12 (sharing 9), sides 9 and 12 (sharing 6).
> - **Flipped along 12 cm:** sides 6, 6, 9, 9 with equal sides next to each other: a **kite**.
> - **Flipped along 9 cm or 6 cm:** also two pairs of equal neighbouring sides (6, 6, 12, 12 and 9, 9, 12, 12), but the shape has a **dent**. Why: the triangle's widest angle (about 104°, between the 6 cm and 9 cm sides) sits at an end of the shared edge, and flipping doubles it to about 208°, more than a straight angle. A kite with a dent is often called a **dart**. It still fits the definition of a kite.

## 4.7 Kites and trapeziums

### Kite

One way of joining the 6-9-12 triangles gave a shape with **two pairs of equal neighbouring sides**. It looks like a kite.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Kite:** a quadrilateral that can be labelled ABCD so that **AB = BC and CD = DA** (two pairs of equal sides next to each other, not overlapping).

![Kite ABCD with AB = BC (single ticks) and CD = DA (double ticks); diagonals BD and AC meet at O](assets/img/class8/math/4/kite.svg)

**Kite property:** the diagonal BD (the one joining the corners where equal sides meet) **bisects ∠ABC and ∠ADC**, and it **bisects the other diagonal AC at right angles**. (The proof is the Check yourself below.)

### Trapezium

What if we ask for only **one** pair of parallel sides?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Trapezium:** a quadrilateral with **at least one pair of parallel opposite sides**.

The parallel sides make co-interior angles with each of the other two sides, so in trapezium PQRS with PQ ∥ SR: **∠S + ∠P = 180° and ∠R + ∠Q = 180°**.

**Example 7.** In trapezium PQRS, PQ ∥ SR, ∠S = 60° and ∠R = 75°. Find ∠P and ∠Q.

![Trapezium PQRS with PQ parallel to SR; 60° at S, 75° at R, and question marks at P and Q](assets/img/class8/math/4/trapezium.svg)

1. PS is a transversal of PQ and SR, so ∠P + ∠S = 180° and ∠P = 180° − 60° = **120°**.
2. QR is a transversal, so ∠Q + ∠R = 180° and ∠Q = 180° − 75° = **105°**.

Check: 60 + 75 + 120 + 105 = 360 ✓.

### Isosceles trapezium

When the two **non-parallel** sides of a trapezium are equal, it is an **isosceles trapezium**. To draw one: draw UV, draw a line parallel to it, then mark X and W on that line so that UX = VW (with a compass from U and from V).

Are the base angles ∠U and ∠V equal? Drop perpendiculars XY and WZ from X and W onto UV.

![Isosceles trapezium UVWX with XW parallel to UV and UX = VW; perpendiculars XY and WZ to UV make a rectangle XWZY in the middle and two triangles UXY and VWZ](assets/img/class8/math/4/iso_trap.svg)

1. XW ∥ UV, so a = 180° − ∠XYZ = 180° − 90° = 90°, and in the same way b = 90° (co-interior angles).
2. So XWZY has four right angles: it is a **rectangle**, and XY = WZ (opposite sides).
3. ∆UXY ≅ ∆VWZ (Check yourself below), so **∠U = ∠V**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In an isosceles trapezium, the angles at the two ends of each parallel side are equal** (∠U = ∠V, and then ∠X = 180° − ∠U = 180° − ∠V = ∠W).

### Check yourself

**1.** In kite ABCD (AB = BC, CD = DA), show that the diagonal BD (i) bisects ∠ABC and ∠ADC, and (ii) bisects AC at right angles.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i)** Compare ∆ABD and ∆CBD:
> - AB = CB and AD = CD (given), and BD is a side of both.
> - So ∆ABD ≅ ∆CBD by **SSS**, and ∠ABD = ∠CBD and ∠ADB = ∠CDB. So BD **bisects ∠ABC and ∠ADC**.
>
> **(ii)** Now compare ∆AOB and ∆COB:
> - AB = CB (given), ∠ABO = ∠CBO (just proved), BO is a side of both.
> - So ∆AOB ≅ ∆COB by **SAS**. Hence **AO = CO** (BD bisects AC), and ∠AOB = ∠COB.
> - ∠AOB and ∠COB lie side by side on the straight line AC, so they add up to 180°; being equal, each is **90°**.

**2.** In the isosceles trapezium, how can we show ∆UXY ≅ ∆VWZ?

> [!ANSWER]
> By **RHS** (right angle, hypotenuse, side):
> - ∠XYU = ∠WZV = 90° (XY and WZ are perpendicular to UV),
> - the hypotenuses are equal: UX = VW (given),
> - XY = WZ (opposite sides of the rectangle XWZY).
>
> So the triangles are congruent, and ∠U = ∠V.

## The family tree

| Shape | Sides | Angles | Diagonals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trapezium** | one pair parallel (at least) | co-interior pairs add to 180° | — |
| **Kite** | two pairs of equal neighbouring sides | one pair of opposite angles equal | one bisects the other at 90° |
| **Parallelogram** | opposite sides parallel and equal | opposite equal, neighbours add to 180° | bisect each other |
| **Rectangle** | parallelogram + | all 90° | bisect each other **and are equal** |
| **Rhombus** | parallelogram + all sides equal | opposite equal | bisect each other **at 90°**, bisect the angles |
| **Square** | rectangle **and** rhombus | all 90° | equal, bisect each other at 90°, bisect the angles |

Each shape lower down has **every** property of the shapes it belongs to. When you are asked "is every X a Y?", check whether **every** X satisfies the **definition** of Y.

### 1. Find all the sides and angles of the quadrilateral made by joining two equilateral triangles with sides 4 cm.

> [!ANSWER]

>
> 1. **Sides:** the shared edge is hidden inside; the four outer edges are sides of equilateral triangles, so **all four sides are 4 cm**. It is a **rhombus**.
> 2. **Angles:** each corner of an equilateral triangle is 60° (three equal angles making 180°). The two corners that belong to only one triangle are **60°** each. The two corners where both triangles meet are 60° + 60° = **120°** each.
>
> So: sides 4, 4, 4, 4 cm; angles 60°, 120°, 60°, 120° (check: 360° ✓).

### 2. Construct a kite whose diagonals are 6 cm and 8 cm long.

> [!ANSWER]
> In a kite, one diagonal (the "spine") **bisects the other at right angles** (kite property). It need not be bisected itself.
> 1. Draw PQ = 6 cm.
> 2. Construct its perpendicular bisector: with the compass open more than 3 cm (we used 5 cm), draw arcs from P and from Q above and below PQ, and join the crossing points. It meets PQ at its midpoint T.
> 3. On this line mark R, **2 cm** below T, and S, **6 cm** above T, so RS = 8 cm.
> 4. Join P, R, Q, S.
>
> ![Answer: PQ = 6 cm with its perpendicular bisector through T; R 2 cm below T and S 6 cm above T; PRQS is a kite](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex2_a.svg)
>
> **Why it is a kite:** ∆PTR and ∆QTR have PT = QT, a right angle at T and TR shared, so they are congruent (SAS) and **RP = RQ**. In the same way **SP = SQ**. Two pairs of equal neighbouring sides: a kite. (Any split of RS other than 4 + 4 works; 4 + 4 gives a rhombus, which is a special kite.)

### 3. Find the remaining angles in these trapeziums.

![Two trapeziums: (i) PQRS with PQ parallel to SR, 135° at S and 105° at R; (ii) ABCD with AB parallel to DC, AD = BC, and 100° at A](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex3_q.svg)

**(i)** PQRS with PQ ∥ SR, ∠S = 135°, ∠R = 105°.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. PS cuts the parallel sides, so ∠P and ∠S are co-interior: ∠P = 180° − 135° = **45°**.
> 2. QR cuts them too: ∠Q = 180° − 105° = **75°**.
>
> Check: 135 + 105 + 45 + 75 = 360 ✓.
>
> ![Answer: the trapeziums with all angles marked: (i) 45° and 75°; (ii) 100°, 100°, 80°, 80°](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex3_a.svg)

**(ii)** ABCD with AB ∥ DC and AD = BC (ticks), ∠A = 100°.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. AD = BC, so this is an **isosceles trapezium**: the angles at the two ends of a parallel side are equal. So ∠B = ∠A = **100°**.
> 2. AD cuts the parallel sides AB and DC, so ∠D = 180° − 100° = **80°**. In the same way ∠C = 180° − 100° = **80°**.
>
> Check: 100 + 100 + 80 + 80 = 360 ✓.

### 4. Draw a Venn diagram showing the sets of parallelograms, kites, rhombuses, rectangles and squares. Then answer the questions.

**(a)** The Venn diagram.

> [!ANSWER]
> ![Answer: Venn diagram: Parallelograms and Kites overlap; their overlap is Rhombuses; Rectangles lie inside Parallelograms and overlap the Rhombuses; the shared part is Squares](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex4_a.svg)
>
> How it is built:
> 1. Rectangles and rhombuses are both inside **parallelograms**.
> 2. Every **rhombus** is a kite (all four sides equal, so certainly AB = BC and CD = DA), so rhombuses are also inside **kites**.
> 3. **Squares** are both rectangles and rhombuses, so they sit where those two overlap.

**(i)** Which quadrilateral is both a kite and a parallelogram?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A rhombus** (and so also a square, which is a special rhombus).
> **Why:** a kite has AB = BC and CD = DA. A parallelogram has opposite sides equal: AB = CD and BC = DA. Put these together: AB = BC = CD = DA, all four sides equal. So it is a rhombus. And every rhombus is both a kite and a parallelogram.

**(ii)** Can a quadrilateral be both a kite and a rectangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, but only a square.** A rectangle is a parallelogram, so by part (i) a kite that is also a rectangle must be a rhombus with right angles, which is a square. And a square really is a kite (AB = BC and CD = DA) and a rectangle. Any rectangle that is not a square has unequal neighbouring sides, so it cannot be a kite.

**(iii)** Is every kite a rhombus? If not, what is the right relationship?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The kite in end question 2 has sides 3.6, 3.6, 6.7, 6.7 cm (about), which are not all equal. The right relationship is the other way round: **every rhombus is a kite, but not every kite is a rhombus.** The set of rhombuses lies inside the set of kites.

### 5. PAIR and RODS are two rectangles. PR = RS = 5 cm, O lies on AI, and ∠ORI = 30°. Find ∠IOD.

![Rectangle PAIR with PR = 5 cm and rectangle RODS with RS = 5 cm, sharing corner R; O lies on side AI; angle ORI = 30°; angle IOD is asked](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex5_q.svg)

> [!ANSWER]

> 2. **At O:** ∠ROD = 90°, because it is a corner of rectangle RODS.
> 3. The ray OI lies between OR and OD, so ∠ROD = ∠ROI + ∠IOD, giving ∠IOD = 90° − 60° = **30°**.
>
> ![Answer: ∠ROI = 60°, the right angle ROD, and ∠IOD = 30°](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex5_a.svg)

### 6. Construct a square whose diagonal is 6 cm, without using a protractor.

> [!ANSWER]
> We need the diagonals to be equal (6 cm each) and to **bisect each other at right angles**. A compass gives the right angle.
> 1. Draw AB = 6 cm.
> 2. With the compass open more than 3 cm (we used 4 cm), draw arcs from A and from B, above and below AB. Join the two crossing points: this is the **perpendicular bisector** of AB. It cuts AB at its midpoint O at 90°.
> 3. With the compass at O, opened to 3 cm, mark C and D on this line, one above and one below.
> 4. Join A, D, B, C.
>
> ![Answer: AB = 6 cm with its perpendicular bisector; C and D 3 cm from O; ADBC is a square](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex6_a.svg)
>
> **Why the bisector is at 90°:** each crossing point of the arcs is the same distance from A as from B; joining A and B to the two crossing points gives a rhombus, and a rhombus's diagonals bisect each other at right angles. **Why ADBC is a square:** its diagonals AB and CD are both 6 cm, cross at their midpoints, and are perpendicular.

### 7. CASE is a square, and U, V, W, X are the midpoints of its sides.

![(a) Square CASE with the midpoints U, X, W, V of its sides joined; (b) a square drawn tilted inside another square, its corners on the outer square's sides](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex7_q.svg)

**(a)** What kind of quadrilateral is UVWX? Show it by reasoning, then check by drawing and measuring.

> [!ANSWER]
> **UVWX is a square.**
> 1. **Sides.** Each corner of CASE cuts off a triangle, for instance ∆CUV. Each has two legs equal to **half** the side of the square and a 90° angle between them. So all four corner triangles are congruent (SAS), and **UV = UX = XW = WV**.
> 2. **Angles.** In ∆CUV, CU = CV, so its two other angles are equal, and they add to 90°: each is **45°**. The same in ∆AUX.
> 3. At U, the three angles ∠CUV, ∠VUX and ∠XUA make a straight line: 45° + ∠VUX + 45° = 180°, so **∠VUX = 90°**. The same at V, W and X.
>
> Four equal sides and four right angles: a **square**. If CASE has side 6 cm, measuring gives sides of about 4.2 cm and right-angled corners.
>
> ![Answer: (a) the corner triangles with 45° angles and right angles at U, V, W, X; (b) four congruent corner triangles with legs t and s − t, and right angles at the inner corners](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex7_a.svg)

**(b)** Find other ways to draw a square inside a square with its corners on the sides of the outer square, as in figure (b).

> [!ANSWER]
> **Choose any distance t, smaller than the side s, and mark a point t from each corner, always going the same way round** (say anticlockwise). Join the four points.
>
> **Why it is a square:**
> 1. Each corner of the big square cuts off a right triangle with legs **t** and **s − t**. All four are congruent (SAS), so the four inner sides are equal.
> 2. At each inner corner, the two triangles' acute angles sit on a straight line together with the inner angle. The two acute angles of a right triangle add up to 90° (since 180° − 90° = 90°), so the inner angle is 180° − 90° = **90°**.
>
> The midpoint square of (a) is the case t = s/2. Every other t gives a tilted square; as t gets close to 0 the inner square almost fills the outer one.

### 8. A quadrilateral has four equal sides and one angle of 90°. Must it be a square? Reason it out, then check by drawing.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
> 1. Four equal sides make it a **rhombus**, and every rhombus is a parallelogram.
> 2. In a parallelogram, neighbouring angles add to 180°. So the two neighbours of the 90° angle are 180° − 90° = **90°**.
> 3. The opposite angle equals the 90° angle (opposite angles are equal).
>
> All four angles are 90° and all four sides are equal, so it is a **square**. (Draw it: two equal sides at 90°, then compass arcs as in 4.5. The fourth corner always lands at a right angle.)

### 9. What kind of quadrilateral has both pairs of opposite sides equal? Justify. (Hint: draw a diagonal.)

> [!ANSWER]
> **A parallelogram.**
>
> ![Answer: quadrilateral ABCD with AB = CD and BC = AD ticked; diagonal AC makes angles 1 and 2 at A, 3 and 4 at C](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex9_a.svg)
>
> 1. Draw diagonal AC. In ∆ABC and ∆CDA: AB = CD, BC = DA (given) and AC is shared, so **∆ABC ≅ ∆CDA** by SSS.
> 2. So ∠1 = ∠4 (∠BAC = ∠DCA) and ∠3 = ∠2 (∠BCA = ∠DAC).
> 3. ∠1 and ∠4 are **alternate angles** for AB and CD with transversal AC, and they are equal, so **AB ∥ CD**.
> 4. ∠2 and ∠3 are alternate angles for AD and BC, so **AD ∥ BC**.
>
> Both pairs of opposite sides are parallel: a parallelogram.

### 10. Do the angles of a quadrilateral with a dent, like ABCD below, also add up to 360°? Reason it out, then check by drawing and measuring.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** The trick is to choose the diagonal that lies **inside** the shape: BD (the diagonal AC lies outside and is no help).
>
> ![Answer: the dented quadrilateral ABCD split by BD into triangles ABD and CBD, with angles 1 and 2 at B, 3 at A, 4 at C, 5 and 6 at D](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex10_a.svg)
>
> 1. In ∆ABD: ∠1 + ∠3 + ∠5 = 180°. In ∆CBD: ∠2 + ∠4 + ∠6 = 180°.
> 2. Adding: all six angles make **360°**.
> 3. The angle at B is ∠1 + ∠2, at A it is ∠3, at C it is ∠4, and the inside angle at D (the big one, more than 180°) is ∠5 + ∠6.
>
> So the four angles add up to 360°. **Measuring** our drawing gives about 32°, 53°, 32° and 242°, which add to 359°: a degree off, because a protractor reading is never perfect.

### 11. True or false? Justify each answer.

**(i)** A quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other must be a square.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.** Equal diagonals that bisect each other give a **rectangle**, and most rectangles are not squares: for instance a 6 cm by 3.5 cm rectangle. For a square the diagonals must also meet at 90°.
>
> ![Answer: counter-examples: (i) a long rectangle with equal bisecting diagonals; (iv) a kite with perpendicular diagonals; (vii) an isosceles trapezium](assets/img/class8/math/4/ex11_a.svg)

**(ii)** A quadrilateral with three right angles must be a rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.** The angles add to 360°, so the fourth angle is 360° − 3 × 90° = 90°. All four angles are 90°, which is the definition of a rectangle.

**(iii)** A quadrilateral whose diagonals bisect each other must be a parallelogram.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.** If the diagonals AC and BD bisect each other at O, then ∆AOB ≅ ∆COD (SAS: OA = OC, OB = OD, vertically opposite angles at O). So ∠OAB = ∠OCD, which are alternate angles, giving AB ∥ CD. In the same way ∆AOD ≅ ∆COB gives AD ∥ BC.

**(iv)** A quadrilateral whose diagonals are perpendicular must be a rhombus.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.** A **kite** that is not a rhombus (sides 3, 3, 5, 5 cm, say) has perpendicular diagonals. For a rhombus the diagonals must also **bisect each other**; in a kite only one of them is cut in half.

**(v)** A quadrilateral whose opposite angles are equal must be a parallelogram.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.** Say ∠A = ∠C = p and ∠B = ∠D = q.
> 1. The angles add to 360°: 2p + 2q = 360°, so **p + q = 180°**.
> 2. ∠A + ∠D = p + q = 180°. These are co-interior angles for AB and DC with transversal AD, so AB ∥ DC.
> 3. ∠A + ∠B = 180° in the same way gives AD ∥ BC.
>
> Both pairs of opposite sides are parallel: a parallelogram.

**(vi)** A quadrilateral in which all the angles are equal is a rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.** Four equal angles adding to 360° are each 360° ÷ 4 = **90°**, and a quadrilateral with all angles 90° is a rectangle.

**(vii)** Isosceles trapeziums are parallelograms.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.** An isosceles trapezium such as the one in the figure (one pair of parallel sides, the other two sides equal but leaning towards each other) has only **one** pair of parallel sides. A parallelogram needs both pairs.

### 12. Paper-folding puzzle: which quad?

Fold a sheet in half, then in half again, so it is a quarter of its size. At the corner that was the **middle** of the sheet, fold over a small triangle and crease it. Open the sheet.

**(a)** What shape do the creases make?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A rhombus.**
>
> ![Answer: three opened sheets with dashed fold lines: one crease makes a rhombus centred on the middle; several parallel creases make nested rhombuses; equal cuts on both folds make a square](assets/img/class8/math/4/which_quad.svg)
>
> **Why:** the triangle's crease cuts the two folds at some distances a and b from the middle of the sheet. When you open the sheet, that one crease is copied in both folds (folding is a mirror), so you get four creases joining points a to the left and right of the middle with points b above and below. These four points are the ends of two **diagonals that lie along the folds**: they are **perpendicular** (the folds cross at 90°) and **bisect each other** (each is copied evenly to both sides). Diagonals that bisect each other at right angles give a rhombus.

**(b)** How would you fold the quarter-sheet to get several rhombuses one inside another?

> [!ANSWER]
> Make **several creases at the middle corner, each parallel to the first**, at different distances from the corner (fold a little, crease, unfold; fold a bit more, crease, unfold, keeping the same slant). Each crease opens into a rhombus. Because the creases are parallel, every rhombus has the **same angles**, just a different size, so they sit one inside another.

**(c)** How would you fold the quarter-sheet so that the creases make a square?

> [!ANSWER]
> Make the crease cut **both folded edges at the same distance** from the corner (a = b), which means folding the corner over at 45°. Then the two diagonals are **equal** as well as perpendicular and bisecting each other, and equal diagonals that bisect each other at 90° give a **square**.

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Angle sum:** the angles of every quadrilateral add up to **360°** (a diagonal splits it into two triangles).
> - **Rectangle:** all angles 90°. Then: opposite sides equal and parallel; diagonals **equal** and **bisect each other**. Equal diagonals that bisect each other always give a rectangle.
> - **Square:** all angles 90° and all sides equal. Diagonals equal, bisect each other **at 90°**, and cut each corner into 45° + 45°.
> - **Parallelogram:** both pairs of opposite sides parallel. Then: opposite sides equal; opposite angles equal; neighbouring angles add to 180°; diagonals bisect each other. Diagonals that bisect each other always give a parallelogram.
> - **Rhombus:** all sides equal. It is a parallelogram, and its diagonals meet at **90°** and bisect its angles.
> - **Kite:** two pairs of equal neighbouring sides. Its main diagonal bisects the other one at 90° and bisects two corner angles.
> - **Trapezium:** at least one pair of parallel sides; each pair of co-interior angles adds to 180°. In an **isosceles** trapezium the angles at the ends of a parallel side are equal.
> - **Families:** square ⊂ rectangle ⊂ parallelogram; square ⊂ rhombus ⊂ parallelogram; rhombus ⊂ kite. Square = rectangle ∩ rhombus.
> - **The method:** draw a diagonal → find two triangles → prove them congruent (SSS, SAS, ASA/AAS, RHS) → read off equal parts.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** a square **is** a rectangle and a rhombus (not "a different shape") • the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other but are **not** usually equal • perpendicular diagonals alone do **not** make a rhombus (a kite has them too) • equal, bisecting diagonals make a rectangle, **not** necessarily a square • write congruent triangles with the **matching corners in the same order** (∆ABD ≅ ∆CDB, not ∆CBD) • in a dented quadrilateral, use the diagonal that lies **inside** • a trapezium needs only **one** pair of parallel sides.
