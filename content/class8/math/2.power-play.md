## What this chapter is about

If you could fold a sheet of paper again and again, how thick would it get? After 46 folds it would reach **past the Moon**. That surprising answer comes from **repeated multiplication**, and this chapter is about the shorthand for it, **powers** like 2⁴⁶.

- How do we write and read numbers like 2⁴⁶ or 10⁻³, and **why** do the rules for them work?
- How many passwords can a lock have? How many outfits can you make?
- How do we write **huge** numbers (the mass of the Earth) and **tiny** ones without counting zeros?
- How do we **estimate** things nobody has measured, like the number of coins equal to your weight?

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** In 5⁴, **5 is the base** and **4 is the exponent** (or **power**). **Exponential growth** means growing by **multiplying** by the same number again and again. **Linear growth** means growing by **adding** the same amount again and again.

## 2.1 Folding paper: how fast doubling grows

**Try this.** Take the largest sheet of paper you can find. Fold it in half, then in half again, and again. How many times can you fold it? Try a newspaper sheet and a tissue too. Most people can't get past 7 or 8 folds, because the paper gets thick and small very quickly.

Now imagine you **could** fold as often as you like. Suppose the sheet is **0.001 cm** thick. Each fold puts one half on top of the other, so the thickness **doubles**.

![Paper after 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 folds: 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 layers, which is 2⁰, 2¹, 2², 2³ and 2⁴](assets/img/class8/math/2/fold_layers.svg)

| Fold | Thickness | Fold | Thickness | Fold | Thickness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.002 cm | 7 | 0.128 cm | 13 | 8.192 cm |
| 2 | 0.004 cm | 8 | 0.256 cm | 14 | 16.384 cm |
| 3 | 0.008 cm | 9 | 0.512 cm | 15 | 32.768 cm |
| 4 | 0.016 cm | 10 | 1.024 cm | 16 | 65.536 cm |
| 5 | 0.032 cm | 11 | 2.048 cm | 17 | ≈ 131 cm |
| 6 | 0.064 cm | 12 | 4.096 cm | | |

(The sign **≈** means "is approximately equal to".)

After 10 folds the paper is just over 1 cm thick. After 17 folds it is about 131 cm, as tall as a young child.

### Check yourself

**1.** Guess: how thick would the paper be after 30 folds? After 45 folds?

> [!ANSWER]
> Most people guess a few metres. The real answers are astonishing:
> - **30 folds: about 10.7 km**, the height at which planes fly (the deepest point of the ocean, the Mariana Trench, is about 11 km deep).
> - **45 folds: about 3,52,000 km**, nearly the distance to the Moon (3,84,400 km). One more fold, **46**, takes it past the Moon.
>
> Our guesses are far too small because we think in **adding** steps, but each fold **multiplies** by 2.

**2.** After 18 folds the thickness is about 262 cm, after 19 folds about 524 cm and after 20 folds about 10.4 m. Fill in the thickness after 21 to 26 folds.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each fold doubles the one before:
>
> | Fold | 21 | 22 | 23 | 24 | 25 | 26 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Thickness | ≈ 21 m | ≈ 42 m | ≈ 84 m | ≈ 168 m | ≈ 335 m | ≈ 671 m |
>
> After 26 folds it is about 670 m. The tallest building in the world, the Burj Khalifa in Dubai, is 830 m tall.

**3.** After 27 folds the thickness is about 1.3 km. Fill in 28, 29 and 30 folds.

> [!ANSWER]
> Doubling each time: **28 folds ≈ 2.7 km**, **29 folds ≈ 5.4 km**, **30 folds ≈ 10.7 km**.

**4.** Fill in the thickness after 31 to 45 folds.

> [!ANSWER]
> Keep doubling from 30 folds ≈ 10.7 km:
>
> | Fold | Thickness | Fold | Thickness | Fold | Thickness |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | 31 | ≈ 21 km | 36 | ≈ 687 km | 41 | ≈ 22,000 km |
> | 32 | ≈ 43 km | 37 | ≈ 1,370 km | 42 | ≈ 44,000 km |
> | 33 | ≈ 86 km | 38 | ≈ 2,750 km | 43 | ≈ 88,000 km |
> | 34 | ≈ 172 km | 39 | ≈ 5,500 km | 44 | ≈ 1,76,000 km |
> | 35 | ≈ 344 km | 40 | ≈ 11,000 km | 45 | ≈ 3,52,000 km |
>
> And 46 folds ≈ **7,04,000 km**, more than the Moon's distance.

**5.** Look at the thickness after fold 4 (0.016 cm) and after fold 6 (0.064 cm). By how much does it increase in two folds?

> [!ANSWER]
> 0.064 ÷ 0.016 = **4 times**. Two folds double it twice: × 2 × 2 = × 4. The same is true from any starting fold: 0.512 cm (fold 9) to 2.048 cm (fold 11) is also × 4.

**6.** Check that after **any** 3 folds, the thickness becomes 8 times as much.

> [!ANSWER]
> Three folds double three times: × 2 × 2 × 2 = **× 8**. Checks from the table:
> - fold 4 → fold 7: 0.016 cm → 0.128 cm, and 0.128 ÷ 0.016 = 8 ✓
> - fold 10 → fold 13: 1.024 cm → 8.192 cm, and 8.192 ÷ 1.024 = 8 ✓
>
> It works from **any** fold because each fold multiplies by 2 no matter how thick the paper already is.

### Every 10 folds multiply by 1024

In the same way, **10 folds multiply the thickness by 2 × 2 × … × 2 (ten 2s) = 1024**, wherever you start:

| Folds | Thickness changes from | Times increased |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | 0.001 cm to 1.024 cm | 1.024 ÷ 0.001 = 1024 |
| 10 to 20 | 1.024 cm to about 10.49 m | 1024 |
| 20 to 30 | about 10.49 m to about 10.74 km | 1024 |
| 30 to 40 | about 10.74 km to about 10,995 km | 1024 |

Each block of 10 folds multiplies by about a thousand. That is why the paper goes from 1 cm to the Moon in just 36 more folds.

![The folds 0 to 46 on a line with the thickness at 10, 17, 26, 30, 40 and 46 folds: 1.02 cm, 1.31 m, 671 m, 10.7 km, 10,995 km and 7,03,687 km](assets/img/class8/math/2/fold_ladder.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Doubling again and again is multiplicative growth, also called exponential growth.** Each step multiplies by the same number, so the growth itself keeps growing. In real life paper can't be folded this many times; this is a **thought experiment** that shows how powerful repeated multiplication is.

## 2.2 Exponential notation

The starting thickness is 0.001 cm. After 1 fold it is 0.001 × 2 cm; after 2 folds, 0.001 × 2 × 2; after 7 folds, 0.001 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 0.128 cm. Writing seven 2s is tiring and easy to miscount, so we use a shorthand: **0.001 × 2⁷**.

You already know n × n = n² and n × n × n = n³. The idea continues:

- n × n × n × n = **n⁴**, read "n raised to the power 4" or "the 4th power of n"
- n × n × n × n × n × n × n = **n⁷**, read "n to the power 7"

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **nᵃ means n multiplied by itself a times.** In 5⁴ = 5 × 5 × 5 × 5 = 625, **5 is the base** and **4 is the exponent** (or power). 5⁴ is the **exponential form** of 625. We read it "5 raised to the power 4", "5 to the power 4" or "5 power 4". The numbers 5¹, 5², 5³, 5⁴, … are called the **powers of 5**.

For instance, 2¹⁰ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 1024, the number that came up with every 10 folds.

More examples:

- 4 × 4 × 4 = 4³ = 64, and (−4) × (−4) × (−4) = (−4)³ = −64.
- a × a × a × b × b = **a³b²** (read "a cubed b squared").
- a × a × b × b × b × b = **a²b⁴**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Don't mix up adding and multiplying.** 4 + 4 + 4 = 3 × 4 = **12**, but 4 × 4 × 4 = 4³ = **64**. Repeated **addition** is multiplication; repeated **multiplication** is a power.

### Check yourself

**1.** The starting thickness of a sheet of paper is v. Which expression gives its thickness after 10 folds? (i) 10v (ii) 10 + v (iii) 2 × 10 × v (iv) 2¹⁰ (v) 2¹⁰v (vi) 10²v

> [!ANSWER]
> Each fold multiplies the thickness by 2, so 10 folds multiply v by ten 2s: v × 2 × 2 × … × 2 = **2¹⁰v**, option **(v)**.
> - 10v and 2 × 10 × v would be right only if each fold *added* the same amount.
> - 2¹⁰ alone forgets the starting thickness v.
> - 10²v multiplies by 10 twice, not by 2 ten times.

### Prime factorisation in exponential form

**Example 1: Write 32400 as a product of powers of primes.** Divide by primes again and again:

```
2 | 32400
2 | 16200
2 |  8100
2 |  4050
5 |  2025
5 |   405
3 |    81
3 |    27
3 |     9
3 |     3
  |     1
```

So 32400 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = **2⁴ × 3⁴ × 5²**. Each prime is written once, with its count as the exponent.

### Signs and zero

- A pair of negatives multiplies to a positive: (−2) × (−2) = 4.
- So an **even** power of a negative number is positive (every negative has a partner), and an **odd** power is negative (one negative is left over).

### Check yourself

**1.** What is (−1)⁵? Is it positive or negative? What about (−1)⁵⁶?

> [!ANSWER]
> - (−1)⁵ = (−1) × (−1) × (−1) × (−1) × (−1). Two pairs make +1 × +1, and one −1 is left over: **(−1)⁵ = −1**, negative.
> - (−1)⁵⁶: 56 is even, so all 56 factors pair up into 28 pairs of +1: **(−1)⁵⁶ = +1**, positive.

**2.** Is (−2)⁴ = 16? Check.

> [!ANSWER]
> (−2)⁴ = (−2) × (−2) × (−2) × (−2) = 4 × 4 = **16**. **Yes.** The exponent 4 is even, so the answer is positive. (But −2⁴ without brackets means −(2⁴) = −16. The brackets matter.)

**3.** What are 0², 0⁵ and 0ⁿ?

> [!ANSWER]
> 0² = 0 × 0 = **0**, 0⁵ = 0 × 0 × 0 × 0 × 0 = **0**, and **0ⁿ = 0** for every counting number n, because anything multiplied by 0 is 0.

## 2.3 Multiplying powers: the stones that shine

Here is a counting riddle. A king has **3 daughters**. Each daughter gets **3 baskets**. Each basket holds **3 silver keys**. Each key opens **3 rooms**. Each room has **3 tables**, each table has **3 necklaces**, and each necklace has **3 diamonds**. How many diamonds are there?

Draw it as a tree: every item splits into 3 at the next level.

![A tree from the king: 3 daughters, 9 baskets, 27 keys, 81 rooms; each level is 3 times the one above](assets/img/class8/math/2/stones_tree.svg)

### Check yourself

**1.** How many rooms are there altogether?

> [!ANSWER]
> Daughters 3, baskets 3 × 3 = 9, keys 9 × 3 = 27, rooms 27 × 3 = **81**. That is four 3s multiplied: **3⁴ = 81**.

**2.** How many diamonds are there? Can you find them with just **one** multiplication, using the numbers you already have?

> [!ANSWER]
> Diamonds are seven levels down (daughters, baskets, keys, rooms, tables, necklaces, diamonds), so there are **3⁷** of them.
>
> We already know 3⁴ = 81 (the rooms). From rooms to diamonds is three more levels (tables, necklaces, diamonds), which is 3³ = 27. So diamonds = 81 × 27 = **2187**: one multiplication.
>
> 3⁷ = (3 × 3 × 3 × 3) × (3 × 3 × 3) = 3⁴ × 3³.

**3.** 3⁷ can also be written as 3² × 3⁵. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> 3⁷ is seven 3s in a row. Put a bracket round the first two and another round the last five:
> 3⁷ = (3 × 3) × (3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3) = **3² × 3⁵**. Any split of the seven 3s works: 3¹ × 3⁶, 3³ × 3⁴, …, as long as the two exponents add up to 7.

### The first law: same base, multiply → add the exponents

**Example 2:** p⁴ × p⁶ = (p × p × p × p) × (p × p × p × p × p × p) = **p¹⁰**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ** (a and b counting numbers).
> **Why it works:** nᵃ is a copies of n multiplied, nᵇ is b more copies. Multiplied together, that is simply a + b copies of n in one long row.

> [!TIP]
> This lets you build big powers from small ones you know. 2¹⁰ = 2⁵ × 2⁵ = 32 × 32 = 1024.

### Check yourself

**Question 1.** Use the rule nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ to find the value of each.

**(i)** 2⁹

> [!ANSWER]
> Split 9 = 4 + 5: 2⁹ = 2⁴ × 2⁵ = 16 × 32 = **512**.

**(ii)** 5⁷

> [!ANSWER]
> Split 7 = 3 + 4: 5⁷ = 5³ × 5⁴ = 125 × 625 = **78125**.

**(iii)** 4⁶

> [!ANSWER]
> Split 6 = 3 + 3: 4⁶ = 4³ × 4³ = 64 × 64 = **4096**.

## 2.4 A power of a power

**Example 3: Find 4⁶ in two ways.** Group the six 4s in two ways:

- (4 × 4 × 4) × (4 × 4 × 4) = 4³ × 4³ = 64 × 64 = 4096. That is the **square** of 4³, written **(4³)²**.
- (4 × 4) × (4 × 4) × (4 × 4) = 4² × 4² × 4² = 16 × 16 × 16 = 4096. That is the **cube** of 4², written **(4²)³**.

In the same way 7⁴ = (7 × 7) × (7 × 7) = (7²)², and 2¹⁰ can be grouped as five pairs, (2²)⁵, or as two groups of five, (2⁵)².

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **(nᵃ)ᵇ = (nᵇ)ᵃ = nᵃˣᵇ** (a and b counting numbers).
> **Why it works:** (nᵃ)ᵇ means b groups with a copies of n in each, which is a × b copies altogether. Picture them in a grid of b rows and a columns: counting by rows or by columns gives the same total, so (nᵇ)ᵃ is the same.

### Check yourself

**Question 1.** Write each as a power of a power in at least two different ways.

**(i)** 8⁶

> [!ANSWER]
> 6 = 2 × 3, so 8⁶ = **(8²)³** = **(8³)²**. Also 8 = 2³, so 8⁶ = (2³)⁶ = **2¹⁸**, which can be written (2⁶)³ or (2⁹)² too.

**(ii)** 7¹⁵

> [!ANSWER]
> 15 = 3 × 5, so 7¹⁵ = **(7³)⁵** = **(7⁵)³**.

**(iii)** 9¹⁴

> [!ANSWER]
> 14 = 2 × 7, so 9¹⁴ = **(9²)⁷** = **(9⁷)²**. Also 9 = 3², so 9¹⁴ = (3²)¹⁴ = **3²⁸**.

**(iv)** 5⁸

> [!ANSWER]
> 8 = 2 × 4, so 5⁸ = **(5²)⁴** = **(5⁴)²**. Also 8 = 2 × 2 × 2, so 5⁸ = ((5²)²)².

## 2.5 Same exponent, different bases: the magical pond

In a magical pond there is one pink lotus. **The number of lotuses doubles every day.** After 30 days, the pond is completely covered.

![The pond on days 26 to 30: 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2 and fully covered](assets/img/class8/math/2/pond.svg)

### Check yourself

**1.** On which day was the pond **half** covered?

> [!ANSWER]
> On **day 29**. The lotuses double every day, so whatever covered the pond on day 30 was half as many on day 29. (Many people say "day 15", thinking of half the time, but doubling growth does most of its growing at the very end.)

**Question 2.** Write the number of lotuses, in exponential form, when the pond was:

**(i)** fully covered

> [!ANSWER]
> Starting from 1 lotus, after 30 doublings there are 1 × 2 × 2 × … × 2 (thirty 2s) = **2³⁰** lotuses (that is 1,07,37,41,824).

**(ii)** half covered

> [!ANSWER]
> One day earlier: **2²⁹** lotuses, and 2²⁹ × 2 = 2³⁰ ✓.

### Two ponds

Another pond **triples** its lotuses every day. Damayanti puts 1 lotus in the doubling pond. After 4 days she moves all of them to the tripling pond. How many lotuses are there 4 days later?

- After the first 4 days: 1 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 2⁴ = 16 lotuses.
- After 4 more days in the tripling pond: 2⁴ × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = **2⁴ × 3⁴** = 16 × 81 = 1296.

Now regroup: 2⁴ × 3⁴ = (2 × 2 × 2 × 2) × (3 × 3 × 3 × 3) = (2 × 3) × (2 × 3) × (2 × 3) × (2 × 3) = **6⁴** = 1296.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **mᵃ × nᵃ = (m × n)ᵃ**, and **mᵃ ÷ nᵃ = (m ÷ n)ᵃ** (n ≠ 0).
> **Why it works:** there are a copies of m and a copies of n. Pair each m with an n and you get a copies of (m × n). For division, the a copies of m on top and a copies of n below pair up into a fractions m/n.

### Check yourself

**1.** What if Damayanti had used the ponds the other way round (tripling pond first, then doubling)? How many lotuses would there be?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1 × 3⁴ = 81 lotuses after 4 days, then 81 × 2⁴ = 81 × 16 = **1296**, the **same**. Multiplication can be done in any order, so 3⁴ × 2⁴ = 2⁴ × 3⁴ = 6⁴.

**2.** Use the rule to find 2⁵ × 5⁵.

> [!ANSWER]
> Same exponent, so multiply the bases: 2⁵ × 5⁵ = (2 × 5)⁵ = 10⁵ = **1,00,000**. Much easier than 32 × 3125!

**3.** Simplify 10⁴ ÷ 5⁴ and write it in exponential form.

> [!ANSWER]
> Same exponent, so divide the bases: 10⁴ ÷ 5⁴ = (10 ÷ 5)⁴ = **2⁴** = 16. Check: 10000 ÷ 625 = 16 ✓

## 2.6 How many combinations?

Estu has **4 dresses and 3 caps**. How many different outfits (one dress and one cap) can he make?

![Lines join each of 3 caps to each of 4 dresses: 12 lines, so 12 outfits](assets/img/class8/math/2/outfits.svg)

**Example 4: Estu's outfits.** For each cap he can choose any of the 4 dresses: 4 + 4 + 4 = 4 × 3 = **12** outfits. Or, for each dress any of the 3 caps: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 3 × 4 = 12. Same answer.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **When you make several choices one after another, multiply the numbers of options.**
> **Why:** each option at the first step can be followed by every option at the second step, so the possibilities branch like a tree, and the number of branches multiplies at each step.

**Try this.** Count your own outfits: how many tops, bottoms and pairs of footwear do you have? Multiply to find how many different outfits you could wear before repeating one.

### Check yourself

**1.** Roxie has 7 dresses, 2 hats and 3 pairs of shoes. In how many different ways can she dress up?

> [!ANSWER]
> Choose a dress (7 ways), then a hat (2 ways for each dress), then shoes (3 ways for each of those): 7 × 2 × 3 = **42** ways.

### The 5-digit lock

Estu and Roxie find an old safe with a **5-digit** lock. Nobody knows the code, so they try every code, and it opens only on the very last one. How many codes did they try?

> [!TIP]
> **If a problem is too big, solve a smaller version first.** Then see how the answer grows.

- **2-digit lock:** 10 choices for the first digit (0 to 9), and for each of them 10 for the second: 10 × 10 = **100** codes (00 to 99).
- **3-digit lock:** each of those 100 codes can be followed by any of 10 digits: 100 × 10 = **1000** codes (000 to 999).
- **5-digit lock:** 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = **10⁵ = 1,00,000** codes, the same as the numbers 00000 to 99999.

![A lock with 5 dials of digits 0 to 9: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10⁵ = 1,00,000 passwords](assets/img/class8/math/2/lock_dials.svg)

### Check yourself

**1.** Estu wants a lock with **6 slots**, each showing a letter A to Z. How many passwords are possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> 26 choices for each of the 6 slots: 26 × 26 × 26 × 26 × 26 × 26 = **26⁶ = 30,89,15,776** passwords. That is more than 3000 times as many as the 5-digit lock, so it is much safer to guess against.

**2.** Try this: think about how many different codes are possible for (i) PIN codes of places in India (for instance 464001 for Vidisha and 796017 for Zemabawk), (ii) mobile numbers, (iii) vehicle registration numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> Multiply the choices for each position:
> - **(i) PIN codes** have 6 digits. The first digit is never 0 (it tells the region, 1 to 9), so there are at most 9 × 10⁵ = **9,00,000** PIN codes. India uses only about 19,000 of them, so there is lots of room.
> - **(ii) Indian mobile numbers** have 10 digits and start with 6, 7, 8 or 9: 4 × 10⁹ = **4 arab** (4 billion) possible numbers, enough for every Indian to have a few.
> - **(iii) A vehicle number** like MH 12 AB 1234 has a fixed state and district, then 2 letters and 4 digits: 26 × 26 × 10⁴ = **67,60,000** numbers per district. When they run out, a district adds a third letter.
>
> Finding out how each code is allotted is a good project: ask at a post office, look at a phone bill, or read a number plate.

## 2.7 The other side of powers: dividing

**Example 5: Halving a line.** A line is 16 units long. 16 = 2⁴.

![A line of 16 units halved again and again: 2⁴ = 16, 2³ = 8, 2² = 4, 2¹ = 2, 2⁰ = 1, and 2⁻¹ = 1/2 unit](assets/img/class8/math/2/halving.svg)

- Erase half: 2⁴ ÷ 2 = (2 × 2 × 2 × 2) ÷ 2 = 2 × 2 × 2 = **2³ = 8** units.
- Erase half again: 2⁴ ÷ 2² = (2 × 2 × 2 × 2) ÷ (2 × 2) = 2 × 2 = **2² = 4** units.
- Halving 3 times: 2⁴ ÷ 2³ = (2 × 2 × 2 × 2) ÷ (2 × 2 × 2) = **2¹ = 2** units.

So 2⁴ ÷ 2³ = 2⁴⁻³ = 2¹.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **nᵃ ÷ nᵇ = nᵃ⁻ᵇ** (n ≠ 0).
> **Why it works:** write nᵃ ÷ nᵇ as a fraction with a copies of n on top and b copies below. Each n below cancels one n on top, leaving a − b copies on top.

### Check yourself

**1.** What is 2¹⁰⁰ ÷ 2²⁵ as a power of 2?

> [!ANSWER]
> Subtract the exponents: 2¹⁰⁰⁻²⁵ = **2⁷⁵**. The 25 twos below cancel 25 of the 100 twos above, leaving 75.

**2.** Why can't n be 0 in the rule nᵃ ÷ nᵇ = nᵃ⁻ᵇ?

> [!ANSWER]
> If n = 0, then nᵇ = 0ᵇ = 0, and we would be **dividing by 0**, which has no meaning. (There is no number that, multiplied by 0, gives 0ᵃ in a definite way: every number works for 0 ÷ 0, so it can't have one answer.)

### When the power is zero

**Example 6: What should 2⁰ mean?** We can't "multiply 2 by itself 0 times". So we **choose** a meaning that keeps the division rule working:

2⁴ ÷ 2⁴ = 16 ÷ 16 = 1, and the rule says 2⁴ ÷ 2⁴ = 2⁴⁻⁴ = 2⁰.

For the rule to stay true, **2⁰ must be 1**. The same argument works for any power and any base: xᵃ ÷ xᵃ = 1 and = x⁰.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **x⁰ = 1** for any x ≠ 0.
> **Why:** any number divided by itself is 1, and the division rule turns xᵃ ÷ xᵃ into x⁰.

### When the power is negative

**Example 7: Halving past 1.** Halve the 16-unit line 5 times: 2⁴ ÷ 2⁵ = (2 × 2 × 2 × 2) ÷ (2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2) = **1/2** unit. The rule says 2⁴ ÷ 2⁵ = 2⁴⁻⁵ = **2⁻¹**. So 2⁻¹ must be 1/2.

Halve it 10 times: 2⁴ ÷ 2¹⁰. Four 2s cancel, leaving 1/(2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2) = 1/2⁶ = 1/64. The rule says this is 2⁴⁻¹⁰ = **2⁻⁶**. So 2⁻⁶ = 1/2⁶.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **n⁻ᵃ = 1/nᵃ** and, the other way round, **nᵃ = 1/n⁻ᵃ** (n ≠ 0).
> For instance 10⁻³ = 1/10³ = 1/1000, 7⁻² = 1/7² = 1/49.
> **Why 1/10⁻³ = 10³:** 1 ÷ 10⁻³ = 1 ÷ (1/10³) = 1 × 10³ = 10³, because dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its flip.

> [!NOTE]
> We didn't pick 2⁰ = 1 and 2⁻¹ = 1/2 at random. They are the **only** meanings that keep nᵃ ÷ nᵇ = nᵃ⁻ᵇ true. With them, **all** the rules (nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ, (nᵃ)ᵇ = nᵃᵇ, nᵃ ÷ nᵇ = nᵃ⁻ᵇ) work for **any integers** a and b, positive, negative or zero.

## 2.10 Scientific notation

Some facts with large numbers:

- The Sun is **30,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,000 m** from the centre of our galaxy, the Milky Way.
- There are about **1,00,00,00,00,000** stars in our galaxy.
- The mass of the Earth is about **59,76,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,000 kg**.

With so many digits it is easy to miscount the zeros, and one zero changes everything: it is the difference between getting ₹5,000 and ₹50,000. Powers of 10 help.

5900 can be written many ways: 590 × 10¹ = 59 × 10² = **5.9 × 10³** = 0.59 × 10⁴. We agree to use the one where the first number is **at least 1 and less than 10**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Scientific notation (standard form):** write a number as **x × 10ʸ**, where **1 ≤ x < 10** and y is an integer. x is the **coefficient** and y the **exponent**.
> For instance 5900 = 5.9 × 10³, 20800 = 2.08 × 10⁴, 80,00,000 = 8 × 10⁶, 308100000 = 3.081 × 10⁸.
> **How to do it:** put the decimal point just after the first non-zero digit, then count how many places the point moved to get there. That count is y.

**Why the exponent matters most.** Mumbai's population, about 2 crore, is 2 × 10⁷. Change the 2 to 3 and the number grows by half (2 crore → 3 crore). Change the 7 to 8 and it becomes **ten times** as big (2 crore → 20 crore). The exponent tells you the size; the first digit fine-tunes it.

**The digits show how sure you are.** Writing the population of Kohima as 1,42,395 suggests you know it to the last person. If you only know it is about 1 lakh 42 thousand, write **1.42 × 10⁵**; if only about 1 lakh 40 thousand, write **1.4 × 10⁵**. Most large numbers we use are estimates, and that is fine.

> [!TIP]
> A museum guide was asked how old a dinosaur skeleton was. "70 million and 15 years," he said. "It was 70 million years old when I started working here 15 years ago!" The joke: 70 million is a **rounded estimate**, so adding 15 years to it is meaningless.

**Try this.** Find three large numbers in a newspaper or a science book (a population, a distance, a budget) and write each in standard form.

**Comparing numbers in standard form.** The Sun to Saturn is 14,33,50,00,00,000 m = 1.4335 × 10¹² m; Saturn to Uranus is 14,39,00,00,00,000 m = 1.439 × 10¹² m; the Sun to the Earth is 1,49,60,00,00,000 m = 1.496 × 10¹¹ m. **Compare the exponents first**; only if they are equal, compare the coefficients.

## 2.11 Did you ever wonder? Estimating big things

In some places people offer goods equal to their own weight, a custom called **Tulābhāra**. It is an old practice, a way of giving thanks that also helps the community. Nanjundappa wants to give jaggery equal to Roxie's weight and wheat equal to Estu's weight. What would they be worth?

**Example 10:** worth of jaggery (₹) = Roxie's weight (kg) × cost of 1 kg of jaggery. We don't know the numbers, so we **assume** sensible ones: Roxie (13 years old) weighs 45 kg and jaggery costs ₹70 a kg, so the jaggery is worth 45 × 70 = **₹3150**. If Estu (11) weighs 50 kg and wheat costs ₹50 a kg, the wheat is worth 50 × 50 = **₹2500**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **How to estimate something nobody told you:**
> 1. **Guess** quickly, without calculating (hundreds? lakhs? crores?).
> 2. Write the **relationship** between the quantities you need.
> 3. Make **reasonable assumptions** for anything you don't know.
> 4. **Calculate**, and see how close your guess was.
>
> Different assumptions give different answers, and that is fine. What matters is a sensible model. Your guesses get better with practice.

### Check yourself

**1.** How many 1-rupee coins would equal Roxie's weight? Guess first (hundreds, thousands, lakhs?), then estimate.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Relationship:** number of coins = Roxie's weight ÷ weight of one coin.
>
> **Assumptions:** Roxie weighs 45 kg = 45,000 g. A 1-rupee coin weighs about 4 g (you can weigh 10 coins on a kitchen scale and divide by 10).
>
> **Calculation:** 45,000 ÷ 4 ≈ **11,000 coins**, so about ₹11,000. The answer is in the **ten-thousands**.

**2.** What if we used 5-rupee coins, or 10-rupee notes? How much money would it be?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Assume** a 5-rupee coin weighs about 6 g and a 10-rupee note about 1 g.
> - 5-rupee coins: 45,000 ÷ 6 = 7,500 coins, worth 7,500 × 5 = about **₹37,500**.
> - 10-rupee notes: 45,000 ÷ 1 = 45,000 notes, worth 45,000 × 10 = about **₹4,50,000**. Notes are so light that your weight in notes is worth a lot!

**3.** When they grow up, Estu wants to donate notebooks worth his weight every year, and Roxie wants to give grain for meals (annadāna) worth her weight every year. How many people might each help in a year? Guess first.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Notebooks:** assume Estu weighs 60 kg as an adult and a notebook weighs about 200 g. 60,000 ÷ 200 = 300 notebooks. If each student needs about 6 notebooks a year, that helps about **50 students**.
>
> **Grain:** assume Roxie weighs 55 kg and one meal uses about 100 g of rice or wheat. 55,000 ÷ 100 = **550 meals**, enough for one meal each for about 550 people (or two meals a day for one person for about 9 months).

**4.** Some pilgrims say, "We walked about 400 km to get here, and arrived early this morning." How long ago did they start?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Assume** people walk about 5 km an hour for about 8 hours a day, so about 40 km a day.
>
> 400 ÷ 40 = **10 days**, so they set out about 10 days ago. (Walking pilgrimages like this, called pādayātra, are made in many faiths in India, such as the Pandharpur Wari or the Sabarimala Yatra.)

**5.** If a person walked non-stop all their life, how many times could they go around the Earth (about 40,000 km)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Assume** a walking speed of 5 km an hour and a life of 70 years, walking day and night.
>
> Distance = 5 × 24 × 365 × 70 = 30,66,000 km. Trips round the Earth = 30,66,000 ÷ 40,000 ≈ **76 times**.

### Linear growth and exponential growth

Roxie reads a story in which people build a **ladder to the Moon**. How many steps would it have? Guess first: thousands, lakhs, crores?

**Example 11:** Assume the steps are 20 cm apart. The Moon is about 3,84,400 km away. 3,84,400 km = 3,84,400 × 1,00,000 cm = 3.844 × 10¹⁰ cm. Number of steps = 3.844 × 10¹⁰ ÷ 20 = **1,92,20,00,000** steps (192 crore 20 lakh, or about 1.9 billion).

Each step adds a fixed 20 cm. This is **linear growth**. To cover the same distance, folding paper needs only **46** folds, because each fold **multiplies** the thickness by 2.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Linear growth is additive** (20 + 20 + 20 + …, 192 crore times). **Exponential growth is multiplicative** (0.001 × 2 × 2 × 2 × …, just 46 times). Given enough steps, multiplying always beats adding.

### Check yourself

**1.** Give some examples of linear growth and of exponential growth.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Linear (the same amount added each time):** saving ₹50 every week; a candle burning 1 cm shorter every hour; walking at a steady speed; a plant that grows 2 cm a week.
>
> **Exponential (multiplied by the same number each time):** the folded paper; the lotus pond; the king's diamonds; a rumour where each person tells 3 new people; bacteria that split into 2 every 20 minutes; the number of possible passwords as a lock gets more dials.

**3.** Estu says, "I am 4070 days old today." How can you find his date of birth?

> [!ANSWER]
> 4070 days ÷ 365 ≈ 11.15 years, so he is 11 years and about 55 days old (minus a couple of days for leap years). Count back 11 years from today and then about 53 more days.
>
> For instance, if today is 28 November 2026: 11 years back is 28 November 2015, which is 4018 days ago (11 × 365 + 3 leap days in 2016, 2020, 2024). We need 4070 − 4018 = 52 more days: back from 28 November is 7 October. So Estu was born on **7 October 2015**. (A calendar or a date calculator confirms this.)

**4.** If you have lived for a million (10⁶) seconds, how old are you?

> [!ANSWER]
> One day is 24 × 60 × 60 = 86,400 seconds. 10,00,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ **11.6 days**: not even two weeks old!

**Question 5.** Think of events or things whose time is of the order of:

**(i)** 10⁵ seconds (about 1.2 days)

> [!ANSWER]
> - One full day and night: 24 hours = 86,400 s ≈ **8.6 × 10⁴ s**, close to 10⁵.
> - A long train journey, say 33 hours from Delhi to Chennai: 33 × 3600 ≈ **1.2 × 10⁵ s**.
> - A weekend holiday of 2 days ≈ **1.7 × 10⁵ s**.

**(ii)** 10⁶ seconds (about 11.6 days)

> [!ANSWER]
> - A two-week school break: 14 days ≈ **1.2 × 10⁶ s**.
> - Full moon to new moon, about 15 days ≈ **1.3 × 10⁶ s**.
> - A hen's egg takes about 21 days to hatch ≈ **1.8 × 10⁶ s**.

**Question 6.** Fill in the seconds (use 1 year ≈ 3.15 × 10⁷ seconds).

**(i)** A fossil of a "terror bird" is about 15 million years old.

> [!ANSWER]
> 15 million years = 1.5 × 10⁷ years. Seconds ≈ 1.5 × 10⁷ × 3.15 × 10⁷ = 4.7 × 10¹⁴, so about **4.7 × 10¹⁴ seconds** (of the order of 10¹⁴, as its row in the table says).

**(ii)** Plants started growing on land about 47 crore (470 million) years ago.

> [!ANSWER]

**Question 7.** Calculate, and write the answer in scientific notation.

**(i)** If you counted one star every second, how long would it take to count all the stars in the universe?

> [!ANSWER]
> About 2 × 10²³ stars at one a second takes **2 × 10²³ seconds**. In years: 2 × 10²³ ÷ (3.15 × 10⁷) ≈ 6 × 10¹⁵ years, hundreds of thousands of times the age of the universe.

**(ii)** If you could drink a 200 ml glass of water every 10 seconds, how long would it take to drink all the water on Earth?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Drops: 2 × 10²⁵, at 16 drops per ml, is (2 × 10²⁵) ÷ 16 = 1.25 × 10²⁴ ml.
> 2. Glasses: (1.25 × 10²⁴) ÷ 200 = 6.25 × 10²¹ glasses.
> 3. Time: 6.25 × 10²¹ × 10 s = **6.25 × 10²² seconds**.

## 2.14 A pinch of history

- In the *Lalitavistara*, a Buddhist text from the first century BCE, the mathematician Arjuna and Prince Gautama discuss names for powers of ten going up by hundreds: a hundred kotis make an ayuta (10⁹), a hundred ayutas a niyuta (10¹¹), and so on up to the **tallakshana (10⁵³)**.
- **Mahaviracharya** listed 24 number names (up to 10²³) in his *Ganita-sara-sangraha*. A Jaina text, the *Amalasiddhi*, named every power of ten up to **10⁹⁶** (dasha-ananta), and a Pali grammar by Kāccāyana went up to **10¹⁴⁰** (asaṅkhyeya).
- These texts built big names from smaller ones, using bases like sahassa (thousand) and koṭi (ten million). We still do: a hundred thousand is a lakh (10² × 10³ = 10⁵), a hundred lakh a crore (10⁷), a hundred crore an arab (10⁹), a hundred arab a kharab (10¹¹), then neel (10¹³), padma (10¹⁵), shankh (10¹⁷) and maha shankh (10¹⁹).
- The international system goes up in thousands: a thousand thousand is a million (10⁶), a thousand million a billion (10⁹), then trillion (10¹²), quadrillion (10¹⁵), quintillion (10¹⁸), sextillion (10²¹), septillion (10²⁴), octillion (10²⁷), nonillion (10³⁰), decillion (10³³).
- **10¹⁰⁰ is called a googol.** The universe has about 10⁷⁸ to 10⁸² atoms, far fewer than a googol. 10 raised to the power googol is a **googolplex**.
- The largest banknote ever printed was a Hungarian note of 1946 worth 1 sextillion (10²¹) pengő; it was never used. In 2009 Zimbabwe printed a 100 trillion (10¹⁴) dollar note, worth only about 30 US dollars at the time.

### Check yourself

**1.** Look at the names million (10⁶), billion (10⁹), trillion (10¹²), quadrillion (10¹⁵), quintillion (10¹⁸), … What does the first part of each name stand for?

> [!ANSWER]
> The first part is a **counting word** from Latin: **bi** = 2, **tri** = 3, **quadr** = 4, **quint** = 5, **sext** = 6, **sept** = 7, **oct** = 8, **non** = 9, **dec** = 10.
>
> It counts how many **extra** groups of a thousand are multiplied onto a thousand: a billion is 1000 × 1000², a trillion is 1000 × 1000³, and in general the "n-illion" is 1000 × 1000ⁿ = 10³ⁿ⁺³. (Check: trillion, n = 3, gives 10¹² ✓; decillion, n = 10, gives 10³³ ✓.)

## Exercises — try first

### 1. Find the units digit of 2²²⁴ ÷ 4³². (Hint: 4 = 2².)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. 4³² = (2²)³² = 2⁶⁴ (power of a power).
> 2. 2²²⁴ ÷ 2⁶⁴ = 2²²⁴⁻⁶⁴ = **2¹⁶⁰**.
> 3. The units digits of the powers of 2 go 2, 4, 8, 6, 2, 4, 8, 6, … repeating every 4 (each time we multiply the last digit by 2: 6 × 2 = 12 brings back 2).
> 4. 160 is a multiple of 4, so 2¹⁶⁰ ends like 2⁴, 2⁸, 2¹², …, in **6**.

### 2. A container holds 5 bottles. Every day a new container is brought in. How many bottles are there after 40 days?

> [!ANSWER]
> Careful: this is **adding**, not multiplying. Each day adds one container of 5 bottles, so after 40 days there are 40 × 5 = **200 bottles** = 2 × 10². (Linear growth, not exponential: the number of bottles in a container doesn't change.)

### 3. Write each number as a product of two or more powers in three different ways (the exponents can be any integers).

**(i)** 64³

> [!ANSWER]
> 64 = 2⁶, so 64³ = (2⁶)³ = 2¹⁸. Three ways (any split of the exponent 18 works):
> - **2¹⁰ × 2⁸**
> - **4⁵ × 4⁴** (4⁹ = (2²)⁹ = 2¹⁸)
> - **8³ × 8³** (8⁶ = (2³)⁶ = 2¹⁸)

**(ii)** 192⁸

> [!ANSWER]
> 192 = 64 × 3 = 2⁶ × 3, so 192⁸ = 2⁴⁸ × 3⁸. Three ways:
> - **2⁴⁸ × 3⁸**
> - **2⁴⁰ × 2⁸ × 3⁸**
> - **2⁴⁰ × 6⁸** (2⁸ × 3⁸ = (2 × 3)⁸ = 6⁸)

**(iii)** 32⁻⁵

> [!ANSWER]
> 32 = 2⁵, so 32⁻⁵ = (2⁵)⁻⁵ = 2⁻²⁵. Three ways (the exponents must add up to −25):
> - **2⁻¹⁰ × 2⁻¹⁵**
> - **2⁻⁵ × 2⁻²⁰**
> - **2⁵ × 2⁻³⁰** (a positive and a negative power also work: 5 + (−30) = −25)

### 4. Is each statement Always True, Only Sometimes True or Never True? Explain.

**(i)** Cube numbers are also square numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Only sometimes true.** 64 = 4³ is also 8², but 8 = 2³ is not a square, and neither is 27 = 3³. A cube is also a square exactly when it is a **sixth power** (like 1, 64 = 2⁶, 729 = 3⁶), because n⁶ = (n²)³ = (n³)².

**(ii)** Fourth powers are also square numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always true.** n⁴ = n² × n² = **(n²)²**, the square of the whole number n².

**(iii)** The fifth power of a number is divisible by the cube of that number.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always true** (for any number other than 0). n⁵ ÷ n³ = n⁵⁻³ = **n²**, a whole number when n is. In other words n⁵ = n³ × n², so n³ is a factor.

**(iv)** The product of two cube numbers is a cube number.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always true.** a³ × b³ = (a × b)³, by the rule mᵃ × nᵃ = (mn)ᵃ. For instance 8 × 27 = 2³ × 3³ = 6³ = 216.

**(v)** q⁴⁶ is both a 4th power and a 6th power (q is a prime number).

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never true.** Since q is prime, q⁴⁶ has only the prime q, used 46 times. To be a 4th power, the 46 q's would have to split into 4 equal groups, so 4 would have to divide 46. It doesn't (46 ÷ 4 = 11.5), and 6 doesn't divide 46 either. So q⁴⁶ is **neither** a 4th power nor a 6th power.

### 5. Simplify and write in exponential form.

**(i)** 10⁻² × 10⁻⁵

> [!ANSWER]
> Add the exponents: 10⁻²⁺⁽⁻⁵⁾ = **10⁻⁷** (= 1/10⁷).

**(ii)** 5⁷ ÷ 5⁴

> [!ANSWER]
> Subtract the exponents: 5⁷⁻⁴ = **5³**.

**(iii)** 9⁻⁷ ÷ 9⁴

> [!ANSWER]
> 9⁻⁷⁻⁴ = **9⁻¹¹** (= 1/9¹¹).

**(iv)** (13⁻²)⁻³

> [!ANSWER]
> Multiply the exponents: 13⁽⁻²⁾ˣ⁽⁻³⁾ = **13⁶**.

**(v)** m⁵n¹²(mn)⁹

> [!ANSWER]
> (mn)⁹ = m⁹n⁹. Then m⁵ × m⁹ = m¹⁴ and n¹² × n⁹ = n²¹. Answer: **m¹⁴n²¹**.

### 6. If 12² = 144, what is each of these?

**(i)** (1.2)²

> [!ANSWER]
> 1.2 = 12/10, so (1.2)² = 144/100 = **1.44**. (One decimal place in 1.2 becomes two in the square.)

**(ii)** (0.12)²

> [!ANSWER]
> 0.12 = 12/100, so (0.12)² = 144/10000 = **0.0144**.

**(iii)** (0.012)²

> [!ANSWER]
> 0.012 = 12/1000, so (0.012)² = 144/1000000 = **0.000144**.

**(iv)** 120²

> [!ANSWER]
> 120 = 12 × 10, so 120² = 144 × 100 = **14400**.
>
> **The pattern:** squaring doubles the number of decimal places (or end zeros), because (12 × 10ᵏ)² = 144 × 10²ᵏ.

### 7. Which of these numbers are the same? 2⁴ × 3⁶, 6⁴ × 3², 6¹⁰, 18² × 6², 6²⁴

> [!ANSWER]
> Write each with primes 2 and 3:
> - 2⁴ × 3⁶
> - 6⁴ × 3² = (2 × 3)⁴ × 3² = 2⁴ × 3⁴ × 3² = **2⁴ × 3⁶**
> - 6¹⁰ = 2¹⁰ × 3¹⁰
> - 18² × 6² = (2 × 3²)² × (2 × 3)² = 2² × 3⁴ × 2² × 3² = **2⁴ × 3⁶**
> - 6²⁴ = 2²⁴ × 3²⁴
>
> So **2⁴ × 3⁶, 6⁴ × 3² and 18² × 6²** are the same number (11,664). Two numbers are equal exactly when they have the same prime factors with the same exponents.

### 8. Which is greater?

**(i)** 4³ or 3⁴

> [!ANSWER]
> 4³ = 64 and 3⁴ = 81, so **3⁴** is greater.

**(ii)** 2⁸ or 8²

> [!ANSWER]
> 2⁸ = 256 and 8² = 64, so **2⁸** is greater.

**(iii)** 100² or 2¹⁰⁰

> [!ANSWER]
> 100² = 10⁴ = 10,000. And 2¹⁰ = 1024 is more than 10³, so 2¹⁰⁰ = (2¹⁰)¹⁰ is more than (10³)¹⁰ = 10³⁰. So **2¹⁰⁰** is far greater.
>
> **Lesson:** a large exponent beats a large base. Repeated multiplication many times wins.

### 9. A dairy makes 8.5 billion packets of milk a year. Each packet needs its own ID code made of the digits 0 to 9. How many digits should the code have?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. A code with n digits gives 10 × 10 × … × 10 = **10ⁿ** different codes (10 choices for each digit).
> 2. We need at least 8.5 billion = 8.5 × 10⁹ codes.
> 3. 9 digits give 10⁹ = 1 billion codes: too few. 10 digits give 10¹⁰ = 10 billion: enough.
>
> So the code needs **10 digits**.

### 10. 64 is a square (8²) and a cube (4³). Are there other numbers that are both squares and cubes? Can you describe all of them?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, infinitely many: exactly the sixth powers** 1⁶ = 1, 2⁶ = 64, 3⁶ = 729, 4⁶ = 4096, 5⁶ = 15625, …
>
> **Why:** for a square, every prime's exponent must be divisible by 2 (pairs); for a cube, by 3 (triplets). To be both, every exponent must be divisible by both 2 and 3, that is by 6. So the number is (something)⁶. And every sixth power works: n⁶ = (n³)² = (n²)³.

### 11. A digital locker has a 5-character code; each character is a digit or a letter (like G89P0, 38098, BRJKW or 003AZ). How many codes are possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each position has 10 digits + 26 letters = 36 choices. Five positions: 36 × 36 × 36 × 36 × 36 = **36⁵ = 6,04,66,176** codes (about 6 crore).

### 12. There are about 10⁹ sheep in the world (2024), and about the same number of goats. How many sheep and goats are there altogether? (i) 20⁹ (ii) 10¹¹ (iii) 10¹⁰ (iv) 10¹⁸ (v) 2 × 10⁹ (vi) 10⁹ + 10⁹

> [!ANSWER]
> 10⁹ + 10⁹ is two lots of 10⁹, which is 2 × 10⁹. So **both (v) and (vi)** are right: they are the same number.
>
> The others are traps: adding two powers does **not** add the exponents (that would be multiplying: 10⁹ × 10⁹ = 10¹⁸), and 20⁹ = 2⁹ × 10⁹ is 512 times too big.

**(ii)** There are about 100 million bee colonies, each with about 50,000 bees. How many honeybees are there?

### 15. Tremendous in Ten! In a game, each player has 10 seconds to write the largest number they can, using digits and arithmetic signs. In round 1, Roxie wrote 10000000000000 and Estu wrote 999999 × 999999. Why is Roxie's number larger?

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **nᵃ** is n multiplied by itself a times; n is the **base**, a the **exponent**.
> - **nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ** • **nᵃ ÷ nᵇ = nᵃ⁻ᵇ** • **(nᵃ)ᵇ = nᵃᵇ** • **mᵃ × nᵃ = (mn)ᵃ** • **mᵃ ÷ nᵃ = (m ÷ n)ᵃ**. Each comes from counting copies of n.
> - **n⁰ = 1** and **n⁻ᵃ = 1/nᵃ** (n ≠ 0), the only choices that keep the division rule true. Then all the rules work for any integers.
> - Even powers of a negative are positive; odd powers are negative.
> - **Choices in a row multiply**: a 5-digit lock has 10⁵ codes, a 6-letter lock 26⁶.
> - **Standard form:** x × 10ʸ with 1 ≤ x < 10. The exponent tells the size; compare exponents first.
> - **Estimate** by guessing, writing the relationship, assuming sensible values, and calculating.
> - **Exponential (×) growth beats linear (+) growth**: 46 folds of paper reach the Moon, a ladder needs 192 crore steps.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** 4³ is 64, not 12 • 2⁰ = 1, not 0 • 2⁻³ = 1/8, not −8 • 10⁹ + 10⁹ = 2 × 10⁹, not 10¹⁸ or 20⁹ • 2³ × 2² = 2⁵ (add) but (2³)² = 2⁶ (multiply) • −2⁴ = −16 but (−2)⁴ = 16 • in standard form the coefficient must be at least 1 and below 10 (59 × 10² is not standard form) • count the digits carefully: 70,04,00,00,000 has 11 digits, so it is 7.004 × 10¹⁰.
