## What this chapter is about

**Area** measures how much flat surface a shape covers: how much paint, rangoli powder or carpet it needs. We already know the area of a rectangle. In this chapter we **build** the area of every other straight-sided shape from it:

- why area is counted in unit squares, and why the perimeter can't do the job;
- the area of a **triangle**, and why one formula works for every triangle;
- the area of any **polygon**, by cutting it into triangles;
- special formulas for the **parallelogram**, **rhombus** and **trapezium**, found by cutting and rearranging (**dissection**), some of them from the ancient Śulba-Sūtras;
- areas in real life: A4 sheets, square inches, acres.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** A **unit square** is a square of side 1 unit (1 cm, 1 m, …); its area is 1 square unit (1 cm², 1 m², …). The **base** of a triangle or parallelogram is any side we choose; its **height** is the perpendicular distance from that side to the opposite vertex (or opposite side). **Dissection** means cutting a shape into pieces and rearranging them.

## 14.1 Rectangles, squares and unit squares

Which rectangle needs more rangoli powder: 7 cm by 4 cm, or 8 cm by 3 cm? Count the unit squares that fit inside:

![A 7 by 4 grid of 28 unit squares and an 8 by 3 grid of 24](assets/img/class8/math/14/two_rectangles.svg)

The first holds 7 × 4 = **28** unit squares, the second 8 × 3 = **24**. So the 7 × 4 rectangle needs more powder.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a rectangle = length × width.** It is measured in square units (cm², m², …).
> **Why:** the rectangle holds "width" rows, each of "length" unit squares. (This works for fractional lengths too, with parts of unit squares.)

A diagonal cuts a rectangle into two congruent triangles, so **each triangle has half the rectangle's area**: a 7 cm × 4 cm rectangle gives two triangles of ½ × 7 × 4 = **14 cm²**.

**Why not use the perimeter?** Two shapes can have the same perimeter but different areas, and a shape with a **bigger** perimeter can even have a **smaller** area:

![A 12 by 1 rectangle (perimeter 26, area 12) and a 5 by 5 square (perimeter 20, area 25)](assets/img/class8/math/14/perimeter_vs_area.svg)

**Try this.** Draw a square on grid paper, then divide it into 4 parts of equal area in as many different ways as you can (not just quarters!). Hint: start from 4 equal strips and push one edge of each strip in while pulling another out by the same amount.

### Check yourself

**1.** How many ways are there to divide a square into 4 parts of equal area?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Infinitely many.** Start with any division into 4 equal parts (four strips, or four quarters). Now bend a shared boundary: push it into one part in one place and out of it by the same amount somewhere else. Both parts keep their area. Since you can bend boundaries in endless ways (zigzags, curves, steps), there are endless equal divisions.

**2.** Find two rectangles where the one with the larger perimeter has the smaller area.

> [!ANSWER]
> **12 cm × 1 cm:** perimeter 2(12 + 1) = 26 cm, area 12 cm². **5 cm × 5 cm:** perimeter 20 cm, area 25 cm² (see the picture above). Long thin shapes have a big boundary for a small inside.

**3.** Give two regions of other shapes where the one with the larger perimeter clearly has the smaller area.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take a **circle** of radius 3 cm (area about 28 cm², perimeter about 19 cm) and a **star** or a very jagged shape that fits inside it: its zigzag boundary can be longer than 19 cm while its area is less than the circle's. Or a comb shape cut from a small square: lots of teeth make the perimeter huge, but the area stays below the square's.

**Question 1.** Find the missing side lengths.

![Two shapes made of rectangles: (i) rectangles of 28, 21, 35 and 14 in² fitted together with some sides given; (ii) a 50 m² rectangle split into 29 m², 11 m² and a strip](assets/img/class8/math/14/missing_sides.svg)

**(i)**

> [!ANSWER]
> Work from the pieces with two numbers known:
> 1. **21 in²** has length 7 in, so its height is 21 ÷ 7 = **3 in**.
> 2. **28 in²** is 4 in + 3 in = 7 in tall (it reaches 4 in above the 21 in² piece and goes down to the same line), so its width is 28 ÷ 7 = **4 in**.
> 3. **35 in²** is 3 in + 4 in = 7 in wide, so its height is 35 ÷ 7 = **5 in**.
> 4. **14 in²** runs from the top of the 35 in² piece down 2 in past it: 5 + 2 = 7 in long, so its width is 14 ÷ 7 = **2 in**. That is the missing side.

**(ii)**

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The 29 m² part is 4 m tall: width 29 ÷ 4 = **7.25 m**.
> 2. The 11 m² part is 4 m tall: width 11 ÷ 4 = **2.75 m**.
> 3. The whole rectangle is 7.25 + 2.75 = 10 m wide and has area 50 m², so it is 50 ÷ 10 = 5 m tall; the bottom strip is 5 − 4 = **1 m** tall.

**Question 2.** A path (shaded) is laid round a rectangular park EFGH; ABCD is the outer edge of the path.

![A green park EFGH inside a larger rectangle ABCD; the yellow band between them is the path](assets/img/class8/math/14/park_path.svg)

**(i)** What must you measure to find the path's area? Choose values and give a formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> Measure the **outer rectangle** ABCD and the **park** EFGH. Then
>
> **Path area = AB × BC − EF × FG** (whole minus park).
>
> For instance AB = 30 m, BC = 20 m, EF = 22 m, FG = 14 m: 600 − 308 = **292 m²**.

**(ii)** If the path's width along each side is given, can you find its area? What else do you need?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not from the widths alone**: a wide path round a big park is bigger than one round a small park. You also need the **park's length l and width b**. If the path is w₁ and w₂ wide on the left and right and w₃ and w₄ wide at the bottom and top:
>
> path area = (l + w₁ + w₂)(b + w₃ + w₄) − l × b.
>
> For instance l = 22, b = 14, widths 4, 4, 3, 3: 30 × 20 − 308 = 292 m². (Or add the rectangles of the path: two long strips 30 × 3 and two short strips 14 × 4: 180 + 112 = 292 ✓.)

**(iii)** Does the path's area change if the outer rectangle is moved (keeping the park inside it)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The path is still (outer area) − (park area), and neither rectangle changes size. Moving the outer rectangle only makes the path wider on one side and narrower on the other.

**3.** A 14 m × 12 m plot has a cross-shaped path through it. What must you measure? Choose values and give a formula.

![A 14 m by 12 m plot with a path of width a running across and one of width b running along, crossing in the middle](assets/img/class8/math/14/cross_path.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Measure the **widths** of the two paths, say a (the path running the 12 m way) and b (the path running the 14 m way).
> 1. The paths are rectangles 12 × a and 14 × b.
> 2. They overlap in a small a × b square, which would be counted twice.
>
> **Area = 12a + 14b − ab.** With a = 2 m and b = 1.5 m: 24 + 21 − 3 = **42 m²**.

**4.** Find the area of the spiral tube (1 unit wide; the lengths shown are along its outer edge). What straight tube has the same area?

![A spiral tube 1 unit wide with outer lengths 20, 20, 20, 15, 15, 10, 10, 5, 5](assets/img/class8/math/14/spiral.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Unbend each corner.** An L-shaped tube with arms 5 and 5 (outer lengths) is a 5 × 1 piece and a 4 × 1 piece: area 9, the same as a straight tube of length 9 (the corner square is counted only once).
>
> ![An L of arms 5 and 5 has the same area as a straight tube of length 9](assets/img/class8/math/14/bent_tube.svg)
>
> The spiral has 9 straight pieces with outer lengths 20 + 20 + 20 + 15 + 15 + 10 + 10 + 5 + 5 = 120, and 8 corners, each counted twice. So the area is 120 − 8 = **112 square units**, the same as a **straight tube of length 112** (and width 1). Counting the unit squares in the drawing confirms 112.

**5.** In the figure, if the square's side is doubled, what happens to the areas of regions 1, 2 and 3?

![A square cut by a diagonal and a line from the centre to a corner into regions 1, 2, 3, drawn with side 4 and side 8](assets/img/class8/math/14/square_regions.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Each region becomes **4 times** as big, so each **increases by 3 times** its old area.
>
> **Why:** doubling the side doubles every length in the figure, so each region keeps its shape but is enlarged by 2 in both directions. A shape doubled in both directions covers 2 × 2 = 4 times the unit squares (each unit square becomes a 2 × 2 block). For instance, region 1 is half the square: s²/2 becomes (2s)²/2 = 4 × s²/2.

**6.** Cut a square into 4 pieces with two perpendicular lines through its middle, and rearrange the pieces into a larger square with a square hole.

![A square cut by two perpendicular lines through its centre, tilted](assets/img/class8/math/14/four_pieces.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> The two lines through the centre cut **four identical pieces** (each is a quarter-turn of the next). Turn each piece so that its **right-angled corner points outwards** and place the four round a centre, each piece rotated a quarter-turn from the last. The long cut edges become the outside of a bigger square, and the pieces' short edges leave a **square hole** in the middle.
>
> The outer square is larger but the area of card is the same: (big square) − (hole) = (original square). Try it with card: it works for any tilt of the lines, as long as they cross at right angles at the centre.

## 14.2 Triangles

Two identical rectangles ABCD have triangles XDC and YDC, with X and Y anywhere on AB. **They have the same area: half the rectangle.** Drop a perpendicular from X to DC: it splits the rectangle into two smaller rectangles, and the triangle is exactly half of each.

![Two identical rectangles with triangles XDC and YDC](assets/img/class8/math/14/tri_in_rect.svg)

**Example 1: area of ∆XDC.** In a 5 × 4 rectangle ABCD with X on AB, drop XY ⊥ DC. Rectangle AXYD is cut in half by XD, and XBCY by XC. So ∆XDC = ½ × 5 × 4 = **10 square units**.

![Rectangle ABCD, 5 by 4, with triangle XDC and the perpendicular XY](assets/img/class8/math/14/tri_rect_7_1.svg)

**Any triangle fits in a rectangle.** Through A draw a line parallel to BC, and drop perpendiculars from B and C to it; BCDE is a rectangle with BC as one side and the triangle's height as the other.

![Triangle ABC inside rectangle BCDE; AX is the height. An obtuse triangle: the height h falls outside, from A to D on BC extended](assets/img/class8/math/14/tri_height.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height.**
> **Why:** the triangle is exactly half of the rectangle "base × height" around it (the height cuts it into two right triangles, each half of a smaller rectangle). For an obtuse triangle, where the height falls outside, ∆ABC = ∆ADC − ∆ADB = ½h × DC − ½h × DB = ½h × (DC − DB) = ½h × BC: the same formula.

**Example 2: using two bases.** In ∆ABC, AX ⊥ BC with AX = 3, BC = 5 and AC = 4. Find the altitude BY to AC.
1. Area = ½ × BC × AX = ½ × 5 × 3 = 7.5.
2. The same area = ½ × AC × BY = 2 × BY.
3. 2 × BY = 7.5, so **BY = 3.75**.

![Triangle ABC with altitude AX = 3 to BC = 5 and altitude BY to AC = 4](assets/img/class8/math/14/altitude_by.svg)

**Example 3: the diagonals of a rectangle make 4 triangles of equal area.** Triangles 1 and 2 share a vertex B, and their bases OD and OB on the diagonal are equal (the diagonals bisect each other), with the **same height** from B. Equal base, equal height, equal area. The same argument works round the rectangle.

![Rectangle ABCD with diagonals meeting at O, making triangles 1, 2, 3, 4](assets/img/class8/math/14/diag_triangles.svg)

> [!TIP]
> **A median halves a triangle.** The line from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side splits a triangle into two triangles with equal bases and the same height, so with equal areas.

### Triangles between parallel lines

Take a base BC and a line l parallel to BC. Every triangle with base BC and third vertex A on l has the **same height**, so the **same area**. What about the perimeter? Reflect C in l to get C′. For any A on l, AC = AC′, so BA + AC = BA + AC′. This is shortest when A lies on the straight line BC′.

![B, C, the line l, and C′ the reflection of C in l; the straight path from B to C′ crosses l at A, making BA + AC shortest](assets/img/class8/math/14/min_perimeter.svg)

**Try this.** Fold a sheet of paper along a straight line l. Mark B and C on one side. Find the point A on the fold that makes BA + AC shortest by poking a pin through C to mark its mirror image on the other half, and joining B to it.

### Check yourself

**1.** In two identical rectangles ABCD, which has the bigger area: ∆XDC or ∆YDC, with X and Y on AB? And ∆XDC or ∆YBC, with Y on the side AD?

> [!ANSWER]
> **All equal: each is half the rectangle.**
> - ∆XDC and ∆YDC: base DC and height = the rectangle's height (X and Y are on the opposite side AB).
> - ∆YBC with Y on the side AD opposite BC: base BC and height = the distance between AD and BC, the rectangle's width.
>
> In each case, base × height = the rectangle's area, so the triangle is half of it.

**2.** Why is BCDE a rectangle? Why is BXAE a rectangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **BCDE:** ED was drawn through A parallel to BC, and BE, CD were drawn perpendicular to ED. So BE and CD are also perpendicular to BC (a line perpendicular to one of two parallel lines is perpendicular to the other). All four angles are 90°, so it is a rectangle.
>
> **BXAE:** AX ⊥ BC, BE ⊥ BC and BE ⊥ ED, so the angles at X, B, E and A are all 90°: a rectangle. So AX = BE: **the triangle's height equals the rectangle's height**.

**3.** For triangles with base BC and third vertex on a line l ∥ BC, which has the largest and the smallest area? The largest and smallest perimeter?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Area: all the same.** Every such triangle has base BC and the same height (the distance between l and BC).
> - **Perimeter: no largest.** Moving A further along l makes BA + AC as long as you like.
> - **Smallest perimeter:** when A is where the straight line from B to C′ (C reflected in l) crosses l, because a straight path is the shortest.

**4.** Does that best A lie on the perpendicular bisector of BC?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** C′ is the reflection of C, so the line BC′ crosses l exactly halfway between B's and C's positions along l: the reflection makes the picture symmetric. So A is directly above the midpoint of BC, and **AB = AC**: the triangle is **isosceles**. (Check: with B = (0, 0), C = (4, 0) and l at height 3, C′ = (4, 6) and BC′ crosses l at x = 2, the middle.)

**3.** ∆SUB is isosceles, SE ⊥ UB, and ∆SEB has area 24. Find the area of ∆SUB.

![Isosceles triangle SUB with SU = SB and SE perpendicular to UB](assets/img/class8/math/14/isosceles_sub.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> In an isosceles triangle the perpendicular from the top vertex meets the base at its **midpoint** (∆SEU ≅ ∆SEB by RHS: SU = SB, SE common, right angles at E). So ∆SEU = ∆SEB = 24, and **∆SUB = 48 square units**.

**4.** Śulba-Sūtras: transform a rectangle into a triangle of equal area.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take a rectangle with base b and height h (area bh).
> 1. Extend the base to length **2b** (double it).
> 2. Join both ends of this doubled base to a point at height **h** above it (for instance the top corner above one end).
>
> The triangle's area is ½ × 2b × h = **bh**, the same. (Or keep the base b and make the height 2h.)

**5.** Śulba-Sūtras: transform a triangle into a rectangle of equal area.

> [!ANSWER]
> For a triangle with base b and height h (area ½bh): draw a rectangle on the same base b with **half the height**, h/2. Its area is b × h/2 = ½bh ✓.
>
> As a dissection: cut the triangle along the line joining the midpoints of its two slanting sides, then cut the top piece down its height and swing the two small triangles down to fill the corners: they make exactly that rectangle.

**Question 6.** ABCD, BCEF and BFGH are identical squares.

![Three squares in an L; a red triangle DCH and a blue triangle in the corner of square ABCD](assets/img/class8/math/14/three_squares.svg)

**(i)** The red region has area 49. What is the blue region's area?

> [!ANSWER]
> Let each square have side s.
> 1. **Red** is triangle DCH: base DC = s, height CH = 2s, area ½ × s × 2s = **s²**. So s² = 49 and s = 7.
> 2. **Blue** is the triangle at A cut off by line DH. DH rises 2s over a run of s, so it crosses AB (height s) halfway along it, at s/2 from A. Blue = ½ × (s/2) × s = s²/4.
>
> Blue = 49 ÷ 4 = **12.25 square units**.

**(ii)** If red and blue together are 180, what is the area of each square?

> [!ANSWER]
> Red + blue = s² + s²/4 = (5/4)s² = 180, so s² = 180 × 4/5 = **144 square units**.

**7.** M and N are the midpoints of XY and XZ. What fraction of ∆XYZ is ∆XMN?

![Triangle XYZ with M, N the midpoints of XY, XZ; NY is joined](assets/img/class8/math/14/midpoint_triangle.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Join NY.
> 1. NY is a median of ∆XYZ (N is the midpoint of XZ), so ∆XNY is **half** of ∆XYZ.
> 2. NM is a median of ∆XNY (M is the midpoint of XY), so ∆XMN is **half** of ∆XNY.
>
> So ∆XMN = ½ × ½ = **¼ of ∆XYZ**.

**8.** Gopal walks from his house to the river, fills a pot, and carries it to his water tank. What is his shortest path?

> [!ANSWER]
> Treat the river bank as a mirror. **Reflect the tank** in the bank, and draw a straight line from the house to the tank's mirror image. Where it meets the river (R) is where he should fetch water: house → R → tank.
>
> ![House, river and tank; the shortest path goes from the house to a point R on the river and then to the tank, lined up with the tank's mirror image](assets/img/class8/math/14/river.svg)
>
> **Why:** from any river point, the distance to the tank equals the distance to its mirror image, so the trip's length equals house → river → image. That is shortest when it is one straight line.

## 14.3 The area of any polygon

Any polygon can be cut into triangles by joining corners with diagonals. **Area of the polygon = sum of the triangles' areas.** For a quadrilateral, join one diagonal: you need its length and the heights of the two triangles on it.

### Check yourself

**1.** What do you need to measure to find the area of a quadrilateral ABCD?

> [!ANSWER]
> Join a diagonal, say BD. Measure **BD** and the **perpendicular distances from A and from C to BD**. Then area = ½ × BD × (distance from A) + ½ × BD × (distance from C) = ½ × BD × (sum of the two distances).

**2.** How can you find the area of a pentagon? Can every polygon be cut into triangles?

> [!ANSWER]
> Join one corner of the pentagon to the two corners not next to it: this makes **3 triangles**; add their areas. **Every polygon can be cut into triangles** (an n-sided polygon into n − 2 of them). For a polygon with an inward-pointing corner, choose diagonals that stay inside (for instance, start from the inward corner).

### Check yourself: Figure it out (polygons)

**1.** Find the area of ABCD, given AC = 22 cm, BM = 3 cm, DN = 3 cm, BM ⊥ AC, DN ⊥ AC.

![Quadrilateral ABCD split by diagonal AC = 22 cm, with heights BM = 3 cm and DN = 3 cm](assets/img/class8/math/14/quad_ac.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The diagonal AC splits ABCD into ∆ABC and ∆ADC, both with base AC = 22 cm.
> 2. ∆ABC = ½ × 22 × 3 = 33 cm² (height BM); ∆ADC = ½ × 22 × 3 = 33 cm² (height DN).
> 3. **Area = 66 cm².**

**2.** ABCD is a rectangle. Find the shaded area.

![Rectangle ABCD, 18 cm by 10 cm; E on AB with AE = 10, F on AD with AF = 6; the shaded region is FECD](assets/img/class8/math/14/shaded_rect.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Whole rectangle minus the two unshaded triangles:
> 1. Rectangle: 18 × 10 = 180 cm².
> 2. ∆AEF: right angle at A, legs AE = 10 and AF = 6: ½ × 10 × 6 = 30 cm².
> 3. ∆EBC: right angle at B, legs EB = 8 and BC = 10: ½ × 8 × 10 = 40 cm².
>
> Shaded = 180 − 30 − 40 = **110 cm²**.

**3.** What measurements do you need to find the area of a regular hexagon?

> [!ANSWER]
> A regular hexagon splits into **6 equilateral triangles** meeting at its centre. So you need just **the side length s** and the **height of one triangle** (the distance from the centre to a side). Area = 6 × ½ × s × (that height). (For s = 2 cm the height is about 1.73 cm, giving about 10.4 cm².)

**4.** What fraction of the rectangle is the blue region?

![A rectangle with a point inside joined to all four corners; the top and bottom triangles are blue](assets/img/class8/math/14/blue_fraction.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Half.** The two blue triangles have bases equal to the top and bottom sides (each the rectangle's length L), and their heights add up to the rectangle's width W (the point's distances to the top and to the bottom). So blue = ½ × L × h₁ + ½ × L × h₂ = ½ × L × (h₁ + h₂) = ½ × L × W, wherever the point is.

**5.** Give a method to make a quadrilateral with half the area of a given one.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Join the midpoints of its four sides in order.** The new quadrilateral (always a parallelogram) has exactly half the area.
>
> **Why:** each corner triangle cut off is a quarter of the triangle made by that corner and the diagonal next to it (as in exercise 7: halving both sides from a corner quarters a triangle). Each diagonal's two triangles together make the whole quadrilateral, so the four corner pieces add up to ¼ + ¼ of it twice, which is ½. What is left in the middle is the other half.

## 14.4 Parallelograms

**Try this.** Cut a parallelogram out of squared paper. Cut along a height, slide the triangle to the other end, and count the unit squares in the rectangle you get.

Drop AX ⊥ DC, cut off triangle AXD and slide it to the other end: it fits exactly against BC, making rectangle ABYX.

![Parallelogram ABCD with height AX; triangle AXD is moved to BYC, giving rectangle ABYX](assets/img/class8/math/14/para_to_rect.svg)

**Why it fits:** ∆BYC ≅ ∆AXD by **RHS**: BY = AX (opposite sides of rectangle ABYX), the right angles at Y and X, and BC = AD (opposite sides of the parallelogram).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a parallelogram = base × height.**
> **Why:** the rectangle ABYX has the same area, with length XY = DC (DX = CY, and adding XC to both) and width AX, the height. Any side can be the base, as long as the height is measured perpendicular to **that** side.

**Example 4.** A parallelogram with base 7 cm and height 4 cm has area 7 × 4 = **28 cm²**, whatever its slant.

### Check yourself

**1.** Can a parallelogram also be cut along a height CZ to another side and rearranged into a rectangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Take AD as the base and drop the height CZ from C to AD (extended if needed). Cut along CZ and slide the triangle cut off to the opposite side, exactly as before. The pieces make a rectangle with sides **AD** (the base) and **CZ** (its height), so area = AD × CZ too: **any side and its own height** give the same area.

### Check yourself: Figure it out (parallelograms)

**Question 1.** These parallelograms are drawn on a grid.

![Six parallelograms (a) to (f), all with base 4 and height 3, slanting by different amounts](assets/img/class8/math/14/para_grid.svg)

**(i)** What can you say about their areas?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are all equal**: each has base 4 and height 3 units, so area 4 × 3 = **12 square units**. Slanting a parallelogram (keeping base and height) doesn't change its area.

**(ii)** What about their perimeters? Which is largest and smallest?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The perimeters differ.** The slanting sides get longer the more the parallelogram leans: their length is √(3² + lean²). The **upright one (a)**, a rectangle, has the smallest perimeter, 2(4 + 3) = 14. The one that leans most (lean 3, here (d)) has the largest: 2(4 + √18) ≈ 16.5.

**(i)**

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The 4 cm height is drawn to the 7 cm base.
> 2. Area = 7 × 4 = **28 cm²**.

**(ii)**

> [!ANSWER]
> 5 × 3 = **15 cm²**. (The diagonal drawn inside doesn't matter: it just splits the parallelogram into two triangles of 7.5 cm² each.)

**(iii)**

> [!ANSWER]
> The 4.8 cm height is measured **perpendicular to the 5 cm sides**, so base 5 and height 4.8: 5 × 4.8 = **24 cm²**.

**(iv)**

> [!ANSWER]
> The 4.4 cm height is perpendicular to the **2 cm** sides: 2 × 4.4 = **8.8 cm²**. Always pair a base with the height drawn to it.

**3.** Find QN in parallelogram PQRS, where SR = 12 cm, the height QM to SR is 6 cm, PS = 7.6 cm and QN ⊥ PS.

![Parallelogram PQRS with SR = 12 cm, height QM = 6 cm and QN perpendicular to PS = 7.6 cm](assets/img/class8/math/14/find_qn.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Area with base SR: 12 × 6 = **72 cm²**.
> 2. Area with base PS: 7.6 × QN.
> 3. 7.6 × QN = 72, so **QN = 72 ÷ 7.6 ≈ 9.47 cm**.

**4.** A rectangle and a parallelogram both have sides 5 cm and 4 cm. Which has the greater area?

![A 5 by 4 rectangle and a parallelogram with sides 5 and 4 on the same base, leaning so that its height is less than 4](assets/img/class8/math/14/rect_vs_para.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **The rectangle.** Put both on the same 5 cm base. The rectangle's height is the full 4 cm; the parallelogram's 4 cm side leans, so its height is **less than 4 cm** (the slanting side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose leg is the height). So 5 × 4 = 20 cm² beats 5 × (less than 4).

**5.** Give methods to make a rectangle with twice the area of a given triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Same base, same height:** draw the rectangle base × height round the triangle (as in 14.2): its area is twice ½ × base × height.
> - **Two copies:** cut out two copies of the triangle and put them together to make a parallelogram; then turn the parallelogram into a rectangle as in 14.4.
> - **Different shape:** any rectangle with area base × height, say base 2b and height h/2.

**6.** Śulba-Sūtras: make a rectangle with the same area as a given triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> Rectangle with the **same base** and **half the height** (b × h/2 = ½bh). By dissection: cut the triangle along the line joining the midpoints of its two slanting sides; cut the small top triangle along its height into two pieces, and turn each down into the empty corner beside it. The pieces fill a rectangle of base b and height h/2.

**7.** Śulba-Sūtras: an isosceles triangle can be turned into a rectangle more simply. How?

> [!ANSWER]
> Cut the isosceles triangle ABC down its height AD (D is the midpoint of BC). The two halves ADB and ADC are **identical right triangles**. Turn one over and put the two together **along their slanting sides**: they make a **rectangle** with sides AD and BD = ½ BC. Area = AD × ½BC = ½ × BC × AD ✓.

**8.** Śulba-Sūtras: turn a rectangle into an isosceles triangle by dissection.

> [!ANSWER]
> Reverse the last answer. Cut the rectangle (sides l and w) along a **diagonal** into two identical right triangles. Turn one over and put the two together along their sides of length w, back to back with their right angles side by side: they make an isosceles triangle with base 2l and height w. Area = ½ × 2l × w = lw ✓.

**9.** Which is bigger: an equilateral triangle or a square with the same side? Two such triangles together, or the square?

> [!ANSWER]
> An equilateral triangle with side s has height **less than s** (the height is a leg of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is s; in fact it is about 0.87s). So:
> - one triangle: ½ × s × 0.87s ≈ 0.43s² **< s²**;
> - two triangles: ≈ 0.87s² **< s²**, because two triangles make a parallelogram with base s and height less than s.
>
> **The square is bigger in both cases.**

## 14.5 Rhombuses and trapeziums

### The rhombus

A rhombus's diagonals are **perpendicular bisectors** of each other. The diagonal BD splits it into two isosceles triangles, and each can be dissected into a rectangle (exercise 7); together they make a rectangle with sides **AC** and **½BD**.

![Rhombus ABCD with diagonals meeting at right angles at O, rearranged into a rectangle with sides AC and half of BD](assets/img/class8/math/14/rhombus_rect.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a rhombus = ½ × (product of the diagonals).**
> **Why:** ∆ABD and ∆CBD share the base BD, and their heights AO and CO add up to AC (the diagonals are perpendicular). So the area is ½ × BD × AO + ½ × BD × CO = ½ × BD × AC.

**Try this.** Cut a rhombus from paper, fold it along both diagonals and cut along the folds: you get 4 identical right triangles. Rearrange them into a rectangle. What are its sides?

### The trapezium

Drop perpendiculars from W and X to ZY. The trapezium becomes a rectangle and two right triangles:

![Trapezium WXYZ with WX = a parallel to ZY, height h, and the pieces x and y at the ends of the base](assets/img/class8/math/14/trapezium_formula.svg)

Area = ½hx + ha + ½hy = ½h(x + 2a + y). The longer side is b = x + a + y, so x + y = b − a, and area = ½h(b − a + 2a) = **½h(a + b)**.

**Another way:** two copies of the trapezium, one turned half round, fit together into a **parallelogram** with base a + b and the same height (the angles at each joint add to 180°, so the edges line up straight). The trapezium is half of it.

![Two copies of a trapezium make a parallelogram with base a + b](assets/img/class8/math/14/trapezium_doubled.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Area of a trapezium = ½ × height × (sum of the parallel sides).**
> **Why:** two copies make a parallelogram with base a + b and the same height h, so one copy is half of h(a + b).

**Example 5.** A trapezium with parallel sides 12 ft and 18 ft, 8 ft apart: ½ × 8 × (12 + 18) = **120 ft²**.

### Check yourself

**1.** Show that adding the areas of ∆ADB and ∆CDB gives the same rhombus formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> With the diagonals meeting at O at right angles, AO and CO are the heights of the two triangles on the base BD:
> 1. ∆ADB + ∆CDB = ½ × BD × AO + ½ × BD × CO
> 2. = ½ × BD × (AO + CO)
> 3. = ½ × BD × AC (since AO + CO = AC) ✓

**2.** Find the areas of trapeziums by cutting them into shapes whose areas you know. How would you do it for a trapezium with parallel sides AB and DC?

> [!ANSWER]
> Drop perpendiculars from A and B to DC (meeting it at M and N). The trapezium becomes a **rectangle ABNM** in the middle and **right triangles** ADM and BCN at the ends. Area = (rectangle) + (two triangles). Measure AB, MD, NC and the height. If one end is already square (a right-angled trapezium), there is only one triangle.

**Question 3.** For a trapezium that leans over (one of the slanting sides leans outwards past the end of the shorter side), complete each approach.

**(i)** Approach 1: rectangle and triangles. Area ABCD = Area ABED + Area ∆BEC, and Area ABED = Area ABEF − Area ∆AFD.

> [!ANSWER]
> Let AB = a, DC = b, height h; drop AF and BE ⊥ the line DC, with F beyond D (because the side AD leans out). Let FD = d, so EC = b + d − a.
> 1. ABEF is a rectangle: area a × h.
> 2. ∆AFD: ½ × d × h. So ABED = ah − ½dh.
> 3. ∆BEC: ½ × (b + d − a) × h.
> 4. Total = ah − ½dh + ½(b + d − a)h = ½h(2a − d + b + d − a) = **½h(a + b)** ✓.

**(ii)** Approach 2: parallelogram and triangle. Draw BG ∥ AD.

> [!ANSWER]
> ABGD is a parallelogram (AB ∥ DG and AD ∥ BG), with base AB = a and height h, so its area is **ah**. What is left is ∆BGC with base GC = b − a and height h: area ½h(b − a). Total = ah + ½h(b − a) = ½h(2a + b − a) = **½h(a + b)** ✓.

**4.** Does Approach 2 work for every trapezium?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, as long as we call the shorter parallel side a.** Then G (on DC, with DG = a) lies between D and C, and BGC is a genuine triangle. (If a = b the trapezium is already a parallelogram and the triangle disappears: area ah = ½h(a + a) ✓.)

## 14.6 Areas in real life

An A4 sheet is 21 cm × 29.7 cm: area 21 × 29.7 = **623.7 cm²**. Furniture is often measured in inches and feet: **1 in = 2.54 cm** and **1 ft = 12 in**. Since a square inch is 2.54 cm by 2.54 cm, **1 in² = 2.54² = 6.4516 cm²**.

Land is measured in m², ft², **acres** (1 acre = 43,560 ft²) and many local units (bigha, gaj, katha, cent, ankanam, …). Big areas use **km²**.

**Try this.** Estimate the area of your desk in A4 sheets (lay sheets on it). Then measure the desk and compute its area. How close was your estimate? Do the same for your classroom in m², and find out the local unit of land area in your region and what it equals in m².

### Check yourself

**1.** Find the area of an A4 sheet.

> [!ANSWER]
> 21 cm × 29.7 cm = **623.7 cm²** (a little over 600 cm², about 0.06 m²).

**2.** Write in centimetres: (i) 5 in (ii) 7.4 in.

> [!ANSWER]
> Multiply by 2.54: **(i)** 5 × 2.54 = **12.7 cm** **(ii)** 7.4 × 2.54 = **18.796 cm** (about 18.8 cm).

**3.** Write in inches: (i) 5.08 cm (ii) 11.43 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> Divide by 2.54: **(i)** 5.08 ÷ 2.54 = **2 in** **(ii)** 11.43 ÷ 2.54 = **4.5 in**.

**4.** How many cm² is 10 in²? Convert 161.29 cm² to in².

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. 10 in² = 10 × 6.4516 = **64.516 cm²**.
> 2. Each in² is 6.4516 cm², so 161.29 cm² = 161.29 ÷ 6.4516 = **25 in²** (a 5 in × 5 in square, since 5 in = 12.7 cm and 12.7² = 161.29).

**5.** How many in² is 1 ft²?

> [!ANSWER]
> A square foot is 12 in by 12 in, so **1 ft² = 12 × 12 = 144 in²**. (Areas scale by the **square** of the length conversion: 12 for lengths, 144 for areas.)

**6.** How many m² is 1 km²? How would you compare your town's area with your school's?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1 km = 1000 m, so **1 km² = 1000 × 1000 = 10,00,000 m²** (ten lakh).
>
> To compare: look up your town's area (in km²) in census or municipal records, and estimate your school's area in m² (pace out its length and width). Convert both to m² and divide. For instance a town of 25 km² = 2,50,00,000 m² compared with a school of 10,000 m² is 2500 times bigger. City areas depend on where the official boundary is drawn, so check how each was measured; the smallest country, Vatican City, is only about 0.44 km².

> 1. Area of a rhombus = ½ × (product of the diagonals).
> 2. ½ × 20 × 15 = **150 cm²**.

### 2. Give a method to convert a rectangle into a rhombus of equal area by dissection.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take a rectangle l long and w wide (area lw).
> 1. Cut it in half across its length: two rectangles, each (l/2) × w.
> 2. Cut each of these along a diagonal: **4 identical right triangles** with legs l/2 and w.
> 3. Put the four right angles together at one point, with the legs lying along two perpendicular lines (flip triangles as needed).
>
> The four long sides (hypotenuses) are equal, so the outline is a **rhombus**, with diagonals 2 × l/2 = **l** and **2w**. Its area is ½ × l × 2w = **lw**, the rectangle's area (no card was added or lost).

### 4. Śulba-Sūtras: convert an isosceles trapezium into a rectangle by dissection.

> [!ANSWER]
> Drop perpendiculars from the two ends of the shorter parallel side to the longer one. This cuts off **two identical right triangles** at the ends (the trapezium is isosceles), leaving a rectangle in the middle. Cut one end triangle off, turn it over and fit it against the other one along their slanting sides: together they make a rectangle with the same height. Put it beside the middle rectangle: one long rectangle of height h and length ½(a + b) (the average of the parallel sides), as the area formula says.

### 5. Trapezium ABCD (AB ∥ DC) is turned into rectangle EFGH of equal area. How do you find E, F, G, H?

![Trapezium ABCD with rectangle EFGH; I and J are the midpoints of the slanting sides AD and BC](assets/img/class8/math/14/trap_to_rect.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Mark **I and J, the midpoints** of the slanting sides AD and BC.
> 2. Through I and J draw lines **perpendicular to DC**. They meet line AB (extended if needed) at H and E, and DC at G and F.
>
> Then ∆AHI ≅ ∆DGI (vertically opposite angles at I, right angles at H and G, AI = DI: AAS), and likewise ∆BEJ ≅ ∆CFJ. Each triangle cut off the trapezium is replaced by an identical one added to the rectangle, so the **areas are equal**. (The rectangle's length GF is the average of AB and DC.)

### 6. Using these ideas, construct a trapezium of area 144 cm².

> [!ANSWER]
> We need ½ × h × (a + b) = 144, i.e. h × (a + b) = 288. Choose **h = 12 cm**, so a + b = 24: say **a = 8 cm and b = 16 cm**.
> 1. Draw DC = 16 cm.
> 2. Draw a line parallel to DC, 12 cm above it.
> 3. Mark AB = 8 cm on that line (anywhere above DC) and join AD and BC.
>
> Check: ½ × 12 × (8 + 16) = 144 cm² ✓. Any h, a and b with h × (a + b) = 288 work, for instance h = 9, a = 12, b = 20.

### 7. A regular hexagon is divided into a trapezium, an equilateral triangle and a rhombus, as shown. Find the ratio of their areas.

![A regular hexagon split into its lower half (a trapezium), a small equilateral triangle and a rhombus](assets/img/class8/math/14/hexagon_split.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> A regular hexagon is made of **6 identical equilateral triangles** round its centre. The trapezium is half the hexagon: **3** triangles. The rhombus is **2** triangles. The small triangle is **1**.
>
> Ratio trapezium : triangle : rhombus = **3 : 1 : 2**.

### 8. ZYXW is a trapezium with ZY ∥ WX, and A is the midpoint of XY. ZA extended meets WX extended at B. Show that the trapezium's area equals the area of ∆ZWB.

![Trapezium ZYXW; the line from Z through A, the midpoint of XY, meets WX extended at B](assets/img/class8/math/14/trapezium_triangle.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **∆ZYA ≅ ∆BXA:** YA = XA (A is the midpoint); ∠ZAY = ∠BAX (vertically opposite); ∠ZYA = ∠BXA (alternate angles, since ZY ∥ WB). So they are congruent (ASA) and have **equal areas**.
> 2. Trapezium ZYXW = (quadrilateral ZAXW) + ∆ZYA.
> 3. ∆ZWB = (quadrilateral ZAXW) + ∆BXA.
> 4. The two right-hand sides are equal, so **trapezium ZYXW = ∆ZWB**.
>
> This gives another proof of the trapezium formula: ∆ZWB has base WB = WX + XB = WX + ZY = a + b and height h, so its area is ½h(a + b).

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Rectangle:** length × width. **Triangle:** ½ × base × height (any side as base, with the height to that side).
> - Triangles with the same base between the same parallels have **equal areas**; a median halves a triangle.
> - **Any polygon:** cut it into triangles and add.
> - **Parallelogram:** base × height. **Rhombus:** ½ × d₁ × d₂. **Trapezium:** ½ × h × (a + b).
> - **Dissection** (cut and rearrange) keeps area: parallelogram → rectangle, two trapeziums → parallelogram, isosceles triangle → rectangle.
> - **Units:** 1 in = 2.54 cm, so 1 in² = 6.4516 cm²; 1 ft² = 144 in²; 1 km² = 10⁶ m²; 1 acre = 43,560 ft².

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** perimeter tells you nothing about area • the height must be **perpendicular to the base you chose** (not a slanting side) • for an obtuse triangle the height can fall **outside** • areas convert by the **square** of the length factor (1 ft² = 144 in², not 12) • a parallelogram with sides 5 and 4 is **not** 20 cm² unless it is a rectangle • the trapezium formula adds the **parallel** sides only.
