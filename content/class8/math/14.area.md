## What area actually measures

**Area is the number of unit squares that fit inside a region** (fractions of a square count).

A 7 cm × 4 cm rectangle holds `7 × 4 =` **28 unit squares**, so its area is **28 sq cm** (written `28 cm²`).

**`Area of a rectangle = length × width`**

### Perimeter tells you nothing about area

> [!WARNING]
> **Two regions can have the same perimeter and very different areas — and a region with the BIGGER perimeter can have the SMALLER area.**
>
> **Example:** a 1 × 10 rectangle has perimeter **22** and area **10**. A 5 × 5 square has perimeter **20** and area **25**.
> **Bigger perimeter, smaller area.** ✓
>
> That's why area needs its own measure — counting unit squares — and can't be read off the boundary.

**A thin spiral or a very jagged shape** makes this dramatic: an enormously long boundary can enclose almost nothing.

## Triangles

**`Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height`**

where the **height** is the perpendicular distance from the base to the opposite vertex.

**Why (for a right-ish triangle):** enclose the triangle in a rectangle whose base and height match. **The diagonal splits a rectangle into two congruent triangles**, so each is half.

**Why it still works when the foot of the height falls OUTSIDE the base:**

```
      A
     /|\
    / | \
   D--B--C
```

`Area(ABC) = Area(ADC) − Area(ADB) = ½h·DC − ½h·DB = ½h(DC − DB) = ½ × h × BC` ✓

**The formula therefore holds for every triangle, acute, right or obtuse.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Any side can be the base — you just have to use ITS height.**
>
> That gives a powerful trick: compute the area once with a convenient base, then use the same area with a different base to **find an unknown height**.

**Worked:** in ∆ABC, `AX = 3` is the height to base `BC = 5`, and `AC = 4`.
`Area = ½ × 5 × 3 = 7.5`. Using AC as base: `½ × 4 × BY = 7.5` → **BY = 3.75**.

### Triangles with the same base and the same height

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **All triangles on the same base with their apex on a line parallel to that base have EQUAL AREA** — no matter how slanted they look.
>
> **Why:** base and height are both fixed, and area depends on nothing else.

**Consequence — the median splits a triangle in half.** Joining a vertex to the **midpoint** of the opposite side gives two triangles with **equal bases** and the **same height**, so **equal areas**.

**Consequence — a rectangle's diagonals make four equal-area triangles.** Take triangles 1 and 2 sharing the point O. With OD and OB as bases, `OB = OD` (diagonals bisect each other) and both triangles have the **same height** from A. So their areas match — and the same argument chains round all four.

### Minimum perimeter on a fixed base

Among all triangles on base BC with apex on a parallel line l, **which has the least perimeter?**

**Answer: the one on the perpendicular bisector of BC** — the isosceles one.

**Why (the mirror trick):** BC is common to all of them, so only `AB + AC` matters. **Reflect C across l to get C′.** Then `AC = AC′`, so the path `B → A → C` has the same length as `B → A → C′`.

The shortest route from B to C′ is the **straight line**, so choose A where BC′ crosses l. By symmetry, that point lies on the perpendicular bisector of BC.

> [!TIP]
> **The same reflection trick solves real problems.** "Walk from your house to the river, then to the water tank — what's the shortest route?" **Reflect the tank across the river line, join the house to the reflection with a straight line, and the point where it crosses the river is where you should fetch water.**

## Any polygon

**Every polygon can be cut into triangles**, so knowing the triangle formula is enough for all of them.

A quadrilateral ABCD splits along diagonal AC into two triangles:

**`Area = ½ × AC × BM + ½ × AC × DN = ½ × AC × (BM + DN)`**

where BM and DN are the perpendiculars from B and D onto AC.

**Worked:** `AC = 22`, `BM = DN = 3` → `½ × 22 × (3 + 3) =` **66 cm²**

## Parallelogram

**`Area of a parallelogram = base × height`**

**Why — by dissection.** Drop `AX ⊥ DC`. Cut off ∆AXD and slide it to the other end.

```
 A ______ B          A ______ B
  /      /     →     |      |
 /      /            |      |
D__X____C           X_______C  Y
```

The triangle ∆AXD is **congruent to ∆BYC** by **RHS** (`BY = AX` as opposite sides of a rectangle, right angles at X and Y, `BC = AD` as opposite sides of the parallelogram). So the parallelogram becomes a rectangle of the **same area**.

That rectangle has sides `AX` (the height) and `XY`. And since `DX = CY`, adding the common part XC gives **`DC = XY`** — the base.

**Cutting a figure into pieces and rearranging them into a different figure of equal area is called DISSECTION.**

> [!TIP]
> **Either pair of sides can be the base** — just use the matching height. A parallelogram with sides 5 and 4 might have area `5 × h₁` or `4 × h₂`; both give the same answer.

> [!WARNING]
> **A rectangle always beats a parallelogram with the same side lengths.**
>
> Sides 5 and 4: the rectangle has area **20**; a slanted parallelogram on base 5 has height **less than 4** (the height is a perpendicular, the side is a slant), so its area is **less than 20**.
>
> **Push a rectangle over and it loses area while keeping its perimeter.**

**Worked:** parallelograms of base 7 cm and height 4 cm → **28 cm²**. Base 5 cm, height 3 cm → **15 cm²**.

**Finding a missing height:** in parallelogram PQRS, base `SR = 12 cm` with height `QM = 6 cm`, and side `PS = 7.6 cm`.

`Area = 12 × 6 =` **72 cm²**. Using PS as the base instead: `7.6 × QN = 72` → **QN = 9.47 cm**.

**One area, two bases, two heights** — that's how you find a height you can't measure directly.

## Rhombus

A rhombus **is** a parallelogram, so `base × height` still works. But its perpendicular diagonals give a neater formula.

**`Area of a rhombus = ½ × product of the diagonals`**

**Why (using the triangles):** the diagonals meet at O at right angles, so

```
Area(ADB) = ½ × AO × BD
Area(CDB) = ½ × CO × BD
Total     = ½ × BD × (AO + CO) = ½ × BD × AC
```

**Why (Śulba-Sūtra dissection):** each diagonal splits the rhombus into two **isosceles** triangles; each can be cut and reassembled into a rectangle, and the two rectangles join into one of sides `AC` and `BD/2`. Area `= AC × BD/2` ✓

**Worked:** diagonals 20 cm and 15 cm → `½ × 20 × 15 =` **150 cm²**

## Trapezium

**`Area of a trapezium = ½ × height × (sum of the parallel sides)`**

### Why — method 1: cut into a rectangle and two triangles

Drop perpendiculars WM and XN from the short parallel side onto the long one.

```
    W__a__X
   /|     |\
h / |     | \ h
 /__|_____|__\
Z x  M   N  y  Y
      <-- b -->
```

```
Area = ½hx + ah + ½hy
     = ½h(x + y + 2a)
```

Now `b = x + a + y`, so **`x + y = b − a`**. Substituting:

```
= ½h(b − a + 2a) = ½h(a + b)   ✓
```

### Why — method 2: two copies make a parallelogram

Take a **second copy** of the trapezium, rotate it 180°, and join it to the first along a slant side.

**The join is straight, not bent:** the two angles meeting there are `x` and `y` with `x + y = 180°` (co-interior angles between the parallel sides). So the result is a **quadrilateral**, not a hexagon.

**And it is a parallelogram:** the other two angles also sum to 180°, making both pairs of sides parallel.

That parallelogram has base `a + b` and height h, so

```
Area of trapezium = ½ × Area of parallelogram = ½h(a + b)   ✓
```

> [!TIP]
> **The "two copies" proof is the same trick that gives the triangle formula** (two triangles make a parallelogram) and the sum `1 + 2 + … + n` (two staircases make a rectangle). **Doubling a shape is often the fastest route to its area.**

**Worked areas:**
- parallel sides 10 ft and 7 ft, height 16 ft → `½ × 16 × 17 =` **136 ft²**
- parallel sides 24 m and 36 m, height 14 m → `½ × 14 × 60 =` **420 m²**
- parallel sides 14 in and 6 in, height 10 in → `½ × 10 × 20 =` **100 in²**
- parallel sides 12 ft and 18 ft, height 8 ft → `½ × 8 × 30 =` **120 ft²**

## Śulba-Sūtra transformations

Ancient Indian altar-building demanded shapes of **exact prescribed area**, so the *Śulba-Sūtras* are full of "turn this shape into that one, keeping the area".

| Transform | Method | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Rectangle → triangle** of equal area | keep the base, **double the height** — or keep the height and double the base | `½ × b × 2h = bh` ✓ |
| **Triangle → rectangle** of equal area | cut along the **mid-height line**, rotate the top piece down | gives a rectangle of base b and height `h/2`: `b × h/2 = ½bh` ✓ |
| **Isosceles triangle → rectangle** (simpler) | drop the altitude AD, then swap the two halves ∆ADB and ∆ADC | the two right triangles reassemble into a rectangle `(b/2) × h` |
| **Parallelogram → rectangle** | cut off the end triangle and slide it across | RHS congruence, as proved above |
| **Rhombus → rectangle** | dissect each isosceles half, join the two rectangles | sides become `AC` and `BD/2` |
| **Rectangle → rhombus** of equal area | make a rhombus with diagonals `d₁ = 2 × length`, `d₂ = width` | `½ × 2l × w = lw` ✓ |

## Comparisons worth knowing

**Equilateral triangle vs square of the same side s:**
Triangle area `= (√3/4)s² ≈ 0.433s²`; square area `= s²`.
**The square is bigger** — more than twice.

**Two equilateral triangles vs a square of the same side:** `2 × 0.433s² = 0.866s² < s²` — **the square still wins**.

**Why:** the triangle's height is `(√3/2)s ≈ 0.866s`, shorter than s, and it's a triangle so you halve again.

**Midpoint triangle.** If M and N are the midpoints of XY and XZ, then

`Area(XMN) = ¼ × Area(XYZ)`

**Why:** ∆XMN has half the base (`XM = ½XY`) **and** half the height (since N is the midpoint of XZ), so `½ × ½ =` **¼**.

**Halving a quadrilateral.** Join a vertex to the **midpoint** of the opposite... more simply: draw a diagonal and then a **median** in one of the two triangles — that median splits its triangle into equal halves, giving you exactly half of that piece to add to the other.

**Converting areas:** to go from `in²` to `cm²`, **multiply by 2.54², not by 2.54.**

> [!WARNING]
> **Lengths scale by k; areas scale by k².** Double every length and the area **quadruples**. This is the single most common mistake in area work.
