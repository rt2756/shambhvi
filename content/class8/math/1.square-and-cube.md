## The locker puzzle — why squares are special

100 lockers, 100 people. Person *n* toggles every *n*th locker. **Which lockers end up open?**

- A locker ends **open** if it is toggled an **odd number of times**.
- **A locker is toggled once by each factor of its number** — locker 6 is touched by persons 1, 2, 3 and 6.
- So the question becomes: **which numbers have an odd number of factors?**

**Factors normally come in PAIRS** — for 6, `1 × 6` and `2 × 3`. Each factor has a **partner** whose product gives the number, so factors usually come in twos, giving an **even** count.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The exception is when a factor is its own partner** — `2 × 2 = 4`, `3 × 3 = 9`, `6 × 6 = 36`. That factor has no separate twin, so the count is **odd**.
>
> **Only square numbers have an odd number of factors.** So the open lockers are **1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100**.

And the lockers touched **exactly twice** are the **prime numbers** (factors 1 and itself) — 2, 3, 5, 7, 11.

## Square numbers

**A number that can be expressed as the product of a number with itself is a square.** Written `n × n = n²` ("n squared").

- The squares of natural numbers are **perfect squares**: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, …
- **Why "square":** the area of a square of side n is `n × n` — the number counts unit squares in a square grid.
- Fractions and decimals can be squared too: `(3/5)² = 9/25`, `(2.5)² = 6.25`.

### Last-digit patterns

**Perfect squares end only in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9** — never in **2, 3, 7 or 8**.

> [!WARNING]
> **The test only works one way.** If a number ends in **2, 3, 7 or 8** you can be **certain it is not a square**. But ending in 6 does **not** make it a square — 16 and 36 are squares, **26 is not**.
>
> A one-way test is still useful: it rules things out instantly.

- A number ending in **1 or 9** has a square ending in **1** (`1²=1, 9²=81, 11²=121, 19²=361`).
- **Zeros double up:** a number with **1 zero** at the end has a square with **2**; 2 zeros → 4 zeros. So **squares can only end in an EVEN number of zeros**.
- **Parity is preserved:** an even number's square is even, an odd number's square is odd.

### Squares are sums of odd numbers

```
1                     = 1  = 1²
1 + 3                 = 4  = 2²
1 + 3 + 5             = 9  = 3²
1 + 3 + 5 + 7         = 16 = 4²
```

**The sum of the first n odd numbers is n².**

**Why (visual proof):** a square grid of dots peels into **inverted L-shaped layers**, and each successive L holds the next odd number of dots. Because the picture works for any size, the rule holds forever.

> [!TIP]
> **This gives a TEST for perfect squares:** subtract 1, 3, 5, 7, … in turn. Reach **exactly 0** and it's a perfect square — and the **number of subtractions is the square root**.
>
> `25 − 1 = 24 − 3 = 21 − 5 = 16 − 7 = 9 − 9 = 0` — five steps, so `√25 = 5` ✓
> `38` overshoots into negatives, so **38 is not a perfect square**.

**Jumping to the next square:** the **nth odd number is `2n − 1`**. Given `35² = 1225`, the 36th odd number is `2×36 − 1 = 71`, so `36² = 1225 + 71 = ` **1296**.

### Squares and triangular numbers

**Adding two consecutive triangular numbers gives a square:**
`1 + 3 = 4` · `3 + 6 = 9` · `6 + 10 = 16`

## Square roots

**If `y = x²`, then x is the square root of y**, written `√y`.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Every perfect square has TWO integer square roots — one positive, one negative.** `8² = 64` and `(−8)² = 64`, so `√64 = ±8`.
>
> (In this chapter we take only the **positive** root.)

### Finding a square root by prime factorisation

**Split the prime factors into two identical groups.** If you can, the number is a perfect square and each group's product is the root.

- `324 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = (2 × 3 × 3) × (2 × 3 × 3) = 18²` → **√324 = 18** ✓
- `156 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 13` — the 3 and 13 have no partners → **not a perfect square**.

> [!TIP]
> Equivalently, **pair up the prime factors**. Every prime must appear an **even** number of times.

### Estimating a square root

To find `√1936`:

1. **Bracket it:** `40² = 1600` and `50² = 2500`, so the answer is **between 40 and 50**.
2. **Use the last digit:** 1936 ends in 6, so the root ends in **4 or 6** → 44 or 46.
3. **Halve the interval:** `45² = 2025 > 1936`, so the root is **below 45**.
4. → **44** ✓

**For non-squares, estimate:** `√250` — since `15² = 225` and `16² = 256`, it lies between 15 and 16, and **much closer to 16**.

**Practical use:** a square cloth of area 125 cm². Since `11² = 121` and `12² = 144`, the largest square handkerchief with a **whole-number** side is **11 cm**.

## Cubic numbers

**A number multiplied by itself three times: `n × n × n = n³`.**

**Perfect cubes:** 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1000, …

- **Why "cube":** `4³ = 64` counts the unit cubes in a cube of edge 4 — each layer is `4 × 4 = 16` cubes, and there are 4 layers.
- 9 is **not** a cube, nor is any number from 10 to 26 (they sit between `2³ = 8` and `3³ = 27`).
- Fractions, decimals and negatives can be cubed: `(−6)³ = −216`.

> [!TIP]
> **Cubes can end in ANY digit** — unlike squares, which are restricted to 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9. So the last-digit test that rules out squares gives you nothing for cubes.

### Cubes are sums of consecutive odd numbers too

```
1                       = 1   = 1³
3 + 5                   = 8   = 2³
7 + 9 + 11              = 27  = 3³
13 + 15 + 17 + 19       = 64  = 4³
21 + 23 + 25 + 27 + 29  = 125 = 5³
```

**The nth cube is the sum of n consecutive odd numbers**, continuing from where the last group stopped.

### Cube roots

**If `y = x³`, then x is the cube root of y**, written `∛y`.

**By prime factorisation — split the factors into THREE identical groups** (or group them in **triplets**):

- `3375 = 3 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 5 × 5 = (3 × 5)³ = 15³` → **∛3375 = 15** ✓
- `500 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 5` — can't make three identical groups → **not a perfect cube**.

> [!NOTE]
> **The Hardy–Ramanujan number, 1729.** G.H. Hardy remarked that his taxicab number 1729 seemed "rather a dull number". **Srinivasa Ramanujan** replied at once that it is **the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways**:
>
> `1729 = 1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³`
>
> Such numbers are now called **taxicab numbers** — the next two are **4104** and **13832**. Of Ramanujan, his colleague John Littlewood said, *"Every positive integer was one of his personal friends."*
