## What a percentage really is

**"Per cent" is Latin *per centum* — "out of a hundred".**

So `25%` means **25 out of every 100** — 25 rupees per 100 rupees, 25 marks per 100 marks, 25 people per 100 people.

**A percentage is just a fraction with denominator 100.**

```
20% = 20/100 = 1/5
33% = 33/100
50% = 50/100 = 1/2
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **`y% of z` means `(y/100) × z`.** Everything in this chapter comes from that one sentence.

## Converting between fractions, decimals and percentages

**Fraction → percentage: multiply by 100.**

`3/4 = (3/4) × 100 =` **75%**

**Why:** a fraction tells you the share of **1**; a percentage tells you the share of **100**. Scaling from 1 to 100 means multiplying by 100.

Or find the equivalent fraction with denominator 100:
`3/4 = 6/8 = 30/40 = 75/100 =` **75%**

**Percentage → fraction: put it over 100 and simplify.**
`24% = 24/100 = 6/25`

| Per cent | 50% | 100% | 25% | 75% | 10% | 1% | 5% | 43% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fraction | 1/2 | 1 | 1/4 | 3/4 | 1/10 | 1/100 | 1/20 | 43/100 |
| Decimal | 0.5 | 1.0 | 0.25 | 0.75 | 0.1 | 0.01 | 0.05 | 0.43 |

### Why 100 and not some other number?

You *could* use "per 10" or "per 1000". But:

- Our number system is **base 10**, so 100 converts to decimals instantly (`31% = 0.31`).
- 100 is **big enough for detail** (per 10 is too coarse) and **small enough to picture**.

> [!NOTE]
> The idea of "per hundred" is ancient. **Kauṭilya's Arthaśhāstra** (4th century BCE) sets interest rates as "panas per month per cent". Roman traders used taxes of 1/20 and 1/100. Italian manuscripts of the 1400s write "xx p cento" for 20%.

### Why percentages beat raw fractions

Which biscuit is sweeter: sugar is `9/34` of one and `13/45` of the other?

Hard to say. As percentages — **26.47%** and **28.88%** — it's obvious at a glance.

**That's the whole point: a common denominator makes comparison instant.**

## Percentage of a quantity

**`y% of z = (y/100) × z`**

**Worked:** 25% sugar in 120 g of biscuits → `(25/100) × 120 =` **30 g**
**Worked:** 35% sugar in 95 g of biscuits → `(35/100) × 95 =` **33.25 g**

> [!WARNING]
> **Never compare percentages of different wholes.** 25% and 35% sound decisive, but 25% of 120 g **beats** 35% of 70 g. **A percentage without its base is meaningless.**

### Mental shortcuts

| Want | Do this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | halve it | `50/100 = 1/2` |
| 25% | quarter it | `25/100 = 1/4` |
| 10% | move the decimal one place | `10/100 = 1/10` |
| 20% | double the 10% | 20 parts is twice 10 parts |
| 5% | halve the 10% | 5 parts is half of 10 |
| 15% | 10% + 5% | `10/100 + 5/100 = 15/100` |
| 40% | 4 × the 10% | |
| 75% | 50% + 25% | |
| 90% | whole − 10% | |

**Why adding works:** `(20/100)y + (5/100)y = (25/100)y` — same denominator, so the numerators just add.

**Worked:** 15% of 287 → 10% is 28.7, 5% is 14.35 → **43.05**

> [!TIP]
> **x% of y always equals y% of x.**
> `5% of 40 = 2` and `40% of 5 = 2` ✓
>
> **Why:** `(x/100) × y = xy/100 = (y/100) × x`. Multiplication doesn't care about order — so flip whichever way is easier. `4% of 75` is horrid; `75% of 4 = 3` is instant.

## Ratios → percentages

Millet : water in kanji is `2 : 7`.

The millet's share of the **whole** is `2/(2+7) = 2/9`.
As a percentage: `(2/9) × 100 =` **22.22%**. Water is `100 − 22.22 =` **77.78%**.

For 500 mL of mixture: `22.22% of 500 =` **111.1 mL of millet**.

> [!WARNING]
> **Ratio terms are parts-to-parts; percentages are part-to-whole.** With `2 : 7` you must first add to get the whole (9) before dividing. Writing `2/7 × 100` is the classic error.

## Percentages above 100

A shopkeeper's daily target is ₹5,000.

| Sales | % of target | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ₹2,000 | 40% | 60% **short** |
| ₹3,500 | 70% | 30% short |
| ₹5,000 | 100% | exactly on target |
| ₹6,000 | 120% | **20% more** than target |
| ₹7,800 | 156% | |
| ₹9,550 | 191% | |

**Above 100% just means "more than the whole you're comparing to".**

`250%` = **2.5 times** the original. A harvest going from 260 kg to 650 kg is `650/260 × 100 =` **250% of last year's**.

> [!TIP]
> **Translate freely:** "half" = 50% = 0.5; "double" = 200% = 2.0; "one and a half times" = 150% = 1.5.

## Percentage increase and decrease

```
percentage increase = (amount of increase / ORIGINAL amount) × 100
percentage decrease = (amount of decrease / ORIGINAL amount) × 100
```

**Worked:** tomatoes ₹30 → ₹42. Increase ₹12. `12/30 × 100 =` **40% increase**.
**Worked:** footfall 160 → 100. Decrease 60. `60/160 × 100 =` **37.5% decrease**.

> [!WARNING]
> **The base is always the ORIGINAL value, not the new one.** Going 30 → 42 is a 40% rise, but coming back 42 → 30 is a `12/42 =` **28.6% fall**. Up 40% and down 40% do **not** cancel.

**Two ways of saying the same thing:**
"1991 population is **165% of** 1961" = "population **increased by 65%**".
Both mean `q = 1.65p`.

> [!TIP]
> **The multiplier shortcut.** Up r% → multiply by `(1 + r/100)`. Down r% → multiply by `(1 − r/100)`.
> Up 5% → × **1.05** · Down 15% → × **0.85** · Up 120% → × **2.2**

## Profit and loss

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cost price (CP)** | what the seller paid |
| **Marked price (MP)** | the price displayed / MRP |
| **Selling price (SP)** | what the customer actually pays |

- `SP > CP` → **profit** = `SP − CP`
- `SP < CP` → **loss** = `CP − SP`

```
profit % = (profit / COST PRICE) × 100
loss %   = (loss   / COST PRICE) × 100
discount % = (discount / MARKED PRICE) × 100
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Profit and loss percentages use the COST price as base. Discount uses the MARKED price.** Mixing them up is the commonest mistake in the chapter.

**Worked:** buy at ₹300, sell at ₹430. Profit ₹130. `130/300 × 100 =` **43.3% profit**.
**Worked:** 10 kg rice cost ₹350, sold for ₹300. Loss ₹50. `50/350 × 100 =` **14.28% loss**.

**Working backwards.** A vase cost ₹2,650, sold at 18% loss.
SP is `100 − 18 = 82%` of CP → `0.82 × 2650 =` **₹2,173**.

**Finding CP from SP.** Sold at ₹80/kg with 12% loss. Then `0.88 × CP = 80` → `CP = 80/0.88 =` **₹90.91**.

**Gross vs net profit.** Sales ₹80,000, goods cost ₹48,000 → **gross profit ₹32,000**. After ₹8,000 of other expenses (transport, salary, electricity) → **net profit ₹24,000**.

> [!TIP]
> **Profit % on cost vs profit % on revenue are different numbers.** Buy at ₹500, sell at ₹750:
> on **cost** → `250/500 =` **50%**; on **revenue** → `250/750 =` **33.33%**.
> "How much did I gain on what I invested?" uses cost. "What share of my sales is profit?" uses revenue.

## Growth and compounding

**Interest** is the extra money paid for the use of money — by a bank on your deposit, or by you on a loan. **p.a.** = *per annum*, per year.

**Principal p, rate r (as a decimal), t terms.**

### Without compounding (simple)

The interest is paid out each year, so **the principal never changes**.

```
interest per term = p × r
total interest    = p × r × t
final amount      = p + prt = p(1 + rt)
```

### With compounding

The interest is **added back**, so next year's interest is earned on a bigger principal.

```
after 1 year:  p(1 + r)
after 2 years: p(1 + r)(1 + r)
after t years: p(1 + r)^t
```

**Why it grows faster:** you earn interest **on the interest**. That's why it's a repeated **multiplication**, not a repeated addition.

**Worked:** ₹6,000 at 10% p.a. for 3 years.

| | Simple | Compound |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | +600 → 6600 | ×1.1 → 6600 |
| Year 2 | +600 → 7200 | ×1.1 → 7260 |
| Year 3 | +600 → 7800 | ×1.1 → **7986** |
| Growth | `6000 × 1.3` = **130%** | `6000 × 1.331` = **133.1%** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Simple interest is LINEAR growth; compound interest is EXPONENTIAL growth.**
>
> Simple adds a fixed amount each year — a straight line. Compound multiplies by a fixed factor each year — a curve that gets steeper. Over long periods the gap becomes enormous.

## Decline and depreciation

Things lose value with use and age. **Depreciation** works exactly like compounding, but the multiplier is **below 1**.

**Worked:** a ₹21,000 TV depreciating 5% a year.
After 1 year: `0.95 × 21000 =` **₹19,950**

**Worked:** a village of 1,250 losing 10% each decade.
After 3 decades: `1250 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 =` **911** ≈ **910 people**

> [!WARNING]
> **You cannot just subtract 30% for three 10% falls.** Each 10% is taken off a *smaller* number than the last. `1250 × 0.9³ = 911`, not `1250 − 375 = 875`.

## Tricky percentages

### "30% + 20% off" is NOT 50% off

On a ₹200 cake:

- **Cakely (30% then 20%):** `200 → 140 → 112`
- **Cakify (50%):** `200 → 100`

**Cakify is cheaper.** The second discount is taken off the **already-reduced** price, so it's worth less.

`0.7 × 0.8 = 0.56`, i.e. an effective **44% discount** — not 50%.

### The 50%-margin, 50%-discount mishap

A shopkeeper buys at x, marks up 50% to `1.5x`, then offers 50% off: `0.5 × 1.5x =` **0.75x**.

She sells at **three-quarters of what she paid** — a **25% loss**, not break-even!

**Why:** the 50% markup is on the *small* number x; the 50% discount is on the *big* number 1.5x. **The same percentage of a bigger base is a bigger amount.**

**Break-even discount:** need `1.5x × (1 − d) = x` → `1 − d = 2/3` → `d =` **33.33%**.

### Percentage or fixed amount?

A shop offers "20% off **or** ₹50 off". Which is better?

- ₹180 purchase: 20% = ₹36 → **take the ₹50**
- ₹225 purchase: 20% = ₹45 → **take the ₹50**
- ₹300 purchase: 20% = ₹60 → **take the 20%**

**Break-even at ₹250**, where both give ₹50.

### Successive changes multiply

Fares up 3% then 4%: `1.03 × 1.04 = 1.0712` → **7.12% overall**, not 7%.

Mice population `+5%, −2%, −3%`: `p × 1.05 × 0.98 × 0.97 = 0.998p` → **slightly less than p**.

> [!TIP]
> **Always chain the multipliers.** Adding percentages of different bases is wrong; multiplying the factors is right.

### The 99% left-handed puzzle

100 people, 99 left-handed. How many left-handers must leave to make it 98%?

The **1 right-hander never leaves**, and must become **2%** of the room. So the room must end with `1 ÷ 0.02 =` **50 people**, meaning 49 left-handers must go.

**Answer: 49** — not 1, which is everyone's first guess.

> [!TIP]
> **Anchor on the quantity that doesn't change.** Here it's the single right-handed person.

### Length up 10%, area unchanged

If length goes up 10% and area stays the same, the breadth multiplier must satisfy `1.1 × m = 1` → `m = 1/1.1 = 0.909…`

So the breadth falls by **9.09%** (which is `1/11`), **not 10%**.

## Worked problems

**Grade needed.** Max marks 75, need 80%. `0.8 × 75 =` **60 marks**.

**Journey.** A cyclist has done 40% of the trip, covering 92 km. Total `= 92 × 100/40 = 230 km`, so **138 km remain** (or directly: 60% is `92 × 60/40 = 138`).

**Discounted price backwards.** A car bought for ₹4,40,000 after 15% discount. `0.85 × MP = 440000` → `MP =` **₹5,17,647**.

**Number from a percentage.** A number increased by 20% becomes 90. `1.2x = 90` → `x =` **75**.

**Two buffaloes.** Sold at ₹80,000 each, one at 5% profit, one at 10% loss.
CP₁ `= 80000/1.05 = ₹76,190` · CP₂ `= 80000/0.90 = ₹88,889`
Total CP ₹1,65,079 vs total SP ₹1,60,000 → an **overall loss of about ₹5,079**.

> [!TIP]
> **Equal selling prices with equal-and-opposite percentages always give a net LOSS.** The loss is taken on a bigger cost price than the profit.

**Rate of work.** Workers pick 20% of a plantation in 18 days. The whole thing takes `18 × 5 =` **90 days** — *assuming the rate stays constant*, which matters because tiredness, weather and ripeness all change real picking speed.

**Two-step composition.** 40% of excursion students are Grade 8, and 60% of those are girls.
Grade 8 girls `= 40% × 60% = 0.4 × 0.6 =` **24% of all students**. Of 160 students that's **38 or 39** (24% of 160 = 38.4).

**Pencils.** SP of 3 = CP of 5. Take CP = ₹1 each: 3 pencils sell for ₹5, cost ₹3.
Profit `= 2/3 × 100 =` **66.67% profit**.

**Buy-one-get-one deals.** With items at ₹100:

| Shop | Deal | Pay for | Receive | Effective price | Discount |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | buy 1 get 1 | 1 | 2 | ₹50 | **50%** |
| B | buy 2 get 1 | 2 | 3 | ₹66.67 | **33.3%** |
| C | buy 3 get 1 | 3 | 4 | ₹75 | **25%** |

**Shop A is cheapest.** For exactly 4 items: A → pay for 2 = ₹200 ✓ cheapest.

**GST.** A ₹8,250 phone with 18% GST costs `8250 × 1.18 =` **₹9,735** — equivalently `8250 + 8250 × 0.18`.
