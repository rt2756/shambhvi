## What this chapter is about

"Mega sale, up to 50% off!" "She scored 83%." "Prices rose 40%." A **percentage** is just a **fraction in disguise**: a fraction whose denominator is 100. This chapter shows how to:

- turn fractions, decimals and ratios into percentages and back;
- find a percentage of a quantity, and the whole from a part;
- use percentages for comparing, for increase and decrease, profit and loss, discounts and taxes;
- see how money **grows** with interest, with and without **compounding**;
- avoid the traps (30% + 20% off is **not** 50% off).

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** **Per cent** (%) means "out of every hundred" (from the Latin *per centum*). The **base** or **whole** is the quantity a percentage is taken of. **Cost price (CP)** is what a seller paid, **marked price (MP)** is the price on the tag, **selling price (SP)** is what the buyer actually pays. **Principal** is money deposited or borrowed; **interest** is the extra paid for it; **p.a.** (per annum) means per year.

## 8.1 Fractions as percentages

25% means 25 out of every 100. So **x% = x/100**, a fraction with denominator 100.
- 50% of a quantity s = (50/100) × s = ½ s
- 20% = 20/100 = 1/5, and 33% = 33/100

**Example 1: 3/4 as a percentage.** Surya's orange paint is 3/4 red. 3/4 means 3 out of every 4, which is 6 out of 8, 30 out of 40, **75 out of 100**. So 3/4 = **75%**.

![A bar split into quarters: 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1 match 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%](assets/img/class8/math/8/three_quarters.svg)

Two ways to find the "out of 100" fraction:
- **Multiply top and bottom** by what turns the denominator into 100: 3/4 = (3 × 25)/(4 × 25) = 75/100.
- **Solve** 3/4 = x/100: multiply both sides by 100, x = (3/4) × 100 = 75.

**Example 2: 2/5 as a percentage.** 2/5 = 4/10 = **40/100 = 40%**. Or x = (2/5) × 100 = 40.

![A bar split into fifths: 1/5, 2/5, … match 20%, 40%, …; the first two fifths are the savings](assets/img/class8/math/8/two_fifths.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Fraction → percentage: multiply the fraction by 100.** **Percentage → fraction: write it over 100 and simplify.** 24% = 24/100 = 12/50 = 6/25.
> **Why:** a fraction is "how much of **one** whole"; a percentage is "how much of **100**". Multiplying by 100 changes "per 1" into "per 100".

**Why use percentages at all?** Biscuit 1 is 9/34 sugar and biscuit 2 is 13/45 sugar. Which is sweeter? Hard to tell. As percentages, 26.47% and 28.89%: clearly biscuit 2. A **common denominator** makes comparing easy, and 100 is a good choice because our numbers are in base 10 (31% = 31/100 = 0.31), and 100 is big enough to show detail but small enough to picture.

> [!NOTE]
> The idea of "per hundred" is old. Kautilya's *Arthaśāstra* (about the 4th century BCE) gives interest rates "per month per cent": a pana and a quarter for fair lending, more for traders crossing forests or seas. Romans taxed trade at 1/20 and 1/100, and 15th-century Italian manuscripts wrote "xx p cento" for 20%.

**Try this.** Play "How close can you get?" with a partner. Each says a number; for the two numbers a < b, both quickly estimate a/b as a percentage (5 seconds). Then work it out. Closest estimate wins the round. Play 10 rounds.

## 8.2 A percentage of some quantity

**Example 3: who ate more sugar?** Madhu ate 120 g of biscuits with 25% sugar. Madhav ate 95 g with 35% sugar. Comparing 25% and 35% isn't enough: the wholes are different.

- **Madhu:** 25% = 1/4, and 1/4 of 120 g = **30 g**. (Or 25 g per 100 g = 5 g per 20 g = 30 g per 120 g.)
- **Madhav:** 35 g per 100 g = 0.35 g per 1 g, so 95 g has 0.35 × 95 = **33.25 g**.

![A double number line: 0, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% match 0, 30, 60, 90, 120 g](assets/img/class8/math/8/sugar_120.svg)

**Madhav ate more sugar**, even though he ate fewer biscuits.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **y% of a quantity Q = (y/100) × Q.**
> **Why:** y% means y parts in every 100, so each 1 unit of Q holds y/100 of the part, and Q units hold (y/100) × Q. Equivalently, solve the proportion y : 100 :: part : Q.

### Working in your head

**Try this.** Make cards for 10%, 25%, 50%, 5% and 1% and cards for 40, 60, 120, 360 and 800. Draw one of each and say the answer aloud within 5 seconds. Swap roles with a friend.


Some percentages are easy fractions: **50% = ½, 25% = ¼, 10% = 1/10, 20% = 1/5, 5% = 1/20, 1% = 1/100**. Build others from these:
- 20% is double 10%; 5% is half of 10%.
- 25% = 20% + 5%; 15% = 10% + 5%; 75% = 50% + 25%; 90% = 100% − 10%.

**Why adding works:** (20/100) × y + (5/100) × y = (25/100) × y by the distributive property.

### The FDP trio: fractions, decimals, percentages

50% = 50/100 = ½ = **0.5**, so finding 50% of 24 is ½ × 24 or 0.5 × 24 = 12. In general **y% = y/100 = the decimal 0.0y** (move the point two places left): 10% = 0.1, 43% = 0.43, 120% = 1.2.

**Example 4: marks for an A.** A test is out of 75; an A needs 80% or more. 80% of 75 = (4/5) × 75 = **60**, or 0.8 × 75 = 60, or "80 out of 100, so 75 × 80/100 = 60 out of 75".

![A double number line from 0 to 75 marks: 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% match 15, 30, 45, 60](assets/img/class8/math/8/marks_75.svg)

**Example 5: millet porridge.** Millet and water are mixed 2 : 7. What percentage is millet, and how much millet is in 500 mL?

![9 equal parts: 2 of millet and 7 of water](assets/img/class8/math/8/millet.svg)

1. Millet is 2 parts out of 2 + 7 = 9, so **2/9** of the mixture.
2. **Estimate first:** 10% of 9 is 0.9, so 20% is 1.8 and 25% is 2.25. Since 2 is between 1.8 and 2.25, the answer is between 20% and 25%.
3. Exactly: (2/9) × 100 = 22.22…%. Water is 100 − 22.22 = 77.78%.
4. In 500 mL: (2/9) × 500 = 111.1 mL of millet (or 5 × 22.22 = 111.1).

**Example 6: how far to Agra?** A cyclist has done 40% of the trip from Delhi to Agra, which is 92 km. How far is left?

![A bar: the first 40% is 92 km done; the remaining 60% is 138 km; the whole is 230 km](assets/img/class8/math/8/cyclist.svg)

- **Halving:** 40% is 92 km, so 20% is 46 km and the remaining 60% is 3 × 46 = **138 km**.
- **Whole first:** 40% of d = 92, so d = 92 × 100/40 = 230 km, and 230 − 92 = **138 km**.
- **Rule of Three:** 40 : 92 :: 60 : r, so r = 92 × 60/40 = **138 km**.

> [!TIP]
> **Estimate before you calculate** and **draw a rough bar.** The bar shows what is known (40% = 92) and what is asked (the other 60%), and the estimate catches silly mistakes.

### Percentages greater than 100

Kishanlal aims to sell ₹5000 a day. Day 1: ₹2000 = (2000/5000) × 100 = 40% of the target (60% short). Day 4: ₹6000 = 120% of the target: the whole 100% plus 1000 more, and 1000 is 20% of 5000.

![Bars for six days of sales against a ₹5000 target: 40%, 70%, 100%, 120%, 156%, 191%](assets/img/class8/math/8/sales_target.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A percentage above 100 means "more than the whole".** 120% = 1.2 times, 200% = double, 250% = 2.5 times. A farmer who harvested 260 kg last year and 650 kg this year harvested (650/260) × 100 = **250%** of last year's crop.

## 8.3 Using percentages

### To compare

**Example 7: which test went better?** Eesha scored 42/50 in English and 70/80 in Science. She lost fewer marks in English but scored more in Science. As percentages: English (42/50) × 100 = **84%**, Science (70/80) × 100 = **87.5%**. **Science went better**: percentages compare scores out of different totals fairly.

**Know your contents.** Two badam (almond) drink mixes list:

| Mix | Sugar | Milk solids | Badam powder | Food chemicals | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mix P | 99 g | 30 g | 12 g | 9 g | 150 g |
| Mix Q | 272 g | 64 g | 40 g | 24 g | 400 g |

Mix P's sugar share is (99/150) × 100 = 66%. To compare the packs, turn every amount into a percentage of its own total.

### Increase and decrease

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Percentage change = (amount of change ÷ original amount) × 100.**
> **Why the original:** "by how much did it change?" is measured against where it started. Tomatoes going from ₹30 to ₹42 rose by ₹12, and 12/30 = **40%**. A theatre's crowd falling from 160 to 100 fell by 60, and 60/160 = **37.5%**.

"Is 165% of" and "increased by 65%" say the same thing: q = 1.65p in both cases (p + 0.65p = 1.65p).

## 8.4 Profit, loss and tax


A sweater travels from maker to wholesaler to shop to customer. Each seller's **selling price** becomes the next one's **cost price**:

![Maker: cost ₹230, marked ₹255, sold ₹253, profit ₹23 = 10%. Wholesaler: cost ₹253, marked ₹310, sold ₹300, profit ₹47 = 18.6%. Shop: cost ₹300, marked ₹480, sold ₹430, profit ₹130 = 43.3%](assets/img/class8/math/8/sweater_chain.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Profit = SP − CP; loss = CP − SP. Profit % (or loss %) = profit (or loss) ÷ CP × 100.**
> **Why the cost price:** it answers "how much did I gain compared with what I paid?". The shop's ₹130 profit on a ₹300 sweater is 130/300 = **43.3%**.

**Example 8: a loss.** Raghu bought 10 kg of rice at ₹35/kg (₹350) and sold it for ₹300. Loss = ₹50, and 50/350 × 100 = **14.29%**.

**Example 9: selling at an 18% loss.** A vase cost ₹2650. Selling at an 18% loss means getting 100% − 18% = **82%** of the cost: 0.82 × 2650 = **₹2173**. (Or: the loss is 0.18 × 2650 = ₹477, and 2650 − 477 = ₹2173.)

A **discount** is a cut in the **marked price**: "30% off" means you pay 70% of the tag.

**Gross and net profit.** If Kishanlal sells ₹80,000 of goods that cost him ₹48,000, his **gross profit** is ₹32,000. After other costs (transport, salaries, electricity) of ₹8,000, his **net profit** is ₹24,000. (In this chapter, "profit" means gross profit.) Businesses also quote profit as a percentage of **sales** (revenue): a fertiliser seller who buys at ₹500 and sells at ₹750 makes 250/500 = 50% on cost but 250/750 = 33.33% of what she earns.

## 8.5 Growth and compounding

A bank pays **10% p.a.** on a fixed deposit (FD). Deposit ₹6000 (the **principal**) for a year and you get 10% of 6000 = ₹600 of **interest**: ₹6600 in all, which is 110% of 6000, or 6000 × 1.1.

For 3 years there are two kinds of FD:
- **No compounding:** the ₹600 interest is paid out each year, and the principal stays ₹6000. Total after 3 years: 6000 + 3 × 600 = **₹7800**.
- **Compounding:** each year's interest is **added to the principal**, so next year's interest is bigger: 6000 → 6600 → 7260 → **₹7986**.

![Bars for years 0 to 3: without compounding 6000, 6600, 7200, 7800; with compounding 6000, 6600, 7260, 7986](assets/img/class8/math/8/compounding.svg)

7800/6000 = 1.3 (a 30% gain) but 7986/6000 = 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1 = 1.331 (a **33.1%** gain).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> For principal p, yearly rate r (as a decimal, so 10% → 0.1) and t years:
> - **No compounding:** amount = **p + p × r × t = p(1 + rt)**; interest = prt.
> - **Compounding:** amount = **p × (1 + r) × (1 + r) × … (t times) = p(1 + r)ᵗ**; interest = p(1 + r)ᵗ − p.
> **Why:** without compounding, the same interest pr is added each year (adding → **linear** growth). With compounding, each year multiplies the whole amount by (1 + r) (multiplying → **exponential** growth), because interest earns interest.

**Decline works the same way, downwards.** A ₹21,000 TV that **depreciates** 5% in a year is worth 95% of 21,000 = 0.95 × 21,000 = **₹19,950**. A village of 1250 people that shrinks 10% each decade has 1250 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 ≈ **911** people after 3 decades.

### Check yourself

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Without compounding:** interest = **p × r × t** (the same pr each year, t times).
> - **With compounding:** interest = final amount − principal = **p(1 + r)ᵗ − p**.
>
> For ₹6000 at 10% for 3 years: 6000 × 0.1 × 3 = ₹1800, and 6000 × 1.331 − 6000 = ₹1986.

## 8.6 Tricky percentages

> [!WARNING]
> **A percentage is a share, not an amount.** Always ask "percentage **of what**?"
> - ₹100 → ₹300 is a 200% gain; ₹1000 → ₹1500 is only a 50% gain, but it gives you **₹500**, more than the ₹200.
> - **30% + 20% off is not 50% off.** The 20% is taken off the already reduced price: a ₹200 cake → ₹140 → ₹112, while 50% off gives ₹100.
> - **Up 50% then down 50% is a loss.** Price x → 1.5x → 0.75x: a 25% loss.

**Example 10: a costly mistake.** Surbhi marks her goods 50% above cost, then offers 50% off to clear stock, thinking she will break even.
1. Cost x → marked 1.5x → after 50% off, 0.75x. She sells at **75% of cost: a 25% loss**.
2. If she took ₹12,000 this way: 0.75x = 12,000, so x = ₹16,000 and she **lost ₹4,000** (25%).
3. To break even, the discount d must bring 1.5x back to x: 1.5x × (1 − d) = x, so 1 − d = 2/3 and **d = 1/3 ≈ 33.33%**.

### Check yourself

**1.** Option A: deposit ₹100, get back ₹300. Option B: deposit ₹1000, get back ₹1500. What percentage gain is each? Which would you choose (once only)?

> [!ANSWER]
> - A: gain ₹200 on ₹100 = **200%**.
> - B: gain ₹500 on ₹1000 = **50%**.
>
> A has the bigger **percentage**, but B gives more **money** (₹500 vs ₹200). If you have ₹1000 to put in, **B** leaves you richer. If you only have ₹100, A is the one you can afford. Percentages compare proportions, not amounts.

**2.** A shop offers either 20% off or ₹50 off on purchases above ₹150. Which is better for a bill of (i) ₹180 (ii) ₹225 (iii) ₹300?

> [!ANSWER]
> 20% off saves 20% of the bill; ₹50 off always saves ₹50. They are equal when 20% of the bill = 50, i.e. a bill of **₹250**.
> - (i) ₹180: 20% = ₹36 → choose **₹50 off**.
> - (ii) ₹225: 20% = ₹45 → choose **₹50 off**.
> - (iii) ₹300: 20% = ₹60 → choose **20% off**.

**3.** Ariba says, "I have 120% of Arun's marbles." What could Arun say about his marbles compared with Ariba's?

> [!ANSWER]
> If Arun has a marbles, Ariba has 1.2a. Then Arun has a/(1.2a) = 1/1.2 = 5/6 ≈ **83.33% of Ariba's marbles**: "I have about 83% of yours", or "I have 1/6 fewer than you".
>
> **Not** "I have 80% of yours": the 20% was of **Arun's** number, so it is a different share of Ariba's. For instance, 10 and 12 marbles: 12 is 120% of 10, but 10 is 83⅓% of 12.

![Horizontal bars by age group, female and male: children 4% and about 5%, teenage 24% and 29%, twenties 26% and 37%, thirties 14% and 25%, forties 7% and 14%, fifties 4% and 9%, seniors 2% and 4%](assets/img/class8/math/8/computer_use.svg)

(i) People in their twenties are the most computer-literate. (ii) Women lag behind across age groups. (iii) There are more people in their twenties than teenagers. (iv) More than a quarter of people in their thirties can use computers. (v) Fewer than 1 in 10 people aged 60+ can. (vi) Half of the people in their twenties can.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **(i) Valid:** twenties have the highest bars for both women (26%) and men (37%).
> - **(ii) Valid:** the female bar is shorter in every group.
> - **(iii) Not valid:** the graph shows **percentages** of each group, not how many people are in each group.
> - **(iv) Not valid:** 14% of women and exactly 25% of men, so overall less than a quarter.
> - **(v) Valid:** 2% and 4%, both well under 10%.
> - **(vi) Not valid:** 26% and 37%, both well under 50%.

### 16. Peaceful knights: place 8 knights on a chessboard so that no knight attacks another. (A knight moves 2 squares one way and 1 square at right angles, an "L".)

> [!ANSWER]
> Put all 8 knights in **one row**:
>
> ![A chessboard with 8 knights on the bottom row](assets/img/class8/math/8/knights.svg)
>
> **Why it works:** every knight move changes the row by 1 or 2, so a knight can never attack a square in its own row. (Another way: put knights only on squares of one colour; a knight's move always lands on the other colour. You could fit 32 knights that way!)

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **x% = x/100.** Fraction → % : × 100. % → decimal: ÷ 100 (50% = 0.5, 120% = 1.2).
> - **y% of Q = (y/100) × Q.** Build mental percentages from 50%, 25%, 10%, 5%, 1%.
> - **Whole from a part:** if 40% is 92, then 1% is 2.3 and 100% is 230.
> - **% change = change ÷ original × 100.** Profit % and loss % use the **cost price**.
> - Increase by r% → × (1 + r/100); decrease by r% → × (1 − r/100). Successive changes **multiply**.
> - **Interest:** no compounding p(1 + rt) (linear); compounding p(1 + r)ᵗ (exponential).
> - Percentages above 100% mean more than the whole: 250% = 2.5 times.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** always ask "% **of what**?" • 30% + 20% off is **44%** off, not 50% • +50% then −50% is a **25% loss** • a 20% rise to 90 starts from 75, not 72 • percentage increase divides by the **original**, not the new value • 120% of Arun's is not "Arun has 80%" • a bigger percentage can mean a smaller amount.
