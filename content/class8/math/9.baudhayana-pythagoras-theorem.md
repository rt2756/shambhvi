## What this chapter is about

About 2800 years ago, in India, the mathematician **Baudhāyana** wrote a *Śulba-Sūtra*: a handbook of rules for building fire altars of exact shapes and sizes. Altar builders needed to make a square **twice** as big as another, or a square as big as **two** squares put together. Solving those practical problems led Baudhāyana to one of the most important facts in all of mathematics:

> In a right-angled triangle, **the square on the longest side equals the two squares on the other sides put together**: a² + b² = c².

In this chapter we will:

- double and halve a square by drawing, folding and cutting;
- meet a strange new number, **√2**, which is neither a fraction nor a decimal that stops;
- see **why** Baudhāyana's theorem is true, by cutting and moving pieces;
- use it to find unknown sides, and hunt for right triangles whose sides are all whole numbers.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** In a right-angled triangle, the side **opposite the right angle** is the **hypotenuse**; it is always the longest side. The other two sides are the **legs**. "The square on a side" means a square drawn with that side as one of its edges; its area is (side)². A right triangle whose two legs are equal is an **isosceles right triangle**; its other two angles are 45° each.

## 9.1 Doubling a square

**How do you draw a square with double the area of a given square?**

A first guess: double each side. But a square with sides twice as long has area 2 × 2 = **4** times as big, not 2 times. Doubling the side quadruples the area.

Baudhāyana's answer: **the diagonal of a square makes a square of double the area.** Draw the square whose side is the diagonal of the given square.

![Left: a square with its diagonal and a dashed square drawn on that diagonal. Right: the same, with the sides of the small square extended; the small square is 2 triangles and the dashed square is 4 of the same triangles](assets/img/class8/math/9/double_square.svg)

**Why it works:** extend the top side and the right side of the original square (Baudhāyana called these "east-west" and "north-south" lines). These two lines cut the dashed square into **4** small triangles, while the original square is made of **2** of the same triangles (cut by its diagonal). All six triangles are the same size, so the new square is 4 ÷ 2 = **2 times** the original. The questions below prove the two facts this needs.

Adding more lines makes a whole sequence: each square has double the area of the one before.

![Three squares on the same dashed 2-by-2 grid: a small square (4 tiny triangles), a tilted square on its diagonal (8 tiny triangles), and the big square (16 tiny triangles)](assets/img/class8/math/9/double_sequence.svg)

**Try this: doubling with paper.** You need two identical paper squares and scissors.
1. Draw both diagonals of square 2 and cut along them: you get four triangles, 5, 6, 7, 8.
2. Lay square 1 down and place one triangle against each of its sides, the long side of the triangle against the side of the square.

**What you will see:** a bigger, tilted square, made of square 1 plus square 2: **double the area**. Its corners are the right-angle corners of the four triangles.

![Square 1 marked into triangles 1 to 4; square 2 cut into triangles 5 to 8; the result: triangles 5 to 8 placed on the four sides of square 1 make a tilted square of double the area](assets/img/class8/math/9/paper_double.svg)

**Why it is a square:** each outer corner is a right angle (the triangle's right angle), and each outer side is two short sides of two triangles joined in a straight line (45° + 90° + 45° = 180° along the side of square 1). All four outer sides are equal, so it is a square.

### Check yourself

**1.** In the doubling figure, why must the extended top side and right side of the original square pass through corners of the dashed square?

> [!ANSWER]
> Look at the bottom-right corner of the original square, where the dashed square also has a corner.
> 1. The dashed square's corner there is **90°**, made by the diagonal of the original square and the next dashed side.
> 2. The diagonal of a square makes **45°** with the square's sides. So the original square's right side, extended upward, makes 45° with the diagonal: it cuts the dashed square's 90° corner into **45° + 45°**. It **bisects** that corner.
> 3. In a square, the line that bisects a corner is the **diagonal** of that square, and it goes to the **opposite corner**.
>
> So the extended right side runs along a diagonal of the dashed square and reaches its far corner. The same argument at the top-left corner shows the extended top side reaches the other far corner.

**2.** Why are all the small triangles (2 in the original square, 4 in the dashed square) congruent?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. The two lines from question 1 are the **diagonals** of the dashed square. A square's diagonals are equal and bisect each other at right angles, so the 4 triangles inside the dashed square each have **two equal legs** (half-diagonals) with a **90°** angle between them.
> 2. Each half-diagonal is as long as a side of the original square (each runs along an extended side, from the original square's corner to the point where the two lines cross, which is the original square's opposite corner).
> 3. The original square's 2 triangles also have two legs equal to its side, with 90° between them.
>
> So all six triangles have two legs equal to the original side and a right angle between them: they are congruent by **SAS**.

## 9.2 Halving a square

Now the other way: **make a square with half the area of a given square.** Run the doubling backwards: join the **midpoints** of the four sides. The tilted square inside has half the area.

![A square with the midpoints of its sides joined to make a tilted inner square; dashed lines through the centre split the big square into 8 equal triangles, 4 of them inside the tilted square](assets/img/class8/math/9/halve_square.svg)

**Why it works:** the lines joining opposite midpoints cut the big square into **8** equal triangles, and the tilted square holds **4** of them. 4 out of 8 is half.

**Try this: halving with paper.** Take a square sheet. Fold each corner in so that the crease runs from the midpoint of one side to the midpoint of the next, and all four corner points meet at the centre.

![A square sheet with the midpoints P, Q, R, S joined by red creases; dashed lines PR and QS meet at the centre O at a right angle; the four corner triangles are shaded](assets/img/class8/math/9/halve_fold.svg)

**What you will see:** the folded sheet is the square PQRS, exactly two layers thick everywhere. The four folded corners exactly cover it, so PQRS is **half** the sheet.

### Check yourself

**1.** Will a square with **half the side** have half the area? How many such squares fill the original?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** If the side is s, the half-side square has area (s/2) × (s/2) = s²/4: only a **quarter** of the area. **Four** such squares fill the original (two across and two up). Halving the side quarters the area, just as doubling the side made it four times as big.

**2.** In the folded sheet, why is PQRS a square, and why is its area half the sheet? (Join PR and QS.)

> [!ANSWER]
> **It is a square.**
> 1. PR joins the midpoints of the bottom and top sides, so it is as long as a side of the sheet; so is QS. **PR = QS.**
> 2. Both pass through the centre O, and O is the midpoint of each (it is halfway across and halfway up): they **bisect each other**.
> 3. PR is upright and QS is level, so they meet at **90°**.
>
> Equal diagonals that bisect each other at right angles make a square.
>
> **Its area is half.** PR and QS cut the sheet into 4 small squares. Each small square is cut by one red crease into 2 congruent triangles (a square's diagonal halves it): one inside PQRS and one in a corner. So the sheet is 8 congruent triangles, and PQRS is 4 of them: **half**. That is also why the folded corners cover PQRS exactly.

## 9.3 The hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle

How long is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are both 1?

![Unit square PEAR with diagonal ER; triangle PER (legs 1 and 1) is shaded; a dashed square REST is drawn on the diagonal](assets/img/class8/math/9/iso_hyp.svg)

We don't know the length c = ER yet, but we know the **area** of the square REST on it, because a square on the diagonal has double the area:

- Area of REST = 2 × area of PEAR = 2 × 1 = **2**.
- Area of REST = c × c = c².

So **c² = 2**. The number whose square is 2 is written **√2** ("the square root of 2"). The hypotenuse is **√2 units**.

> [!NOTE]
> From now on, all lengths are in some fixed unit unless a unit is given.

### How big is √2?

**Trapping it.** 1² = 1 is less than 2, and 2² = 4 is more than 2, so 1 < √2 < 2. We call 1 a **lower bound** and 2 an **upper bound**. Now close in, one decimal place at a time:

| try | square | | try | square | | try | square |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.3 | 1.69 | | 1.41 | 1.9881 | | 1.413 | 1.996569 |
| 1.4 | 1.96 | | 1.42 | 2.0164 | | 1.414 | 1.999396 |
| 1.5 | 2.25 | | | | | 1.415 | 2.002225 |

So 1.4 < √2 < 1.5, then 1.41 < √2 < 1.42, then **1.414 < √2 < 1.415**. We could go on forever, and we would have to:

**√2 is not a decimal that stops.** Suppose some decimal that stops, like 1.414…d, had square exactly 2. Its last digit d is not 0 (otherwise drop it). When you square it, the last digit of the answer comes from d × d, and d × d ends in 1, 4, 9, 6, 5, 6, 9, 4 or 1 for d = 1 to 9: **never 0**. So the square has a non-zero digit at its last decimal place, and cannot be 2.000… exactly. So the decimal for √2 **never ends**: √2 = 1.41421356…

**√2 is not a fraction either.** Suppose √2 = m/n with m, n counting numbers. Squaring: 2 = m²/n², so **2n² = m²**. Now count the factor 2 in the prime factorisation of each side. In any square number every prime appears an **even** number of times (each prime of m appears twice in m × m). So m² has an even number of 2s, and n² has an even number of 2s, which makes 2n² have an **odd** number of 2s. One number cannot have both an odd and an even number of 2s in its prime factorisation, so no such m and n exist. This proof is over 2300 years old (it appears in Euclid's *Elements*).

**Try this: measure √2.** Draw a square with sides 10 cm and measure its diagonal as carefully as you can. You should get a little over 14.1 cm, because the diagonal is 10 × √2 ≈ 14.14 cm. A ruler can only ever show a few decimal places, but the decimal of √2 goes on for ever.

> [!TIP]
> **Estimating a square root:** first trap it between two whole-number squares (16 < 18 < 25 gives 4 < √18 < 5), then try one-decimal numbers in between (4.2² = 17.64, 4.3² = 18.49). Squaring is easy, so it is the checking tool.

### The general rule for isosceles right triangles

![Square PQRS of side a with diagonal SQ = c; a dashed square SQVU is drawn on the diagonal](assets/img/class8/math/9/iso_general.svg)

If the equal legs are a and the hypotenuse is c, the square on c has double the area of the square on a:

> [!NOTE]
> **A bigger square has a bigger side.** If c² = 2 and c were 1 or less, c² would be 1 or less. This simple fact is what lets us trap square roots between bounds.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Isosceles right triangle: c² = 2a²**, so **c = a × √2**.
> **Why:** the square on the hypotenuse is the "square on the diagonal" of the square on a leg, which has double its area.

**Example 1.** The equal sides of an isosceles right triangle are 12. Find the hypotenuse.
1. c² = 2a² = 2 × 12² = 2 × 144 = 288, so c = √288.
2. 16² = 256 and 17² = 289, so √288 is between 16 and 17 (just under 17).

**Example 2.** The hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle is √72. Find the other two sides.
1. c² = 2a² gives (√72)² = 2a², that is, 72 = 2a².
2. So a² = 72 ÷ 2 = 36 and **a = 6**. Each leg is 6.

## 9.4 Combining two different squares

We can join two **equal** squares into one: the new side is the diagonal of either.

![Two equal squares plus each other become one tilted square of double the area](assets/img/class8/math/9/combine_same.svg)

What about two squares of **different** sizes? We want one square whose area is a² + b².

![A square of area a squared plus a square of area b squared gives a square of area a squared plus b squared, whose side is unknown](assets/img/class8/math/9/combine_diff.svg)

Baudhāyana's remarkable answer: **make a right-angled triangle whose legs are the sides of the two squares. The square on its hypotenuse has area equal to the two squares together.** He also explained why, with a cut-and-move construction. Let the squares have sides a and b, with b the larger.

**Step 1.** Put the two squares side by side on a straight line. Along the bottom the length is a + b. Mark the point P that is a distance **b** from the left end; it sits b − a inside the big square, and a from the right end.

![Squares of side a and b side by side; along the bottom the lengths are a, b − a, a; a dashed line marks a strip of width a at the right of the big square](assets/img/class8/math/9/dissect_1.svg)

**Step 2.** Draw two cuts from P: one to the top-left corner of the small square, one to the top-right corner of the big square. They cut off two right triangles, **W** (legs b along the bottom and a up the left side) and **X** (legs a along the bottom and b up the right side). So W and X are congruent right triangles with legs a and b.

![The two cuts from P make right triangles W and X, each with legs a and b](assets/img/class8/math/9/dissect_2.svg)

**Step 3.** Slide W and X up to the top, into the places **T** and **U**. With the middle piece **V**, they make a tilted four-sided shape whose sides are the four hypotenuses.

![W and X moved to the top as T and U; together with V they make a tilted square on the hypotenuse; at P the angles x and 90° − x sit either side of the square's right-angled corner](assets/img/class8/math/9/dissect_3.svg)

**Why the tilted shape is a square:**
1. **Sides.** Each side is the hypotenuse of one of the congruent triangles T, U, W, X, so all four sides are equal: the shape is a **rhombus**.
2. **The angle at P.** In W, call the angle at P (opposite the leg a) **x**. The other acute angle of W, opposite the leg b, is then 90° − x (the three angles make 180° and one is 90°). X is congruent to W, and its angle at P is opposite its leg b, so it is **90° − x**. Along the straight bottom edge: x + (corner at P) + (90° − x) = 180°, so the **corner at P is 90°**.
3. **A rhombus with one right angle is a square:** neighbouring angles of a rhombus add up to 180° and opposite angles are equal, so all four corners are 90°.

**Why its area is a² + b²:** the two squares are made of V + W + X. The new square is made of V + T + U. Since T, U are just W, X moved, the areas are equal.

> [!TIP]
> **Doubling is a special case.** When the two squares are equal (a = b), the right triangle is isosceles and its hypotenuse is the diagonal of the square: the theorem gives c² = a² + a² = 2a², the doubling rule of 9.1.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Baudhāyana's theorem (the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem):** if a right-angled triangle has legs a and b and hypotenuse c, then
> **a² + b² = c²**.
> **Why it works:** the two squares on the legs can be cut into pieces that rebuild exactly the square on the hypotenuse (steps 1-3).

![A 3-4-5 right triangle with a grid square on each side: 9 small squares plus 16 small squares equal 25 small squares](assets/img/class8/math/9/squares_345.svg)

Baudhāyana was the first person known to state this theorem in full generality. It is also called the **Pythagorean theorem**, after the Greek thinker Pythagoras (about 500 BCE), who lived a couple of centuries later and also admired and studied it. The name "Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem" makes sure everyone knows which theorem is meant.

**Try this: combine two squares with paper.** Cut out a square of side a and a square of side b, tape them side by side, and make the two cuts of step 2 (from the point b along the bottom). You now have three pieces. Rearrange them into one square of side c. Then draw a right triangle with legs a and b and the square on its hypotenuse: your pieces cover that square exactly.

**Example 3.** Draw a right triangle with legs 3 cm and 4 cm and measure the hypotenuse. You should get about 5 cm. The theorem says exactly: c² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25, so **c = 5 cm**.

**Try this: a right angle from a rope.** Take a loop of string with 12 equally spaced knots. Peg it out as a triangle with sides of 3, 4 and 5 spaces. **What you will see:** the corner between the 3 and the 4 sides is a right angle; check it with a set-square. **Why:** a right triangle with legs 3 and 4 has hypotenuse 5, so the rope triangle has the same three sides as that right triangle, and by SSS it is congruent to it, right angle and all. Builders in many ancient lands set out square corners with cords like this.

> [!WARNING]
> **√(a² + b²) is not a + b.** With legs 3 and 4, a + b = 7, but c = 5. The hypotenuse is always shorter than the two legs together: going straight across is shorter than going round the corner.

> [!TIP]
> **To find the hypotenuse:** c² = a² + b² (add). **To find a leg:** a² = c² − b² (subtract). The hypotenuse is always the side **opposite the right angle**, so find the right angle first.

### Check yourself

**1.** Does Baudhāyana's method work when the two squares are the same size? Does it agree with the doubling method?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, and yes.** If a = b, the right triangle with legs a and a is an isosceles right triangle, and its hypotenuse is the **diagonal** of the square of side a. The method says: the square on that diagonal has area a² + a² = 2a². That is exactly the doubling rule of 9.1 (the square on the diagonal has double the area). In the cut-and-move picture, b − a = 0, so P is at the corner where the squares meet and the two cuts are the two squares' diagonals.

**2.** Explain why every angle of the four-sided shape T + U + V in step 3 is a right angle, so that it is a square.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **It is a rhombus.** Its four sides are the hypotenuses of the congruent right triangles T, U, W, X, so they are all equal.
> 2. **The corner at P is 90°.** In each triangle (legs a and b), call the angle opposite the leg a "x"; the angle opposite the leg b is then 90° − x, because the three angles make 180° and one of them is 90°. At P, triangle W has its angle x (opposite its leg a) and triangle X has its angle 90° − x (opposite its leg b). These two angles and the corner fill the straight bottom edge: x + corner + (90° − x) = 180°, so the corner is **90°**.
> 3. **The other corners.** In a rhombus, neighbouring angles add up to 180° (it is a parallelogram), so the two corners next to P are 180° − 90° = 90°, and the opposite corner equals the corner at P: 90°.
>
> Four equal sides and four right angles: a **square**. (You can also check any corner directly: at every corner, one triangle's x and another's 90° − x sit beside it along a straight line.)

## 9.5 Right triangles with whole-number sides

Baudhāyana's Śulba-Sūtra lists right triangles whose sides are all whole numbers: (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25), (12, 35, 37) and (15, 36, 39). A triple of counting numbers (a, b, c) with **a² + b² = c²** is called a **Baudhāyana triple** (also a Pythagorean triple).

**Example 4: checking a triple.** Is (12, 35, 37) a triple? 12² + 35² = 144 + 1225 = 1369, and 37² = 1369. **Yes.**

### Scaling a triple

The triples (3, 4, 5), (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20) are 1, 2, 3, 4 times (3, 4, 5). This suggests: **(3k, 4k, 5k) is a triple for every counting number k.** Check: (3k)² + (4k)² = 9k² + 16k² = 25k² = (5k)². True. So there are **infinitely many** triples.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Scaling rule:** if (a, b, c) is a triple, so is (ka, kb, kc) for any counting number k.
> **Why:** (ka)² + (kb)² = k²a² + k²b² = k²(a² + b²) = k²c² = (kc)². Multiplying every side by k multiplies every square by k².

> [!TIP]
> **Quick primitive test:** a triple is primitive when no number bigger than 1 divides all three. It is enough to check the two **legs**. **Why:** if a prime p divides both a and b, it divides a² + b², which is c² = c × c; and a prime that divides a product of two numbers must divide one of them, so p divides c as well.

A triple whose three numbers have **no common factor bigger than 1** is called **primitive**. (3, 4, 5) is primitive; (9, 12, 15) is not (all divisible by 3), it is a **scaled version** of (3, 4, 5).

### Making triples from odd numbers

You know that adding the odd numbers gives the squares: 1 = 1², 1 + 3 = 2², 1 + 3 + 5 = 3², and in general **1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2n − 1) = n²** (the nth odd number is 2n − 1).

![A 5-by-5 square of dots: a 4-by-4 block in blue plus an L-shaped border of 9 dots in red](assets/img/class8/math/9/odd_gnomon.svg)

The first n − 1 odd numbers add up to (n − 1)², and adding the next odd number 2n − 1 gives n²:

> **(n − 1)² + (2n − 1) = n²**

(You can also check it by multiplying out: (n − 1)² = n² − 2n + 1, and adding 2n − 1 gives n².)

> [!NOTE]
> **Why the border has 2n − 1 dots:** growing an (n − 1)-by-(n − 1) square into an n-by-n square adds a row of n dots and a column of n dots, but the corner dot is in both, so the border has n + n − 1 = 2n − 1 dots.

**Try this: borders that are squares.** Grow a 4 × 4 square of counters into a 5 × 5 square: the border needs 9 = 3 × 3 counters. Now grow a 12 × 12 square into a 13 × 13 square: how many counters in the border? (25 = 5 × 5.) Whenever the border is itself a square number, you have found a Baudhāyana triple.

**Now the trick:** if the odd number 2n − 1 is itself a **square**, this is one square plus another square making a square, a Baudhāyana triple!

**Example 5.** 9 = 3² is odd. 9 = 2 × 5 − 1, so n = 5: (5 − 1)² + 9 = 5², that is **4² + 3² = 5²**.

**Example 6.** 25 = 5² is odd. 25 = 2 × 13 − 1, so n = 13: 12² + 25 = 13², that is **5² + 12² = 13²**.

> - (3, 4, 5), (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20): the (3, 4, 5) family, scaled by 1, 2, 3, 4;

**2.** Is (30, 40, 50) a Baudhāyana triple?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 30² + 40² = 900 + 1600 = 2500 = 50². It is (3, 4, 5) scaled by 10.

**3.** Is (300, 400, 500) a Baudhāyana triple?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** 300² + 400² = 90 000 + 160 000 = 250 000 = 500². It is (3, 4, 5) scaled by 100: every square is 100² = 10 000 times as big, so the equation still balances.

**4.** Is (5, 12, 13) a primitive triple?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** It is a triple (25 + 144 = 169 = 13²), and 5 and 13 are prime with 12 not a multiple of either, so the three numbers have no common factor bigger than 1.

**5.** What are all the primitive triples with numbers up to 20?

> [!ANSWER]
> From the list in question 1, remove the scaled ones (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20). The primitive ones are **(3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13) and (8, 15, 17)**.

**6.** Write 5 scaled versions of each of these primitive triples. Are the scaled versions primitive?

> [!ANSWER]
> Multiply by k = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6:
> - (3, 4, 5): (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20), (15, 20, 25), (18, 24, 30)
> - (5, 12, 13): (10, 24, 26), (15, 36, 39), (20, 48, 52), (25, 60, 65), (30, 72, 78)

**7.** If (a, b, c) is a triple and f (bigger than 1) is a common factor of all three, is (a/f, b/f, c/f) a triple? Check with (9, 12, 15) and justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** For (9, 12, 15) with f = 3: (3, 4, 5), and 9 + 16 = 25 ✓.
>
> **Why in general:** a/f, b/f, c/f are counting numbers because f divides each. Dividing both sides of a² + b² = c² by f² gives (a/f)² + (b/f)² = (c/f)². This is the scaling rule run backwards. So every non-primitive triple can be shrunk to a smaller one.

**8.** What is the sum of the first (n − 1) odd numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(n − 1)².** The sum of the first n odd numbers is n², so with n − 1 in place of n, the first n − 1 odd numbers add to (n − 1)². For instance the first 4 odd numbers: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 = 4².

### Check yourself: generating triples

**1.** Find 5 more Baudhāyana triples using the odd-square idea.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take the next odd squares. If 2n − 1 = (odd square), then n = (odd square + 1) ÷ 2, and the triple is (√(odd square), n − 1, n).
> 1. 7² = 49: n = 25, so 24² + 49 = 25²: **(7, 24, 25)**. Check: 49 + 576 = 625 ✓.
> 2. 9² = 81: n = 41: **(9, 40, 41)**. Check: 81 + 1600 = 1681 = 41² ✓.
> 3. 11² = 121: n = 61: **(11, 60, 61)**. Check: 121 + 3600 = 3721 = 61² ✓.
> 4. 13² = 169: n = 85: **(13, 84, 85)**. Check: 169 + 7056 = 7225 = 85² ✓.
> 5. 15² = 225: n = 113: **(15, 112, 113)**. Check: 225 + 12544 = 12769 = 113² ✓.

**2.** Does this method ever give a non-primitive triple? (Hint: look at the two biggest numbers.)

> [!ANSWER]
> **No, it always gives primitive triples.** In every triple it makes, the two biggest numbers are n − 1 and n, which are **next to each other**. If some number f > 1 divided both n − 1 and n, it would also divide their difference, n − (n − 1) = 1. Nothing bigger than 1 divides 1. So n − 1 and n have no common factor, and neither can all three numbers.

**3.** Are there primitive triples this method cannot make? Give examples.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** The method only makes triples in which the hypotenuse is **one more** than a leg. So any primitive triple where the gap is bigger is missed, for instance **(8, 15, 17)** (17 − 15 = 2), **(20, 21, 29)** and **(12, 35, 37)** (37 − 35 = 2). Check (20, 21, 29): 400 + 441 = 841 = 29², and 20, 21, 29 have no common factor, so it is primitive.

## 9.6 A puzzle that lasted 350 years

There are infinitely many squares that are the sum of two squares. In the 1600s the French mathematician **Pierre de Fermat** wondered: can a **cube** be the sum of two cubes? A fourth power the sum of two fourth powers? In other words, does xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ have a solution in counting numbers when n is bigger than 2?

Fermat wrote in the margin of a book that it has **no** solution, and that he had a wonderful proof that the margin was too small to hold. Nobody ever found his proof, and for more than 300 years the best mathematicians tried and failed. In 1963 a 10-year-old boy, **Andrew Wiles**, read about the problem in a library book and decided he would solve it. In **1994**, as a grown-up mathematician, he did. The statement is now called **Fermat's Last Theorem**.

## 9.7 Using the theorem

**Try this: squares on dot paper.** On dot paper, draw as many differently sized squares as you can with all four corners on dots (tilted ones count). Work out each area by the theorem: a side that goes p dots across and q dots up has length² = p² + q². Which whole-number areas up to 20 can you make? End question 8 explains the pattern.


**Example 7: the lotus in the lake** (a problem from Bhāskarāchārya's *Līlāvatī*, 12th century). A lotus bud stands 1 unit above the water of a lake. A breeze bends the stem over, and the bud touches the water 3 units from where it stood. How deep is the lake?

![A lake: a vertical lotus stem x under water and 1 above; the bent stem of length x + 1 reaches the surface 3 units away; a right triangle with sides x, 3 and x + 1](assets/img/class8/math/9/lotus.svg)

1. Let the depth be **x**. The stem is x + 1 long (x under water plus 1 above).
2. Assume the stem first stood straight up. Then the stem under water (x), the 3 units along the surface, and the bent stem (x + 1) make a **right triangle**, with x + 1 as the hypotenuse.
3. By the theorem: 3² + x² = (x + 1)², so 9 + x² = x² + 2x + 1.
4. Take x² from both sides: 9 = 2x + 1, so 2x = 8 and **x = 4**.

The lake is **4 units** deep (and the stem is 5: a 3-4-5 triangle!). It seemed there was too little information, but the right angle hid an equation.

### 1. Find the diagonal of a square with side 5 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> The diagonal is the hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle with legs 5 cm.
> 1. d² = 5² + 5² = 50, so **d = √50 cm**.

### 3. Find the side of a rhombus whose diagonals are 24 and 70.

> [!ANSWER]
> The diagonals of a rhombus **bisect each other at right angles**, so they cut it into four congruent right triangles.
>
> ![Answer: rhombus with half-diagonals 12 and 35 meeting at a right angle; the side is 37](assets/img/class8/math/9/ex3_a.svg)
>
> 1. The legs of each triangle are half the diagonals: 24 ÷ 2 = **12** and 70 ÷ 2 = **35**.
> 2. The side of the rhombus is the hypotenuse: s² = 12² + 35² = 144 + 1225 = 1369.
> 3. 37 × 37 = 1369, so **the side is 37**.

### 4. Is the hypotenuse the longest side of a right triangle? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** c² = a² + b², and b² is more than 0, so **c² is bigger than a²**. A bigger square has a bigger side, so c > a. In the same way c² > b², so c > b.
>
> (Another reason: the right angle is the biggest angle of the triangle, since the other two share the remaining 90°, and the longest side is always opposite the biggest angle.)

### 5. True or false: every Baudhāyana triple is either primitive or a scaled version of a primitive triple.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.**
> 1. Take any triple (a, b, c). Let g be the **largest** number that divides all three (g = 1 if there is no bigger common factor).
> 2. If g = 1, the triple is primitive.
> 3. If g > 1, divide by g: (a/g, b/g, c/g) is again a triple (dividing both sides of a² + b² = c² by g²).
> 4. This smaller triple is primitive: if some f > 1 divided all of a/g, b/g, c/g, then g × f would divide a, b, c, which is bigger than the largest common factor g. Impossible.
> 5. So (a, b, c) = g × (a primitive triple): a scaled version.
>
> For instance, (15, 36, 39): g = 3, and (5, 12, 13) is primitive.

> 2. **6 by 8**, diagonal 10
> 3. **5 by 12**, diagonal 13
> 4. **8 by 15**, diagonal 17
> 5. **7 by 24**, diagonal 25

### 7. Construct a square whose area is the difference of the areas of squares with sides 7 and 5.

> [!ANSWER]

> 1. Draw AB = 5.
> 2. At B draw a line perpendicular to AB.
> 3. With the compass at A, open to **7**, cut the perpendicular at C. Then AC = 7.
> 4. BC is the side we want: BC² = 7² − 5² = 24. Draw the square on BC.

### 8. Squares on a dot grid. Using dots of a square grid as corners, can you draw a square with area:

**(a)** 2 square units?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Go 1 across and 1 up for each side (turning a quarter turn at each corner). Each side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 1 and 1, so side² = 1 + 1 = **2**, and the area (side²) is 2.
>
> ![Answer: three squares on a dot grid: area 2 (sides 1 across, 1 up), area 4 (sides 2 across), area 5 (sides 1 across, 2 up)](assets/img/class8/math/9/ex8_a.svg)

**(b)** 3 square units?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** If a side goes p dots across and q dots up (whole numbers), the area is side² = **p² + q²** (by the theorem). The next side is the same move turned a quarter turn (q across, p up the other way), so it also lands on dots, and every grid square is made like this. Can p² + q² = 3? The squares are 0, 1, 4, 9, …; no two of them add up to 3 (0 + 1 = 1, 1 + 1 = 2, 0 + 4 = 4). So **no grid square has area 3**.

**(c)** 4 square units?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes:** an ordinary upright square with side 2 (p = 2, q = 0): area 2² + 0² = **4**.

**(d)** 5 square units?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes:** sides going 1 across and 2 up (p = 1, q = 2): area 1² + 2² = **5**. It is the tilted square on the right of the figure.

**(e)** If the grid goes on forever, which whole-number areas are possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> Exactly the numbers that are a **sum of two squares** p² + q² (p, q whole numbers, not both 0): **1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 13, 16, 17, 18, 20, 25, 26, 29, …** Numbers such as 3, 6, 7, 11, 12, 14, 15, 19, 21 are **impossible**, because no two squares add up to them.

### 9. Find the area of an equilateral triangle with side 6. (Hint: an altitude bisects the opposite side.)

> [!ANSWER]
> ![Answer: equilateral triangle ABC with side 6; the altitude AD meets BC at a right angle and cuts it into 3 and 3; the height is h](assets/img/class8/math/9/ex9_a.svg)
>
> 1. **The altitude bisects the base.** Draw AD perpendicular to BC. In ∆ADB and ∆ADC: ∠ADB = ∠ADC = 90°, AB = AC = 6 (hypotenuses), and AD is shared. So they are congruent (**RHS**), and **BD = DC = 3**.
> 2. **The height.** In right ∆ADB: h² = 6² − 3² = 36 − 9 = 27, so **h = √27**, between 5.1 and 5.2 (5.19² ≈ 26.94, 5.20² = 27.04).
> 3. **The area.** Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 6 × √27 = **3 × √27** square units, which is about 3 × 5.196 ≈ **15.6** square units.

### 10. Puzzle: find the colours.

Three closed boxes hold only red balls, only blue balls and only green balls. They are labelled RED, BLUE and GREEN, but **every** label is wrong. You may open just **one** box. How can you find which label belongs to which box?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Open any one box, say the one labelled RED.**
> 1. Its label is wrong, so it holds **blue or green** balls. Suppose you see **blue**.
> 2. Two boxes are left, labelled BLUE and GREEN, holding red and green (in some order).
> 3. The box labelled GREEN cannot hold green (wrong label), so it holds **red**. Then the box labelled BLUE holds **green**.
>
> If you had seen **green** in the RED box instead, then the BLUE-labelled box cannot be blue, so it is red, and the GREEN-labelled box is blue. Either way, one look settles everything, because "every label is wrong" leaves only one possibility for the last two boxes.

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Doubling a square:** the square on its **diagonal** has double the area. **Halving:** join the midpoints of the sides. Doubling the side makes the area **4** times bigger.
> - **Isosceles right triangle:** c² = 2a², so c = a√2. For legs 1, c = **√2 ≈ 1.414**.
> - **√2** is between 1.414 and 1.415; its decimal never ends, and it is **not a fraction** (2n² = m² is impossible: the number of 2s would be both odd and even).
> - **Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem:** in a right triangle with legs a, b and hypotenuse c, **a² + b² = c²**. The squares on the legs can be cut up to fill the square on the hypotenuse exactly.
> - **Hypotenuse:** add the squares. **Leg:** subtract the squares. The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle and is the longest side.
> - **Baudhāyana triples** (a, b, c): (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25)… Scaling a triple by k gives another; every triple is a primitive one scaled. (n − 1)² + (2n − 1) = n² turns each odd square into a triple.
> - **Fermat's Last Theorem:** xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no counting-number solutions for n > 2 (proved by Andrew Wiles, 1994).

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** doubling the **side** quadruples the area, it does not double it • to find a **leg**, subtract (c² − a²), don't add • make sure c is the side **opposite the right angle** before using a² + b² = c² • √(a² + b²) is **not** a + b (√(9 + 16) = 5, not 7) • √2 is not 1.41 or 1.414 exactly: those are only bounds • a scaled triple like (6, 8, 10) is a triple but **not primitive**.
