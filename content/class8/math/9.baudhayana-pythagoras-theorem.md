## Doubling a square

**Baudhāyana's question** (*Śulba-Sūtra*, c. 800 BCE): *how do you build a square with exactly **twice** the area of a given square?*

**The obvious guess is wrong.** Doubling each side gives `2 × 2 =` **4 times** the area, not twice.

**Baudhāyana's answer** (Verse 1.9):

> The diagonal of a square produces a square of double the area of the original square.

**Why it works.** Draw the tilted square on the diagonal, then add horizontal and vertical lines through it:

- The **original** square splits into **2** congruent right triangles.
- The **new** tilted square splits into **4** of those same triangles.

`4 ÷ 2 = 2` → **double the area** ✓

> [!TIP]
> **The paper version:** cut two identical squares. Leave the first whole; cut the second along both diagonals into 4 triangles. Arrange those 4 triangles around the first square and you get a square of **double** the area.

Repeating gives a sequence of squares of areas 1, 2, 4, 8, … built from 2, 4, 8 triangles.

## Halving a square

Reverse the construction: draw the tilted square whose **corners are the midpoints** of the original square's sides.

```
     ·---R---·
     |  / \  |
     S       Q
     |  \ /  |
     ·---P---·
```

**PQRS has exactly half the area.**

**Why:** the four corner triangles cut off are congruent (SAS: two half-sides and the right angle between them), and they fit exactly inside PQRS in pairs — so the corners and the inner square are equal in area.

> [!WARNING]
> **Halving the SIDE does not halve the area** — it quarters it, since area goes as side². Four half-size squares fill the original, not two.

## The hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle

A unit square is made of **two** isosceles right triangles with legs 1.

The square built on its **diagonal** has **twice** the area, i.e. area **2**.

If the diagonal (the hypotenuse) is c, then `c² = 2`, so **c = √2**.

**In general, for legs a and hypotenuse c:  `c² = 2a²`, so `c = a√2`.**

**Worked:** legs 12 → `c = √(2 × 144) = √288`. Since `16² = 256` and `17² = 289`, the hypotenuse is **between 16 and 17** (very close to 17).
**Worked:** hypotenuse √72 → `72 = 2a²` → `a² = 36` → **a = 6**.
**Worked:** hypotenuse 10 → `100 = 2a²` → `a² = 50` → `a = √50`, between **7 and 8**.

## What kind of number is √2?

### Trapping it between bounds

`1² = 1 < 2` and `2² = 4 > 2`, so **1 `<` √2 `<` 2**.

Squeeze further by squaring candidates:

```
1.4²   = 1.96      1.5²   = 2.25       →  1.4   < √2 < 1.5
1.41²  = 1.9881    1.42²  = 2.0164     →  1.41  < √2 < 1.42
1.414² = 1.999396  1.415² = 2.002225   →  1.414 < √2 < 1.415
```

### It never terminates

**Why:** suppose √2 were a terminating decimal. Its **last digit after the point would be non-zero**, and squaring it would produce a **non-zero last digit** too (the last digit of the square is the last digit of that digit squared). But 2 = 2.000… has **only zeros** after the point.

**Contradiction — so the decimal expansion of √2 goes on forever.**

### It is not a fraction either

Suppose `√2 = m/n` with m, n counting numbers. Then

```
2 = m²/n²
2n² = m²
```

**In the prime factorisation of any square number, every prime appears an EVEN number of times.** But on the left, the prime 2 appears an **odd** number of times (all the 2s inside n², plus one more); on the right, an **even** number.

**Impossible.** So **√2 cannot be written as a fraction.**

> [!NOTE]
> This proof is due to **Euclid** (*Elements*, c. 300 BCE). Numbers like √2 — neither a terminating decimal nor a fraction — are called **irrational**. `√2 = 1.41421356…`

## Combining two DIFFERENT squares

**Baudhāyana** (Verse 1.12):

> The area of the square produced by the diagonal is the sum of the areas of the squares produced by the two sides.

**The recipe:** make a right-angled triangle whose two perpendicular sides are the sides of the two squares. **The square on its hypotenuse has area equal to the sum of the two.**

### Why it works — Baudhāyana's own construction

(Verse 2.1) *Mark a rectangular portion of the larger square using a side of the smaller square. The diagonal of this rectangle is the side of the required square.*

1. Set the two squares (sides a and b, with `b > a`) side by side.
2. Inside the bigger square, mark off a rectangle `a` by `b`, and draw its **diagonal** — this creates a right triangle with legs a and b.
3. Build **four** copies of that triangle around the diagonal, forming a tilted 4-sided figure.
4. **It is a square:** all four sides are hypotenuses of congruent triangles (so equal), and at each corner the two acute angles `x` and `90 − x` sit either side of the joint, leaving `180 − x − (90 − x) =` **90°**.
5. The pieces of that square are exactly the pieces of the two original squares, rearranged.

**Therefore:**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Baudhāyana's Theorem.** In a right-angled triangle with legs a, b and hypotenuse c,
>
> **`a² + b² = c²`**
>
> **Baudhāyana was the first person in history to state this in full generality**, around 800 BCE. **Pythagoras** (c. 500 BCE) admired and studied it some 300 years later, which is why it is also called the Pythagorean theorem — and often the **Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem** so everyone knows which one is meant.

**Worked:** legs 3 and 4 → `9 + 16 = 25 = c²` → **c = 5**
**Worked:** legs 5 and 12 → `25 + 144 = 169` → **c = 13**
**Worked:** leg 8, hypotenuse 17 → `b² = 289 − 64 = 225` → **b = 15**

> [!TIP]
> **Rearranged forms:** `c = √(a² + b²)` for the hypotenuse, and `a = √(c² − b²)` for a leg. **Subtract when you're finding a leg, add when you're finding the hypotenuse** — the hypotenuse is always the biggest, so a leg calculation must shrink.

## Baudhāyana triples

**A triple `(a, b, c)` of whole numbers with `a² + b² = c²`.** Baudhāyana lists (Verse 1.13):

**(3, 4, 5) · (5, 12, 13) · (8, 15, 17) · (7, 24, 25) · (12, 35, 37) · (15, 36, 39)**

### Scaling always works

**If `(a, b, c)` is a triple, so is `(ka, kb, kc)`.**

**Why:** `(ka)² + (kb)² = k²a² + k²b² = k²(a² + b²) = k²c² = (kc)²` ✓

So (3, 4, 5) generates (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20), (30, 40, 50), … — **infinitely many triples**.

**A triple with no common factor greater than 1 is PRIMITIVE.**
(3, 4, 5) ✓ primitive · (9, 12, 15) ✗ (all divisible by 3) · (5, 12, 13) ✓ primitive · (8, 15, 17) ✓ primitive

**Triples with all numbers ≤ 20:** (3, 4, 5), (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20). The only **primitive** one is (3, 4, 5).

### Generating primitive triples from odd squares

Recall: **the sum of the first n odd numbers is n²**, and **the nth odd number is `2n − 1`**.

Splitting off the last term:

**`(n − 1)² + (2n − 1) = n²`**

> [!TIP]
> **Now pick n so that `2n − 1` is itself a perfect square.** Then all three terms are squares and you have a triple.
>
> - `2n − 1 = 9` → `n = 5` → `4² + 3² = 5²` → **(3, 4, 5)**
> - `2n − 1 = 25` → `n = 13` → `12² + 5² = 13²` → **(5, 12, 13)**
> - `2n − 1 = 49` → `n = 25` → `24² + 7² = 25²` → **(7, 24, 25)**
> - `2n − 1 = 81` → `n = 41` → `40² + 9² = 41²` → **(9, 40, 41)**
> - `2n − 1 = 121` → `n = 61` → `60² + 11² = 61²` → **(11, 60, 61)**

**Are these always primitive?** Yes. Notice that in every one, **the larger leg is exactly 1 less than the hypotenuse** — consecutive numbers share no factor, so the triple can have no common factor.

**But the method misses some.** (8, 15, 17) is primitive yet the legs differ from the hypotenuse by 2 and 9 — it never appears in this list.

## Fermat's Last Theorem

Baudhāyana triples inspired **Pierre de Fermat** (17th century) to ask: is there a **cube** that is the sum of two cubes? A fourth power that is the sum of two fourth powers?

He claimed that

**`xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ` has NO solution in positive integers when n `>` 2**

and wrote in the margin of his book: *"I have found a truly marvellous proof of this statement, but the margin is too small to contain it."*

> [!NOTE]
> Nobody ever found his proof, and the claim resisted **over 300 years** of attempts. In 1963 a 10-year-old boy, **Andrew Wiles**, read about it and resolved to solve it. **He proved it in 1994.**
>
> Squares are special: `a² + b² = c²` has infinitely many whole-number solutions. Cubes and higher have **none at all**.

## Applications

### Bhāskarāchārya's lotus problem (*Līlāvatī*)

*A lotus stands 1 unit above the water. A breeze pushes its tip down until it touches the water 3 units away. How deep is the lake?*

Let the underwater stem be **x**, so the whole stem is `x + 1`. When it bends, the stem becomes the **hypotenuse** of a right triangle with legs 3 and x.

```
9 + x² = (x + 1)²
9 + x² = x² + 2x + 1
     9 = 2x + 1
     x = 4
```

**The lake is 4 units deep.** The `x²` cancels — which is why what looks like too little information is actually plenty.

### Standard results

| Shape | Result | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Square, side s | diagonal `= s√2` | it's an isosceles right triangle |
| Square, side 5 | diagonal `= √50 ≈` **7.07 cm** | |
| Rhombus, diagonals p and q | side `= √((p/2)² + (q/2)²)` | diagonals bisect **at 90°**, making four right triangles |
| Rhombus, diagonals 24 and 70 | side `= √(12² + 35²) = √1369 =` **37** | |
| Equilateral triangle, side s | height `= (s/2)√3`, area `= (√3/4)s²` | the altitude **bisects** the base, giving legs s/2 and h |
| Equilateral triangle, side 6 | `h = √(36 − 9) = √27 ≈ 5.196`, area `≈` **15.59 sq units** | |

**Is the hypotenuse always the longest side?** **Yes.** Since `c² = a² + b²` and both a², b² are positive, `c² > a²` and `c² > b²`.

**Every Baudhāyana triple is a primitive triple or a scaled version of one** — **true**: divide out the HCF and what remains is primitive.

### Squares of any whole-number area on a grid

On a dot grid you can draw a tilted square with **vertices at dots** whose side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with whole-number legs.

- Legs 1, 1 → area `1 + 1 =` **2**
- Legs 2, 0 → area **4**
- Legs 2, 1 → area `4 + 1 =` **5**
- Legs 2, 2 → area **8** · legs 3, 1 → area **10**

**Area 3 is impossible** — no two whole squares add to 3.

> [!TIP]
> **The achievable areas are exactly the numbers expressible as `p² + q²` with p, q whole numbers**: 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 13, 16, 17, 18, 20, …

### Building bigger multiples of a square

**Triple the area:** put a square of side `a√2` (double area) next to one of side a. The hypotenuse of the right triangle with legs `a√2` and `a` gives a square of area `2a² + a² = 3a²` ✓

**Five times:** legs `a` and `2a` give `a² + 4a² = 5a²` ✓ (Baudhāyana, Verse 1.10)

**Difference of two squares:** to build a square of area `7² − 5²`, make a right triangle with **hypotenuse 7 and one leg 5** — the other leg is `√24`, and the square on it has area `49 − 25 =` **24** ✓
