## Why humans counted

Even in the **Stone Age**, people needed to count: how much food they had, how many animals in their livestock, details of trade, offerings in rituals — and to **track passing days**, predicting the new moon, full moon or the onset of a season.

## The mechanism of counting

To count a collection you need a **standard sequence with a fixed order** — call it a **number system**. You count by making a **one-to-one mapping** between the objects and that sequence.

**Three kinds of standard sequence** have been used in history:

| Method | Example | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical objects** — one stick per cow | sticks, pebbles, body parts | **unending**, but you need as many sticks as objects — hopeless for large collections |
| **Names or sounds** | a, b, c, … z | **convenient**, but **runs out** — only 26 |
| **Written symbols** | I, II, III, IV, V … | the Roman system — but needs **more and more new symbols** for bigger numbers |

> **The challenge: find an UNENDING standard sequence that is also EASY to count with.** Sticks give the first without the second; letters give the second without the first.

- The symbols of a written number system are called **numerals** — 0, 1, 5, 36, 193 are numerals of the Hindu system.

## Early systems

- **Body parts** — groups in Papua New Guinea counted (and still count) using hands and body parts as the standard sequence.
- **Tally marks** — notches cut on bone or cave walls, one per object. The **Lebombo bone** (South Africa, ~44,000 years old, 29 notches) and the **Ishango bone** (D.R. Congo, 20,000–35,000 years old) are among the oldest known mathematical artefacts.
- **Counting in twos** — the **Gumulgal** of Australia: *urapon* (1), *ukasar* (2), *ukasar-urapon* (3), *ukasar-ukasar* (4)… i.e. `3 = 2+1`, `4 = 2+2`, `5 = 2+2+1`. Anything above 6 was just *ras*.

> [!NOTE]
> **A genuine historical puzzle:** the Gumulgal (Australia), the Bakairi (South America) and the Bushmen (South Africa) developed **equivalent** counting-in-twos systems despite being **geographically far apart with no known contact**. One theory is common ancestors whose descendants migrated.

### Why grouping arose

**Most humans cannot see the size of a collection of 5 or more at a single glance.** That limit of perception probably pushed people to **replace every group of (say) 5 tally marks with a new symbol** — the first step towards efficient representation.

**Common group sizes across systems: 2, 5, 10 and 20.**

## Landmark numbers — the Roman system

**Landmark numbers** are the numbers given their own basic symbol.

| I | V | X | L | C | D | M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | 10 | 50 | 100 | 500 | 1000 |

**To write a number:** group it into as many of the **largest** landmark number as possible, then the next, and so on.

`27 = 10 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1` → **XXVII**
`2367 = 1000+1000+100+100+100+50+10+5+1+1` → **MMCCCLXVII**

**The advance:** grouping by a **sequence** of landmark numbers, not just one group size, is far more efficient than tallies.

> [!WARNING]
> **The Roman system is poor at arithmetic**, especially multiplication and division. **Why:** its landmark numbers do **not** step up by a constant factor — 1→5 is ×5, 5→10 is ×2, 10→50 is ×5, 50→100 is ×2. So regrouping during a calculation has to be done carefully, case by case.
>
> Users relied on an **abacus** — and only specially trained people could work it.

## The idea of a base

### The Egyptian system (~3000 BCE)

**Each landmark number is 10 times the previous one** — so they are all **powers of 10**: 1, 10, 100, 1000, …

`324 = 100 + 100 + 100 + 10 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1` — written with three "hundred" symbols, two "ten" symbols and four "one" symbols.

### Base-n, defined

> **A number system is base-n when (a) the first landmark number is 1, and (b) every next landmark number is the current one multiplied by a fixed number n.**

- Its landmark numbers are the **powers of n**: `1, n, n², n³, …`
- Egyptian is **base-10** (also called **decimal**). A base-5 system would have landmarks 1, 5, 25, 125, 625, …

**Example in base 5:** `143 = 125 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1`

### Why a base makes arithmetic easy

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In a base-n system, the product of any two landmark numbers is another landmark number.**
>
> **Why:** every landmark is a power of n, and `nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ` — still a power of n.
>
> That single fact is what makes multiplication manageable. In the Roman system it fails (`V × L = 250`, which has no symbol), which is exactly why Roman multiplication is so painful.

**Addition is easier too:** you always regroup by the **same** factor. Ten `|` always make one `∩`, every time — no case-by-case thinking.

### The shortcoming that remained

**The Egyptian system still needed a NEW SYMBOL for every higher power of 10.** To write ever-larger numbers you'd need an **unending sequence of symbols** — the original problem resurfacing in a new form.

> [!TIP]
> Note the consequence: **no Egyptian numeral can contain the same symbol 10 or more times** — ten of any symbol would simply be replaced by one of the next.

## Place value — the final idea

### Mesopotamia (base 60)

The Mesopotamian (Babylonian) system became a **base-60 (sexagesimal)** system with a very efficient representation.

**Why 60 is debated** — theories include links to their 30-day lunar month, the ease of writing fractions, and their earlier landmark sequence (1, 10, 60, 600, 3600…) reducing to powers of 60.

> [!NOTE]
> **Base 60 is still with us:** `1 hour = 60 minutes` and `1 minute = 60 seconds`.

### The Indian system

**The place-value idea solves the symbol problem completely: instead of a new symbol for each power, the POSITION of a digit tells you which power it stands for.** Ten digits then suffice for every number, however large.

**History:**

- Ancient Indian texts such as the **Yajurveda Samhita** named numbers by powers of 10 — *eka* (1), *dasha* (10), *shata* (100), *sahasra* (1000), *āyuta* (10,000) — up to 10¹² and beyond.
- Writing with **ten digits including 0** originated in India around 2000 years ago. The first known instance is the **Bakhshali manuscript** (c. 3rd century CE), where **zero appears as a dot**.
- **Aryabhata** (c. 499 CE) was the first to fully explain the system and do elaborate scientific computation with it.
- Transmitted to the Arab world by ~800 CE, popularised by **Al-Khwārizmī** (whose name gives us **"algorithm"**) in *On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals* (c. 825) and by **Al-Kindi** (c. 830).
- Reached Europe by ~1100 CE; **Fibonacci** (c. 1200) argued for adopting it, but **Roman numerals were so ingrained** that it took several more centuries — by the 17th century, not adopting it would have impeded scientific progress.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why the name is confused.** European scholars learned the numerals from the Arab world and so called them **"Arabic numerals"**; Arab scholars themselves called them **"Hindu numerals"**. The European term spread during colonisation. Today the accurate terms are **Hindu numerals, Indian numerals** or **Hindu-Arabic numerals** — and "Hindu" here means a **geography and people**, not a religion.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Laplace** (1749–1827) on the Indian system:
> *"The ingenious method of expressing every possible number using a set of ten symbols … emerged in India. The idea seems so simple nowadays that its significance and profound importance is no longer appreciated."*
>
> His point is worth holding on to: **the system is so familiar that we forget it was an invention at all** — and one that took thousands of years to arrive at.
