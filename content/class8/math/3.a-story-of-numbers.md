## Probe and ponder

Reema finds a paper covered in strange wedge-shaped marks. Her father says they are **numbers**, written in Mesopotamia about 4000 years ago!

- How would you count your cows if you knew **no number words** at all?
- Why is **MMCMXCIX** so much harder to work with than **2999**?
- What job does the humble **0** do that no other digit can?

## Why humans counted

Even in the **Stone Age**, people needed to count: how much food they had, how many animals in their livestock, details of trade, offerings in rituals — and to **track passing days**, predicting the new moon, full moon or the onset of a season.

## The mechanism of counting

To count a collection you need a **standard sequence with a fixed order** — call it a **number system**. You count by making a **one-to-one mapping** between the objects and that sequence.

**Three kinds of standard sequence** have been used in history:

| Method | Example | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical objects** — one stick per cow | sticks, pebbles, body parts | **unending**, but you need as many sticks as objects — hopeless for large collections |
| **Names or sounds** | a, b, c, … z | **convenient**, but **runs out** — only 26 |
| **Written symbols** | I, II, III, IV, V … | the Roman system — but needs **more and more new symbols** for bigger numbers |

> **The challenge: find an UNENDING standard sequence that is also EASY to count with.** Sticks give the first without the second; letters give the second without the first.

**Using the sticks (Method 1) for the herd questions:**
- **Did every cow come back?** As each cow enters, set aside one stick. A stick left over = a cow missing.
- **Fewer cows than the neighbour?** Pair your sticks with the neighbour's sticks, one with one. Whoever has sticks left unpaired has more.
- **How many more do we need?** The neighbour's **unpaired sticks** — that's the gap, no number words needed.

**Why it works:** two collections have the **same size exactly when they can be paired off one-to-one** with nothing left over. Counting is just pairing a collection with a standard sequence.

- The symbols of a written number system are called **numerals** — 0, 1, 5, 36, 193 are numerals of the Hindu system.

## Early systems

- **Body parts** — groups in Papua New Guinea counted (and still count) using hands and body parts as the standard sequence.
- **Tally marks** — notches cut on bone or cave walls, one per object. The **Lebombo bone** (South Africa, ~44,000 years old, 29 notches) and the **Ishango bone** (D.R. Congo, 20,000–35,000 years old) are among the oldest known mathematical artefacts.
- **Counting in twos** — the **Gumulgal** of Australia: *urapon* (1), *ukasar* (2), *ukasar-urapon* (3), *ukasar-ukasar* (4)… i.e. `3 = 2+1`, `4 = 2+2`, `5 = 2+2+1`. Anything above 6 was just *ras*.

> [!NOTE]
> **A genuine historical puzzle:** the Gumulgal (Australia), the Bakairi (South America) and the Bushmen (South Africa) developed **equivalent** counting-in-twos systems despite being **geographically far apart with no known contact**. One theory is common ancestors whose descendants migrated.

### Why grouping arose

**Most humans cannot see the size of a collection of 5 or more at a single glance.** That limit of perception probably pushed people to **replace every group of (say) 5 tally marks with a new symbol** — the first step towards efficient representation.

**Common group sizes across systems: 2, 5, 10 and 20.** (Look at the Roman numerals: after I, II, III they jump to V — a new symbol for a group of 5.)

> [!TIP]
> **Try it:** glance at 3 dots — you just *see* "three". Glance at 8 scattered dots — you have to count. That is the limit of perception at work.

**The drawback of one group size only:** to write 1345 counting by 5s you need 269 fives — still far too many marks. One group size is not enough for big numbers.

### Figure it Out — worked (3.1)

1. **Arithmetic with sticks:** **add** by putting both heaps together; **subtract** by removing one stick from each heap for each pair until one heap is empty — what's left is the difference; **multiply** by laying out one heap again and again, once for each stick of the other heap; **divide** by dealing the sticks into groups of the given size and counting the groups (left-over sticks are the remainder).
2. **Extending a–z beyond 26:** use two-letter strings — aa, ab, …, az, ba, … zz — then three letters, and so on. (One way; there are many.)

## Landmark numbers — the Roman system

**Landmark numbers** are the numbers given their own basic symbol.

| I | V | X | L | C | D | M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | 10 | 50 | 100 | 500 | 1000 |

**To write a number:** group it into as many of the **largest** landmark number as possible, then the next, and so on.

`27 = 10 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1` → **XXVII**
`2367 = 1000+1000+100+100+100+50+10+5+1+1` → **MMCCCLXVII**

**Subtractive rule:** 4 is written **IV** (1 less than 5), 9 is **IX**, 40 is **XL** (10 less than 50), 90 **XC**, 400 **CD**, 900 **CM**. (The Romans weren't always consistent — 40 was sometimes XXXX.)

**Worked (book):**
- 1222 = M + CC + XX + II → **MCCXXII**
- 2999 = MM + CM + XC + IX → **MMCMXCIX**
- 302 → **CCCII**
- 715 = D + CC + X + V → **DCCXV**

**Adding Roman numerals (without converting):** collect all the symbols, then regroup from the smallest up.
- `CCXXXII + CCCCXIII` → **CCCCCC, XXXX, IIIII** → IIIII = V, and 5 Cs make a D, so **D C XXXX V** → write XXXX as XL: **DCXLV** (= 645)
- `LXXXVII + LXXVIII` → collect: **LL, XXXXX, VV, IIIII**. Regroup from the bottom: IIIII = V (now VVV); VV = X (now X V, and six Xs); XXXXX = L (now one X left, and LLL); LL = C. Result **C L X V = CLXV** (= 165). Notice the regrouping factor keeps switching between 5 and 2 — that is the pain.

**Multiplying landmark numbers** is where Romans struggle: `V × L = CCL` (250), `V × D = MMD` (2500), `VII × IX = LXIII` (63), and `L × D` = 25,000 has **no symbol at all** — you'd need 25 Ms!

**The advance:** grouping by a **sequence** of landmark numbers, not just one group size, is far more efficient than tallies.

### Figure it Out — worked (3.2)

1. **Different number words for different objects** (a Pacific island people): probably because the counting was tied to the *kind* of thing counted — long things, round things, people — as the word sounds and the counting habits grew up separately.
2. **Gumulgal arithmetic** (urapon = 1, ukasar = 2): turn each name into a heap of 2s and 1s, then work with heaps.
   - 9 + 7 = 16 → **eight ukasars**
   - 9 − 6 = 3 → **ukasar-urapon**
   - 9 × 4 = 36 → **eighteen ukasars**
   - 16 ÷ 4 = 4 → **ukasar-ukasar**
3. **Why the Hindu system beats Roman:** it is a **place-value** system, it has a **zero**, the landmarks go up by the **same factor (10)** every time, and so +, −, ×, ÷ follow simple digit-by-digit rules.

> [!WARNING]
> **The Roman system is poor at arithmetic**, especially multiplication and division. **Why:** its landmark numbers do **not** step up by a constant factor — 1→5 is ×5, 5→10 is ×2, 10→50 is ×5, 50→100 is ×2. So regrouping during a calculation has to be done carefully, case by case.
>
> Users relied on an **abacus** — and only specially trained people could work it.

## The idea of a base

### The Egyptian system (~3000 BCE)

**Each landmark number is 10 times the previous one** — so they are all **powers of 10**: 1, 10, 100, 1000, …

`324 = 100 + 100 + 100 + 10 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1` — written with three "hundred" symbols, two "ten" symbols and four "one" symbols.

**Book practice:** 10458 = one 10000, four 100s, five 10s, eight 1s; 1023 = one 1000, two 10s, three 1s; 2660 = two 1000s, six 100s, six 10s; 784; 1111 (one of each of four symbols); 70707 = seven 10000s, seven 100s, seven 1s.

### Base-n, defined

> **A number system is base-n when (a) the first landmark number is 1, and (b) every next landmark number is the current one multiplied by a fixed number n.**

- Its landmark numbers are the **powers of n**: `1, n, n², n³, …`
- Egyptian is **base-10** (also called **decimal**). A base-5 system would have landmarks 1, 5, 25, 125, 625, …

**Example in base 5:** `143 = 125 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1` — one 125, three 5s, three 1s.

**Worked (book) — base 5** (landmarks 1, 5, 25, 125, 625):
- 15 = three 5s
- 50 = two 25s
- 137 = 125 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1 → one 125, two 5s, two 1s
- 293 = 125 + 125 + 25 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1 → two 125s, one 25, three 5s, three 1s
- 651 = 625 + 25 + 1 → one 625, one 25, one 1

**Can every number be written in our base-5 system?** Every positive whole number, yes (keep taking the biggest landmark that fits). But **zero** can't — there's no symbol for "nothing".

**Base 7** landmarks: 1, 7, 49, 343, 2401, … In general, **base n → 1, n, n², n³, …**

### Why a base makes arithmetic easy

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **In a base-n system, the product of any two landmark numbers is another landmark number.**
>
> **Why:** every landmark is a power of n, and `nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ` — still a power of n.
>
> That single fact is what makes multiplication manageable. In the Roman system it fails (`V × L = 250`, which has no symbol), which is exactly why Roman multiplication is so painful.

**Multiplying by 10 in Egyptian numerals:** replace every symbol by the **next** landmark symbol (each | becomes ∩, each ∩ becomes a hundred-coil, …). **Why:** `(100 + 10 + 1) × 10 = 1000 + 100 + 10` — the distributive law, plus "landmark × 10 = next landmark". In base 5, multiplying by 5 does the same: **shift every symbol up one landmark**.

**Addition is easier too:** you always regroup by the **same** factor. Ten `|` always make one `∩`, every time — no case-by-case thinking.

### The abacus

From about the 11th century even Roman-numeral users calculated on an **abacus**: a board of lines, each line a power of 10 (1, 10, 100, 1000 …). Counters on a line count that power; a counter **above** a line is worth 5 of it. 3426 = 3 counters on the 1000-line, 4 on the 100-line, 2 on the 10-line, and 6 ones as one counter above + 1 on the line.

**2907 + 43:** bring the counters together line by line. Ones: 7 + 3 = 10 → clear them and **carry one counter** to the 10-line. Tens: 0 + 4 + 1 = 5. Result **2950**. That "carry" is exactly what we do on paper — because it's a base-10 system.

### The shortcoming that remained

**The Egyptian system still needed a NEW SYMBOL for every higher power of 10.** To write ever-larger numbers you'd need an **unending sequence of symbols** — the original problem resurfacing in a new form.

> [!TIP]
> Note the consequence: **no Egyptian numeral can contain the same symbol 10 or more times** — ten of any symbol would simply be replaced by one of the next.

## Place value — the final idea

### Mesopotamia (base 60)

The Mesopotamian (Babylonian) system became a **base-60 (sexagesimal)** system with a very efficient representation.

**Why 60 is debated** — theories include links to their 30-day lunar month, the ease of writing fractions, and their earlier landmark sequence (1, 10, 60, 600, 3600…) reducing to powers of 60.

> [!NOTE]
> **Base 60 is still with us:** `1 hour = 60 minutes` and `1 minute = 60 seconds`.

**Making it compact.** Give each power of 60 its own mark and write how many of each: `640 = 10 × 60 + 40` → "ten 60s and forty". `7530 = 2 × 3600 + 5 × 60 + 30`. **No power can appear 60 or more times** — 60 of them make the next power (`1 × 3600 + 70 × 60 + 2 = 2 × 3600 + 10 × 60 + 2`).

Then the Mesopotamians did something brilliant: they **dropped the power marks altogether**. The **rightmost** group counts 1s, the next group to its left counts 60s, the next 3600s… **The position tells you the power.** This is a **positional (place-value) system** — the highest point in the story: **finitely many symbols, unendingly many numbers.**

**Worked (book):** 63 = **1 | 3**; 132 = **2 | 12**; 200 = **3 | 20**; 60 = **1 | (blank)**; 3605 = **1 | (blank) | 5**.

> [!WARNING]
> **The defect: blanks.** With a gap meaning "none of this power", how do you tell 1 | 1 (= 61) from 1 | blank | 1 (= 3601)? Scribes didn't keep gaps consistent, so numbers were misread. Later Mesopotamians used a **placeholder symbol** for an empty place — like our 0 — but only in the middle of a number, never at the end, so 60 and 3600 still looked the same as 1.

**Lesson: a place-value system NEEDS a zero.**

### The Mayan system (Central America, 3rd–10th century CE)

Place value invented **independently**, with a **shell** symbol as zero. Dots = 1, bars = 5, for digits 1–19, stacked **bottom to top**. Landmarks: **1, 20, 360, 7200** — the third is 360, not 400 (probably for their calendar), so it is **not a true base-20 system** and loses some computing advantages.

**Worked (book):** 77 = 3 × 20 + 17 → (3 | 17); 100 = 5 × 20 + 0 → (5 | shell); 361 = 1 × 360 + 0 × 20 + 1 → (1 | shell | 1); 721 = 2 × 360 + 0 + 1 → (2 | shell | 1).

### Chinese rod numerals (3rd–17th century)

A **base-10 place-value** system made with rods. Units, hundreds, ten-thousands use upright **zong** rods; tens, thousands use flat **heng** rods — **alternating** so neighbouring places look different. `2634` = heng-2, zong-6, heng-3, zong-4. A blank meant an empty place.

**Why alternate?** With only upright rods, 41 (four rods, then one rod) could be misread as 23, 32, 122… if the spacing slips. Switching direction marks where each place begins. Add a zero symbol and this would be a fully developed place-value system.

### The Indian system

**The place-value idea solves the symbol problem completely: instead of a new symbol for each power, the POSITION of a digit tells you which power it stands for.** Ten digits then suffice for every number, however large.

**History:**

- Ancient Indian texts such as the **Yajurveda Samhita** named numbers by powers of 10 — *eka* (1), *dasha* (10), *shata* (100), *sahasra* (1000), *āyuta* (10,000) — up to 10¹² and beyond.
- Writing with **ten digits including 0** originated in India around 2000 years ago. The first known instance is the **Bakhshali manuscript** (c. 3rd century CE), where **zero appears as a dot**.
- **Aryabhata** (c. 499 CE) was the first to fully explain the system and do elaborate scientific computation with it.
- Transmitted to the Arab world by ~800 CE, popularised by **Al-Khwārizmī** (whose name gives us **"algorithm"**) in *On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals* (c. 825) and by **Al-Kindi** (c. 830).
- Reached Europe by ~1100 CE; **Fibonacci** (c. 1200) argued for adopting it, but **Roman numerals were so ingrained** that it took several more centuries — by the 17th century, not adopting it would have impeded scientific progress.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why the name is confused.** European scholars learned the numerals from the Arab world and so called them **"Arabic numerals"**; Arab scholars themselves called them **"Hindu numerals"**. The European term spread during colonisation. Today the accurate terms are **Hindu numerals, Indian numerals** or **Hindu-Arabic numerals** — and "Hindu" here means a **geography and people**, not a religion.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Our system = base 10 + place value + a zero digit.** `375 = 3 × 10² + 7 × 10 + 5 × 1`. Each position holds a **single** digit 0–9, and 0 fills empty places, so **there is never any ambiguity**: 305, 35 and 3050 all look different.

**Zero as a NUMBER, not just a gap.** Indian mathematicians treated 0 as a number in its own right. **Aryabhata** (499 CE) used its properties (a + 0 = a, a × 0 = 0) in big computations; **Brahmagupta** (628 CE) wrote the rules of arithmetic with 0 and negative numbers — a set closed under +, −, × (today called a *ring*). This laid foundations for algebra.

## The evolution of ideas in five steps

| Step | Idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Count in groups of **one** number | Gumulgal: ukasar-ukasar-urapon |
| 2 | Group using **landmark numbers** | Roman: I V X L C D M |
| 3 | Landmarks = **powers of one number** (a base) | Egyptian: 1, 10, 100, 1000 … |
| 4 | **Position** shows the landmark (place value) | Mesopotamian, Mayan, Chinese |
| 5 | **0** as a positional digit **and** as a number | Indian: 1729, 305 |

## Figure it Out — worked (end of chapter)

1. **Chinese alternation:** see above — it marks the boundary between places.
2. **Base 2 with ukasar = 1, urapon = 0:** 1 = uk, 2 = uk-ur, 3 = uk-uk, 4 = uk-ur-ur, 5 = uk-ur-uk, 8 = uk-ur-ur-ur. Unlike Gumulgal's system (which just piles up 2s and stops at 6), place value lets two symbols write **every** number.
3. **Where do numerals and 0 matter?** Money, phone numbers, clocks, computers (all based on 0 and 1), measurement, science — imagine a bank ledger in Roman numerals!
4. **Other bases.** With 8 fingers we'd use base 8, digits 0–7. **25 = 3 × 8 + 1 → "31" in base 8**. In base 5, 25 = 1 × 5² → **"100"**. In base 2, 25 = 16 + 8 + 1 → **"11001"**.

**Book (3.3):** an Egyptian numeral can never show one symbol 10 or more times, since ten of them become the next symbol. A **base-4** system needs landmarks 1, 4, 16: e.g. 7 = one 4 + three 1s; 16 = one sixteen.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Laplace** (1749–1827) on the Indian system:
> *"The ingenious method of expressing every possible number using a set of ten symbols … emerged in India. The idea seems so simple nowadays that its significance and profound importance is no longer appreciated."*
>
> His point is worth holding on to: **the system is so familiar that we forget it was an invention at all** — and one that took thousands of years to arrive at.

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - Counting = a **one-to-one mapping** with a standard ordered sequence (a **number system**); the written symbols are **numerals**.
> - Tallies → grouping (2s, 5s, 10s, 20s) → **landmark numbers** (Roman) → landmarks as **powers of a base** (Egyptian) → **place value** (Mesopotamian base 60, Mayan, Chinese rods) → **zero** (Indian).
> - A base makes arithmetic easy because **landmark × landmark = another landmark** (`nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ`).
> - Place value needs only finitely many symbols — but it **needs a zero** to avoid ambiguity.
> - Our digits came from India (Bakhshali manuscript, Aryabhata, Brahmagupta), travelled via the Arab world (Al-Khwārizmī) to Europe (Fibonacci).

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** "Arabic numerals" actually came from India • the Roman landmarks do NOT go up by a fixed factor (×5, ×2, ×5, ×2…) • Egyptian was base 10 but NOT place value — it needed a new symbol per power • the Mayan system is not a true base 20 (third landmark 360) • Mesopotamian is base **60**, which is why an hour has 60 minutes • 25 in base 5 is written **100**, not 50.
