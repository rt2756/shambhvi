## What this chapter is about

Multiplication and addition are linked by one rule, the **distributive property**:

**a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c**

This chapter grows that one rule into a powerful toolkit:

- By how much does a product grow when you add 1 to one of the numbers? To both?
- How can you multiply 3874 by 11, or 9734 by 99, in one line?
- Why is 65² = 3600 + 600 + 25, and 55² = 3600 − 600 + 25?
- Why do different-looking formulas for the same pattern always turn out equal?

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** A **term** is a part of an expression joined by + or − (in 3a² − 2ab + 7 the terms are 3a², −2ab and 7). **Like terms** have exactly the same letters with the same powers (2ab and −5ab are like terms; a²b and ab² are not). To **expand** means to multiply out the brackets. An **identity** is an equality that is true for **every** value of the letters, like a(b + 8) = ab + 8a.

## 6.1 The distributive property

Take 3 rows of 7 dots and split each row into 5 + 2. The whole array is 3 × 7 = 21 dots; the two parts are 3 × 5 = 15 and 3 × 2 = 6 dots.

![3 rows of 5 blue dots and 2 orange dots: 3 × (5 + 2) = 3 × 5 + 3 × 2 = 21](assets/img/class8/math/6/distributive_dots.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Distributive property:** a(b + c) = ab + ac, and (a + b)c = ac + bc.
> **Why:** a rows of (b + c) dots can be counted all at once, or as a rows of b dots plus a rows of c dots. Same dots, same total. The second form follows from the first because the order of multiplying doesn't matter: (a + b)c = c(a + b) = ca + cb.

**Try this.** Lay out 4 rows of 9 counters. Put a pencil between the 6th and 7th columns. Count the two parts separately and add. Now move the pencil to another place. Do you always get 36?

We usually leave out the × sign next to a bracket: a(b + c) means a × (b + c), just as 5a means 5 × a.

### How a product grows

**Example 1: what happens to 23 × 27 when the numbers change?**
- **27 goes up by 1:** 23 × (27 + 1) = 23 × 27 + 23. The product grows by **23**: one more column of 23.
- **23 goes up by 1:** (23 + 1) × 27 = 23 × 27 + 27. It grows by **27**.
- **Both go up by 1:** treat (23 + 1) as one number and distribute:
  (23 + 1)(27 + 1) = (23 + 1) × 27 + (23 + 1) × 1 = 23 × 27 + 27 + 23 + 1.
  The product grows by 27 + 23 + 1 = **51**, not just 23 + 27.

With letters: if a and b both go up by 1,

(a + 1)(b + 1) = (a + 1)b + (a + 1) × 1 = **ab + (a + b + 1)**.

**Why the extra 1?** Picture an a × b rectangle of dots. Adding a row adds b dots, adding a column adds a dots, and the **corner** where the new row and column meet adds 1 more.

If a goes **up** by 1 and b goes **down** by 1:

(a + 1)(b − 1) = (a + 1)b − (a + 1) × 1 = ab + b − a − 1.

So the change is **b − a − 1**, which can be positive, zero or negative.

### Identity 1: increasing by any amounts

If a goes up by m and b goes up by n:

(a + m)(b + n) = (a + m)b + (a + m)n = **ab + mb + an + mn**.

![A rectangle (a + m) by (b + n) split into four parts ab, an, mb, mn](assets/img/class8/math/6/identity1_area.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Identity 1:** (a + m)(b + n) = ab + an + mb + mn. **Multiply each term of the first bracket by each term of the second, and add.**
> **Why:** distribute twice. The rectangle shows it too: the big rectangle is made of exactly these four smaller ones.

Identity 1 also handles decreases, because "decrease by v" is "increase by −v". The signs come from the rules for multiplying integers (− × + = −, − × − = +):

- (a + u)(b − v) = ab − av + ub − uv
- (a − u)(b + v) = ab + av − ub − uv
- (a − u)(b − v) = ab − av − ub + uv

**Integers obey the distributive property too**, so all these identities hold for negative values of the letters as well.

**More than two terms.** The rule "each term times each term" works for any number of terms.

**Example 2: expand (a + b)(a + b).**
(a + b)(a + b) = a × a + a × b + b × a + b × b = a² + ab + ba + b². Since ba = ab (like terms), ab + ba = 2ab. So **(a + b)(a + b) = a² + 2ab + b²**.

**Example 3: expand (a + b)(a² + 2ab + b²).**
1. Multiply a by each term: a³ + 2a²b + ab².
2. Multiply b by each term: a²b + 2ab² + b³.
3. Collect like terms: 2a²b + a²b = 3a²b and ab² + 2ab² = 3ab².
4. Answer: **a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³**.

(a × a² = a³ because a × (a × a) is three a's multiplied together.)

**Example 4: expand (3a/2)(a − b + 1/5).**
(3a/2) × a = (3/2)a², (3a/2) × b = (3/2)ab, (3a/2) × (1/5) = (3/10)a. So the answer is **(3/2)a² − (3/2)ab + (3/10)a**. No two of these are like terms (a², ab and a are different), so it cannot be simplified further.

> [!NOTE]
> **A pinch of history.** The distributive property was used without being named by mathematicians in Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, China and India; Euclid used it in geometric form and Āryabhaṭa in algebraic form. **Brahmagupta** stated it plainly in his *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE) and called it *khaṇḍa-guṇana*, "multiplication by parts": break the multiplier into parts, multiply by each part, and add.

### Check yourself

**1.** By how much does 23 × 27 grow if (a) 23 goes up by 1, (b) 27 goes up by 1, (c) both go up by 1?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **(a)** (23 + 1) × 27 = 23 × 27 + 27: it grows by **27** (one more row of 27).
> 2. **(b)** 23 × (27 + 1) = 23 × 27 + 23: it grows by **23**.
> 3. **(c)** (23 + 1)(27 + 1) = 23 × 27 + 27 + 23 + 1: it grows by **51**.
>
> Check: 23 × 27 = 621, 24 × 28 = 672, and 672 − 621 = 51 ✓. The general rule: (a + 1)(b + 1) − ab = a + b + 1.

**2.** Expand (a + 1)(b + 1) by treating (b + 1) as one term first. Do you get the same answer?

> [!ANSWER]
> (a + 1)(b + 1) = a(b + 1) + 1 × (b + 1) = ab + a + b + 1.
>
> **Yes, the same**: ab + a + b + 1. Which bracket you open first doesn't matter, because either way every term of one bracket meets every term of the other exactly once.

**3.** When one number goes up by 1 and the other goes down by 1, does the product always increase? Find 3 examples where it decreases.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** (a + 1)(b − 1) = ab + b − a − 1, so the change is **b − a − 1**. It is negative when b − a − 1 < 0, that is, when **b is not bigger than a**.
> - a = 5, b = 3: 15 → 6 × 2 = 12 (down by 3 = 3 − 5 − 1)
> - a = 10, b = 10: 100 → 11 × 9 = 99 (down by 1)
> - a = 7, b = 2: 14 → 8 × 1 = 8 (down by 6)
>
> It increases only when b is at least 2 more than a (e.g. 23 × 27 → 24 × 26 = 624, up by 3).

**4.** Do these rules still work when a and b are negative? Try a = −5, b = 8 and a = −4, b = −5.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes**, because integers follow the distributive property.
> - a = −5, b = 8: ab = −40; (a + 1)(b + 1) = (−4)(9) = −36. Change = +4, and a + b + 1 = −5 + 8 + 1 = 4 ✓.
> - a = −4, b = −5: ab = 20; (a + 1)(b + 1) = (−3)(−4) = 12. Change = −8, and a + b + 1 = −4 − 5 + 1 = −8 ✓.
>
> So the formula "increase = a + b + 1" still works; a negative "increase" just means the product went down.

**5.** Use Identity 1 to find how a product ab changes when (i) one number is decreased by 2 and the other increased by 3; (ii) one is decreased by 3 and the other by 4. Check by multiplying out directly.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i)** Take m = −2, n = 3: (a − 2)(b + 3) = ab + 3a − 2b − 6. **Change: 3a − 2b − 6.**
> Directly: (a − 2)(b + 3) = (a − 2)b + (a − 2) × 3 = ab − 2b + 3a − 6 ✓.
>
> **(ii)** Take m = −3, n = −4: (a − 3)(b − 4) = ab − 4a − 3b + 12. **Change: −4a − 3b + 12.**
> Directly: (a − 3)(b − 4) = (a − 3)b − (a − 3) × 4 = ab − 3b − 4a + 12 ✓.
>
> Number check for (i) with a = 10, b = 5: 8 × 8 = 64 and 50 + 30 − 10 − 6 = 64 ✓.

**6.** Check that (a + u)(b − v) = ab + ub − av − uv is what Identity 1 gives with m = u and n = −v.

> [!ANSWER]
> Identity 1: (a + m)(b + n) = ab + an + mb + mn. Put m = u, n = −v:
> 1. an = a × (−v) = −av
> 2. mb = ub
> 3. mn = u × (−v) = −uv
>
> So (a + u)(b − v) = ab − av + ub − uv, the same four terms ✓. The integer rule "+ × − = −" supplies every sign.

### Check yourself: Figure it out (6.1)

**1.** In the multiplication table, each number is (row number) × (column number). In a 3 × 3 frame, the middle number is pq. Write expressions for the other eight numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> The middle number pq is in row p and column q. The row above is p − 1, the row below p + 1; the column to the left is q − 1, to the right q + 1. Each entry is (its row) × (its column):
>
> | | column q − 1 | column q | column q + 1 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **row p − 1** | (p − 1)(q − 1) | (p − 1)q | (p − 1)(q + 1) |
> | **row p** | p(q − 1) | **pq** | p(q + 1) |
> | **row p + 1** | (p + 1)(q − 1) | (p + 1)q | (p + 1)(q + 1) |
>
> Check with the frame around 4 × 6 = 24 (p = 4, q = 6): top-left 3 × 5 = 15, bottom-right 5 × 7 = 35 ✓.

**Question 2.** Expand the products.

**(i)** (3 + u)(v − 3)

> [!ANSWER]
> Each term times each term: 3 × v + 3 × (−3) + u × v + u × (−3) = **3v − 9 + uv − 3u**.

**(ii)** (2/3)(15 + 6a)

> [!ANSWER]
> (2/3) × 15 + (2/3) × 6a = **10 + 4a**. (2/3 of 15 is 10; 2/3 of 6 is 4.)

**(iii)** (10a + b)(10c + d)

> [!ANSWER]
> 10a × 10c + 10a × d + b × 10c + b × d = **100ac + 10ad + 10bc + bd**.
>
> This is how two-digit numbers multiply: with a = 2, b = 3, c = 4, d = 1, 23 × 41 = 800 + 20 + 120 + 3 = 943 ✓.

**(iv)** (3 − x)(x − 6)

> [!ANSWER]
> 3x − 18 − x² + 6x = **−x² + 9x − 18**. (−x × x = −x², and −x × −6 = +6x.)

**(v)** (−5a + b)(c + d)

> [!ANSWER]
> **−5ac − 5ad + bc + bd**. Each of −5a and b multiplies each of c and d.

**(vi)** (5 + z)(y + 9)

> [!ANSWER]
> 5y + 45 + zy + 9z = **5y + yz + 9z + 45**. No two terms are like terms, so this is the simplest form.

**3.** Find 3 examples where a product stays the same when one number goes up by 2 and the other goes down by 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. We need (a + 2)(b − 4) = ab.
> 2. Expand: ab − 4a + 2b − 8 = ab.
> 3. Take ab from both sides: −4a + 2b − 8 = 0, so 2b = 4a + 8, **b = 2a + 4**.
>
> Examples: a = 1, b = 6: 6 → 3 × 2 = 6 ✓. a = 2, b = 8: 16 → 4 × 4 = 16 ✓. a = 3, b = 10: 30 → 5 × 6 = 30 ✓.

**Question 4.** Expand.

**(i)** (a + ab − 3b²)(4 + b)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Times 4: 4a + 4ab − 12b²
> 2. Times b: ab + ab² − 3b³
> 3. Collect like terms (4ab + ab = 5ab): **4a + 5ab + ab² − 12b² − 3b³**

**(ii)** (4y + 7)(y + 11z − 3)

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Times 4y: 4y² + 44yz − 12y
> 2. Times 7: 7y + 77z − 21
> 3. Collect (−12y + 7y = −5y): **4y² + 44yz − 5y + 77z − 21**

**5.** Expand (i) (a − b)(a + b), (ii) (a − b)(a² + ab + b²), (iii) (a − b)(a³ + a²b + ab² + b³). Do you see a pattern? What is the next identity? Check it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **(i)** a² + ab − ba − b² = **a² − b²**
> **(ii)** a³ + a²b + ab² − a²b − ab² − b³ = **a³ − b³**
> **(iii)** a⁴ + a³b + a²b² + ab³ − a³b − a²b² − ab³ − b⁴ = **a⁴ − b⁴**
>
> **Pattern:** in each case, the a-row and the b-row of products cancel in the middle ("telescope"), leaving only the first term a^n and the last term −b^n.
>
> **Next:** (a − b)(a⁴ + a³b + a²b² + ab³ + b⁴) = **a⁵ − b⁵**. Check: times a gives a⁵ + a⁴b + a³b² + a²b³ + ab⁴; times −b gives −a⁴b − a³b² − a²b³ − ab⁴ − b⁵. Everything cancels except a⁵ − b⁵ ✓.

## 6.2 Fast multiplication

### Multiplying by 11

3874 × 11 = 3874 × (10 + 1) = 38740 + 3874. Write the two numbers one under the other:

```
  3 8 7 4 0
+   3 8 7 4
-----------
  4 2 6 1 4
```

Each digit of the answer is the **sum of two neighbouring digits** of 3874. For a number with digits d c b a:

```
  d    c     b     a     0
+      d     c     b     a
-------------------------
  d  (c+d) (b+c) (a+b)   a
```

**Try this.** Multiply 2345 by 11 with a calculator. Now look at its digits: 2, 2 + 3, 3 + 4, 4 + 5, 5. Do you see them in the answer?

**The rule for × 11:** write the last digit; then, moving left, write the sum of each pair of neighbouring digits (carrying when a sum is 10 or more); finish with the first digit (plus any carry).

**Example 5: 3874 × 11 in one line.**
1. Last digit: **4**.
2. 7 + 4 = 11: write **1**, carry 1.
3. 8 + 7 + 1 (carry) = 16: write **6**, carry 1.
4. 3 + 8 + 1 = 12: write **2**, carry 1.
5. First digit 3 + 1 = **4**.

Reading from the left: **42614**.

### Multiplying by 101, 1001, … and by 99, 999

dcba × 101 = dcba × 100 + dcba. The shift is now **two** places, so each digit is added to the digit **two places to its left**:

```
  d  c   b    a    0  0
+       d    c    b  a
----------------------
  d  c (b+d) (a+c) b  a
```

For × 1001 the shift is three places, for × 10001 four places, and so on. For × 99, use 99 = 100 − 1: 9734 × 99 = 973400 − 9734 = 963666.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Why these shortcuts work:** 11 = 10 + 1, 101 = 100 + 1, 99 = 100 − 1. By the distributive property, multiplying by them means "the number shifted left, plus (or minus) the number itself".

> [!NOTE]
> Quick methods like these, built on the distributive property, were described by **Brahmagupta** (628 CE), who called them *iṣṭa-guṇana*, and later by **Śrīdharācārya** (about 750 CE) and **Bhāskarācārya** in *Līlāvatī* (1150 CE).

### Check yourself

**1.** Find 5678 × 11 in one line.

> [!ANSWER]
> Work from the right:
> 1. Last digit: **8**.
> 2. 7 + 8 = 15 → **5**, carry 1.
> 3. 6 + 7 + 1 = 14 → **4**, carry 1.
> 4. 5 + 6 + 1 = 12 → **2**, carry 1.
> 5. First digit 5 + 1 = **6**.
>
> **5678 × 11 = 62458.** Check: 56780 + 5678 = 62458 ✓

**2.** Describe a general rule for multiplying any number by 11 in one line.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Write down the **units digit** of the number.
> 2. Moving left, add each digit to its **right-hand neighbour** and write the units digit of the sum, carrying any tens to the next step.
> 3. Finally write the **first digit** plus any carry.
>
> **Why:** N × 11 = N × 10 + N. Shifting N one place left lines each digit up with its right-hand neighbour, so each column holds the sum of two neighbouring digits.

**3.** Evaluate (i) 94 × 11 (ii) 495 × 11 (iii) 3279 × 11 (iv) 4791256 × 11.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **(i)** 4; 9 + 4 = 13 → 3 carry 1; 9 + 1 = 10. **1034**
> - **(ii)** 5; 9 + 5 = 14 → 4 carry 1; 4 + 9 + 1 = 14 → 4 carry 1; 4 + 1 = 5. **5445**
> - **(iii)** 9; 7 + 9 = 16 → 6 c1; 2 + 7 + 1 = 10 → 0 c1; 3 + 2 + 1 = 6; 3. **36069**
> - **(iv)** 6; 5 + 6 = 11 → 1 c1; 2 + 5 + 1 = 8; 1 + 2 = 3; 9 + 1 = 10 → 0 c1; 7 + 9 + 1 = 17 → 7 c1; 4 + 7 + 1 = 12 → 2 c1; 4 + 1 = 5. **52703816**

**4.** Multiply 3874 by 101 in one line, and give a general rule for × 101.

> [!ANSWER]
> 3874 × 101 = 387400 + 3874. Each digit is added to the digit **two places** to its left:
> 1. Last two digits stay: **7 4**.
> 2. Next: 8 + 4 = **12** → 2, carry 1.
> 3. Next: 3 + 7 + 1 = **11** → 1, carry 1.
> 4. Then the first two digits 3, 8 with the carry: 38 + 1 = **39**.
>
> **3874 × 101 = 391274.** Check: 387400 + 3874 = 391274 ✓
>
> **Rule:** copy the last two digits; then add each digit to the one two places to its right, carrying as needed; finish with the first two digits (plus carry).

**5.** Extend the rule to × 1001, × 10001, …

> [!ANSWER]
> 1001 = 1000 + 1, so N × 1001 = (N shifted **3** places) + N: add each digit to the digit **three places** to its right. For 10001 the gap is **4** places, for 100001 it is 5, and so on.
>
> When N has **no more digits than the gap**, nothing overlaps, so the answer is just N written twice: 1234 × 10001 = **12341234** (and dcba × 10001 = dcbadcba).

**6.** Find (i) 89 × 101 (ii) 949 × 101 (iii) 265831 × 1001 (iv) 1111 × 1001 (v) 9734 × 99 (vi) 23478 × 999.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **(i)** 8900 + 89 = **8989** (89 is short, so it is just written twice)
> - **(ii)** 94900 + 949 = **95849**
> - **(iii)** 265831000 + 265831 = **266096831**
> - **(iv)** 1111000 + 1111 = **1112111**
> - **(v)** 9734 × (100 − 1) = 973400 − 9734 = **963666**
> - **(vi)** 23478 × (1000 − 1) = 23478000 − 23478 = **23454522**
>
> For (v) and (vi) the distributive property works with subtraction: a(b − c) = ab − ac.

## 6.3 Squares of sums and differences

A square of side 65 can be cut into four pieces: a 60 × 60 square, a 5 × 5 square and two 60 × 5 rectangles.

![A 65 by 65 square split into 60 × 60 = 3600, two strips 60 × 5 = 300 and a 5 × 5 = 25 corner](assets/img/class8/math/6/square_65.svg)

So 65² = (60 + 5)² = 60² + 2 × (60 × 5) + 5² = 3600 + 600 + 25 = **4225**. The distributive property gives the same four parts: (60 + 5)(60 + 5) = 60 × 60 + 60 × 5 + 5 × 60 + 5 × 5.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Identity 1A:** (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b².
> **Why:** (a + b)(a + b) = a² + ab + ba + b², and ab + ba = 2ab. In the picture: two squares (a² and b²) and **two** a × b rectangles.

> [!WARNING]
> **(a + b)² is not a² + b².** The two rectangles 2ab are easy to forget: (5 + 3)² = 64, but 5² + 3² = 34.

**Example 6: expand (6x + 5)² two ways.**
- **Distributive property:** (6x + 5)(6x + 5) = 36x² + 30x + 30x + 25 = **36x² + 60x + 25**.
- **Identity 1A** with a = 6x, b = 5: (6x)² + 2 × 6x × 5 + 5² = **36x² + 60x + 25**.

If you forget the identity, just multiply out: it always works.

Now 55² = (60 − 5)². Start with the 60 × 60 square and cut off a 5-wide strip on the right and at the bottom.

![55 × 55 inside 60 × 60: remove the two 60 × 5 strips, then add back the 5 × 5 corner that was removed twice](assets/img/class8/math/6/square_55.svg)

Removing both strips removes the little 5 × 5 corner **twice**, so we add it back once:

55² = 60² − 60 × 5 − 5 × 60 + 5² = 3600 − 300 − 300 + 25 = **3025**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Identity 1B:** (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b².
> **Why:** (a − b)(a − b) = a² − ab − ba + b² (−b × −b = +b²). Or use Identity 1A with −b in place of b: (a + (−b))² = a² + 2a(−b) + (−b)² = a² − 2ab + b².

**Try this.** Draw a square of side 65 split as 30 + 35, and another split as 52 + 13. Work out the four pieces of each. Do both give 4225?

### Check yourself

**1.** Write 65² as (30 + 35)² and as (52 + 13)². Do you get the same area?

> [!ANSWER]
> - (30 + 35)² = 30² + 2 × 30 × 35 + 35² = 900 + 2100 + 1225 = **4225** ✓
> - (52 + 13)² = 52² + 2 × 52 × 13 + 13² = 2704 + 1352 + 169 = **4225** ✓
>
> Any split of the side gives the same square, so the four pieces always add up to the same area. The split 60 + 5 is just the easiest to calculate.

**2.** For integers a and b, is (a + b)² always greater than a² + b²? If not, when is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> (a + b)² − (a² + b²) = 2ab. So:
> - **greater** when ab > 0: a and b both positive or both negative (3, 4: 49 > 25);
> - **equal** when a = 0 or b = 0 (0, 5: 25 = 25);
> - **smaller** when one is positive and the other negative (3, −4: 1 < 25).
>
> So **not always**: only when a and b have the same sign (and neither is 0).

**3.** Use Identity 1A to find 104² and 37².

> [!ANSWER]
> - 104² = (100 + 4)² = 10000 + 800 + 16 = **10816**
> - 37² = (30 + 7)² = 900 + 420 + 49 = **1369** (or (40 − 3)² = 1600 − 240 + 9 = 1369 with Identity 1B)
>
> **Why split like this:** squares of round numbers (100², 30²) and of small numbers (4², 7²) are easy, and 2ab is a simple multiplication.

**4.** Use Identity 1A to expand (i) (m + 3)² (ii) (6 + p)².

> [!ANSWER]
> - **(i)** (m + 3)² = m² + 2 × m × 3 + 3² = **m² + 6m + 9**
> - **(ii)** (6 + p)² = 6² + 2 × 6 × p + p² = **36 + 12p + p²**

**5.** Expand (3j + 2k)² with the identity and with the distributive property.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Identity 1A** (a = 3j, b = 2k): (3j)² + 2 × 3j × 2k + (2k)² = **9j² + 12jk + 4k²**
> - **Distributive property:** (3j + 2k)(3j + 2k) = 9j² + 6jk + 6jk + 4k² = **9j² + 12jk + 4k²** ✓
>
> Watch out: (3j)² = 9j², not 3j², because both the 3 and the j are squared.

**6.** Find the general expansion of (a − b)² using geometry, as for 55².

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw a square of side a. Inside it, the square of side a − b sits in one corner.
> 2. Cut off a strip of width b along one side: area a × b. Cut off a strip of width b along the next side: area b × a.
> 3. The corner b × b belonged to **both** strips, so it was removed twice. Add it back once.
>
> (a − b)² = a² − ab − ba + b² = **a² − 2ab + b²** (the 55² picture with a = 60, b = 5).

**7.** Use Identity 1B to find 99² and 58².

> [!ANSWER]
> - 99² = (100 − 1)² = 10000 − 200 + 1 = **9801**
> - 58² = (60 − 2)² = 3600 − 240 + 4 = **3364**

**Question 8.** Expand with Identity 1B and with the distributive property.

**(i)** (b − 6)²

> [!ANSWER]

## 6.4 Two patterns and a third identity

**Pattern 1.** Look at these: 2(2² + 1²) = 3² + 1², 2(3² + 1²) = 4² + 2², 2(6² + 5²) = 11² + 1², 2(5² + 3²) = 8² + 2².

Twice a sum of two squares is again a sum of two squares: **2(a² + b²) = (a + b)² + (a − b)²**. **Why:** add Identities 1A and 1B. The 2ab and −2ab cancel, leaving 2a² + 2b².

**Pattern 2.** 9 × 9 − 1 × 1 = 10 × 8, 8 × 8 − 6 × 6 = 14 × 2, 7 × 7 − 2 × 2 = 9 × 5, 10 × 10 − 4 × 4 = 14 × 6. The difference of two squares is (sum) × (difference).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Identity 1C:** (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b².
> **Why:** (a + b)(a − b) = a² − ab + ba − b², and −ab + ba = 0.

The picture shows it too: from an a × a square, take away a b × b corner. Cut the L-shape into two rectangles and move one: they make a rectangle (a + b) long and (a − b) wide.

![Left: a² − b² as an L-shape cut into pieces A and B; right: B moved under A to make a rectangle of sides (a + b) and (a − b)](assets/img/class8/math/6/diff_squares.svg)

**Try this.** Cut a square of paper with side 10 cm. Cut a 4 cm square from one corner. Cut the L that is left into two rectangles, and rearrange them into one long rectangle. Measure it: it should be 14 cm by 6 cm, area 84 = 100 − 16.

**Śrīdharācārya's trick for squares.** Rearranging Identity 1C: **a² = (a + b)(a − b) + b²**. Choose b to make a + b or a − b a round number.
- 31² = (31 + 1)(31 − 1) + 1² = 32 × 30 + 1 = **961**
- 197² = (197 + 3)(197 − 3) + 3² = 200 × 194 + 9 = **38809**

## 6.5 Mind the mistake, mend the mistake

Each line below was "simplified" by someone. Check each one: is there a mistake? What went wrong? What is the correct answer?

**Try this.** A quick test for any simplification: put in a small number for each letter (say 2 or 3) at the start and at the end. If the two values differ, there is a mistake somewhere. (If they agree, it is probably right, but test one more value to be safe.)

### Check yourself: mind the mistake

**1.** −3p(−5p + 2q) = −3p + 5p − 2q = p − 2q

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** The −3p must **multiply** each term, not be added to it. −3p × −5p = 15p² and −3p × 2q = −6pq.
>
> **Correct: 15p² − 6pq.**

**2.** 2(x − 1) + 3(x + 4) = 2x − 1 + 3x + 4 = 5x + 3

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** The 2 and the 3 were only multiplied into the first term of each bracket. 2 × (−1) = −2 and 3 × 4 = 12.
>
> **Correct:** 2x − 2 + 3x + 12 = **5x + 10**.

**3.** y + 2(y + 2) = (y + 2)² = y² + 4y + 4

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** "y + 2 lots of (y + 2)" is not "(y + 2) times (y + 2)". Multiply first: 2(y + 2) = 2y + 4.
>
> **Correct:** y + 2y + 4 = **3y + 4**. (Check y = 1: 1 + 2 × 3 = 7 = 3 + 4 ✓, but (1 + 2)² = 9 ✗.)

**4.** (5m + 6n)² = 25m² + 36n²

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** The middle term 2ab was forgotten (the two rectangles in the square picture).
>
> **Correct:** 25m² + 2 × 5m × 6n + 36n² = **25m² + 60mn + 36n²**.

**5.** (−q + 2)² = q² − 4q + 4

> [!ANSWER]
> **Correct.** (−q + 2)² = (2 − q)² = 4 − 4q + q², and (−q)² = q². No mistake.

**6.** 3a(2b × 3c) = 6ab × 9ac = 54a²bc

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** The distributive property is for a **sum** inside the bracket, not a product. 3a × (2b × 3c) is just one product of everything: 3 × 2 × 3 × a × b × c.
>
> **Correct: 18abc.** (The mistake multiplied by 3a twice.)

**7.** (1/2)(10s − 6) + 3 = 5s − 3 + 3 = 5s

> [!ANSWER]
> **Correct.** Half of 10s is 5s and half of −6 is −3; then −3 + 3 = 0. No mistake.

**8.** 5w² + 6w = 11w²

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** 5w² and 6w are **not like terms** (w² and w are different), so they can't be added. Try w = 2: 20 + 12 = 32, but 11 × 4 = 44.
>
> **Correct: 5w² + 6w** stays as it is (or w(5w + 6)).

**9.** 2a³ + 3a³ + 6a²b + 6ab² = 5a³ + 12a²b²

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** 2a³ + 3a³ = 5a³ is right, but 6a²b and 6ab² are not like terms (a²b has two a's, ab² has two b's), and adding them can't change the powers either.
>
> **Correct: 5a³ + 6a²b + 6ab².**

**10.** (x + 2)(x + 5) = (x + 2)x + (x + 2)5 = x² + 2x + 5x + 10 = x² + 7x + 10

> [!ANSWER]
> **Correct.** Each step distributes properly, and 2x + 5x = 7x. Check x = 1: 3 × 6 = 18 = 1 + 7 + 10 ✓.

**11.** (a + 2)(b + 4) = ab + 8

> [!ANSWER]
> **Wrong.** Only first × first and last × last were multiplied. Every term must meet every term (Identity 1).
>
> **Correct: ab + 4a + 2b + 8.**

**12.** ab² + a²b + a²b² = ab(a + b + ab)

> [!ANSWER]
> **Correct.** Multiply back: ab × a = a²b, ab × b = ab², ab × ab = a²b². All three terms come back ✓. Taking out a common factor is the distributive property used backwards.

## 6.6 This way or that way, all ways lead to the bay

Here is a pattern of circles:

![Steps 1 to 4 of a circle pattern: 3, 8, 15 and 24 circles](assets/img/class8/math/6/circles_pattern.svg)

People see this pattern in different ways, and each way gives a formula for step k:

- **(k + 1)² − 1:** a (k + 1) by (k + 1) square with one circle missing.
- **k² + 2k:** a k by k square, plus a row of k and a column of k.
- **k(k + 1) + k:** k rows of (k + 1), plus k more.
- **k(k + 2):** move the last row up to make k rows of (k + 2).

![Step 3 coloured four ways, one for each formula; each counts 15](assets/img/class8/math/6/circles_four_ways.svg)

They look different, but expanding each one gives the same thing:

| (k + 1)² − 1 | k² + 2k | k(k + 1) + k | k(k + 2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| = k² + 2k + 1 − 1 = **k² + 2k** | = **k² + 2k** | = k² + k + k = **k² + 2k** | = **k² + 2k** |

> [!TIP]
> When two people describe the same pattern correctly, their formulas **must** be equal for every k. Expanding them is how you check. If they don't match, one of them has made a mistake.

> 3. Whole park: (2g + 4w)(g + 2w) = 2g² + 4gw + 4gw + 8w² = 2g² + 8gw + 8w².
> 4. Take away the two plots (2g²): path area = **8gw + 8w² = 8w(g + w)** sq ft.
>
> (If the strip between the plots were only w wide, the length would be 2g + 3w and the path area 7gw + 6w².)

### 12. Coin Conjoin. Ten coins form a triangle pointing up. Turn it upside down by sliding one coin at a time. What is the fewest moves? A 3-coin triangle needs 1 move and a 6-coin triangle needs 2. What about 15 coins? Is there a rule?

![The 10-coin triangle before and after: three corner coins move and seven stay](assets/img/class8/math/6/coin_flip.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> **10 coins: 3 moves.** Keep the 7 coins in the middle where they are. Move the top coin to below the bottom row, and the two bottom corner coins up to the ends of the second row.
>
> **15 coins: 5 moves**, and 21 coins: 7 moves.
>
> **Rule:** the fewest moves is the number of coins **divided by 3, rounded down** (3 → 1, 6 → 2, 10 → 3, 15 → 5, 21 → 7). **Why it's about a third:** the best you can do is place the upside-down triangle so it overlaps the old one as much as possible; the coins outside the overlap sit near the three corners, roughly a third of all the coins.

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Distributive property:** a(b + c) = ab + ac. It turns "multiply a sum" into "add the products".
> - **Identity 1:** (a + m)(b + n) = ab + an + mb + mn: **each term times each term**. Signs follow the integer rules, so it covers subtraction too.
> - If a and b each grow by 1, ab grows by **a + b + 1** (the extra 1 is the corner).
> - **1A:** (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b². **1B:** (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b². **1C:** (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b².
> - **Fast multiplying:** × 11 adds neighbouring digits; × 101 adds digits two apart; × 99 = × 100 − the number.
> - Squares near round numbers: 104² = (100 + 4)², 99² = (100 − 1)², 98 × 102 = 100² − 2².
> - Different correct formulas for one pattern **expand to the same expression**.
> - Only **like terms** can be added: 5w² + 6w stays as it is.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** (a + b)² is **not** a² + b² (don't lose 2ab) • (a + 2)(b + 4) is **not** ab + 8 • multiply **every** term inside a bracket: 2(x − 1) = 2x − **2** • y + 2(y + 2) is 3y + 4, not (y + 2)² • 3a(2b × 3c) = 18abc: distribute over **sums** only • (3j)² = 9j², not 3j² • a minus sign in front of a bracket changes **every** sign inside.
