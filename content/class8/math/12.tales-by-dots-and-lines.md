## The mean is a balance point

Last year the mean was a **fair share**: add everything, split equally. This year there's a second, more powerful picture.

For 3 and 7 the mean is 5 — exactly **halfway**. But for **10, 10, 11, 17** the mean is `48/4 =` **12**, which is *not* halfway between 10 and 17.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The mean is the point where the total distance to the values on the left equals the total distance to the values on the right.** It's the balancing point of the dot plot, not the midpoint of the extremes.
>
> For 10, 10, 11, 17 with mean 12:
> left `= 2 + 2 + 1 =` **5** · right `= 5` ✓

**There is only ONE such balance point.** Move right of 12 and every left distance grows while every right distance shrinks — the balance breaks. Move left and the opposite happens.

## How the mean moves

| Change | Effect on the mean | Why |
|---|---|---|
| add a value **greater** than the mean | **increases** | the right side gets heavier, so the balance point shifts right |
| add a value **less** than the mean | **decreases** | |
| add a value **equal** to the mean | **no change** | it adds zero distance to either side |
| remove a value greater than the mean | **decreases** | |
| remove a value less than the mean | **increases** | |

> [!TIP]
> **You can add several values and keep the mean fixed**, as long as they balance. Two values with mean 12 (say 9 and 15) leave a mean of 12 alone. So do **two below and one above** — e.g. adding 10, 11 and 15 to a mean-12 set: deficits `2 + 1 = 3` and surplus `3` ✓

### Adding a constant to everything

Data `8, 3, 10, 13, 4, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 5` has sum 79 over 11 values → mean **7.18**.
Add 10 to every value → the new mean is **17.18**, exactly 10 more.

**Why (algebra):**

```
[(x₁+3) + (x₂+3) + … + (xₙ+3)] / n
= (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ + 3n) / n
= (x₁ + … + xₙ)/n  +  3n/n
= a + 3
```

**Adding c to every value adds c to the mean.** The whole dot plot slides sideways; the balance point slides with it.

**Subtracting works the same way** — subtract 2 from everything and the mean drops by 2.

### Multiplying everything

Multiply every value by 5 → the mean is multiplied by 5.

**Why:**

```
(5x₁ + 5x₂ + … + 5xₙ)/n = 5(x₁ + … + xₙ)/n = 5a
```

using the **distributive property** to pull the 5 out.

> [!TIP]
> **Practical use.** 24 students' heights average 150.2 cm, but everyone was wearing 1 cm shoes. You do **not** need to remeasure — just subtract 1 from the mean: **149.2 cm**.

## How the median moves

**The median is the middle value of the sorted data** (the average of the two middle values if there is an even count).

- Adding a value **greater** than the median **raises** it (or leaves it).
- Adding a value **less** than the median **lowers** it (or leaves it).
- Adding **one value below and one above** typically leaves it unchanged.

**Worked:** data with median 8. Add 11 (bigger than 8). Now the two middle values are 8 and 11, so the median becomes `(8 + 11)/2 =` **9.5**.

> [!WARNING]
> **"Removing a value less than the median decreases the median" is FALSE.** Taking a small value away pushes the middle *up*, so the median **increases or stays the same** — never decreases.

## Finding a missing value

**If you know the mean and all but one value, you can recover the missing one.**

**Worked:** ten wrestlers' weights `42, 40, 39, 33, 48, 38, 42, 35, 32, w` have mean 39.2.

```
sum = 39.2 × 10 = 392
known sum = 349
w = 392 − 349 = 43 kg
```

**Worked:** 15 trees average 25.6 coconuts, but one tree's count was recorded **3 too high**.

```
recorded total = 25.6 × 15 = 384
true total     = 384 − 3 = 381
correct mean   = 381/15 = 25.4
```

> [!TIP]
> **The key move: turn the mean back into a TOTAL.** `sum = mean × count`. Everything else is arithmetic on the total.

## Mean and median from a frequency table

| Family size | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 3 | 11 | 9 | 7 | 3 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

> [!WARNING]
> **Do NOT average the row of values.** `(3+4+…+10)/8 = 6.5` is **wrong** — it pretends each family size occurred once.

**Correct mean — weight each value by its frequency:**

```
sum   = 3×3 + 4×11 + 5×9 + 6×7 + 7×3 + 8×1 + 9×1 + 10×1
      = 9 + 44 + 45 + 42 + 21 + 8 + 9 + 10 = 188
count = 3 + 11 + 9 + 7 + 3 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 36
mean  = 188/36 = 5.22
```

**Median without writing 36 numbers — use a running total:**

```
after 3s:      3 values   (positions 1–3)
after 4s:     14 values   (positions 4–14)
after 5s:     23 values   (positions 15–23)
```

With 36 values, the median is the average of the **18th and 19th**. Both fall in the 15–23 block, so both are **5** → **median = 5**.

## Spreadsheets

A **spreadsheet** is a grid of **cells** named by column letter and row number — `E5` is column E, row 5.

**A range is written `Start:End`**: `B3:G3` is one row of six cells, `D2:D6` is one column of five.

| Formula | What it does |
|---|---|
| `=SUM(B3:G3)` | total of one student's marks across subjects |
| `=AVERAGE(B7:D7)` | one student's average over three subjects |
| `=AVERAGE(G2:G23)` | the class average in the subject in column G |

> [!TIP]
> **Why formulas beat a calculator:** change one mark and every total and average that depends on it updates **automatically**. The spreadsheet stores the *rule*, not just the answer.

## Line graphs

**A line graph joins data points with segments. It is the right choice for showing CHANGE OVER TIME.**

**Why not a column graph?** 13 years × 4 countries would need **52 bars** — cluttered and hard to compare. A line graph shows each country's trend as one continuous path.

### Reading a line graph in two steps

**Step 1 — identify what is given.** What's on each axis? What's the scale? Which line is which? (Different **marker shapes** matter, not just colours — they survive black-and-white printing and help readers who can't distinguish colours.)

**Step 2 — infer and interpret.** Describe trends, peaks, troughs, and compare lines.

**Example — monthly maximum temperature:**

- **Punjab** rises from ~19 °C in January to ~38 °C in June, dips to just under 35 °C in July, stays flat to September, then falls steadily to ~23 °C in December.
- **Kerala** is nearly **flat all year** — peak ~33 °C in April, low ~29 °C in July.
- **Conclusion:** Punjab's temperature **varies far more**; Kerala's summer and winter maxima are almost the same.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Steepness = rate of change.** A steeper segment means a bigger change over the same time gap. That's how you compare "which year saw the fastest growth" without reading off numbers.

### What a line graph does NOT tell you

From a graph of space launches by the USA, China, Russia and the world:

- **The three countries' counts don't add up to the world count** ⇒ other countries exist but aren't shown.
- **"Nepal launched nothing"** cannot be concluded — Nepal simply isn't on the graph.
- Claims about years **outside** the plotted range are guesses, not readings.

> [!WARNING]
> **A graph only supports claims about what it actually plots.** Absence from a chart is not evidence of absence in the world.

### Knowing how the data was made

Monthly *average* rainfall means: rainfall totals for that month collected **over several years**, then averaged. Monthly *maximum* temperature means the highest reading across the state's weather stations that month.

> [!TIP]
> **Understanding how data was produced tells you what it can and can't answer** — and warns you about bias or missing pieces before you draw conclusions.

**Rainfall example:** Kovalam, Udupi and Mumbai (**west coast**) peak in **June–August** — the south-west monsoon. Rameswaram and Chennai (**east coast**) peak in **October–December** — the north-east monsoon. January–March are dry almost everywhere.

## Other ways of showing data

**Infographic** — a map or picture carrying the data, e.g. states shaded on a scale from −100 (wheat preferred) to +100 (rice preferred). A **geographic split** jumps out immediately in a way a table never could.

**Activity strip** — one day drawn as 48 boxes of 30 minutes each, coloured by activity. Comparing three strips shows at a glance which day was a school day, when lunch break falls, and which afternoon held a long film.

**Smooth-looking line graphs** — with 80 closely spaced points (like sleep duration by age from 6 to 75), the line looks like a curve. It reveals that sleep falls from ~9.5 h at age 6 to ~8 h between 30 and 50, then rises again to ~8.5 h. Seventy columns would have buried that pattern.

## Facts worth checking yourself

- **The mean of the first 50 natural numbers is 25.5** — for any evenly spaced list, **the mean equals the average of the first and last** (`(1 + 50)/2`).
- **The mean of the first 50 odd numbers is 50** (`(1 + 99)/2`).
- **The mean of the first 50 multiples of 4 is 102** (`(4 + 200)/2`).
- **"The average of two even numbers is even" is FALSE** — `(2 + 4)/2 = 3`.
- **"The average of two multiples of 5 is a multiple of 5" is FALSE** — `(5 + 10)/2 = 7.5`.
- **The average of five multiples of 5 need not be a multiple of 5** — `(5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 10)/5 = 6`.
- **If one person loses 2 kg and two gain 1 kg, the total is unchanged**, so the **mean stays the same** — but the **median may move**, depending on who changed.
