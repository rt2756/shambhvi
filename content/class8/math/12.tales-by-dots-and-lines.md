## What this chapter is about

Data tells stories, if we know how to read it. This chapter looks again at the **mean** and **median**, and then at **line graphs** and other pictures of data.

- Why is the mean the "balance point" of the data?
- What happens to the mean or median when you add, remove or change values?
- How do you find a mean or median quickly from a frequency table, or with a spreadsheet?
- How do you read a line graph, and what can (and can't) you conclude from it?

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** The **mean** (average) is the sum of the values divided by how many there are. The **median** is the middle value when the data is sorted (or the average of the two middle values when there is an even number of them). **Frequency** is how many times a value occurs. A **dot plot** shows each value as a dot above a number line. A **line graph** joins points with straight lines, usually to show change over time.

## 12.1 The mean as a balance point

The mean of 3 and 7 is (3 + 7) ÷ 2 = 5, exactly halfway between them. With more values the mean is not always halfway between the smallest and largest, but it is always the **balance point**:

![Dot plots of 6, 7, 8 (mean 7) and 10, 10, 11, 17 (mean 12), with the distances below and above the mean](assets/img/class8/math/12/balance.svg)

For 10, 10, 11 and 17 (mean 12): the values below the mean are 2, 2 and 1 away from it (total 5), and the value above is 5 away (total 5). **The total distance below the mean equals the total distance above it.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The mean is the one point where the total distance to the values below it equals the total distance to the values above it.**
> **Why:** sum of (value − mean) over all values = (sum of values) − n × mean = 0, because the mean is the sum divided by n. The values above the mean add positive amounts and those below add negative amounts, so the two totals must be equal.

**There is only one such point.** Move the "centre" up from 12 and every distance below grows while every distance above shrinks, so they can't stay equal; moving down does the opposite.

**Adding a value.** Add a value **above** the mean and the mean moves up; **below**, it moves down. For 5, 5, 10, 24 (mean 11), adding 17 gives 61 ÷ 5 = **12.2**:

![Before: 5, 5, 10, 24 with mean 11. After adding 17: mean 12.2](assets/img/class8/math/12/new_value.svg)

> [!TIP]
> **Fair-share view:** the mean is what each value would be if the total were shared out equally. A newcomer bringing more than the fair share raises everyone's share; one bringing less lowers it; one bringing exactly the fair share changes nothing.

**Try this.** Take 10 counters in stacks of 2, 3 and 5. Even them out so that each stack has the same number (the mean). Now add a stack of 6 and even them out again. Did the mean go up?

### Check yourself

**1.** Find and mark the mean of each collection: (a) 4, 6, 14 (b) 9, 9, 12, 18 (c) 1, 5, 6, 12. Explain how it is the centre.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **(a)** (4 + 6 + 14) ÷ 3 = 24 ÷ 3 = **8**. Below: 8 − 4 = 4 and 8 − 6 = 2, total 6. Above: 14 − 8 = 6. Equal ✓.
> 2. **(b)** 48 ÷ 4 = **12**. Below: 3 + 3 = 6. Above: 6. ✓
> 3. **(c)** 24 ÷ 4 = **6**. Below: 5 + 1 = 6. Above: 6 (12 − 6). The value 6 is at the mean and adds 0. ✓
>
> In (a) the mean 8 is **not** halfway between 4 and 14 (that would be 9): the two smaller values pull it down.

**2.** Check that "distance below = distance above" holds for 3, 7 and for 8, 9 too.

> [!ANSWER]
> - 3 and 7, mean 5: below 5 − 3 = 2, above 7 − 5 = 2 ✓.
> - 8 and 9, mean 8.5: below 0.5, above 0.5 ✓.
>
> With two values the mean is always exactly halfway, because each is the same distance from it.

**3.** What happens to the mean when a value is **removed**? When does it go up, down or stay the same?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Removing a value **above** the mean: the mean goes **down** (we lose some of the "above" distance, so the balance point moves down).
> - Removing a value **below** the mean: the mean goes **up**.
> - Removing a value **equal** to the mean: the mean **stays the same**.
>
> For instance 2, 4, 6, 12 has mean 6. Remove 12: mean 4. Remove 2: mean 22 ÷ 3 ≈ 7.3. Remove 6: mean 18 ÷ 3 = 6.

**4.** What happens if a value equal to the mean is included or removed? Explain with fair shares.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The mean doesn't change.** That value is exactly the fair share. Including it adds one more person with exactly a fair share, so nobody's share changes; removing it takes away one person who had exactly the fair share. In algebra: if n values have total nm, adding m gives total (n + 1)m, and (n + 1)m ÷ (n + 1) = m.

## 12.2 Keeping or shifting the mean

### Check yourself

**1.** Data with mean 9. Can you include or remove **2** values and keep the mean at 9?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes:** include (or remove) two values the **same distance** from 9 on opposite sides, such as **7 and 11**, or 5 and 13, or 9 and 9. Their distances below and above cancel, so the balance point stays at 9. (In total they add 2 × 9 = 18, exactly two fair shares.)

**2.** Can you include or remove **3** values and keep the mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes**, as long as the three values add up to 3 × 9 = 27, for instance **8, 9, 10**, or 9, 9, 9, or 5, 10, 12. Then the distances below and above the mean balance.

**3.** Include 2 values below the mean and 1 above, keeping the mean at 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> The two below must be as far below (in total) as the one above is above. For instance **7 and 8** (2 + 1 = 3 below) with **12** (3 above). Check: 7 + 8 + 12 = 27 = 3 × 9 ✓.

**4.** Include 2 values above the mean and 1 below, keeping the mean at 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> For instance **10 and 11** (1 + 2 = 3 above) with **6** (3 below). Check: 10 + 11 + 6 = 27 ✓.

### Relatively unchanged

**Example 1: everyone gets 10 more.** The data 8, 3, 10, 13, 4, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 5 has sum 79 and mean 79 ÷ 11 ≈ **7.18**. Add 10 to every value: 18, 13, 20, … The dot plot just slides 10 to the right, so the mean is **7.18 + 10 = 17.18**, with no new adding up.

![The data and the same data with 10 added to every value: the whole plot shifts 10 to the right](assets/img/class8/math/12/shift_plots.svg)

**Why, with algebra:** if x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ = na, then (x₁ + 3) + (x₂ + 3) + … + (xₙ + 3) = na + 3n, and dividing by n gives **a + 3**.

**Example 2: every value doubled.** (2x₁ + 2x₂ + … + 2xₙ) ÷ n = 2 × (x₁ + … + xₙ) ÷ n = **2a**, by the distributive property. The same works for any multiplier: multiply every value by 5 and the mean is multiplied by 5.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Adding (or subtracting) the same number c to every value adds (or subtracts) c to the mean. Multiplying every value by k multiplies the mean by k. The **median** behaves the same way.

### Check yourself

**1.** Find the mean of 8, 3, 10, 13, 4, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 5. Then find the mean of the data with 10 added to every value, quickly.

> [!ANSWER]
> Sum = 79, so the mean is 79 ÷ 11 ≈ **7.18**. With 10 added to each of the 11 values, the sum grows by 11 × 10 = 110, so the mean grows by 110 ÷ 11 = 10: **≈ 17.18**.

**2.** Explain with algebra what happens to the mean when 2 is subtracted from every value.

> [!ANSWER]
> If the values x₁, …, xₙ have mean a, their sum is na. After subtracting 2 from each: (x₁ − 2) + … + (xₙ − 2) = na − 2n. Divide by n: **a − 2**. The mean goes down by exactly 2.

**3.** Explain it with fair shares.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take 2 away from every person and the total goes down by 2 for each person. Sharing the smaller total equally, each fair share is 2 less than before. (If each of 5 friends gives back ₹2, the money per person drops by ₹2.)

## 12.3 The median, and finding unknowns

> [!TIP]
> **The median only cares about order.** Making the largest value huge doesn't move the median at all, but it moves the mean. That is why the median is often used for things like incomes, where a few very large values would distort the mean.

**Example 7: median after adding a value.** 3, 5, 8, 11, 14 has median 8. Add 11: now there are more values above 8 than below it, so the median moves up, to the average of the two middle values: (8 + 11) ÷ 2 = **9.5**.

![3, 5, 8, 11, 14 with median 8; after including 11, median 9.5](assets/img/class8/math/12/median_shift.svg)

**Example 3: a smudged weight.** A coach recorded ten wrestlers' weights: 42, 40, 39, 33, 48, 38, 42, 35, 32 and one smudged value w, with mean 39.2 kg.
1. Total = 10 × 39.2 = 392.
2. The nine known weights add to 349.
3. w = 392 − 349 = **43 kg**.

**Example 4: a miscounted tree.** Venkayya's 15 coconut trees average 25.6 coconuts. One tree's count was 3 too many.
1. Recorded total = 15 × 25.6 = 384.
2. Correct total = 384 − 3 = 381.
3. Correct mean = 381 ÷ 15 = **25.4**.

You don't need the individual counts: **mean × number of values = total**. **Why:** the mean was found by dividing the total by the number of values, so multiplying undoes the division.

## 12.4 Frequency tables and spreadsheets

### Mean and median from a frequency table

**Example 6: family sizes.** 36 students' family sizes:

| Family size | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Students | 3 | 11 | 9 | 7 | 3 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

> [!WARNING]
> The mean is **not** (3 + 4 + … + 10) ÷ 8 = 6.5. That ignores how often each size occurs.

**Mean:** multiply each value by its frequency: 9 + 44 + 45 + 42 + 21 + 8 + 9 + 10 = 188, and 188 ÷ 36 ≈ **5.22**.

**Why multiply by the frequency:** the value 4 really appears 11 times in the data, so it must be added 11 times; 4 × 11 is just a quick way to add it 11 times.

**Median:** with 36 values, the median is the average of the 18th and 19th. Add up frequencies from the smallest: 3 → positions 1-3; 3 + 11 = 14 → positions 4-14 are 4s; 14 + 9 = 23 → positions 15-23 are 5s. So the 18th and 19th are both 5: **median 5**.

### Check yourself: Figure it out (mean and median)

**1.** Find the mean of (i) the first 50 natural numbers (ii) the first 50 odd numbers (iii) the first 50 multiples of 4. What do you notice?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. **(i)** 1, 2, …, 50 are evenly spaced, so the mean is halfway between the first and last: (1 + 50) ÷ 2 = **25.5**.
> 2. **(ii)** 1, 3, …, 99: (1 + 99) ÷ 2 = **50**.
> 3. **(iii)** 4, 8, …, 200: (4 + 200) ÷ 2 = **102**. (This is 4 times (i): every value is 4 times a natural number.)

> Balance check: below 9 the distances are 3 + 2 + 2 = 7; above, 1 + 3 = 4 so far, so the new value must add 3 more above: 9 + 3 = 12 ✓.

**5.** Find the median of 8, 10, 19, 23, 26, 34, 40, 41, 41, 48, 51, 55, 70, 84, 91, 92. (i) Include one value without changing the median. (ii) Include two values without changing it. (iii) Remove one value without changing it.

> [!ANSWER]
> 16 values, so the median is the average of the 8th and 9th: (41 + 41) ÷ 2 = **41**.
> 1. **(i)** Include **41**: 17 values, the 9th (middle) is 41.
> 2. **(ii)** Include one value **below 41 and one above** (say 5 and 100), or 41 and 41: 18 values, and the 9th and 10th are still both 41. **Why:** one new value on each side moves the middle up by half a place and down by half a place, which cancel.
> 3. **(iii)** Remove **41** (one of the two): 15 values, the 8th is 41.
>
> **Why it works:** the two middle values are both 41, and these changes keep a 41 in the middle position(s).

**Question 6.** Always, sometimes or never true?

**(i)** Removing a value less than the median decreases the median.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.** Removing a value from below leaves more values above, so the middle moves **up** or stays the same. For 1, 3, 5, 7 (median 4): remove 1 → 3, 5, 7, median 5 (up). For 1, 5, 5, 5, 9 (median 5): remove 1 → median 5 (same). It can never go down.

**(ii)** Including a value less than the mean decreases the mean.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always.** The new value adds more distance below the mean than above, so the balance point must move down. (Algebra: if the old total is nm and the new value is v < m, the new mean is (nm + v)/(n + 1) < (nm + m)/(n + 1) = m.)

**(iii)** Including any 4 values does not affect the median.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes.** Two below and two above the median keep it (for 1, 5, 9: add 0, 2, 20, 30 → 0, 1, 2, 5, 9, 20, 30, median 5). Four values all above it change it (add 20, 21, 22, 23 → median 20).

**(iv)** Including 4 values less than the median increases the median.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.** Adding values below pushes the middle position down: the median decreases or (if many values equal the median) stays the same, but it can't increase.

## 12.5 Line graphs

A **line graph** marks a value for each time (month, year, …) and joins the points with lines, so you can see how something **changes over time**.

![Monthly maximum temperature in 2023 (approximate): Kerala stays between about 29 and 33 °C; Punjab rises from about 19 °C in January to 38 °C in June and falls to 23 °C in December](assets/img/class8/math/12/temperature.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Reading a graph in two steps.**
> 1. **What is given:** the title, what each axis shows and its units, the scale, the meaning of each line or colour.
> 2. **What it says:** describe the pattern (rising, falling, flat, highest, lowest), compare the lines, and draw only the conclusions the data supports.
> **Why both steps:** a graph can only answer questions about what it actually measured. Knowing how the data was collected (for instance, the hottest reading of the month from all the weather stations in a state) tells you what you can and can't conclude.

**Example 5: reading the temperature graph.** Punjab's monthly maximum rises from about 19 °C in January to about 38 °C in June, dips to about 35 °C in July, stays roughly flat to September and falls to about 23 °C in December. Kerala's stays almost flat, between about 29 °C (July) and 33 °C (April). **Punjab's temperature varies far more over the year.** (Kerala is near the sea and close to the equator; Punjab is far inland.)

The steeper a line segment, the faster the change. **Why:** the slope is (change in value) ÷ (change in time); a bigger change in the same time makes a steeper segment. A line graph is better than a bar graph for **many points over time**, where bars would be crowded.

> [!NOTE]
> **Rainfall around the coast.** Average monthly rainfall graphs for six coastal cities show a clear pattern: Kovalam, Udupi and Mumbai (west coast) get most of their rain in **June-August** from the south-west monsoon, and much more of it; Rameswaram and Chennai (east coast) get most of theirs in **October-December** from the north-east monsoon; Puri, on the east coast but further north, peaks in July-September. January-March is dry everywhere.

**(i)** Fill a 3 × 3 grid with 9 different numbers so that the average of every row, column and diagonal is 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each line of three must add to 30. Doubling the classic magic square (whose lines add to 15) works:
>
> | 8 | 18 | 4 |
> |---|---|---|
> | 6 | 10 | 14 |
> | 16 | 2 | 12 |
>
> Rows: 30, 30, 30. Columns: 8 + 6 + 16 = 30, 18 + 10 + 2 = 30, 4 + 14 + 12 = 30. Diagonals: 8 + 10 + 12 = 30, 4 + 10 + 16 = 30. Every average is 10 ✓.

**(ii)** Can you change a few numbers and still get 10 everywhere?

> [!ANSWER]

>
> | 11 | 13 | 6 |
> |---|---|---|
> | 5 | 10 | 15 |
> | 14 | 7 | 9 |
>

The centre must always be **10**: the middle row, middle column and both diagonals all pass through it, and adding those four lines counts the centre 4 times and every other square once, so 4 × 30 = 120 = (total of all 9 numbers, 90) + 3 × centre, giving centre = 10.

### 3. Fill the blanks so that the median of 5, 21, 14, __, __, __ is 13. How many possibilities are there with counting numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> With 6 values, the median is the average of the 3rd and 4th: they must add to **26**. Since 14 is one of the values, the middle pair must be **(12, 14)** or **(13, 13)** (any other pair adding to 26 would have 14 sitting between them).
> - **(13, 13):** blanks 13, 13 and any c ≤ 13, e.g. 5, 13, 13, 14, 21, 1. There are 13 choices for c.
> - **(12, 14):** blanks 12, x ≤ 12 and y ≥ 14, e.g. 1, 5, 12, 14, 21, 30. y can be **any** number from 14 upwards.
>
> So there are **infinitely many** possibilities (unless the blanks have a largest allowed value).

### 5. True or false? Justify, using algebra if needed.

**(i)** The average of two even numbers is even.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not always.** (2a + 2b) ÷ 2 = a + b, which is even only when a + b is even. 2 and 4 average 3 (odd); 2 and 6 average 4 (even).

**(ii)** The average of any two multiples of 5 is a multiple of 5.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not always.** (5a + 5b) ÷ 2 = 5(a + b)/2, which may not even be a whole number: 5 and 10 average **7.5**. It is a multiple of 5 only when a + b is even.

**(iii)** The average of any five multiples of 5 is a multiple of 5.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Not always.** (5a + 5b + 5c + 5d + 5e) ÷ 5 = a + b + c + d + e, a whole number, but not necessarily a multiple of 5: 5, 5, 5, 5, 10 average **6**.

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - The **mean** is the balance point: total distance below = total distance above.
> - Including a value above the mean raises it; below, lowers it; equal, no change. Several values keep the mean if their total equals (number of values) × mean.
> - Adding c to every value adds c to the mean (and median); multiplying every value by k multiplies the mean by k.
> - **Total = mean × number of values**: use it to find a missing or corrected value.
> - From a frequency table: mean = Σ(value × frequency) ÷ Σ frequency; median: count up the frequencies to the middle position.
> - **Spreadsheets:** cells like E5; =SUM(B2:G2), =AVERAGE(G2:G23).
> - **Line graphs** show change over time. Read in two steps: what is given, then what it says, and claim only what the data shows.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** the mean of a frequency table is **not** the average of the different values • the mean is not always halfway between the smallest and largest value • an average says nothing about **every** individual ("all rural 15-year-olds…") • a graph of percentages says nothing about **how many** people • things missing from a graph (Nepal, power cuts) can't be concluded either way • removing a value below the median can never lower it.
