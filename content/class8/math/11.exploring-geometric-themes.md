## What this chapter is about

Two geometric themes:

1. **Fractals**: shapes made of smaller copies of themselves, again and again (a fern, a tree, a coastline). You will build three famous ones and count their pieces with algebra.
2. **Visualising solids**: imagining solids in your head, unfolding them into flat **nets**, finding the **shortest path** across a box, and drawing solids on paper with **projections** (front, top and side views) and **isometric** drawings.

> [!NOTE]
> **Words we will use.** A **fractal** is self-similar: it contains smaller copies of itself. A **face** is a flat surface of a solid, an **edge** is where two faces meet, a **vertex** is a corner. A **net** is a flat shape that folds into a solid. A **projection** is the "shadow" of an object on a plane when light comes straight at the plane.

## 11.1 Fractals

A fern leaf is made of smaller leaves shaped like the whole leaf; each of those is made of even smaller copies. Trees, clouds, coastlines, mountains and lightning are similar. Mathematicians build such shapes **step by step** with a simple rule.

### The Sierpinski carpet

Start with a square (step 0). Cut it into 9 equal squares and remove the middle one (step 1). Do the same to each of the 8 remaining squares (step 2), and so on.

![Steps 0, 1, 2 of the Sierpinski carpet: 1 square; 8 squares and 1 hole; 64 squares and 9 holes](assets/img/class8/math/11/carpet_steps.svg)

Let Rₙ be the number of squares remaining at step n and Hₙ the number of holes.
- Each remaining square makes **8** squares at the next step: Rₙ₊₁ = 8Rₙ, so **Rₙ = 8ⁿ**.
- Each remaining square also makes **one new hole**, and old holes stay: **Hₙ₊₁ = Hₙ + Rₙ**.

**Example 1: the carpet at step 3.** R₃ = 8³ = **512** squares, and H₃ = 1 + 8 + 64 = **73** holes. **Why:** going from step 2 to step 3, each of the 64 squares makes one new hole, so 9 + 64 = 73. Each small square is (1/3)³ = 1/27 of the side, so the remaining area is 512 × (1/27)² = 512/729 ≈ 0.70 of the original.

### The Sierpinski gasket (triangle)

Start with an equilateral triangle. Join the midpoints of its sides: this makes 4 equal triangles. Remove the middle one. Repeat on the 3 that remain.

![Steps 0, 1, 2 of the Sierpinski triangle: 1 triangle; 3 triangles and 1 hole; 9 triangles and 4 holes](assets/img/class8/math/11/gasket_steps.svg)

### The Koch snowflake

Start with an equilateral triangle. Split each side into 3 equal parts, raise an equilateral "bump" on the middle part and remove the base of the bump. Repeat on every side of the new shape.

![Steps 0, 1, 2 of the Koch snowflake: 3 sides, 12 sides, 48 sides](assets/img/class8/math/11/koch_steps.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In a fractal sequence, **each step multiplies** the number of pieces by a fixed number (8 for the carpet, 3 for the gasket, 4 for the snowflake's sides). So the counts grow like powers: 8ⁿ, 3ⁿ, 3 × 4ⁿ.
> **Why:** every piece is treated the same way, so every piece turns into the same number of new pieces.

**Example 2: the snowflake at step 3** (starting side 1). Sides: 3 × 4³ = **192**, each of length (1/3)³ = 1/27. Perimeter = 192/27 ≈ **7.1**, more than double the starting 3. **Why it keeps growing:** every step swaps each side for 4 pieces each a third as long, so the perimeter is multiplied by 4/3 every time.

> [!NOTE]
> **Fractals in art.** The Kandariya Mahadev temple at Khajuraho (completed around 1025 CE) is a tall tower made of smaller copies of itself, with even smaller copies on those; temples in Madurai, Hampi, Rameswaram and Varanasi show similar patterns. Fulani wedding blankets from Nigeria have diamonds inside diamonds, and the artist M. C. Escher drew lizards that repeat at smaller and smaller sizes.

**Try this.** Draw the Sierpinski triangle on a large sheet: draw a big equilateral triangle, mark the midpoints, shade the three corner triangles, and repeat inside each shaded triangle two more times. How many shaded triangles do you have?

### Check yourself

**1.** Draw steps 0 to 2 of the Sierpinski carpet.

> [!ANSWER]
> Step 0: one square. Step 1: split it into a 3 × 3 grid and remove the middle: 8 squares, 1 hole. Step 2: do the same inside each of the 8 squares: 8 × 8 = **64** squares and 1 + 8 = **9** holes (see the carpet picture above). Use squared paper with a 9 × 9 square so that step 2 lands on whole squares.

**2.** Find a formula for the number of holes Hₙ.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each step adds one hole for every square remaining at the step before: H₀ = 0, H₁ = 1, H₂ = 1 + 8, H₃ = 1 + 8 + 8², and so on.
>
> **Hₙ = 1 + 8 + 8² + … + 8ⁿ⁻¹.** (This sum equals (8ⁿ − 1)/7: for n = 2, (64 − 1)/7 = 9 ✓.)

**3.** Show that joining the midpoints of an equilateral triangle's sides makes 4 identical equilateral triangles.

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the triangle have side s.
> 1. **Corner triangles:** each has two sides of length s/2 (half-sides) with the 60° angle of the big triangle between them. So it is isosceles, and its two base angles are equal: (180° − 60°) ÷ 2 = 60°. All its angles are 60°, so it is **equilateral** with side s/2.
> 2. **Middle triangle:** its three sides are the bases of the three corner triangles, each s/2. So it is equilateral with side s/2 too.
> 3. All four have sides s/2, s/2, s/2, so they are **identical** (congruent by SSS).

### Check yourself: Figure it out (Sierpinski)

**1.** Draw steps 0 to 2 of the Sierpinski triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> See the gasket picture above: step 1 has 3 triangles and the middle removed; step 2 has 9 small triangles and 4 holes (1 big, 3 small). Isometric dot paper makes the midpoints easy to find.

**2.** Find the number of holes and of remaining triangles at each step.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Triangles:** each remaining triangle becomes 3, so Tₙ = **3ⁿ** (1, 3, 9, 27, …).
> - **Holes:** each remaining triangle adds one hole: Hₙ = 1 + 3 + 3² + … + 3ⁿ⁻¹ = **(3ⁿ − 1)/2** (0, 1, 4, 13, …).
>
> Check step 2: 9 triangles and 4 holes ✓.

**3.** Find the area remaining at step n of each Sierpinski shape, starting from area 1.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Carpet:** each step keeps 8 of every 9 equal small squares, so the area is multiplied by 8/9: area = **(8/9)ⁿ**.
> - **Triangle:** each step keeps 3 of every 4 equal small triangles: area = **(3/4)ⁿ**.
>
> Both get smaller and smaller towards 0 as n grows: the finished fractals have **no area at all**, though they still have infinitely many pieces.

### Check yourself: Figure it out (Koch)

**1.** Draw steps 0 to 2 of the Koch snowflake.

> [!ANSWER]
> Step 0: an equilateral triangle (side 9 cm is convenient). Step 1: on each side, raise a bump on the middle 3 cm: a six-pointed star with 12 sides. Step 2: put a bump on the middle third (1 cm) of each of the 12 sides: 48 sides (see the snowflake picture).

**2.** How many sides does step n have?

> [!ANSWER]
> Every side becomes 4 sides (the bump replaces 1 part by 2), so the number of sides is multiplied by 4 each step: **3 × 4ⁿ** (3, 12, 48, 192, …).

**3.** If the starting triangle has side 1, what is the perimeter at step n?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Each step makes the sides 1/3 as long, so at step n each side is **(1/3)ⁿ**.
> 2. Perimeter = (number of sides) × (side) = 3 × 4ⁿ × (1/3)ⁿ = **3 × (4/3)ⁿ**.
>
> Each step multiplies the perimeter by 4/3 (3, 4, 5⅓, 7.11, …), so it **grows without limit**, even though the snowflake always fits inside a circle around the first triangle.

## 11.2 Building solids in your imagination

> "When I get an idea I start at once building it up in my imagination" — the inventor Nikola Tesla described working out machines entirely in his mind first.

**Try this.** Picture your name written on a board, and read the letters backwards **by looking** at them in your mind, not by saying them. Try a friend's name.

A solid looks different from different sides. Its outline from one viewpoint is its **profile**: a cartoon character who runs through a wall leaves a hole shaped like his profile.

### Check yourself

**1.** Cut the four corners off an imaginary square, each cut joining the midpoints of two neighbouring sides. What shape is left? How can the four corners make another square?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **smaller square, turned 45°**, with half the area: its sides are the four cuts, all equal, and each angle is 180° − 45° − 45° = 90°.
>
> The four cut-off corners are right-angled isosceles triangles. Put their right angles together at one point: their long sides make a square **the same size** as the one left over (each holds half the original area).

**2.** Mark each side of an equilateral triangle into thirds and cut off each corner as far as the marks. What shape do you get?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **regular hexagon**. Each cut-off corner is an equilateral triangle of side s/3 (two sides s/3 with 60° between them). So the 3 cuts are s/3 long, and the 3 middle thirds left are s/3 long: 6 equal sides, and every angle is 180° − 60° = 120°.

**3.** Mark each side of a square into thirds and cut off each corner as far as the marks. What shape is left?

> [!ANSWER]
> An **octagon**, but **not a regular one**. The four middle thirds have length s/3, while each cut is the long side of a right isosceles triangle with legs s/3, which is about 0.47s. All 8 angles are equal (135°), but the sides alternate between two lengths.

**4.** Describe a solid and a viewpoint that give (a) a square outline (b) a circular outline (c) a triangular outline.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **(a) Square:** a cube seen face-on; a square pyramid seen from above; a cylinder whose height equals its diameter, seen from the side.
> - **(b) Circle:** a sphere from any side; a cylinder or a cone seen from above.
> - **(c) Triangle:** a cone or a pyramid seen from the side; a triangular prism seen end-on.

**5.** Find solids with these pairs of profiles: (8) rectangle and circle, (9) circle and triangle, (10) rectangle and triangle, (11) trapezium and circle, (12) pentagon and rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Profiles | A solid that works | How to look |
> |---|---|---|
> | rectangle, circle | **cylinder** | from the side, from above |
> | circle, triangle | **cone** | from above, from the side |
> | rectangle, triangle | **triangular prism** (like a tent) | from the side, end-on |
> | trapezium, circle | **frustum** (a bucket: a cone with its tip cut off) | from the side, from above |
> | pentagon, rectangle | **pentagonal prism** | end-on, from the side |

**6.** Is there only one solid for each pair, or can you find others?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Many solids work.** A cylinder of any size gives a rectangle and a circle; so does a coin-shaped disc (a very flat rectangle). A square pyramid gives a triangle from the side and a square from above, and so does a tent with a square floor. Two profiles don't fix a solid, which is why engineers use **three** views, and even those can be shared (see 11.5).

## 11.3 Faces, edges, vertices and nets

Many solids can be made by folding flat card: cuboids, cylinders, cones, prisms and pyramids.

- A **prism** has two identical polygons as opposite faces (its ends), with every other face a parallelogram (a rectangle in a right prism). Triangular, pentagonal, hexagonal prisms are named after the end.
- A **pyramid** has a polygon base and one point outside it joined to every vertex of the base. A triangular pyramid is also called a **tetrahedron**.
- A cuboid or cube has **6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices**.

**Example 3: a hexagonal prism** (like an unsharpened pencil). It has 2 hexagon ends and 6 rectangle sides: **8 faces**; 6 + 6 edges round the ends and 6 along the length: **18 edges**; 6 + 6 = **12 vertices**. **Why:** every vertex of one end is joined to the matching vertex of the other end by one edge.

A **net** is a flat shape that folds into the solid (we ignore the extra flaps used for gluing). A cube has many nets; here is the cross-shaped one:

![The cross-shaped net of a cube: a column of four squares with one square on each side of the second](assets/img/class8/math/11/cube_net_basic.svg)

> [!TIP]
> **Is it a cube net?** Imagine rolling a die across the squares. Each square should touch a **different** face of the die. If two squares would land on the same face, they overlap when folded, and it is not a net. (Five squares in a row, or a 2 × 2 block, always fail.)

**Try this.** Cut out an equilateral-triangle net for an octahedron (8 triangles in a zigzag, as in the picture) and fold it. An octahedron is two square pyramids stuck together at their bases. Like the cube, it has exactly 11 different nets.

![A net of an octahedron: a zigzag strip of 7 equilateral triangles with one more hanging below](assets/img/class8/math/11/octa_net.svg)

> [!NOTE]
> A solid with 12 pentagon faces, the **dodecahedron**, also folds from a net, and mathematicians have counted exactly **43,380** different nets for it.

### Check yourself

**1.** A prism's end polygons have 10 sides. How many faces, edges and vertices? What if they have n sides?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Faces:** 2 ends + 10 side faces = **12**; in general **n + 2**.
> - **Edges:** 10 on each end + 10 joining them = **30**; in general **3n**.
> - **Vertices:** 10 on each end = **20**; in general **2n**.
>
> Check with a cube (n = 4): 6 faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices ✓.

**2.** A pyramid's base has 10 sides. How many faces, edges and vertices? What if it has n sides?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Faces:** the base + 10 triangles = **11**; in general **n + 1**.
> - **Edges:** 10 round the base + 10 up to the top = **20**; in general **2n**.
> - **Vertices:** 10 on the base + the top = **11**; in general **n + 1**.
>
> For both solids, faces + vertices − edges = 2 (12 + 20 − 30 = 2; 11 + 11 − 20 = 2). This is **Euler's formula**, true for every such solid.

**1.** Which of these are nets of a regular tetrahedron (four equilateral triangles)? (a) a big triangle cut into four; (b) a strip of four triangles; (c) three triangles round one corner with a fourth on the outside. Are there others?

**2.** Draw a net, with measurements, that folds into a regular tetrahedron.

**3.** Draw a net that folds into a square pyramid.

> [!ANSWER]
> Draw a **6 cm square** and on each of its sides an **equilateral triangle of side 6 cm** (or any isosceles triangles with the same two equal sides). Fold the triangles up: their tips meet at the apex.
>
> ![A square with an equilateral triangle on each side](assets/img/class8/math/11/pyramid_net.svg)

**4.** Unfold a cylinder by cutting along its height. What are the sides of the rectangle you get?

> [!ANSWER]
> The rectangle's height is the **height h** of the cylinder, and its width is the distance round the circle, the **circumference 2πr**. The net is that rectangle plus two circles of radius r.
>
> ![A rectangle 2πr by h with a circle above and below](assets/img/class8/math/11/cylinder_net.svg)

**5.** Slit a cone along a line from its tip O to the base and unroll it. What do you get?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **sector** of a circle (a slice of a round pizza) with centre O. Its radius is the slant length l of the cone, and its curved edge has length 2πr, the circumference of the base. Every point of the base circle is the same distance l from O, which is why the edge is part of a circle centred at O.
>
> ![A sector of radius l and a base circle](assets/img/class8/math/11/cone_net.svg)

**6.** What surface do you get if you roll up a sector-like shape where O is **not** the centre of the curved edge?

> [!ANSWER]
> The points of the base edge are then at **different** distances from O, so the tip is not straight above the centre of the base: you get a **slanted (oblique) cone**, leaning to one side. Try it with paper: cut a curved edge that bulges more on one side, roll it, and the cone leans.

**7.** Draw a net for a triangular prism.

> [!ANSWER]
> Three equal rectangles in a row (each as long as the prism, say 11 cm by 5 cm) and an **equilateral triangle of side 5 cm** on each end of the middle rectangle. Fold the rectangles into a tube with a triangular cross-section; the triangles close the ends.
>
> ![Three rectangles in a column with a triangle at each end](assets/img/class8/math/11/prism_net.svg)

**8.** Can you make a paper cut-out that wraps a ball perfectly, with no wrinkles, gaps or overlaps?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** A sphere curves in **every** direction at once. Paper can bend in one direction (round a cylinder or cone) but can't stretch, so it can't hug a ball without wrinkling or overlapping. That is why a globe's map is printed on many thin, pointed strips (gores), and even those only nearly fit.

## 11.4 Shortest paths on a box

On a flat sheet, the shortest path between two points is a straight line. On the **surface** of a box, an ant can't go through the box, only over it. **Unfold the box into a net**: a path on the box becomes a path of the same length on the net, so the shortest path is a **straight line on a suitable net**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **To find the shortest path over a box:** unfold the faces the path crosses so they lie flat, draw the straight line, and check it stays inside the net. Try **every** way of unfolding the faces in between: different nets give different lengths, and the shortest of them wins.
> **Why:** folding and unfolding doesn't stretch the card, so lengths don't change; and on flat paper a straight line is shortest.

**Example 4: a box 6 cm long, 4 cm deep and 4 cm tall.** The ant is at the centre of the front face (6 × 4) and the laddu at the centre of the top face (6 × 4). Unfold the top face up above the front face: the two centres lie on one vertical line, 2 cm below and 2 cm above the shared edge. The shortest path is **4 cm**, straight up and over. **Why it is shortest:** on the unfolded net it is a straight line, and no path on the box can be shorter than the shortest path on the net.

### Check yourself

**1.** An ant is at the centre of a side face of a box and a laddu at the centre of the top face. What is the shortest path?

> [!ANSWER]
> Unfold the top face up so it lies flat next to the side face. The two centres are then joined by a **straight line that crosses the shared edge at right angles**, at the middle of the edge. On the box this is the path straight up the side face, over the edge, and straight across the top.
>
> Its length = (half the height of the side face) + (half the depth of the top face).

**2.** Now the laddu sits at the centre of a top edge (an edge between the top and front faces), and the ant is on the side face. A path along the faces with a bend, and a straight-looking path: are either shortest? How can you be sure?

> [!ANSWER]
> Unfold the faces into a net. A path that **bends** on the net (for instance one that goes along a face to the edge and then turns) is not the shortest, because the straight segment between the same two points on the net is shorter. The shortest path is the one that is a **straight line on the net**, crossing the edge at a slant.
>
> To be **sure**, compare the straight lines on all the different nets that join the two faces; the shortest one is the answer.

**3.** A box is 8 cm long, 4 cm wide and 4 cm tall. An ant sits at the centre of the right end face (4 × 4), and a laddu sits on the bottom edge of the front face, 2 cm from the right end. Find the shortest path.

> [!ANSWER]
> Unfolding the end face **onto the top face** puts the straight line outside the net (it would cut through the box). Instead, unfold the end face **beside the front face**:
>
> ![The front face 8 × 4 with the 4 × 4 end face unfolded to its right; the ant is at the centre of the end face, the laddu on the bottom edge 2 cm from the right end](assets/img/class8/math/11/ant_net.svg)
>
> 1. The ant is 2 cm into the end face, so 2 + 2 = **4 cm** across from the laddu.
> 2. It is 2 cm above the bottom edge.
> 3. Shortest path = √(4² + 2²) = √20 ≈ **4.47 cm**. (Unfolding over the bottom face gives the same length.)

**4.** A box is 30 cm long, 12 cm wide and 12 cm tall. The ant is on one end wall, in the middle, 1 cm below the top. The laddu is on the other end wall, in the middle, 1 cm above the bottom. What is the shortest path?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Straight over the top:** 1 cm up to the top edge, 30 cm along the top, 11 cm down the other end: 1 + 30 + 11 = **42 cm**.
> - **A cleverer unfolding** that crosses five faces (the end, the top, a side wall, the bottom and the other end) lays the trip out as the long side of a right triangle with legs **24 cm and 32 cm**:
>
> ![A right triangle with legs 32 cm and 24 cm; its long side is 40 cm](assets/img/class8/math/11/spider_fly_best.svg)
>
> By the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem, √(24² + 32²) = √1600 = **40 cm**, shorter than 42 cm. This is the shortest: it beats every other unfolding.

## 11.5 Projections: drawing solids on paper

Drop a perpendicular from a point P to a plane; where it meets the plane, O, is the **projection** of P. Projecting every point of an object gives its projection, the shape of the hole it would punch going straight through the plane. A segment of length l projects to a segment of length p:

![Segment AB of length l above a plane; its projection DC has length p; AE is parallel to the plane, making a right angle at E](assets/img/class8/math/11/segment_projection.svg)

Engineers draw three projections, on three planes at right angles:
- the **front view** (on a vertical plane in front of the object),
- the **top view** (on the horizontal plane below it),
- the **side view** (on a vertical plane to its side).

| Solid | Front view | Top view | Side view |
|---|---|---|---|
| cube | square | square | square |
| cuboid | rectangle | rectangle | rectangle |
| cylinder (standing) | rectangle | circle | rectangle |
| cone (standing) | triangle | circle with its centre (the tip) | triangle |
| square pyramid | triangle | square with diagonals | triangle |
| triangular prism (lying) | rectangle | rectangle with a middle line | triangle |
| sphere | circle | circle | circle |

![Front, top and side views of a cylinder, cone, sphere, square pyramid and triangular prism](assets/img/class8/math/11/solid_views.svg)

> [!TIP]
> **Parallel lines project to parallel lines.** So a parallelogram's projection is always a parallelogram (or a segment, if it is seen edge-on), never some other quadrilateral.

**Try this.** In a dark room, shine a torch straight at a wall with a cardboard square between them. Move the torch closer and farther. Then go outside on a sunny day and turn the square in the sunlight: watch its shadow on flat ground change from a square to rectangles and parallelograms.

### Check yourself

**1.** In the segment picture, compare the projection p with the length l. When are they equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> AECD is a rectangle (AE is drawn parallel to DC, and AD, EC are both perpendicular to the plane), so AE = DC = **p**. Triangle AEB has a right angle at E, so its hypotenuse AB = l is its longest side: **p ≤ l**.
>
> They are **equal** when E = B, that is, when the segment is **parallel to the plane**. (If the segment is perpendicular to the plane, p = 0: it projects to a single point.)

**2.** What shapes can the projection of a square be, as you turn it?

> [!ANSWER]
> - a **square** (when it is parallel to the plane),
> - a **rectangle** (when it is tilted about one of its sides or a line parallel to a side),
> - a **parallelogram** (tilted any other way),
> - a **segment** (when it is edge-on).
>
> Never a kite or a trapezium: its two pairs of parallel sides stay parallel.

**3.** What is the projection of a parallelogram? Can it be a quadrilateral that is not a parallelogram? What about a regular n-sided polygon?

> [!ANSWER]
> A parallelogram projects to a **parallelogram** (or a segment), never to any other quadrilateral, because each pair of parallel sides projects to a pair of parallel sides.
>
> A regular n-gon projects to an **n-sided polygon** (or a segment) whose sides may be stretched or squashed by different amounts; sides that were parallel (as in a regular hexagon) stay parallel. It is regular again only when the polygon is parallel to the plane.

**4.** Find another object that makes the same projection as a cone.

> [!ANSWER]
> Seen from the side, a cone projects to a **triangle**: a square pyramid, a triangular card or a tent seen end-on give the same triangle. Seen from above, it projects to a **circle**: a cylinder, a ball or a coin give the same circle. One projection doesn't tell you the object, which is why three views are used.

### Check yourself: Figure it out (projections)

**1.** A segment has front, top and side views. Is there a relation between their lengths?

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the segment run Δx across, Δy back and Δz up, so its length is l with l² = Δx² + Δy² + Δz² (Baudhāyana–Pythagoras twice).
> - Front view (loses depth): f² = Δx² + Δz²
> - Top view (loses height): t² = Δx² + Δy²
> - Side view (loses width): s² = Δy² + Δz²
>
> Adding: **f² + t² + s² = 2l²**. Each view is at most as long as the segment itself.

**2.** Find the front, top and side views of a cube, cuboid, parallelepiped, cylinder, cone, prism and pyramid.

> [!ANSWER]
> With each solid standing on its base and facing the front plane, see the table and the picture above. Two more:
> - **parallelepiped** (a "slanted cuboid", leaning to the right): front view a parallelogram, top view a rectangle, side view a rectangle.
> - **triangular prism standing on a triangle**: front view a rectangle, top view a triangle, side view a rectangle.
>
> The views change if you turn the solid, so always say how it is placed.

**3.** Match each set of three views (front, top, side) to its solid: (a) circle, circle, circle; (b) rectangle, circle, rectangle; (c) triangle, circle with a dot in the middle, triangle; (d) rectangle, rectangle with a line along the middle, triangle; (e) triangle, square with both diagonals, triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **(a) sphere** — round from every side.
> - **(b) standing cylinder** — straight sides, round top.
> - **(c) standing cone** — the dot in the top view is the tip, straight above the centre.
> - **(d) triangular prism lying on a side face** — the line in the top view is the ridge along the top; the end is a triangle.
> - **(e) square pyramid** — the diagonals in the top view are the four sloping edges meeting at the apex.

**4.** Shine a torch at an object in front of a wall. What happens to the shadow as you move the torch closer or farther? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Closer → bigger shadow; farther → smaller**, and closer to the object's real size. A torch sends out rays that **spread** from one point, so near the torch they spread a lot and make a big shadow. Far away the rays are nearly **parallel**, and parallel rays straight at the wall make a shadow equal to the **projection**. The Sun is so far away that its rays are practically parallel: shadows in sunlight that falls straight on a surface are projections.

## 11.6 Solids made of cubes

Engineers often describe a solid by its three views. For solids built of identical cubes, each view is a pattern of squares.

**Example 5: views of an L of three cubes** (two side by side on the floor, one on top of the left one). Front view: an L of 3 squares. Top view: 2 squares side by side (the top cube is above the left one, so it adds no new square). Side view (from the right): a column of 2. **Why the top view has only 2 squares:** a view shows **where** there are cubes in that direction, not how many are stacked behind each other.

### Check yourself: Figure it out (cube solids)

**(iv)** Can you make other letter combinations from one solid?

> Yes, many. The rule: a cube at (across, back, up) shows in the front view at (across, up), in the top view at (across, back) and in the side view at (back, up). **6.** What shapes can the projection of a cube make as you turn it?

> [!ANSWER]
> - a **square** (looking straight at a face),
> - a **rectangle** (turned about one axis),
> - a **hexagon** (tilted so that you see three faces; a **regular** hexagon when you look straight along a corner-to-corner diagonal).

## 11.7 Isometric drawing

Balance a cube on one corner and look straight down: all 12 edges look **the same length**, and the outline is a **regular hexagon**. This is the **isometric** ("equal measure") projection. Tiling the plane with such hexagons gives **isometric dot paper**, on which the three directions of a solid (length, depth, height) are three directions of lines.

![A 2 × 2 × 2 cube drawn on isometric dots](assets/img/class8/math/11/iso_grid_cube.svg)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> On isometric paper: **height** is drawn straight up, **length** and **depth** slant at 30° to the horizontal, one each way. Count **one dot-gap per unit** in every direction.
> **Why it works:** parallel edges of the solid stay parallel in the drawing, and in this projection a unit along any of the three directions has the same length, so you can measure straight off the paper.

**Example 6: drawing a 1 × 1 × 1 cube.** Start at the front-bottom corner. Draw 1 gap up (height), 1 gap slanting up-right (length) and 1 gap slanting up-left (depth). Complete the three visible faces with lines parallel to these: a hexagon outline with a "Y" inside. **Why only three faces show:** from this viewpoint the top, front and one side face you; the other three are hidden behind.

**Try this.** On isometric dot paper, draw the five tetris shapes as rows of cubes (four in a line, a 2 × 2 square, an L, a T and an S). Draw faint lines first, then darken only the visible edges.

![The five tetris shapes as cubes: I, O, L, T and S](assets/img/class8/math/11/tetris_iso.svg)

### Check yourself

**1.** Why do all the edges of a cube balanced on a corner project to the same length?

> [!ANSWER]
> The three edges at the top corner go down at the **same angle** to the vertical, by symmetry: turning the cube a third of a turn about the vertical diagonal swaps them. Edges at the same angle to the plane shrink by the **same amount**. Every other edge is parallel to one of these three, so all 12 project to the same length (about 0.82 of the real length).

**2.** Draw the tetris shapes as cubes on isometric paper.

> [!ANSWER]
> See the picture of the five shapes above. For each, choose a direction for its long row (length, depth or height), draw the front corner, then count dot-gaps along each edge. A row of 4 cubes along the depth axis is a 1 × 1 × 4 box: draw its outline edge by edge (1 up, 4 slanting back, …) rather than cube by cube, so you don't have to rub out hidden lines.

### 1. Besides the 5 tetris shapes, are there other ways to glue four cubes together face to face?

### 2. Draw these solids on isometric dot paper.

![Three solids made of cubes, two cubes deep: an L, a T and a staircase](assets/img/class8/math/11/iso_shapes.svg)

> [!ANSWER]
> Draw each **edge by edge**: start at a front corner, and for each edge decide which direction it goes (up, along the length, or along the depth) and whether it goes forwards or backwards, then count dot-gaps. For the staircase: from the bottom front corner go 3 gaps along the length, 1 up, back 1 along the length, 1 up, back 1, 1 up, back 1, down 3; then draw the depth edges (2 gaps each) from every corner you can see, and join their ends. Shade the tops, fronts and sides differently to make it look solid.

### 3. A picture shows a ball rolling along a square loop of steps, always going **downhill** from step to step, yet arriving back where it started. Is there anything strange? Recreate it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **It is impossible in real life.** Going down at every step, the ball would end up lower than it started, so it can't return to the same step. Each corner of the drawing is fine on its own, but the whole loop can't be built.
>
> **Why the drawing fools us:** on isometric paper, a move of 1 unit along the length, 1 unit up and 1 unit back along the depth lands on the **same spot on the paper**, even though in space it is a different place. The artist uses this to join the end of the loop to its start. To recreate it, draw a square loop of steps on isometric paper, dropping one step-height on each side, and make the last side's drop "hidden" by this trick.

### 4. The impossible triangle is drawn from cubes, with three bars meeting at right angles.

![An "impossible triangle" drawn with cubes on isometric paper](assets/img/class8/math/11/penrose.svg)

**(i)** Can it be built from real cubes? What are the front, top and side profiles of a real attempt?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No, not as a closed triangle.** You can build three bars at right angles: 4 cubes along the length, then 4 up, then 4 going **towards you** (along the depth). But the end of the third bar is in a different place from the start of the first; it only **looks** joined from one special viewpoint.
>
> The real model's views are three **L shapes**: from the front, a row of 5 with a column of 4 rising at the right end; from the top, a row of 5 with 3 more squares coming forward at the right end; from the side, a column of 5 with a row of 3 at the top. None of them looks like a triangle.

**(ii)** Recreate it on an isometric grid.

> [!ANSWER]
> Draw three bars of cubes: along the length, then straight up, then along the depth **towards you**, each the same number of cubes. On the paper the last bar's end falls exactly on the first bar's start. Draw the first cube **last**, so it seems to sit in front of the last bar (the picture above is drawn this way by the script).

**(iii)** Why does the illusion work?

> [!ANSWER]
> An isometric drawing **throws away depth**. Moving +1 in length, +1 in height and −1 in depth lands on the same point of the paper, because on the paper those three directions add up to nothing. So two cubes that are really far apart (one near the viewer, one far) are drawn on top of each other, and our brain assumes they touch. Drawing one cube over the other in the "wrong" order completes the trick.

## In a nutshell

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - **Fractals** repeat a rule at smaller and smaller scales. Sierpinski carpet: 8ⁿ squares, area (8/9)ⁿ. Sierpinski triangle: 3ⁿ triangles, area (3/4)ⁿ. Koch snowflake: 3 × 4ⁿ sides, perimeter 3 × (4/3)ⁿ.
> - **Prism** with n-sided ends: n + 2 faces, 3n edges, 2n vertices. **Pyramid** with an n-sided base: n + 1 faces, 2n edges, n + 1 vertices. Faces + vertices − edges = 2.
> - A cube has **11 nets**; no net has 5 squares in a row or a 2 × 2 block. A regular tetrahedron has **2** nets.
> - **Shortest path on a box** = the shortest straight line over all suitable nets.
> - **Projections:** front, top and side views; parallel lines stay parallel; a projected length is at most the real length.
> - **Isometric drawing:** up for height, 30° slants for length and depth, equal units in all three.

> [!WARNING]
> **Top traps:** a staircase of six squares **is** a cube net • a 2 × 2 block in a shape means **not** a cube net • two or even three views may fit several different solids • a straight line on the net must stay **inside** the net • torchlight shadows are bigger than the projection; sunlight shadows (falling straight on) equal it • the Koch snowflake's perimeter grows without limit even though its size doesn't.
