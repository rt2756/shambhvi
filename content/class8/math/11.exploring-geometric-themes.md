Two themes in this chapter: **fractals** — shapes that repeat themselves at smaller and smaller scales — and **visualising solids** on flat paper.

## Part A — Fractals

**A fractal is a self-similar shape: it contains smaller copies of itself, which contain smaller copies still, forever.**

**In nature:** a **fern** frond is made of smaller fronds, made of smaller ones again. So are trees (trunk → limbs → branches → branchlets), clouds, coastlines, mountains and lightning.

### Sierpinski Carpet

**Recipe:** take a square, cut it into 9 equal squares, **remove the middle one**. Repeat on each of the 8 survivors. Forever.

**Counting the pieces.** Let `Rₙ` be the number of remaining squares and `Hₙ` the number of holes at step n.

**Every surviving square becomes 8 surviving squares**, so `Rₙ₊₁ = 8Rₙ`:

```
R₀ = 1
R₁ = 8
R₂ = 8² = 64
Rₙ = 8ⁿ
```

**Every surviving square makes one new hole, and old holes stay**, so `Hₙ₊₁ = Hₙ + Rₙ`:

```
H₀ = 0
H₁ = 1
H₂ = 1 + 8 = 9
H₃ = 1 + 8 + 64 = 73
Hₙ = 1 + 8 + 8² + … + 8ⁿ⁻¹
```

**Area remaining.** Each step keeps **8 of 9** parts, so if the starting square has area 1:

**`Area at step n = (8/9)ⁿ`**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> `(8/9)ⁿ` shrinks towards **0** as n grows — the true Sierpinski Carpet has **zero area** but is still full of points. That's what makes fractals strange and wonderful.

### Sierpinski Gasket (Triangle)

**Recipe:** join the midpoints of an equilateral triangle to make 4 small ones, **remove the middle** one. Repeat on the 3 survivors.

**Why joining midpoints gives 4 identical equilateral triangles:** each midpoint segment is parallel to a side and half its length (each corner triangle is isosceles with the 60° angle between two equal half-sides, so it's equilateral). The middle triangle is bounded by three such segments, so it too has all sides equal to half the original.

```
Triangles remaining:  Tₙ = 3ⁿ
Holes:                Hₙ₊₁ = Hₙ + Tₙ,  so Hₙ = 1 + 3 + 9 + … + 3ⁿ⁻¹
Area remaining:       (3/4)ⁿ
```

### Koch Snowflake

**Recipe:** start with an equilateral triangle. Cut each side into **thirds**, build an equilateral triangle on the middle third, and **remove that middle third**. Each straight side becomes a **bump** of 4 shorter segments. Repeat.

```
Number of sides:  Sₙ = 3 × 4ⁿ
Length of a side: (1/3)ⁿ   (starting side 1)
Perimeter:        Pₙ = 3 × (4/3)ⁿ
```

> [!TIP]
> **`(4/3)ⁿ` grows without limit — the Koch Snowflake has INFINITE perimeter, yet it fits inside a small circle so its area is finite.** Every step multiplies the perimeter by 4/3 while adding only a sliver of area.

### Fractals in art

- **Kandariya Mahadev Temple, Khajuraho** (c. 1025 CE) — the temple is made of smaller copies of the whole temple, which carry smaller copies again. Similar patterns appear at Madurai, Hampi, Rameswaram and Varanasi.
- **Nigerian Fulani wedding blankets** — diamonds within diamonds within diamonds.
- **M. C. Escher's *Smaller and Smaller*** — the same lizard repeating at ever-shrinking scale.

## Part B — Visualising solids

### Faces, edges and vertices

**Faces** are the flat surfaces, **edges** the line segments where faces meet, **vertices** the points where edges meet.

A cube or cuboid: **6 faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices**.

**Prism:** two **congruent parallel polygons** joined by parallelograms. Named after the polygon — triangular prism, pentagonal prism, …

**Pyramid:** one polygon **base** plus a point (apex) joined to every base vertex. A triangular pyramid is a **tetrahedron**.

| Solid | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|---|---|---|---|
| n-sided **prism** | `n + 2` | `3n` | `2n` |
| 10-sided prism | 12 | 30 | 20 |
| n-sided **pyramid** | `n + 1` | `2n` | `n + 1` |
| 10-sided pyramid | 11 | 20 | 11 |

**Why the prism counts:** n side faces plus 2 ends; n edges top, n bottom, n verticals; n vertices top and n bottom.
**Why the pyramid counts:** n side faces plus 1 base; n base edges plus n slanting; n base vertices plus 1 apex.

> [!TIP]
> Check with **Euler's rule**: `F + V − E = 2` for every one of these. Prism: `(n+2) + 2n − 3n = 2` ✓ Pyramid: `(n+1) + (n+1) − 2n = 2` ✓

### Nets

**A net is a flat shape that folds up into a solid — the solid "unfolded".**

| Solid | Number of distinct nets |
|---|---|
| Cube | **11** |
| Regular tetrahedron | **2** |
| Octahedron | **11** |
| Dodecahedron | **43,380** |
| **Sphere** | **none — impossible** |

(Two nets count as the same if one is a rotation or flip of the other.)

**Net of a cylinder:** two circles plus a **rectangle**. The rectangle's height is the cylinder's height, and its **width is the circumference of the circle**, `2πr` — because that edge has to wrap exactly once round the rim.

**Net of a cone:** a circle plus a **sector** (a "pizza slice") of a bigger circle. **Why a sector:** every point on the base rim is the same slant distance `l` from the apex, so unrolled they all sit on a circle of radius `l` centred at the apex.

> [!NOTE]
> **A sphere has no net.** No flat paper can wrap a ball without wrinkles, gaps or overlaps — which is exactly why every world map distorts either area or shape.

### Shortest path on a cuboid

On a flat plane the shortest path is a straight line. On a cuboid's **surface**, the trick is:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Unfold the cuboid into a net. A path on the surface becomes a path of the same length on the net, and vice versa.**
>
> So the shortest surface path is whichever path becomes a **straight line** on some unfolding.

**Why this works:** unfolding doesn't stretch anything — every face keeps its exact shape and size, so every path keeps its exact length.

**But the unfolding matters!** Different unfoldings give different straight-line distances, and some straight lines on a net fall **outside** the net entirely (so they aren't real paths). **You must check several unfoldings and take the shortest.**

**Worked:** an ant and a laddu on opposite sides of a box. One unfolding gives a right triangle with legs 24 cm and 32 cm:

`d² = 24² + 32² = 576 + 1024 = 1600` → **d = 40 cm**

Another unfolding of the same box gives 42 cm — so 40 cm is the better route, and you'd check the rest too.

### Projections

**The projection of a point P onto a plane is the foot O of the perpendicular from P.** The projections of all a solid's points form its projection.

**Length changes.** If a segment of length `l` makes an angle with the plane, its projection has length `p` where

```
p ≤ l    always
p = l    exactly when the segment is PARALLEL to the plane
p = 0    when it is perpendicular to the plane
```

**Why:** drop perpendiculars from both ends. The original segment becomes the **hypotenuse** of a right triangle whose horizontal leg is the projection — and the hypotenuse is always the longest side.

**Key facts:**

- **Parallel lines project to parallel lines.** So a parallelogram always projects to a **parallelogram** (never to a general quadrilateral), and a polygon's projection has the same number of sides — unless it collapses to a line.
- **A projection does not determine the solid.** Many different objects give the same shadow — a cube, a cuboid and a square-based prism can all project to the same square.

**That's why engineers use three views:**

| View | Plane | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Front view** | vertical plane | looking from the front |
| **Top view** | horizontal plane | looking from above |
| **Side view** | side plane | looking from the side |

**Shadows and projections.** A torch shining perpendicular to a wall casts a shadow like the projection, but **scaled up** — and the closer the torch, the bigger the shadow. Move the light infinitely far away and the shadow becomes exactly the projection.

> [!TIP]
> **The Sun is that "infinitely distant torch".** Its rays arrive essentially parallel, so a sunlit shadow on a perpendicular surface *is* the projection.

### Isometric projection and isometric grids

**Balance a cube on one corner and project it straight down. All twelve edges project to segments of EQUAL length** — that's what *isometric* means ("equal measure" in Greek).

**The outline is a regular hexagon**, and a glass cube would show all its edges.

**Tile the plane with those hexagons and you get an isometric grid**, with three edge directions:

```
   |     height
   ╲     depth
   ╱     length
```

**Why the grid works so well:** parallel edges project to parallel lines, so the solid's three families of parallel edges become the grid's three directions — and because the projection is isometric, **one unit along any axis is the same length on paper**. So you can count units directly.

**Drawing tip:** build edge by edge, counting units along each axis, and either erase lines that later get hidden or draw faintly first and darken the visible ones.

### Impossible figures

An "impossible triangle" can be drawn on an isometric grid but **cannot be built** from real cubes.

**Why the illusion works:** the isometric projection **throws away depth information**. Two edges that meet on the paper might be far apart in space — the drawing joins them anyway, and your brain accepts the join.

> [!TIP]
> **Every small piece of an impossible figure IS buildable.** Only the whole thing contradicts itself — which is exactly why the eye is fooled.
