<!-- ===== Consecutive numbers ===== -->

The sum of four consecutive numbers is 34. Find them.

> [!ANSWER]
> Sum = **count × average**, so the average is `34 ÷ 4 = 8.5`.
>
> Four consecutive numbers centred on 8.5 are **7, 8, 9, 10**. ✓ `7+8+9+10 = 34`

---

p is the greatest of five consecutive numbers. Write the other four in terms of p.

> [!ANSWER]
> **p − 1, p − 2, p − 3, p − 4.**
>
> (Their sum is `5p − 10 = 5(p − 2)` — always a multiple of 5, since p − 2 is the middle one.)

---

Show that **every odd number** can be written as a sum of two consecutive numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> Write the odd number as `2k + 1`. Then
>
> `2k + 1 = k + (k + 1)` ✓
>
> For 15: `k = 7`, so `15 = 7 + 8`.

---

Which numbers can **never** be written as a sum of two or more consecutive numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The powers of 2** — 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, …
>
> **Why:** a run of n consecutive numbers has sum `n × (average)`. If n is odd, n itself is an odd factor of the sum. If n is even, the average ends in ·5, so the sum is (n/2) × an odd number. **Either way there's an odd factor bigger than 1** — and a power of 2 has none.

---

The middle number of five consecutive **even** numbers is 5p. Write the other four.

> [!ANSWER]
> Consecutive even numbers go up in **2s**, so they are
>
> **5p − 4, 5p − 2, 5p, 5p + 2, 5p + 4.**

<!-- ===== Parity ===== -->

Take 3, 4, 5, 6 and put `+` or `−` between them in all 8 ways. What do all the answers have in common?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every one is even.** (18, 6, 8, −4, 10, −2, 0, −12)
>
> And it isn't a fluke about these numbers — 5, 6, 7, 8 does the same.

---

Prove that all 8 values of `a ± b ± c ± d` have the same parity.

> [!ANSWER]
> Flip **one** sign, say `+b` to `−b`. The value changes by
>
> `(a + b − c − d) − (a − b − c − d) = 2b`
>
> — an **even** change. Two numbers differing by an even amount have the **same parity**.
>
> Any of the 8 expressions can be turned into any other by flipping signs one at a time, so **all 8 share one parity**. ✓

---

Give the two-line version of the same proof, using only parity rules.

> [!ANSWER]
> `odd ± odd = even`, `even ± even = even`, `odd ± even = odd`.
>
> Notice **the sign never appears in these rules** — so `a + b` and `a − b` always have the same parity. Extend one term at a time: `a ± b ± c` matches `a ± b ± c`, and so on for d.

---

Without computing, say whether each is even: `809 + 214`, `708 − 477`, `119 × 303`, `4 × 347 × 3`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `809 + 214` — odd + even = **odd**
> - `708 − 477` — even − odd = **odd**
> - `119 × 303` — odd × odd = **odd**
> - `4 × 347 × 3` — one factor is even ⇒ **even**

---

Which of these are even for **every** integer value of the letters: `2a + 2b`, `3g + 5h`, `4m + 2n`, `13k − 5k`, `x² + 2`?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `2a + 2b = 2(a + b)` — **always even**
> - `4m + 2n = 2(2m + n)` — **always even**
> - `13k − 5k = 8k` — **always even**
> - `3g + 5h` — **not always**: `g=1, h=1` → 8 (even), `g=1, h=0` → 3 (odd)
> - `x² + 2` — **not always**: `x=6` → 38 (even), `x=3` → 11 (odd)
>
> **The test:** can you pull out a factor of 2? If yes it's always even; if not, hunt a counterexample.

---

Write three algebraic expressions that are even for every integer value, and explain why.

> [!ANSWER]
> `6p − 4q`, `10a + 2b + 8`, `2(n² + n)` — for example.
>
> **Why:** each is literally `2 × (a whole number)`. Having 2 as a factor **is** what "even" means.

---

<!-- ===== Multiples of 4 ===== -->

When does adding two even numbers give a multiple of 4?

> [!ANSWER]
> Every even number is either `4p` or `4p + 2`. Three cases:
>
> - `4p + 4q = 4(p+q)` — **multiple of 4** ✓
> - `(4p+2) + (4q+2) = 4(p+q+1)` — **multiple of 4** ✓ (the two 2s combine into a 4)
> - `4p + (4q+2) = 4(p+q) + 2` — **not** a multiple of 4 ✗
>
> **So: both of the same kind ⇒ yes; one of each ⇒ no.**

---

Why does the multiple-of-4 story feel like the odd/even story?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it *is* the same story one level up. **Remainder 2 plays the role of "odd":**
>
> two leftovers of 2 combine into a full 4 (like odd + odd = even), while a single leftover survives (like odd + even = odd).

---

<!-- ===== Divisibility laws ===== -->

If 8 divides two numbers separately, must it divide their sum? Their difference?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both, always.**
>
> Write them `8a` and `8b`. Then `8a ± 8b = 8(a ± b)` — **the 8 factors straight out**.
>
> Generally: if a divides M and a divides N, then a divides `M + N` and `M − N`.

---

A number is divisible by 8. Must the two numbers that add up to it also each be divisible by 8?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes true only.**
>
> `72 = 48 + 24` — both multiples of 8 ✓
> `72 = 50 + 22` — neither is ✗
>
> The law goes one way (parts ⇒ whole), not backwards.

---

If a number is divisible by 7, are all its multiples divisible by 7?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always.**
>
> `7j × m = 7(jm)` — multiplying keeps the 7 as a factor.

---

If a number is divisible by 12, is it divisible by every **factor** of 12?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always.**
>
> `12m = 2 × 6 × m = 3 × 4 × m` — every factor of 12 is already sitting inside the product, so 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12 all divide it.

---

If a number is divisible by 7, is it divisible by every **multiple** of 7?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes only.**
>
> 42 = 7 × 6 is divisible by 14 (since 2 is a factor of 6) but **not** by 28 (4 isn't a factor of 6).
>
> `7k` is divisible by `7m` only when **m is a factor of k**.

---

A number is divisible by 9 and by 4. What else must divide it? What if it were divisible by 6 and 4?

> [!ANSWER]
> - 9 and 4 share no factor, so `LCM(9,4) = 36` — the number **must** be a multiple of **36**.
> - 6 and 4 **share** a factor of 2, so `LCM(6,4) = 12` only. It need **not** be a multiple of 24 — **12 itself** is the counterexample.
>
> **The rule is LCM, not product.**

---

Can the sum of an odd number and an even number ever be a multiple of 6?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.**
>
> odd + even = **odd**, and every multiple of 6 is even.
>
> **Algebraically:** if `2n + (2m + 1) = 6j`, then `2(n + m) = 6j − 1` — an even number equal to an odd number. Impossible.

---

<!-- ===== Remainders ===== -->

Write an expression for all numbers leaving remainder 3 when divided by 5. Is there more than one correct answer?

> [!ANSWER]
> **`5k + 3`** — giving 3, 8, 13, 18, 23, …
>
> **`5k − 2`** (for k ≥ 1) generates exactly the same list. "3 more than a multiple of 5" and "2 short of a multiple of 5" describe the same numbers.

---

661 leaves remainder 3 on division by 7, and 4779 leaves remainder 5. Find the remainders of `4779 + 661` and `4779 − 661` **without dividing**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Write `4779 = 7p + 5` and `661 = 7q + 3`.
>
> **Sum:** `7p + 7q + 8 = 7(p + q + 1) + 1` → **remainder 1**
> **Difference:** `7p + 5 − 7q − 3 = 7(p − q) + 2` → **remainder 2**
>
> Only the **remainders** matter; the multiples of 7 disappear.

---

Find all numbers leaving remainder 2 when divided by 3 **and** remainder 2 when divided by 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> `x − 2` must be a multiple of both 3 and 4, hence a multiple of `LCM(3,4) = 12`.
>
> So `x =` **12n + 2**: 14, 26, 38, 50, …

---

Find the smallest number leaving remainder 2 on ÷3, 3 on ÷4 and 4 on ÷5. Why is it the smallest?

> [!ANSWER]
> Every remainder is **one less than its divisor**, so `x + 1` is a multiple of 3, 4 and 5.
>
> The smallest such is `LCM(3,4,5) = 60`, so `x =` **59**.
>
> **Why smallest:** any smaller answer would need a smaller common multiple of 3, 4, 5 — and there isn't one.

---

Pebble riddle: grouping by 3 leaves 1, pairing leaves 1, grouping by 5 leaves 1, grouping by 7 leaves none, and there are fewer than 100. How many?

> [!ANSWER]
> Remainder 1 for 2, 3 and 5 means the count is `LCM(2,3,5) × k + 1 = 30k + 1`: **31, 61, 91**.
>
> Of these, only **91 = 7 × 13** is a multiple of 7.
>
> → **91 pebbles.**

---

Tathagat says: "add any three numbers that each leave remainder 2 when divided by 6, and the sum is a multiple of 6." Is he right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> `(6a+2) + (6b+2) + (6c+2) = 6a + 6b + 6c + 6 = 6(a + b + c + 1)` ✓
>
> **Why it works:** the three leftover 2s add to 6 — exactly one more group.

---

Snehal says: "take a number leaving remainder 8 on ÷12, and another that is 4 short of a multiple of 12; their sum is always a multiple of 8." Examine the claim.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
>
> The numbers are `12a + 8` and `12b − 4`. Their sum is `12a + 12b + 4 = 4(3a + 3b + 1)` — always a multiple of **4**, but the bracket `3a+3b+1` need not be even.
>
> **Counterexample:** `20 + 8 = 28`, which is not a multiple of 8.

<!-- ===== Divisibility shortcuts ===== -->

Why does looking only at the **units digit** settle divisibility by 2, 5 and 10?

> [!ANSWER]
> Every place value above the units — 10, 100, 1000, … — is a **multiple of 10**, and therefore a multiple of 2 and of 5 as well.
>
> Those places contribute **nothing** to the remainder, so the units digit is the whole story.

---

Why do you check only the **last two digits** for divisibility by 4, and the **last three** for 8?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `100 = 4 × 25`, so every place from **hundreds up** is already a multiple of 4 — only tens and units are left.
> - `1000 = 8 × 125`, so every place from **thousands up** is already a multiple of 8 — only the last three digits are left.

---

Prove the "add the digits" test for 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each place value is **one more than a multiple of 9**: `1 = 0+1`, `10 = 9+1`, `100 = 99+1`, `1000 = 999+1`.
>
> So for 7309:
> `7(999+1) + 3(99+1) + 0(9+1) + 9(0+1) = (7×999 + 3×99 + 0×9) + (7+3+0+9)`
>
> The first bracket is **always a multiple of 9**. So the number and its **digit sum leave the same remainder** on division by 9. ✓

---

Which of 123, 405, 8888, 93547, 358095 are divisible by 9?

> [!ANSWER]
> Digit sums: `6`, `9`, `32`, `28`, `30`.
>
> Only `405` has a digit sum that's a multiple of 9 → **only 405**.

---

Find the remainder when 7309 is divided by 9, without dividing.

> [!ANSWER]
> `7 + 3 + 0 + 9 = 19 → 1 + 9 = 10 → 1 + 0 = 1`
>
> **Remainder 1.**

---

If `31z5` is a multiple of 9, find z. Why is there only one answer here, and when would there be two?

> [!ANSWER]
> `3 + 1 + z + 5 = 9 + z`. For a multiple of 9 we need `9 + z` to be 9 or 18 → `z = 0` or `z = 9`.
>
> **Both work** — `3105 = 9 × 345` and `3195 = 9 × 355`. **There are two answers**, because a single digit can raise the digit sum to the *next* multiple of 9 as well as hit the current one.

---

Find the smallest multiple of 9 with no odd digits.

> [!ANSWER]
> The digits must come from 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and sum to a multiple of 9.
>
> Two even digits summing to 18: `8 + ?` … the smallest arrangement is **288** (`2+8+8 = 18`).
>
> (No 1- or 2-digit even-only number works: single digits max at 8, and pairs of even digits can reach 18 only as 8+... which needs a third digit since 10 isn't a digit.)

---

How many multiples of 9 lie between 4300 and 4400?

> [!ANSWER]
> `4300 ÷ 9 = 477.7…` so the first is `478 × 9 =` **4302**. The last below 4400 is `488 × 9 =` **4392**.
>
> From 478 to 488 inclusive is `488 − 478 + 1 =` **11 multiples**.

---

Why does the same digit-sum trick work for **3**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because 9, 99, 999 … are **also multiples of 3**.
>
> So each place value is again "one more than a multiple of 3", and the same proof runs word for word.

---

Explain why divisibility by 11 uses **alternating** signs.

> [!ANSWER]
> Place values sit alternately just above and just below multiples of 11:
>
> `1 = 11×0 + 1` (one MORE) · `10 = 11×1 − 1` (one LESS) · `100 = 11×9 + 1` (one MORE) · `1000 = 11×91 − 1` (one LESS)
>
> So each digit contributes `+digit` or `−digit`, alternating from the units place.

---

Is 328105 divisible by 11? If not, what is the remainder?

> [!ANSWER]
> From the units digit: `5 − 0 + 1 − 8 + 2 − 3 = −3`.
>
> So it is **3 short of** a multiple of 11 → **not divisible**, and the remainder is `11 − 3 =` **8**.

---

Test 5529 and 90904 for divisibility by 11.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **5529:** `9 − 2 + 5 − 5 = 7` → not divisible, remainder **7**.
> - **90904:** `4 − 0 + 9 − 0 + 9 = 22` → 22 is a multiple of 11 → **divisible** ✓

---

To test divisibility by 6 you check 2 and 3. Why can't you test 24 by checking 4 and 6?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **4 and 6 share a factor of 2**. Together they only guarantee `LCM(4,6) = 12`.
>
> **Counterexample: 12** — divisible by 4 and by 6, but not by 24.
>
> Use **8 and 3** instead: `24 = 2³ × 3`, and 8 and 3 have **nothing in common**, so `8 × 3 = 24` really is guaranteed.

---

State the general rule for splitting a divisor into two easier tests.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Split it into factors that are coprime** (share no common factor).
>
> Then their LCM equals their product, so passing both tests really does prove divisibility by the whole thing.
>
> 6 = 2 × 3 ✓ · 15 = 3 × 5 ✓ · 24 = 8 × 3 ✓ · but 24 ≠ 4 × 6 for this purpose ✗

<!-- ===== Digital roots ===== -->

What is a digital root, and what does it actually tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> Add the digits repeatedly until a single digit is left. `489710 → 29 → 11 →` **2**.
>
> **It is the remainder on division by 9** — except that multiples of 9 show a digital root of **9** instead of 0.
>
> That follows straight from the digit-sum proof: each digit-summing step preserves the remainder mod 9.

---

Write the digital roots of any 12 consecutive numbers. What do you see?

> [!ANSWER]
> They cycle **1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 1, 2, 3, …**
>
> **Why:** adding 1 to a number adds 1 to its remainder mod 9, wrapping round after 9.

---

An 8-digit number has digital root 5. What is the digital root of 10 more than it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **6.**
>
> `10` leaves remainder **1** on division by 9, so adding 10 pushes the digital root up by 1.

---

Start with any number and keep adding 11. What happens to the digital roots?

> [!ANSWER]
> They go up in **2s** (wrapping round at 9), because `11` leaves remainder **2** on division by 9.
>
> Starting from digital root 3: 3, 5, 7, 9, 2, 4, 6, 8, 1, 3, …

---

What is the digital root of `9a + 36b + 13`?

> [!ANSWER]
> **4.**
>
> `9a` and `36b` are both multiples of 9, so they contribute **nothing** to the remainder. Only 13 counts: `1 + 3 =` 4.

---

Sreelatha says any multiple of 9 stays a multiple of 9 when its digits are reversed. Is she right? Do other shuffles work?

> [!ANSWER]
> **She's right — and so is every other shuffle.**
>
> **Why:** the test depends only on the **digit sum**, and rearranging digits never changes their sum.
>
> `918 = 9 × 102` → `819 = 9 × 91` ✓ → `891 = 9 × 99` ✓

<!-- ===== Cryptarithms ===== -->

Solve `PQ × 8 = RS` (letters are distinct digits, no leading zero).

> [!ANSWER]
> **12 × 8 = 96**, so P=1, Q=2, R=9, S=6.
>
> **Why:** for the product to stay 2-digit, `PQ ≤ 12`. `10 × 8 = 80` repeats a digit (Q = S = 0); `11` repeats P and Q. Only **12** survives.

---

Solve `GH × H = 9K`.

> [!ANSWER]
> **24 × 4 = 96** — G=2, H=4, K=6.
>
> **Why the near-misses fail:** `16 × 6 = 96` matches the multiplier but makes K = H = 6 (two letters, one digit ✗). `31 × 3 = 93` uses a multiplier that isn't the units digit ✗.

---

In `BYE × 6 = RAY`, show that B = 1 and Y must be even and at most 6.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **B = 1:** if B were 2, `BYE ≥ 200` and the product would exceed 1200 — 4 digits, not 3.
> - **Y ≤ 6:** if Y = 7, `170 × 6 = 1020` — already 4 digits.
> - **Y is even:** the product ends in Y, and the units digit of the product is the units digit of `6 × E`, which is always **even**.

---

What are the three rules every cryptarithm follows, and where do you attack first?

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Each letter is one digit. 2. Different letters mean different digits. 3. No number starts with 0.
>
> **Attack the first digit** (the size of the answer limits it) **and the last digit** (multiplication tables limit it). The middle falls out afterwards.

<!-- ===== Consecutive products ===== -->

Is the product of two consecutive integers always even? Of three consecutive integers always a multiple of 6?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both yes.**
>
> - Of any **two** in a row, one must be **even** → the product has a factor 2.
> - Of any **three** in a row, one is even and one is a multiple of **3** → the product has factors 2 and 3 → multiple of **6**.
>
> Generally: the product of n consecutive integers is a multiple of **n!**, because the run must contain a multiple of n, of n−1, and so on.

---

Show that the sum of three consecutive even numbers is always a multiple of 6.

> [!ANSWER]
> `2n + (2n + 2) + (2n + 4) = 6n + 6 = 6(n + 1)` ✓
>
> **Why it works:** the middle number is `2n + 2`, and the sum is always **3 × the middle number** — three times an even number is a multiple of 6.

---

Deepak claims: "some multiples of 11 stay multiples of 11 when doubled, but others don't." Is he right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — all of them do.**
>
> `11k × 2 = 11(2k)` — the 11 survives multiplication by anything. This is the "multiples inherit" law.

---

Find three consecutive numbers where the first is a multiple of 2, the second of 3 and the third of 4. How often do such runs occur?

> [!ANSWER]
> **2, 3, 4** works. So does **14, 15, 16** and **26, 27, 28**.
>
> **The pattern:** we need `n ≡ 0 (mod 2)`, `n+1 ≡ 0 (mod 3)` and `n+2 ≡ 0 (mod 4)`. The last forces n to be even and 2 short of a multiple of 4, so `n = 4m + 2`; combined with the multiple-of-3 condition the runs repeat **every 12 numbers**.

---

**Always, sometimes or never:** the product of a multiple of 6 and a multiple of 3 is a multiple of 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always.**
>
> `6a × 3b = 18ab = 9(2ab)` — the 6 supplies one 3 and the other factor supplies another, giving `3 × 3 = 9`. ✓

---

**Always, sometimes or never:** `8(7b − 3) − 4(11b + 1)` is a multiple of 12.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.**
>
> Expand: `56b − 24 − 44b − 4 = 12b − 28 = 12(b − 3) + 8`.
>
> The `12(b − 3)` part is a multiple of 12, but **8 is always left over** — so the expression leaves remainder 8 whatever b is.
>
> **The lesson:** expand fully before judging. Seeing `12b` appear is not enough; the constant has to cooperate too.

---

Which Venn diagram is right: multiples of 4, 8 and 32?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Three nested circles**: multiples of 32 inside multiples of 8, inside multiples of 4.
>
> **Why:** every multiple of 32 is a multiple of 8 (`32 = 8 × 4`), and every multiple of 8 is a multiple of 4. The containment runs one way only — 12 is a multiple of 4 but not of 8.
