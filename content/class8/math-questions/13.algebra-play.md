<!-- ===== Think of a number ===== -->

Explain why this always gives 2: think of a number, double it, add four, divide by two, subtract your number.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> x → 2x → 2x + 4 → x + 2 → (x + 2) − x = 2
> ```
>
> **The x cancels at the last step**, so the answer is 2 whatever you started with.

---

State the general principle behind every "think of a number" trick.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The trick works exactly when the unknown cancels out.**
>
> The steps are built so the starting number disappears, leaving a constant that the performer already knows.

---

How would you change that trick so the answer is 3? Or 5?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Change what you add at step 3.**
>
> Add **six** → `2x + 6 → x + 3 → 3`. Add **ten** → answer **5**.
>
> **The answer is always half of what you add**, because the next step halves everything.

---

Invent a longer trick that always ends at 7, and prove it works.

> [!ANSWER]
> Think of x · multiply by 3 (`3x`) · add 21 (`3x + 21`) · divide by 3 (`x + 7`) · subtract your number → **7**.
>
> **Why:** dividing by 3 turns the `+21` into `+7`, and the subtraction removes the x.

<!-- ===== Date trick ===== -->

In the date trick, work out what the sequence of steps produces algebraically.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> month M, day D
> ×5:        5M
> +6:        5M + 6
> ×4:       20M + 24
> +9:       20M + 33
> ×5:      100M + 165
> +D:      100M + 165 + D
> ```

---

Why does subtracting 165 reveal the date?

> [!ANSWER]
> It leaves **`100M + D`**.
>
> Since a day is at most **31** — two digits — **D sits in the last two digits and M is everything before them**. The `× 100` parked M safely out of the way.

---

Find the dates for final answers 1269, 394 and 296.

> [!ANSWER]
> Subtract 165 each time:
>
> - `1269 − 165 = 1104` → M = 11, D = 4 → **4 November**
> - `394 − 165 = 229` → M = 2, D = 29 → **29 February**
> - `296 − 165 = 131` → M = 1, D = 31 → **31 January**

---

Why do the steps use `×5, ×4, ×5` rather than just `×100`?

> [!ANSWER]
> `5 × 4 × 5 = 100` — **the chain does exactly the same job**, but disguised, so the trick isn't obvious.
>
> The `+6` and `+9` are camouflage too; they just accumulate into the constant **165**.

---

Design your own version of the date trick and say what number would be subtracted.

> [!ANSWER]
> Any chain multiplying M by 100 works. For example: `×2, +3, ×10, +1, ×5`
>
> ```
> 2M → 2M + 3 → 20M + 30 → 20M + 31 → 100M + 155
> ```
>
> Add D, and the decoding number is **155**.
>
> **The rule:** whatever constant your chain generates is what you subtract.

<!-- ===== Number pyramids ===== -->

State the number-pyramid rule and fill: bottom row 6, 2, 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Each number is the sum of the two directly below it.**
>
> ```
>       13
>     8     5
>  6     2     3
> ```

---

Fill this pyramid: top 10, left-middle 4, bottom-left 1.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>       10
>     4     6      (10 − 4 = 6)
>  1     3     3   (4 − 1 = 3, then 6 − 3 = 3)
> ```
>
> **Each blank is a subtraction** — if `4 + ? = 10` then `? = 6`.

---

Fill this pyramid: top 60, bottom row 12, c, 8 with the middle row unknown.

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the middle row be a, b. Then `a = 12 + c`, `b = c + 8`, and `a + b = 60`:
>
> ```
> (12 + c) + (c + 8) = 60
> 20 + 2c = 60
> c = 20
> ```
>
> So `a = 32`, `b = 28`:
>
> ```
>       60
>    32     28
> 12     20     8
> ```
>
> **When arrows don't chain from a corner, name the unknown and write an equation.**

---

Write the top of a 3-row and a 4-row pyramid in terms of the bottom row.

> [!ANSWER]
> - 3 rows (bottom a, b, c): **`a + 2b + c`**
> - 4 rows (bottom a, b, c, d): **`a + 3b + 3c + d`**

---

Why are the coefficients 1, 2, 1 and 1, 3, 3, 1?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They're Pascal's triangle** — the binomial numbers.
>
> **Why:** each bottom cell contributes once for every **path upward** to the apex. Corner cells have only one path; middle cells have several, so their values get counted more times.

---

Find the top without building the pyramid: bottom rows (a) 4, 13, 8; (b) 7, 11, 3; (c) 10, 14, 25.

> [!ANSWER]
> Use `a + 2b + c`:
>
> - `4 + 26 + 8 =` **38**
> - `7 + 22 + 3 =` **32**
> - `10 + 28 + 25 =` **63**

---

Find the top: bottom rows (a) 8, 19, 21, 13; (b) 7, 18, 19, 6; (c) 9, 7, 5, 11.

> [!ANSWER]
> Use `a + 3b + 3c + d`:
>
> - `8 + 57 + 63 + 13 =` **141**
> - `7 + 54 + 57 + 6 =` **124**
> - `9 + 21 + 15 + 11 =` **56**

---

Put the first three Virahāṅka–Fibonacci numbers 1, 2, 3 in the bottom row of a 3-row pyramid. What do you get?

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>       8
>    3     5
> 1     2     3
> ```
>
> **Every number in the pyramid is a Virahāṅka–Fibonacci number**, and the top (8) is the next one along.

---

Why does that happen?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **the pyramid rule IS the Fibonacci rule** — "add the two neighbours" builds both.
>
> So each row is a **shifted copy** of the sequence, and every entry is automatically a term of it.

---

If a 29-row pyramid has the first 29 Virahāṅka–Fibonacci numbers on the bottom, which term is at the top?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **57th** — i.e. the `(2n − 1)`th term for an n-row pyramid.
>
> **Check the pattern:** 3 rows → term 5 (`8`); 4 rows → term 7 (`21`); n rows → term `2n − 1`.

<!-- ===== Grids ===== -->

A 2 × 2 block from a calendar has top-left a. Write the other three entries and the sum.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
>   a     a+1
>  a+7   a+8
> ```
> (the `+7` because a week is 7 days).
>
> **Sum = `4a + 16`.**

---

A friend picks a 2 × 2 calendar block and tells you the sum is 36. Find the four numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> `4a + 16 = 36` → `4a = 20` → `a = 5`.
>
> The block is **5, 6, 12, 13** ✓ (they add to 36)

---

Same trick, sum 40. Find the block.

> [!ANSWER]
> `4a + 16 = 40` → `a = 6` → **6, 7, 13, 14**.

---

Invent a 3 × 3 version of the calendar trick.

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the **centre** be c. The nine entries are `c−8, c−7, c−6, c−1, c, c+1, c+6, c+7, c+8`.
>
> All the offsets **cancel in pairs**, so the **sum is `9c`**.
>
> **The trick:** divide the sum by 9 and you have the middle date instantly.

---

In a shape grid, the first row reads `■ + ■ + ■ = 27` and the second `▲ + ▲ + ■ = 19`. Find both shapes.

> [!ANSWER]
> First row has only **one kind of shape**: `3■ = 27` → **■ = 9**.
>
> Substitute: `2▲ + 9 = 19` → `2▲ = 10` → **▲ = 5**.

---

What's the strategy for any shape grid?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Attack the row or column with the fewest different shapes first** — ideally one that has only one shape, which solves immediately.
>
> Each shape you find turns the next row into a simpler equation.

<!-- ===== Largest product ===== -->

Fill 2, 3, 5 into `□□ × □` to make the largest product. List all options and find the winner.

> [!ANSWER]
> Six options: `23×5, 25×3, 32×5, 35×2, 52×3, 53×2`.
>
> **Step 1:** within each multiplier, the bigger tens digit wins — leaving `53×2`, `52×3`, `32×5`.
>
> **Step 2:** `52×3 = 156` beats `53×2 = 106`. Then
> ```
> 32 × 5 = 150 + 10 = 160
> 52 × 3 = 150 +  6 = 156
> ```
>
> **Winner: `32 × 5 = 160`.**

---

Why did `32 × 5` beat `52 × 3` when the digits are the same?

> [!ANSWER]
> Expand both: the **tens contribution is `10 × 3 × 5 = 150` in each case** — identical.
>
> The difference is only in the units term: `2 × 5 = 10` versus `2 × 3 = 6`. The **larger multiplier attached to the units digit wins**.

---

State the general rule for `□□ × □` with digits p `<` q `<` r, and prove it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Put the LARGEST digit r as the single multiplier, and the other two in decreasing order as the two-digit number: `qp × r`.**
>
> **Proof:** the two survivors are `qp × r` and `rp × q`.
> ```
> qp × r = 10qr + pr
> rp × q = 10rq + pq
> ```
> The first terms are **identical** (`10qr = 10rq`). Since `r > q`, we have `pr > pq`, so `qp × r` wins.

---

Fill 1, 3, 7 for the largest product. And 3, 5, 9.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **1, 3, 7** → largest digit 7 as multiplier, others descending → **`31 × 7 = 217`** (compare `71 × 3 = 213`)
> - **3, 5, 9** → **`53 × 9 = 477`** (compare `93 × 5 = 465`)

<!-- ===== Divisibility tricks ===== -->

Take a 2-digit number, reverse it, subtract. Why is the answer always divisible by 9?

> [!ANSWER]
> The number is `10a + b` and its reverse `10b + a`:
>
> ```
> (10b + a) − (10a + b) = 9b − 9a = 9(b − a)
> ```
>
> **Always a multiple of 9** ✓

---

In that trick, what is the quotient when you divide by 9?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The difference of the two digits, `b − a`.**
>
> `74 − 47 = 27`, and `27 ÷ 9 = 3 = 7 − 4` ✓

---

What if `a > b` in that trick?

> [!ANSWER]
> The difference `9(b − a)` comes out **negative**, but it's still a multiple of 9.
>
> Take the numbers the other way round (bigger minus smaller) and the quotient is the **absolute** difference of the digits.

---

Now reverse and **add** instead. `31 + 13 = 44`, `28 + 82 = 110`, `12 + 21 = 33`. Prove the pattern.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (10a + b) + (10b + a) = 11a + 11b = 11(a + b)
> ```
>
> **Always divisible by 11**, and the **quotient is the digit sum `a + b`**.
>
> Check: `110 ÷ 11 = 10 = 2 + 8` ✓

---

Take a 3-digit number `abc`, cycle the digits to get `bca` and `cab`, and add all three. Show the sum is divisible by 37 and by 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> ```
> (100a + 10b + c) + (100b + 10c + a) + (100c + 10a + b)
> = 111a + 111b + 111c
> = 111(a + b + c)
> ```
>
> **Each digit lands once in each place value**, so each contributes `100 + 10 + 1 = 111`.
>
> Since **`111 = 3 × 37`**, the sum is divisible by both **37 and 3** ✓

---

Take `abc` and repeat it to make `abcabc`. Divide by 7, then 11, then 13. What happens, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You get back `abc` exactly.**
>
> **Why:** `abcabc = abc × 1001`, and
>
> **`1001 = 7 × 11 × 13`**
>
> So dividing by all three undoes the multiplication.

<!-- ===== Word problems ===== -->

A farm has 55 heads and 150 legs of horses and hens. How many of each?

> [!ANSWER]
> **With algebra:** h horses, `55 − h` hens.
> `4h + 2(55 − h) = 150` → `2h + 110 = 150` → `h = 20`.
> **20 horses, 35 hens.**
>
> **Without algebra:** if all 55 were hens there'd be 110 legs. The extra `150 − 110 = 40` legs come from horses having **2 extra legs each**, so `40 ÷ 2 =` **20 horses**.

---

A mother is 5 times her daughter's age. In 6 years she'll be 3 times as old. How old is the daughter?

> [!ANSWER]
> Daughter d, mother `5d`.
>
> `5d + 6 = 3(d + 6)` → `5d + 6 = 3d + 18` → `2d = 12` → **d = 6**.
>
> **Daughter 6, mother 30.** Check: in 6 years they'll be 12 and 36 ✓

---

Naina has twice as many cows as Gauri. If Naina gave Gauri 3 cows they'd be equal. How many does each have?

> [!ANSWER]
> Gauri g, Naina `2g`.
>
> `2g − 3 = g + 3` → **g = 6**.
>
> **Gauri 6, Naina 12.** Check: 9 and 9 ✓

---

A dosa cart costs ₹5,000/day in rent and ₹10 per dosa in ingredients. At 100 dosas a day, what price gives a ₹2,000 profit?

> [!ANSWER]
> `100p − (5000 + 100 × 10) = 2000`
> `100p − 6000 = 2000` → `p =` **₹80**.

---

Same cart. If customers will pay only ₹50, how many dosas must you sell for a ₹2,000 profit?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each dosa earns `50 − 10 =` **₹40 of contribution**.
>
> `40n − 5000 = 2000` → `40n = 7000` → **n = 175 dosas**.

---

Karim's coins double each round; he pays the genie 8 coins each time; after the third doubling he holds exactly 8. How many did he start with?

> [!ANSWER]
> Let him start with x:
>
> ```
> round 1: 2x − 8
> round 2: 4x − 24
> round 3 (after doubling): 2(4x − 24) = 8x − 48 = 8
> 8x = 56  →  x = 7
> ```
>
> **He started with 7 coins.**
>
> Check: `7 → 14, pay 8 → 6 → 12, pay 8 → 4 → 8, pay all → 0` ✓

---

For what fee should Karim have agreed, if he wanted to gain?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Any fee LESS than his starting coins.**
>
> Doubling then paying c leaves `2x − c`, which beats x only when **`x > c`**.
>
> Karim had 7 against a fee of 8 — one coin short, so he lost ground every single round.

---

How should the genie set the fee to end up with all of Karim's coins?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Above whatever Karim currently holds.**
>
> Then `2x − c < x` each round, so the pile shrinks every time until it's gone. Because the genie knows Karim's exact holding, it can always choose such a fee.

---

Three ponds each double the flowers dipped in them. A man leaves the same number at each of three shrines and finishes with none. How many did he start with?

> [!ANSWER]
> Start with x, leave f at each shrine:
>
> ```
> pond 1 → 2x,       leave f → 2x − f
> pond 2 → 4x − 2f,  leave f → 4x − 3f
> pond 3 → 8x − 6f,  and this must equal f
> ```
>
> `8x − 6f = f` → **`8x = 7f`**.
>
> Taking `f = 8`: **he started with 7 flowers and left 8 at each shrine**.
>
> Check: `7 → 14, leave 8 → 6 → 12, leave 8 → 4 → 8, leave 8 → 0` ✓

<!-- ===== The 1/3 sequence ===== -->

Evaluate `1/3`, `(1+3)/(5+7)` and `(1+3+5)/(7+9+11)`. What do you notice?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `1/3`
> - `4/12 = 1/3`
> - `9/27 = 1/3`
>
> **Every one is 1/3.**

---

Prove that the pattern continues forever.

> [!ANSWER]
> The numerator is the sum of the **first n odd numbers = `n²`**.
>
> The denominator is the sum of the **next n odd numbers** — that is, the first 2n odds minus the first n:
>
> `(2n)² − n² = 4n² − n² = 3n²`
>
> So the fraction is `n²/3n² =` **1/3**, always ✓
