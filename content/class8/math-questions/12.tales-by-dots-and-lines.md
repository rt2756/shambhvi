<!-- ===== Mean as balance ===== -->

Find the mean of 3 and 7, and of 8 and 9. What do you notice about two-number means?

> [!ANSWER]
> `(3+7)/2 =` **5** and `(8+9)/2 =` **8.5**.
>
> **For exactly two numbers the mean is always the midpoint** — halfway between them.

---

Is the mean always halfway between the smallest and largest values? Test with 10, 10, 11, 17.

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> Mean `= 48/4 =` **12**, but the midpoint of 10 and 17 is **13.5**. The three low values pull the mean down.

---

If the mean isn't the midpoint of the extremes, in what sense is it the "centre"?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It's the BALANCE POINT: the total distance to the values on the left equals the total distance to the values on the right.**
>
> For 10, 10, 11, 17 with mean 12:
> left `= 2 + 2 + 1 =` **5**, right `= 5` ✓
>
> Picture the dot plot as weights on a ruler — the mean is where it balances.

---

Can there be two different balance points?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No, exactly one.**
>
> Move to the right of the mean and **every** left distance grows while **every** right distance shrinks — so the two sides can no longer be equal. Moving left does the reverse.

---

<!-- ===== How the mean moves ===== -->

What happens to the mean when you add a value greater than it? Less than it? Equal to it?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Greater → the mean increases.** The right side gets heavier, so the balance point shifts right.
> - **Less → the mean decreases.**
> - **Equal → no change**, since it adds zero distance to either side.

---

What happens to the mean when you **remove** a value?

> [!ANSWER]
> The opposite of adding it:
>
> - remove a value **above** the mean → the mean **decreases**
> - remove a value **below** the mean → the mean **increases**
> - remove a value **equal** to the mean → **no change**

---

Can you add two values and leave the mean unchanged? What about three?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes to both** — as long as the surpluses and deficits balance.
>
> - **Two values:** for a mean of 12, add 9 and 15 (deficit 3, surplus 3) ✓
> - **Three values:** two below and one above works — add 10, 11 and 15 (deficits `2 + 1 = 3`, surplus `3`) ✓
>
> Equally, **two above and one below**: add 14, 15 and 7 (surpluses `2 + 3 = 5`, deficit `5`) ✓

---

Every value in a collection is increased by 10. What happens to the mean? Prove it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The mean increases by 10 too.**
>
> ```
> [(x₁+10) + (x₂+10) + … + (xₙ+10)] / n
> = (x₁ + … + xₙ + 10n) / n
> = (x₁ + … + xₙ)/n + 10n/n
> = a + 10
> ```
>
> **Picture:** the whole dot plot slides sideways by 10 and the balance point slides with it.

---

Every value is multiplied by 5. What happens to the mean? Prove it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **The mean is multiplied by 5.**
>
> ```
> (5x₁ + 5x₂ + … + 5xₙ)/n
> = 5(x₁ + … + xₙ)/n        [distributive property]
> = 5a
> ```

---

24 students' average height is 150.2 cm, but they were all wearing 1 cm shoes. Must the teacher remeasure everyone?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Every value is 1 cm too big, so the mean is exactly 1 cm too big.
>
> Correct average `= 150.2 − 1 =` **149.2 cm**.

---

<!-- ===== Median ===== -->

What happens to the median when a value greater than it is added?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It increases or stays the same.**
>
> Adding a big value pushes the middle of the sorted list to the right.
>
> **Example:** a data set with median 8; add 11. The two middle values become 8 and 11, so the median is `(8+11)/2 =` **9.5**.

---

**Always, sometimes or never:** removing a value less than the median will decrease the median.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.**
>
> Taking away a **small** value pushes the middle **up**, so the median increases or stays the same. It can never go down.

---

**Always, sometimes or never:** including any 4 values will not affect the median.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sometimes.**
>
> If two land below and two above (and the middle values don't change), the median holds. But adding four large values will certainly shift it up.

---

**Always, sometimes or never:** including 4 values less than the median will increase the median.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never.** Adding values **below** the median can only pull the middle **down** (or leave it), never up.

---

Find the median of 8, 10, 19, 23, 26, 34, 40, 41, 41, 48, 51, 55, 70, 84, 91, 92.

> [!ANSWER]
> There are **16** values, so the median is the average of the **8th and 9th**: `(41 + 41)/2 =` **41**.

---

Following on: what single value could you add to that data without changing the median? What two values? What one value could you remove?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Add one:** **41** itself. With 17 values the median is the 9th, which is still 41.
> - **Add two:** one below 41 and one above — e.g. **20 and 60**. With 18 values the 9th and 10th are still both 41.
> - **Remove one:** **41**. With 15 values the median is the 8th, still 41.

<!-- ===== Missing values ===== -->

Ten wrestlers' weights are 42, 40, 39, 33, 48, 38, 42, 35, 32 and w, with mean 39.2. Find w.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Turn the mean back into a total:** `sum = 39.2 × 10 = 392`.
>
> The nine known weights add to **349**, so `w = 392 − 349 =` **43 kg**.

---

The mean of 8, 13, 10, 4, 5, 20, y, 10 is 10.375. Find y.

> [!ANSWER]
> `sum = 10.375 × 8 = 83`.
>
> Known values add to `8+13+10+4+5+20+10 =` 70, so `y = 83 − 70 =` **13**.

---

A data set of 15 values has mean 134. Find the sum of the data.

> [!ANSWER]
> `sum = mean × count = 134 × 15 =` **2,010**.

---

A farmer's 15 trees average 25.6 coconuts, but one tree's count was recorded 3 too high. What is the correct average?

> [!ANSWER]
> Recorded total `= 25.6 × 15 =` 384.
> True total `= 384 − 3 =` 381.
> Correct mean `= 381/15 =` **25.4**.
>
> **Note you never needed the individual counts** — only the total.

---

Two students join a class whose average height was 150.2 cm. Which statements are true: (a) the average must rise; (b) it stays the same; (c) you need the new students' heights; (d) you must remeasure everyone?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(c) only.**
>
> You need the two new heights (and the old class size), but you do **not** need to remeasure anyone — the old total is already known from `mean × count`.
>
> (a) and (b) are guesses; the average could go either way.

---

Following on: the new students are 149 cm and 152 cm. What happens to the average?

> [!ANSWER]
> Their mean is `(149 + 152)/2 = 150.5`, which is **above** 150.2.
>
> So **the class average increases** — slightly, since only 2 values were added to 24.

<!-- ===== Frequency tables ===== -->

Family sizes in a class: size 3 (×3), 4 (×11), 5 (×9), 6 (×7), 7 (×3), 8 (×1), 9 (×1), 10 (×1). A student computes `(3+4+…+10)/8 = 6.5`. What's wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The frequencies were ignored.** That calculation pretends each family size occurred exactly once.
>
> **Correct:**
> `sum = 3×3 + 4×11 + 5×9 + 6×7 + 7×3 + 8×1 + 9×1 + 10×1 = 188`
> `count = 36`
> `mean = 188/36 =` **5.22**

---

Find the median family size from that table without writing out 36 numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Use a running total of frequencies:**
>
> - after the 3s: positions **1–3**
> - after the 4s: positions **4–14**
> - after the 5s: positions **15–23**
>
> With 36 values the median is the average of the **18th and 19th**, both of which fall in the 15–23 block. Both are **5** → **median = 5**.

---

Dart throws: number of trials 1–10 with student counts 1, 0, 0, 1, 4, 9, 12, 15, 10, 10. Describe this data.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Minimum:** 1 trial · **Maximum:** 10 trials
> - **Total students:** `1+0+0+1+4+9+12+15+10+10 =` **62**
> - **Mean:** `(1×1 + 4×1 + 5×4 + 6×9 + 7×12 + 8×15 + 9×10 + 10×10)/62 = (1+4+20+54+84+120+90+100)/62 = 473/62 ≈` **7.63**
> - **Median:** the 31st and 32nd values. Running totals: 1, 1, 1, 2, 6, 15, 27, 42 — both fall in the "8 trials" block → **median 8**
>
> **In words:** almost everyone needed 5 or more throws, most needed 7–10, and only two students managed it in fewer than 5.

<!-- ===== Spreadsheets ===== -->

In a spreadsheet, how are cells named, and what does `B3:G3` mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> Cells are named by **column letter + row number** — `E5` is column E, row 5.
>
> `B3:G3` is a **range**: all cells from B3 across to G3 — one row of six cells.

---

Write formulas for (a) one student's total across six subjects in row 3, and (b) the class average in the subject in column G, rows 2 to 23.

> [!ANSWER]
> - (a) **`=SUM(B3:G3)`**
> - (b) **`=AVERAGE(G2:G23)`**

---

Why use spreadsheet formulas instead of a calculator?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the spreadsheet stores the **rule**, not just the answer.
>
> Change one mark and **every** total and average that depends on it recalculates automatically — no risk of forgetting to redo a sum.

<!-- ===== Line graphs ===== -->

When is a line graph the right choice?

> [!ANSWER]
> **When showing CHANGE OVER TIME.**
>
> Joining points with segments makes trends visible as continuous paths, so you can follow one series and compare it with another at a glance.

---

Why is a line graph better than a clustered column graph for 13 years of data from 4 countries?

> [!ANSWER]
> A column graph would need **13 × 4 = 52 bars** — crowded and almost impossible to read.
>
> A line graph gives **four clean lines**, and the shape of each line *is* the trend.

---

On a line graph, what does the steepness of a segment tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The rate of change.** A steeper segment means a **bigger change over the same time interval**.
>
> That's how you compare "which year saw the fastest growth" without reading off any numbers.

---

A line graph shows space launches by the USA, China, Russia and the world. The three countries' totals don't add up to the world total. What does that tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> That **other countries launched objects too, but aren't shown** on this graph.
>
> The visualisation is selective, not complete.

---

From that same graph, can you conclude that Nepal launched nothing between 2012 and 2024?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Nepal simply isn't plotted.
>
> **A graph only supports claims about what it actually plots. Absence from a chart is not evidence of absence in the world.**

---

Why does it matter to know **how** data was collected?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it tells you **what the data can and cannot answer**, and warns you of bias or gaps.
>
> "Monthly average rainfall" means totals for that month collected over **several years** then averaged — so it describes a typical year, not any particular one. "Monthly maximum temperature" is the highest reading among the state's stations that month — one hot corner can set it.

---

Punjab's maximum temperature goes 19 °C in January up to 38 °C in June and back to 23 °C in December; Kerala stays between 29 °C and 33 °C all year. What's the conclusion?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Punjab's temperature varies far more than Kerala's.**
>
> Kerala's summer and winter maxima are almost the same — a swing of about 4 °C against Punjab's 19 °C.

---

West-coast cities peak in rainfall during June–August; east-coast cities peak in October–December. Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two different monsoons.
>
> - The **south-west monsoon** (June–September) hits the **west coast** first — Kovalam, Udupi, Mumbai.
> - The **north-east monsoon** (October–December) brings rain to the **east coast** — Rameswaram, Chennai.
>
> January–March are dry almost everywhere.

---

A sleep-duration graph plots 80 points from age 6 to 75 and looks like a smooth curve. Why is a line graph better here than a column graph?

> [!ANSWER]
> A column graph would need **70+ columns** — cluttered and heavy, burying the pattern.
>
> The line stays light and shows the shape clearly: sleep falls from about **9.5 h at age 6** to about **8 h between 30 and 50**, then rises again to about **8.5 h**.

<!-- ===== Facts to check ===== -->

Find the mean of the first 50 natural numbers, the first 50 odd numbers and the first 50 multiples of 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> - First 50 naturals (1…50): `(1 + 50)/2 =` **25.5**
> - First 50 odd (1…99): `(1 + 99)/2 =` **50**
> - First 50 multiples of 4 (4…200): `(4 + 200)/2 =` **102**
>
> **Why the shortcut works:** for any **evenly spaced** list, values pair up symmetrically about the centre — so the mean is just the average of the first and last.

---

**True or false:** the average of two even numbers is even.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
>
> `(2 + 4)/2 = 3` — odd. In fact `(2m + 2n)/2 = m + n`, which is even only when m and n have the same parity.

---

**True or false:** the average of any two multiples of 5 is a multiple of 5.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
>
> `(5 + 10)/2 = 7.5` — not even a whole number. `(5m + 5n)/2 = 5(m+n)/2`, which is a multiple of 5 only when `m + n` is even.

---

**True or false:** the average of any five multiples of 5 is a multiple of 5.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.**
>
> `(5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 10)/5 = 30/5 =` **6** — not a multiple of 5.
>
> (It is always a multiple of 5 **divided by** something, i.e. `5 × (sum of the m's)/5` — and that fraction need not be whole.)

---

Fill the blanks so the median of 5, 21, 14, __, __, __ is 13. How many possibilities are there if only counting numbers are allowed?

> [!ANSWER]
> With **6** values, the median is the average of the **3rd and 4th** when sorted, so those two must average 13.
>
> One clean choice: add **12, 14 and 1** → sorted `1, 5, 12, 14, 14, 21` → median `(12+14)/2 =` **13** ✓
>
> **Infinitely many possibilities** exist in principle, but with counting numbers you need any pair `(a, b)` with `a + b = 26` sitting in the 3rd and 4th positions, plus one value low enough or high enough not to disturb them.

---

Fill the blanks so the mean of 3, 11, __, __, 15, 6 is 6.5.

> [!ANSWER]
> `sum = 6.5 × 6 = 39`. Known values `3 + 11 + 15 + 6 =` 35, so the **two blanks must add to 4**.
>
> With counting numbers: **(1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 1)** — and (0, 4)/(4, 0) if zero is allowed. So **3 possibilities** with counting numbers.

---

Build a 3×3 grid of nine distinct numbers whose average along every row, column and diagonal is 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> Every line of 3 must **sum to 30**. A magic square works:
>
> | | | |
> |---|---|---|
> | 4 | 15 | 11 |
> | 17 | 10 | 3 |
> | 9 | 5 | 16 |
>
> Rows: `30, 30, 30` ✓ Columns: `30, 30, 30` ✓ Diagonals: `4+10+16 = 30`, `11+10+9 = 30` ✓
>
> **Why it works:** "average 10 over 3 values" is the same condition as "sum 30" — so this is just a magic square with magic constant 30.

---

Give two examples each of: 3 numbers with mean 8; 4 numbers with median 15.5; 6 numbers whose mean equals the median.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Mean 8 (sum 24):** `7, 8, 9` and `1, 3, 20`
> - **Median 15.5:** `10, 15, 16, 30` and `1, 15, 16, 100` — the two middle values must average 15.5
> - **Mean = median:** `1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6` (both 3.5) and `10, 10, 10, 10, 10, 10` (both 10)
>
> **Why symmetric data works:** when the values are balanced about the centre, the balance point and the middle value coincide.

---

In a group, one person loses 2 kg and two people gain 1 kg each. What happens to the mean weight? To the median?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The mean is unchanged** — the total changed by `−2 + 1 + 1 =` **0**, and the count didn't change.
>
> **The median may or may not move** — it depends on *whose* weights changed and where they sat in the sorted order. Without that information you can't say.
