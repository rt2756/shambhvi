<!-- ===== Fractals ===== -->

What is a fractal? Give three examples from nature.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A self-similar shape — it contains smaller copies of itself, which contain smaller copies again, at every scale.**
>
> In nature: a **fern** (fronds made of smaller fronds), a **tree** (trunk → limbs → branches → branchlets), and a **coastline** (bays containing smaller bays). Clouds, mountains and lightning too.

---

Describe how the Sierpinski Carpet is built.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take a square. Divide it into **9 equal squares** and **remove the middle one**.
>
> Repeat the same procedure on each of the **8 remaining** squares. And again on their survivors. Forever.

---

How many squares remain at step n of the Sierpinski Carpet? Derive the formula.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every surviving square becomes 8 surviving squares**, so `Rₙ₊₁ = 8Rₙ`.
>
> `R₀ = 1`, `R₁ = 8`, `R₂ = 64`, …
>
> **`Rₙ = 8ⁿ`**

---

How many holes are there at step n of the Sierpinski Carpet?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Each surviving square makes one NEW hole, and every old hole stays**: `Hₙ₊₁ = Hₙ + Rₙ`.
>
> `H₀ = 0`, `H₁ = 1`, `H₂ = 1 + 8 = 9`, `H₃ = 1 + 8 + 64 = 73`
>
> **`Hₙ = 1 + 8 + 8² + … + 8ⁿ⁻¹`**

---

If the starting square has area 1, what area remains at step n of the Sierpinski Carpet? What happens in the limit?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each step keeps **8 parts out of 9**, so the area is multiplied by 8/9 each time:
>
> **Area = `(8/9)ⁿ`**
>
> As n grows, `(8/9)ⁿ` shrinks towards **0**. The true fractal has **zero area** — yet infinitely many points remain. That is what makes fractals so strange.

---

Show that joining the midpoints of an equilateral triangle gives four identical equilateral triangles.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each corner triangle has two sides that are **halves of the original sides**, with the original 60° angle between them — so it is **isosceles with a 60° apex**, hence **equilateral**, with side `s/2`.
>
> The middle triangle is bounded by the three midpoint segments, each of which equals the third side of a corner triangle — also `s/2`.
>
> All four are equilateral with side **s/2** ✓

---

For the Sierpinski Gasket, find the number of triangles remaining and the area at step n.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each triangle becomes 3, so **`Tₙ = 3ⁿ`**.
>
> Each step keeps **3 parts out of 4**, so **area = `(3/4)ⁿ`** — again shrinking to 0.
>
> Holes: `Hₙ = 1 + 3 + 9 + … + 3ⁿ⁻¹`.

---

Describe how the Koch Snowflake is built, and find the number of sides at step n.

> [!ANSWER]
> Start with an equilateral triangle. Divide each side into **thirds**, build an equilateral triangle on the middle third and **remove that middle third** — so each straight side becomes a **bump of 4 shorter segments**.
>
> Each side becomes **4** sides, so **`Sₙ = 3 × 4ⁿ`** (starting from 3).

---

Find the perimeter of the Koch Snowflake at step n, starting with side length 1. What happens as n grows?

> [!ANSWER]
> At step n there are `3 × 4ⁿ` sides, each of length `(1/3)ⁿ`.
>
> **`Pₙ = 3 × 4ⁿ × (1/3)ⁿ = 3 × (4/3)ⁿ`**
>
> Since `4/3 > 1`, the perimeter **grows without limit** — the Koch Snowflake has **infinite perimeter** but sits inside a small circle, so its **area is finite**.

---

Where do fractals appear in art and architecture?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Kandariya Mahadev Temple, Khajuraho** (c. 1025 CE) — the whole temple shape repeats in smaller and smaller copies on itself. Also at Madurai, Hampi, Rameswaram, Varanasi.
> - **Nigerian Fulani wedding blankets** — diamonds inside diamonds inside diamonds.
> - **M. C. Escher's *Smaller and Smaller*** — the same lizard at ever-shrinking scale.

<!-- ===== Visualising ===== -->

Cut off the four corners of a square, each cut running between the midpoints of adjacent edges. What is left, and what can the corners make?

> [!ANSWER]
> What's left is a **tilted square** with **half the area** of the original.
>
> The four cut-off corners are congruent right triangles that can be reassembled into **another square of the same size** as the tilted one — since together they equal half the original too.

---

Mark the sides of an equilateral triangle into thirds and cut off each corner as far as the marks. What shape results?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **regular hexagon** — each cut adds one new side, and the six sides that remain are all one-third of the original side.

---

Mark a square's sides into thirds and cut off each corner as far as the marks. What shape results?

> [!ANSWER]
> An **octagon** — but **not a regular one**. The four remaining side-pieces are `s/3` long, while the four new cut edges are `(s/3)√2` — longer, because they're diagonals of little squares.

---

Name a solid whose profile can be a square from one direction and a circle from another.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **cylinder** — circular from the end, rectangular (square if its height equals its diameter) from the side.
>
> A **cone** gives circle-and-triangle; a **square pyramid** gives square-and-triangle.

<!-- ===== Faces, edges, vertices ===== -->

How many faces, edges and vertices does a prism with 10-sided ends have? What about n sides?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Faces:** n side faces + 2 ends = **n + 2** → **12**
> - **Edges:** n top + n bottom + n verticals = **3n** → **30**
> - **Vertices:** n top + n bottom = **2n** → **20**

---

How many faces, edges and vertices does a pyramid with a 10-sided base have? What about n sides?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Faces:** n slanting + 1 base = **n + 1** → **11**
> - **Edges:** n base + n slanting = **2n** → **20**
> - **Vertices:** n base + 1 apex = **n + 1** → **11**

---

Check Euler's rule `F + V − E = 2` for a general prism and a general pyramid.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Prism:** `(n + 2) + 2n − 3n = 2` ✓
> - **Pyramid:** `(n + 1) + (n + 1) − 2n = 2` ✓
>
> The n cancels every time, which is why the rule holds for **every** prism and pyramid, not just the ones you've counted.

---

What is the difference between a prism and a pyramid?

> [!ANSWER]
> - A **prism** has **two congruent parallel polygon faces** joined by parallelograms — it has a constant cross-section.
> - A **pyramid** has **one polygon base** and a single apex point joined to every base vertex — it tapers to a point.

<!-- ===== Nets ===== -->

What is a net?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A flat shape that folds up into a solid** — the solid "unfolded" onto a plane.
>
> (Tabs or flaps used for gluing are *not* part of the net; only the faces are.)

---

How many distinct nets do a cube, a regular tetrahedron and a dodecahedron have?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Cube: 11** (also an octahedron: 11)
> - **Regular tetrahedron: 2**
> - **Dodecahedron: 43,380**
>
> Two nets count as the same if one is a **rotation or a flip** of the other.

---

Describe the net of a cylinder, and give the rectangle's dimensions.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Two circles plus one rectangle.**
>
> The rectangle's height is the **cylinder's height h**, and its width is the **circumference `2πr`**.
>
> **Why:** that edge must wrap exactly once around the rim, so it has to be the same length as the rim.

---

What is the net of a cone, and why is it a sector rather than a rectangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A circle (the base) plus a sector of a larger circle.**
>
> **Why a sector:** every point on the base rim is exactly the **slant height `l`** away from the apex. Unrolled, all those points sit at distance l from a single point — which is the definition of a circle's arc, centred at the apex.

---

Can a sphere be made from a net? Why does this matter beyond mathematics?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** No flat sheet can wrap a ball without wrinkles, gaps or overlaps.
>
> **Why it matters:** this is exactly why **every world map distorts something** — area, shape, or distance. There's no way to flatten a sphere faithfully.

<!-- ===== Shortest paths ===== -->

An ant on one face of a cuboid wants to reach a laddu on another. How do you find the shortest surface path?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Unfold the cuboid into a net, then draw the straight line.**
>
> **Why:** unfolding doesn't stretch anything — every face keeps its exact size and shape — so **every path keeps its exact length**. The shortest path on the net is a straight line, and that line folds back into the shortest path on the box.

---

Why isn't one unfolding enough?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **different unfoldings give different straight-line distances**, and some straight lines on a net fall **outside** the net entirely — meaning they don't correspond to any real path on the box.
>
> You have to list the sensible unfoldings and take the **shortest** valid one.

---

One unfolding of a box gives a right triangle with legs 24 cm and 32 cm between the ant and the laddu. How long is that path?

> [!ANSWER]
> `d² = 24² + 32² = 576 + 1024 = 1600` → **d = 40 cm**.
>
> If another unfolding gives 42 cm, the **40 cm route wins** — but you'd still check the remaining unfoldings.

---

Why is the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem the right tool for these path problems?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because on the **flattened net**, the ant's route is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the horizontal and vertical distances travelled across the unfolded faces.
>
> The theorem turns "how far along the surface?" into simple arithmetic.

<!-- ===== Projections ===== -->

Define the projection of a point onto a plane.

> [!ANSWER]
> Drop a **perpendicular** from the point P to the plane; the foot of that perpendicular, O, is the **projection** of P.
>
> The projections of all a solid's points together form the projection of the solid.

---

Is a segment's projection ever longer than the segment? When is it equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Never longer.** `p ≤ l` always.
>
> **Why:** dropping perpendiculars from both ends makes the original segment the **hypotenuse** of a right triangle whose horizontal leg is the projection — and the hypotenuse is always the longest side.
>
> `p = l` exactly when the segment is **parallel to the plane**; `p = 0` when it is **perpendicular** to it.

---

What can the projection of a parallelogram look like?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Always another parallelogram** (or, in the extreme case, a line segment).
>
> **Why:** parallel lines always project to parallel lines, so both pairs of opposite sides stay parallel.
>
> You can test this physically: hold a paper parallelogram in sunlight and turn it — the shadow stays a parallelogram however you tilt it.

---

What can you say about the projection of a regular n-sided polygon?

> [!ANSWER]
> It has **n sides** too (unless it collapses to a line), because each side projects to a side.
>
> **But it need not be regular** — the sides get shortened by different amounts depending on their direction relative to the plane.

---

Does the projection of a solid tell you what the solid is?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Many different solids give the same projection — a cube, a tall cuboid and a square prism can all cast the same square shadow.
>
> **That's why engineers use three views**, from three mutually perpendicular directions.

---

Name the three standard views and the planes they use.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Front view** — projection on the **vertical plane** (looking from the front)
> - **Top view** — projection on the **horizontal plane** (looking from above)
> - **Side view** — projection on the **side plane** (looking from the side)

---

How does the size of a torch shadow change as you move the torch, and what happens as it moves infinitely far?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Closer torch → bigger shadow**, because the light rays spread out more steeply from a near source.
>
> As the torch moves further away the rays become more nearly **parallel**, and the shadow shrinks towards the **exact projection**.

---

Why is a sunlit shadow essentially a projection?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the Sun is so far away that its rays reaching us are effectively **parallel** — it's the "infinitely distant torch".
>
> So on a surface perpendicular to the rays, the shadow **is** the projection.

---

Give the front, top and side views of a cylinder standing upright.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Front view:** a **rectangle** (width = diameter, height = height)
> - **Side view:** the same **rectangle**
> - **Top view:** a **circle**

---

Give the front, top and side views of a cone standing on its base.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Front view:** a **triangle**
> - **Side view:** the same **triangle**
> - **Top view:** a **circle** (with the apex projecting to its centre)

<!-- ===== Isometric ===== -->

What is an isometric projection, and where does the name come from?

> [!ANSWER]
> A projection in which **all the edges of a cube project to segments of EQUAL length** — obtained by balancing the cube on one corner and projecting straight down.
>
> **Isometric** is Greek for **"equal measure"**.
>
> Its outline is a **regular hexagon**.

---

Why does an isometric grid work so well for drawing solids?

> [!ANSWER]
> Two reasons:
>
> 1. **Parallel lines project to parallel lines**, so the solid's three families of parallel edges (length, depth, height) become the grid's three directions.
> 2. The projection being **isometric** means **one unit along any axis is the same length on paper** — so you can just count grid units.

---

Which grid directions represent height, depth and length?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Vertical `|`** → **height**
> - **One diagonal `╲`** → **depth**
> - **The other diagonal `╱`** → **length**
>
> Moving along a grid direction on paper corresponds to moving along that axis of the solid.

---

How many distinct shapes can you make by gluing four cubes face-to-face in a flat layer? What if you allow 3D arrangements?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Flat (like Tetris): 5** — the I, O, T, L/J and S/Z shapes.
>
> **In 3D there are more** — you can lift a cube out of the plane, giving extra genuinely three-dimensional arrangements that no flat shape matches.

---

An "impossible triangle" is drawn on an isometric grid. Could you build it from real cubes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> **Why the illusion works:** an isometric projection **throws away depth information**. Two edges that appear to meet on the paper may be far apart in space — the drawing joins them anyway, and the eye accepts the join.
>
> **Every small piece of it IS buildable**; only the whole figure contradicts itself.

---

Would the top, front and side views of an impossible triangle look impossible too?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — each single view is perfectly ordinary.**
>
> The contradiction only appears when you try to reconcile all three at once, which is precisely what a single isometric drawing tempts you to do.
