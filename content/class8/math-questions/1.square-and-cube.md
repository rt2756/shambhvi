<!-- ===== The locker puzzle ===== -->

In the 100-locker puzzle, why does a locker end up **open**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it was **toggled an odd number of times**.
>
> Each locker is toggled once by **each factor of its number** — locker 6 is touched by persons 1, 2, 3 and 6. So the question is really: **which numbers have an odd number of factors?**

---

Why do most numbers have an **even** number of factors?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **factors come in pairs**. Each factor has a **partner** such that the pair multiplies to the number — for 6, `1 × 6` and `2 × 3`.
>
> Pairing them up means the count is even.

---

Which numbers have an **odd** number of factors, and why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The square numbers.**
>
> **Why:** in a square, one factor is **its own partner** — `6 × 6 = 36`. That factor has no separate twin to pair with, so the total count comes out **odd**.

---

Which lockers remain open at the end?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **squares**: **1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100** — ten lockers.

---

Which lockers are toggled **exactly twice**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **prime numbers** — each has exactly two factors, **1 and itself**.
>
> The first five are **2, 3, 5, 7, 11**.

---

<!-- ===== Squares ===== -->

Why are 1, 4, 9, 16 called **squares**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because they count the **unit squares in a square grid** — the area of a square with side n is `n × n`.
>
> We write `n × n = n²`, read "n squared".

---

Which digits can a perfect square **end** in?

> [!ANSWER]
> Only **0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9**.
>
> **Never 2, 3, 7 or 8.**

---

Is every number ending in 6 a perfect square?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** 16 and 36 are squares, but **26 is not**.
>
> **The test only works one way:** ending in 2, 3, 7 or 8 proves a number is **not** a square, but ending in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9 proves **nothing**. It's still useful — it rules candidates out instantly.

---

Without calculating, say which of these cannot be perfect squares: **2032, 2048, 1027, 1089**.

> [!ANSWER]
> **2032** (ends in 2), **2048** (ends in 8) and **1027** (ends in 7) are **definitely not** squares.
>
> **1089** ends in 9, so it *might* be — and indeed `33² = 1089`.

---

`200² = 40000` has four zeros; `20² = 400` has two. What's the rule?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The number of zeros doubles.** A number with **1 zero** at the end has a square ending in **2** zeros; 2 zeros → 4 zeros; 3 zeros → 6 zeros.
>
> So **a perfect square can only end in an EVEN number of zeros** — a number ending in exactly 3 zeros is never a square.

---

Which of these have **6** in the units place: **38², 34², 46², 56², 74², 82²**?

> [!ANSWER]
> A square ends in 6 when the number ends in **4 or 6**.
>
> **34², 46², 56²** — and also **74²**. (38² ends in 4; 82² ends in 4.)

---

<!-- ===== Sum of odd numbers ===== -->

What do you get by adding the odd numbers from 1?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The square numbers.** `1 = 1²`, `1+3 = 4 = 2²`, `1+3+5 = 9 = 3²`, `1+3+5+7 = 16 = 4²`.
>
> **The sum of the first n odd numbers is n².**

---

Explain **with a picture** why this happens.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **square grid of dots** peels apart into **inverted L-shaped layers**.
>
> The corner is 1 dot, the L round it is 3, the next L is 5, then 7 — **each L is the next odd number**, and together they rebuild the whole square.
>
> Because the picture works for a square of **any** size, the rule holds forever. (A **visual proof** can be complete by itself, with no words.)

---

Use successive subtraction to test whether **25** is a perfect square.

> [!ANSWER]
> Subtract 1, 3, 5, 7, … in turn:
>
> `25−1 = 24` · `24−3 = 21` · `21−5 = 16` · `16−7 = 9` · `9−9 = 0`
>
> It reaches **exactly 0** in **5 steps** → **25 is a perfect square, and √25 = 5**.
>
> The number of subtractions *is* the square root.

---

Test **38** the same way.

> [!ANSWER]
> `38−1 = 37` · `−3 = 34` · `−5 = 29` · `−7 = 22` · `−9 = 13` · `−11 = 2` · `−13 = −11`
>
> It **overshoots into the negatives** without hitting 0 → **38 is not a perfect square**.

---

What is the **nth odd number**? Use it to find `36²` given `35² = 1225`.

> [!ANSWER]
> The nth odd number is **`2n − 1`**.
>
> `1225` is the sum of the first 35 odd numbers, so `36²` needs the **36th** odd number added: `2 × 36 − 1 = 71`.
>
> `36² = 1225 + 71 = ` **1296**

---

Given `125² = 15625`, what is `126²`?

> [!ANSWER]
> Add the **126th odd number**, `2 × 126 − 1 = 251`:
>
> `126² = 15625 + 251 = ` **15876**

---

What do you get by adding **two consecutive triangular numbers**?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **square number**: `1 + 3 = 4` · `3 + 6 = 9` · `6 + 10 = 16` · `10 + 15 = 25`.

---

<!-- ===== Square roots ===== -->

Define the **square root**. How many integer square roots does a perfect square have?

> [!ANSWER]
> If `y = x²`, then **x is the square root of y**, written `√y`.
>
> **Every perfect square has TWO integer square roots — one positive, one negative**, because `8² = 64` **and** `(−8)² = 64`. So `√64 = ±8`.
>
> (By convention we usually take the positive one.)

---

Use prime factorisation to find **√324**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `324 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3`
>
> Split into **two identical groups**: `(2 × 3 × 3) × (2 × 3 × 3) = 18 × 18`
>
> → **√324 = 18**

---

Is **156** a perfect square? Show why.

> [!ANSWER]
> `156 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 13`
>
> The **3 and the 13 have no partners** — the factors can't be split into two identical groups.
>
> → **156 is not a perfect square.**

---

What must every prime satisfy in the factorisation of a perfect square?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Each prime must appear an EVEN number of times**, so the factors pair up.
>
> That's the same condition as "split into two identical groups", just stated per prime.

---

Estimate **√1936** without a calculator.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1.** Bracket it: `40² = 1600`, `50² = 2500` → the root is **between 40 and 50**.
>
> **2.** Last digit is **6**, so the root ends in **4 or 6** → 44 or 46.
>
> **3.** Check the middle: `45² = 2025 > 1936` → the root is **below 45**.
>
> **4.** So it must be **44**. ✓

---

Estimate **√250**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `15² = 225` and `16² = 256`, so `15 < √250 < 16`.
>
> Since **256 is much closer to 250 than 225 is**, `√250` is **approximately 16** (and a little less than 16).

---

A square cloth has area **125 cm²**. What is the largest square handkerchief with a **whole-number** side that can be cut from it?

> [!ANSWER]
> 125 isn't a perfect square. The nearest are `11² = 121` and `12² = 144`.
>
> Since `144 > 125`, a 12 cm square won't fit → the largest whole-number side is **11 cm**.

---

Find the length of the side of a square whose area is **441 m²**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `441 = 3 × 3 × 7 × 7 = (3 × 7)² = 21²`
>
> → side = **21 m**

---

<!-- ===== Cubes ===== -->

What is a **perfect cube**? List the first six.

> [!ANSWER]
> A number obtained by **multiplying a number by itself three times**: `n × n × n = n³`.
>
> **1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216.**

---

Why is `4³ = 64` the number of unit cubes in a cube of edge 4?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each **layer** is a `4 × 4` square of unit cubes = **16**, and there are **4 such layers**.
>
> `16 × 4 = 64` — which is `4 × 4 × 4`.

---

Which digits can a **cube** end in? How does that compare with squares?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A cube can end in ANY digit** — 0 through 9.
>
> **Squares** are restricted to **0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9**. So the last-digit trick that rules out squares gives you **nothing** for cubes.

---

Is **9** a perfect cube?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** `2³ = 8` and `3³ = 27`, so 9 falls **between two consecutive cubes** — and so does every number from 10 to 26.

---

Continue the pattern and state the rule: `1 = 1³`, `3 + 5 = 8 = 2³`, `7 + 9 + 11 = 27 = 3³`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `13 + 15 + 17 + 19 = 64 = 4³` · `21 + 23 + 25 + 27 + 29 = 125 = 5³`
>
> **The nth cube is the sum of n consecutive odd numbers**, each group carrying on from where the last one stopped.

---

Without adding, find `91 + 93 + 95 + 97 + 99 + 101 + 103 + 105 + 107 + 109`.

> [!ANSWER]
> That's **10 consecutive odd numbers** — so by the pattern it equals **10³ = 1000**.

---

<!-- ===== Cube roots ===== -->

Use prime factorisation to find **∛3375**.

> [!ANSWER]
> `3375 = 3 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 5 × 5`
>
> Split into **three identical groups**: `(3 × 5) × (3 × 5) × (3 × 5) = 15³`
>
> → **∛3375 = 15**
>
> (Equivalently, group into **triplets**: `(3 × 3 × 3) × (5 × 5 × 5) = 3³ × 5³`.)

---

Is **500** a perfect cube?

> [!ANSWER]
> `500 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 5`
>
> The two 2s **cannot be split into three identical groups** — you'd need three of them.
>
> → **500 is not a perfect cube.**

---

How does the prime-factorisation test differ between squares and cubes?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Square:** split the factors into **two** identical groups — each prime must appear an **even** number of times.
>
> **Cube:** split into **three** identical groups — each prime must appear a number of times that is a **multiple of 3**.

---

<!-- ===== Taxicab ===== -->

What is special about **1729**?

> [!ANSWER]
> It is the **smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways**:
>
> `1729 = 1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³`
>
> It's called the **Hardy–Ramanujan number**: when G.H. Hardy called his taxicab number "rather a dull number", **Ramanujan** immediately pointed out this property. Numbers like it are **taxicab numbers** — the next two are **4104** and **13832**.
