<!-- ===== Exponential growth ===== -->

A sheet 0.001 cm thick is folded repeatedly. Why does its thickness reach the Moon in only 46 folds?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the thickness **doubles with every fold** — this is **exponential (multiplicative) growth**.
>
> Doubling starts slowly (just over 1 cm after 10 folds) but accelerates ferociously: by fold 30 it's ~10.7 km, and by fold 46 it's **more than 7,00,000 km**.
>
> Our intuition badly underestimates repeated doubling, which is the whole point of the example.

---

By what factor does the thickness grow every **10 folds**, and why is that the same wherever you start?

> [!ANSWER]
> By **1024** — that is, `2¹⁰`.
>
> **Why it doesn't depend on the starting point:** each fold multiplies by 2, so ten folds multiply by `2 × 2 × … = 2¹⁰` regardless of what you began with. Going from fold 0→10, 10→20 or 30→40 all multiply by the same 1024.

<!-- ===== Notation ===== -->

In `5⁴ = 625`, name each part.

> [!ANSWER]
> **5 is the base**, **4 is the exponent (or power)**, and `5⁴` is the **exponential form** of 625.
>
> Read as "5 raised to the power 4".

---

What is the difference between `4 + 4 + 4` and `4 × 4 × 4`?

> [!ANSWER]
> `4 + 4 + 4 = 3 × 4 = ` **12** — repeated **addition** is multiplication.
>
> `4 × 4 × 4 = 4³ = ` **64** — repeated **multiplication** is a power.
>
> Mixing the two up is one of the commonest errors with exponents.

---

Express in exponential form: **(a)** `6 × 6 × 6 × 6` · **(b)** `5 × 5 × 7 × 7 × 7` · **(c)** `a × a × a × c × c × c × c × d`

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a)** `6⁴` · **(b)** `5² × 7³` · **(c)** `a³c⁴d`
>
> (A single `d` is `d¹`, which we write simply as `d`.)

---

Express **32400** as a product of powers of its prime factors.

> [!ANSWER]
> `32400 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 5`
>
> = **`2⁴ × 3⁴ × 5²`**

---

Express **648** in the same way.

> [!ANSWER]
> `648 = 8 × 81 = (2 × 2 × 2) × (3 × 3 × 3 × 3)`
>
> = **`2³ × 3⁴`**

---

Evaluate: **(a)** `(−4)³` · **(b)** `(−2)⁴` · **(c)** `(−1)⁵⁶` · **(d)** `(−1)⁵`

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a) −64** — odd power, so the minus survives.
>
> **(b) +16** — even power, the minuses pair off.
>
> **(c) +1** — 56 is even.
>
> **(d) −1** — 5 is odd.
>
> **The rule:** minuses cancel in pairs, so **even power → positive, odd power → negative**.

<!-- ===== The laws ===== -->

State and explain **`nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ`**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Multiplying powers of the **same base** means **adding the exponents**.
>
> **Why:** `nᵃ` is a copies of n, `nᵇ` is b more copies. Writing them one after another gives **a + b copies of n in a row** — which is `nᵃ⁺ᵇ`.

---

Simplify `p⁴ × p⁶`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(p×p×p×p) × (p×p×p×p×p×p)` = **ten** p's = **`p¹⁰`**

---

Write `4⁶` as a power of a power in **two** different ways, and verify.

> [!ANSWER]
> `4⁶ = (4³)² = 64² = 4096` and `4⁶ = (4²)³ = 16³ = 4096` ✓
>
> **Why both work:** `(nᵃ)ᵇ = nᵃᵇ`, and `3 × 2 = 2 × 3 = 6`.

---

State the rule for **dividing** powers of the same base, and explain it.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`nᵃ ÷ nᵇ = nᵃ⁻ᵇ`** (n ≠ 0).
>
> **Why:** write both out as products. **b of the copies on top cancel with the b below**, leaving `a − b` copies on top.

---

Simplify `2¹⁰⁰ ÷ 2²⁵`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `2¹⁰⁰⁻²⁵ = ` **`2⁷⁵`**

---

Simplify `2⁵ × 5⁵` using a law of exponents.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Same power, different bases:** `mᵃ × nᵃ = (mn)ᵃ`
>
> `2⁵ × 5⁵ = (2 × 5)⁵ = 10⁵ = ` **1,00,000**
>
> **Why:** regroup `(2×2×2×2×2) × (5×5×5×5×5)` into `(2×5) × (2×5) × (2×5) × (2×5) × (2×5)`.

---

<!-- ===== Zero and negative exponents ===== -->

**Derive** why `x⁰ = 1` rather than just stating it.

> [!ANSWER]
> Use the division law with equal exponents:
>
> `2⁰ = 2⁴⁻⁴ = 2⁴ ÷ 2⁴ = 16 ÷ 16 = 1`
>
> In general `xᵃ ÷ xᵃ = xᵃ⁻ᵃ = x⁰`, and **anything non-zero divided by itself is 1**.
>
> So **`x⁰ = 1` is forced** by wanting the division law to keep working — it isn't an arbitrary choice.

---

**Derive** the meaning of a negative exponent.

> [!ANSWER]
> `2⁴ ÷ 2⁵ = 2⁴⁻⁵ = 2⁻¹` by the law.
>
> But written out, `(2×2×2×2) / (2×2×2×2×2) = 1/2`.
>
> Both must be the same, so **`2⁻¹ = 1/2`**. In general **`n⁻ᵃ = 1/nᵃ`** (n ≠ 0).

---

Write as ordinary fractions: **(a)** `2⁻⁴` · **(b)** `10⁻⁵` · **(c)** `(−7)⁻²`

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a)** `1/2⁴ = 1/16` · **(b)** `1/10⁵ = 1/100000` · **(c)** `1/(−7)² = 1/49` (positive, since the power is even).

---

Simplify and write in exponential form: **(a)** `2⁻⁴ × 2⁷` · **(b)** `3² × 3⁻⁵ × 3⁶` · **(c)** `p³ × p⁻¹⁰`

> [!ANSWER]
> **(a)** `2⁻⁴⁺⁷ = ` **`2³`** = 8
>
> **(b)** `3²⁻⁵⁺⁶ = ` **`3³`** = 27
>
> **(c)** `p³⁻¹⁰ = ` **`p⁻⁷`** = `1/p⁷`
>
> The addition law works unchanged with negative exponents — that's the point of defining them this way.

---

Why must **n ≠ 0** in the division law?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because `nᵃ ÷ nᵇ` would mean **dividing by zero**, which is not defined.
>
> Same reason `x⁰ = 1` excludes x = 0.

---

`4⁷` is how many times larger than `4⁵`? And `4²` compared with `4⁻²`?

> [!ANSWER]
> `4⁷ ÷ 4⁵ = 4² = ` **16 times** larger.
>
> `4² ÷ 4⁻² = 4²⁻⁽⁻²⁾ = 4⁴ = ` **256 times** larger.
>
> Note the double negative: subtracting −2 means **adding** 2.

---

<!-- ===== Combinations ===== -->

Roxie has **7 dresses, 2 hats and 3 pairs of shoes**. How many ways can she dress?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Multiply the choices at each step:** `7 × 2 × 3 = ` **42 ways**.
>
> For each of the 7 dresses there are 2 hats, and for each of those 14 combinations there are 3 pairs of shoes.

---

How many **5-digit passwords** are possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> Each digit has **10 choices** (0–9), and there are 5 digits:
>
> `10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10⁵ = ` **1,00,000**

---

That problem looks hard at first. What technique makes it easy?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Solve a simpler version first.**
>
> A **2-digit** lock: `10 × 10 = 100`. A **3-digit** lock: for each of those 100, ten choices for the third digit → `1000`. The pattern `10ⁿ` is then obvious.
>
> This technique is worth reaching for whenever a problem seems too big.

---

Estu wants a lock with **6 slots using letters A–Z**. How many passwords, and is it safer?

> [!ANSWER]
> `26⁶` possibilities — over **300 million**.
>
> **Far safer** than `10⁵ = 1,00,000`, because both the **number of choices per slot** (26 instead of 10) and the **number of slots** (6 instead of 5) went up.

---

Lotuses **double** every day and fill a pond on **day 30**. On which day was it **half** full?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Day 29.**
>
> **Why:** the number doubles each day, so the final doubling takes the pond from **half full to completely full**. The day before "full" must be "half".

<!-- ===== Powers of 10 ===== -->

Write **5642** using powers of 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(5 × 10³) + (6 × 10²) + (4 × 10¹) + (2 × 10⁰)`

---

Write **561.903** using powers of 10.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(5 × 10²) + (6 × 10¹) + (1 × 10⁰) + (9 × 10⁻¹) + (0 × 10⁻²) + (3 × 10⁻³)`
>
> Digits **after** the decimal point use **negative** powers of 10.

---

Why is **scientific notation** useful for very large numbers?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because with many digits it becomes **difficult to read numbers correctly** — you can **miscount the zeros** or misplace commas.
>
> The book's example: getting **₹5,000 when you should have got ₹50,000**. In many situations **the number of zeros matters more than the leading digits**, and scientific notation states it explicitly as a power.

---

Write **5900** in standard scientific notation, and explain what makes it "standard".

> [!ANSWER]
> **`5.9 × 10³`**
>
> It could also be written `59 × 10²` or `0.59 × 10⁴`, but the **standard** form has exactly **one non-zero digit before the decimal point** — which makes any two numbers instantly comparable by their powers.

---

The mass of the Earth is 59,76,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,000 kg. Write it in scientific notation.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`5.976 × 10²⁴` kg**
>
> Far easier to read, write and compare than counting 22 zeros.
