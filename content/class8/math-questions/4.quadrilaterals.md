<!-- ===== Basics and the 360° sum ===== -->

Where does the word **quadrilateral** come from, and what does it tell you?

> [!ANSWER]
> Latin **quadri** = four, **latus** = side. So it literally means **"four sides"** — a closed figure made of four straight sides.

---

Four straight segments are drawn, but two of them cross each other. Is the figure a quadrilateral?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** A quadrilateral must be **closed** and its sides must **not cross**. A crossed figure (a "bow tie") isn't one of the shapes this chapter is about.

---

Prove that the angles of **any** quadrilateral add up to 360°.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Draw one diagonal.** It cuts the quadrilateral into **two triangles**.
>
> Each triangle's angles add to 180°, so the six angles together give `180 + 180 = 360°`.
>
> **Why those six are the quadrilateral's four:** two corners are untouched by the diagonal, and the other two are each split into two pieces by it — the pieces add back up to the full corner angle. Nothing is lost or gained.

---

Three angles of a quadrilateral are 85°, 100° and 120°. Find the fourth.

> [!ANSWER]
> `85 + 100 + 120 = 305`, so the fourth is `360 − 305 =` **55°**.

---

Can a quadrilateral have exactly **three** right angles?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Three 90°s use 270°, so the fourth is forced to be `360 − 270 =` **90°** as well. You can't stop at three — the shape becomes a rectangle whether you like it or not.

---

The angles of a quadrilateral are in the ratio 1 : 2 : 3 : 4. Find them.

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the parts be x, 2x, 3x, 4x. Then `10x = 360` → `x = 36`.
>
> The angles are **36°, 72°, 108°, 144°**.

---

<!-- ===== Rectangles ===== -->

Give **three different definitions of a rectangle**, all of which describe the same shape.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. All angles 90° **and** opposite sides equal.
> 2. **All angles 90°** (the shortest one).
> 3. **Diagonals equal and bisecting each other.**
>
> All three pick out exactly the same set of shapes — which is why proving they agree is worth doing.

---

Definition 1 above says "all angles 90° **and** opposite sides equal". Show that the second half is **unnecessary**.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take ABCD with all four angles 90° and join BD.
>
> - At B: `∠3 + ∠1 = 90°`.
> - In ∆BCD: `∠3 + ∠2 + 90 = 180`, so `∠3 + ∠2 = 90°`.
> - Both equal `90 − ∠3`, so **∠1 = ∠2**.
>
> Now ∆BAD and ∆DCB share side BD, have a right angle each, and have ∠1 = ∠2 → **AAS** → congruent → `AD = CB` and `AB = CD`.
>
> **The opposite sides had to be equal.** Stating it was repeating yourself.

---

Prove that the **diagonals of a rectangle are equal**.

> [!ANSWER]
> In rectangle ABCD, compare ∆ADC and ∆DAB:
>
> - `AB = CD` (opposite sides)
> - `∠BAD = ∠CDA = 90°`
> - `AD` is **common**
>
> → **SAS** → `∆ADC ≅ ∆DAB` → the matching sides **AC = BD**. ✓

---

A carpenter has one 8 cm strip and wants two strips whose four endpoints form a rectangle. How long is the second strip, and where should she join them?

> [!ANSWER]
> The strips are the **diagonals**.
>
> - Diagonals of a rectangle are **equal** → the second strip is **8 cm**.
> - Diagonals of a rectangle **bisect each other** → join them at their **midpoints** (4 cm from each end).

---

At what angle must the carpenter cross the two strips?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Any angle at all** — it makes no difference.
>
> **Why:** let one angle at O be x. ∆AOB is isosceles (both halves of equal diagonals), so its base angles are `a = 90 − x/2`. ∆AOD is isosceles too, base angles `b = x/2`.
>
> Each corner of the quadrilateral is `a + b = 90 − x/2 + x/2 =` **90°** — the x **cancels**.

---

Why is this carpenter's trick actually used on building sites?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it needs **no protractor**. Two equal strings pegged at their midpoints give an exactly rectangular outline no matter how you swing them.
>
> Carpenters in Europe and house-builders in Mozambique are both recorded using it.

---

Draw a quadrilateral whose diagonals are both 8 cm, bisect each other, and cross at 140°. What shape is it?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A rectangle.**
>
> Draw AB = 8 cm, mark its midpoint O, draw a line through O at 140° to AB, and cut 4 cm arcs each side of O to get C and D. Join A–C–B–D.
>
> Equal + bisecting is all a rectangle needs, so the 140° is irrelevant.

---

In rectangle ABCD the diagonals meet at O, and the diagonal AC makes an angle of **30°** with side AB. Find `∠CAD`, `∠ABD`, `∠ADB` and `∠ACD`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `∠CAD = 90 − 30 =` **60°** — the diagonal splits the right angle at A.
> - `∠ABD =` **30°**: ∆AOB is **isosceles** (OA = OB, halves of equal diagonals), so `∠OBA = ∠OAB = 30°`.
> - `∠ADB = 90 − 30 =` **60°** — the right angle at D minus `∠BDC`, and `∠BDC = ∠DBA = 30°` (alternate angles, `AB || DC`).
> - `∠ACD =` **30°** — alternate angles again, `∠ACD = ∠CAB = 30°`.

---

PQRS is a rectangle whose diagonals meet at O with `∠QOR = 110°`. Find `∠OQR` and `∠OQP`.

> [!ANSWER]
> ∆QOR is **isosceles** (OQ = OR, halves of equal diagonals), so
> `∠OQR = ∠ORQ = (180 − 110)/2 =` **35°**.
>
> The full corner `∠PQR = 90°`, so `∠OQP = 90 − 35 =` **55°**.

---

Why are the opposite sides of a rectangle **parallel**? (Don't just say "they look it".)

> [!ANSWER]
> Side AB is a **transversal** cutting AD and BC. The co-interior angles are `∠A + ∠B = 90 + 90 = 180°`.
>
> **When co-interior angles add to 180°, the lines are parallel.** So `AD || BC`, and the same argument gives `AB || DC`.

---

PL and AM are two **perpendicular diameters** of a circle with centre O. What kind of figure is APML?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A square.**
>
> **Why:** PL and AM are its diagonals. They are **equal** (all diameters of a circle are equal), they **bisect each other** (both pass through the centre), and they meet at **90°** — the three conditions for a square.

---

You have two sticks of equal length and a thread, but no paper and no protractor. How do you construct an exact 90°?

> [!ANSWER]
> Lay the sticks so their **midpoints coincide** at O, in any crossing position. Run the thread round the four endpoints.
>
> Equal diagonals bisecting each other ⇒ the figure is a **rectangle** ⇒ each of its corners is exactly **90°**.

---

<!-- ===== Squares ===== -->

What extra condition turns "equal diagonals bisecting each other" into a **square**?

> [!ANSWER]
> They must also cross at **90°**.
>
> **Why:** in a square, `∆BOA ≅ ∆BOC` by SSS (`BA = BC`, `OA = OC`, `OB` common). So `∠BOA = ∠BOC`, and since they lie on a straight line they add to 180° → **90° each**.

---

Construct a square of diagonal 6 cm **without a protractor**. Describe the steps.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw AC = 6 cm and mark its midpoint O.
> 2. Construct the **perpendicular bisector** of AC (compass arcs from A and C) — this gives the 90° without a protractor.
> 3. On that perpendicular, mark B and D with `OB = OD = 3 cm`.
> 4. Join A–B–C–D.
>
> Equal diagonals + bisecting + 90° ⇒ square. ✓

---

Show that a diagonal of a square makes **45°** with each side.

> [!ANSWER]
> In ∆ADC: `∠1 + ∠3 + 90 = 180`, so `∠1 + ∠3 = 90°`.
>
> Since `AD = DC`, the triangle is **isosceles**, so `∠1 = ∠3`.
>
> Therefore `∠1 = ∠3 =` **45°** — the diagonal **bisects** the 90° corner.

---

Is every rectangle a square? Is every square a rectangle?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every square is a rectangle** — it has all four angles 90°, which is the whole definition of a rectangle.
>
> **Not every rectangle is a square** — a 5 cm × 2 cm rectangle has 90° angles but unequal sides.

---

CASE is a square and U, V, W, X are the **midpoints** of its sides. What kind of quadrilateral is UVWX?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A square.**
>
> **Why:** each of the four corner triangles is right-angled with both legs equal to **half a side** — so the four are congruent (SAS), making `UV = VW = WX = XU`. The two acute angles in each corner triangle are 45° each, so the angle left at each vertex of UVWX is `180 − 45 − 45 =` **90°**. Equal sides + 90° angles = square.

---

If a quadrilateral has **four equal sides and one angle of 90°**, must it be a square?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> Four equal sides makes it a **rhombus**, and every rhombus is a parallelogram — so its **opposite angles are equal** and **adjacent angles add to 180°**.
>
> One angle is 90° → its opposite is 90° → each adjacent is `180 − 90 = 90°`. All four are 90°, so it's a square.

---

<!-- ===== Parallelograms ===== -->

Define a parallelogram, and show a rectangle is one.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A quadrilateral whose opposite sides are parallel.**
>
> A rectangle's opposite sides *are* parallel (proved from the 180° co-interior rule), so **every rectangle is a parallelogram** — specifically, one whose angles all happen to be 90°.

---

In a parallelogram, why do **adjacent** angles add to 180°?

> [!ANSWER]
> `AB || CD`, and AD cuts across both — it is a **transversal**.
>
> `∠A` and `∠D` are **co-interior** angles on the same side of the transversal, and those always add to 180° when the lines are parallel.

---

Prove that the **opposite angles** of a parallelogram are equal.

> [!ANSWER]
> Let `∠P = x`.
>
> - `∠R = 180 − x` (adjacent to ∠P).
> - `∠A = 180 − ∠R = 180 − (180 − x) =` **x**.
>
> So `∠P = ∠A`. The two subtractions **undo each other**. Likewise `∠R = ∠E = 180 − x`.

---

One angle of a parallelogram is 65°. Find the other three.

> [!ANSWER]
> Adjacent angles = `180 − 65 =` **115°**; opposite angles are equal.
>
> The four angles are **65°, 115°, 65°, 115°**.

---

Prove that the **opposite sides** of a parallelogram are equal.

> [!ANSWER]
> In ∆ABD and ∆CDB:
>
> - opposite angles of the parallelogram are equal,
> - the **alternate angles** across the transversal BD are equal,
> - **BD is common**.
>
> → **AAS** → `∆ABD ≅ ∆CDB` → `AD = CB` and `AB = CD`. ✓

---

Prove that the diagonals of a parallelogram **bisect each other**.

> [!ANSWER]
> In parallelogram EASY with diagonals meeting at O, compare ∆AOE and ∆YOS:
>
> - `AE = YS` (opposite sides equal),
> - the alternate angles at A and Y are equal,
> - the alternate angles at E and S are equal.
>
> → **ASA** → congruent → `OA = OY` and `OE = OS`, so **O is the midpoint of both diagonals**. ✓

---

Are the diagonals of a parallelogram equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — that's the rectangle's private property.**
>
> Squash a rectangle sideways: one diagonal stretches while the other shrinks. Only when the angles come back to 90° do they match again.

---

Construct a parallelogram whose diagonals are 7 cm and 5 cm, crossing at 140°.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw AB = 7 cm, mark midpoint O.
> 2. Draw a line through O at 140° to AB.
> 3. Cut arcs of **2.5 cm** (half of 5) on both sides of O — call them C and D.
> 4. Join A–C–B–D.
>
> Diagonals bisecting each other is exactly what makes it a parallelogram; the unequal lengths keep it from being a rectangle.

---

<!-- ===== Rhombus ===== -->

Define a rhombus, and prove that every rhombus is a parallelogram.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A quadrilateral with all four sides equal.**
>
> In rhombus GAME, draw diagonal AE. ∆GAE is isosceles (GE = GA) so `a = d`; ∆MAE is isosceles so `b = c`; and `∆GAE ≅ ∆MAE` by **SSS** gives `a = b`. So **all four angles round the diagonal are equal**.
>
> Those equal angles are **alternate angles**, so `EM || GA` and `GE || AM`. **Both pairs of opposite sides are parallel ⇒ parallelogram.** ✓

---

Rhombus ABCD has `∠A = 50°`. Find all four angles, and the angle the diagonal makes with a side.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Angles:** since it's a parallelogram, `∠C = ∠A =` **50°** and `∠B = ∠D = 180 − 50 =` **130°**.
>
> **Diagonal angle:** in ∆ADB, `a + a + 50 = 180` → **a = 65°** — half of 130°, confirming the diagonal bisects the corner.

---

Prove that the diagonals of a rhombus meet at **90°**.

> [!ANSWER]
> In rhombus GAME with diagonals meeting at O, compare ∆GEO and ∆MEO:
>
> - `GE = ME` (all sides equal),
> - `GO = MO` (diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other),
> - `EO` is common.
>
> → **SSS** → `∠GOE = ∠MOE`. They lie on a straight line, so they add to 180° → **90° each**. ✓

---

The diagonals of a rhombus are 4 cm and 5 cm. Construct it.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw AC = 5 cm and construct its **perpendicular bisector** through midpoint O.
> 2. Mark B and D on that perpendicular with `OB = OD = 2 cm`.
> 3. Join A–B–C–D.
>
> **Why it works:** bisecting each other at 90° forces all four sides equal (each is the hypotenuse of a 2.5–2 right triangle).

---

What is the difference between a rhombus and a square?

> [!ANSWER]
> Both have **four equal sides**. A square adds **90° corners**.
>
> So a **square is a rhombus that is also a rectangle** — it sits in the overlap of the two families.

---

<!-- ===== Kite ===== -->

Define a kite. How is it different from a parallelogram?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A kite is ABCD with `AB = BC` and `CD = DA`** — the equal sides are **adjacent** (next to each other).
>
> In a parallelogram the equal sides are **opposite**. That single change of which pair matches gives a completely different shape.

---

In kite ABCD (`AB = BC`, `CD = DA`), prove that BD bisects `∠ABC` and cuts AC at right angles.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Step 1:** `∆ABD ≅ ∆CBD` by **SSS** (`AB = CB`, `AD = CD`, `BD` common). So `∠ABD = ∠CBD` — **BD bisects ∠ABC** (and `∠ADC` too).
>
> **Step 2:** now `∆AOB ≅ ∆COB` by **SAS** (`AB = CB`, the bisected angles at B, `OB` common). So `AO = OC`, and `∠AOB = ∠COB` on a straight line → **90° each**. ✓

---

Is every rhombus a kite? Is every kite a rhombus?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every rhombus is a kite** — with all four sides equal, `AB = BC` and `CD = DA` are automatically true.
>
> **Not every kite is a rhombus** — a kite with sides 6, 6, 9, 9 has two different lengths.
>
> That inheritance is why a rhombus's diagonals meet at 90° — it's a kite property.

---

Can a quadrilateral be both a **kite** and a **rectangle**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — but only if it is a square.**
>
> Kite gives `AB = BC` and `CD = DA`; rectangle gives `AB = CD` and `BC = DA`. Chain them: `AB = BC = CD = DA` — all four sides equal, with 90° angles. That's a **square**.

---

Which quadrilateral is both a **kite** and a **parallelogram**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The rhombus.**
>
> Parallelogram gives opposite sides equal; kite gives adjacent sides equal. Together, all four sides are equal.

---

Construct a kite whose diagonals are 6 cm and 8 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> 1. Draw the **long** diagonal BD = 8 cm.
> 2. Construct a perpendicular to BD at **any** point O on it (not the midpoint — that would give a rhombus).
> 3. Mark A and C on that perpendicular with `OA = OC = 3 cm`.
> 4. Join A–B–C–D.
>
> **Why:** one diagonal bisecting the other at right angles is exactly the kite property proved above.

---

<!-- ===== Trapezium ===== -->

Define a trapezium, and state its angle property.

> [!ANSWER]
> **A quadrilateral with at least one pair of parallel opposite sides.**
>
> If `PQ || SR`, then `∠S + ∠P = 180°` and `∠R + ∠Q = 180°` — each slanted side is a **transversal**, and its co-interior angles sum to 180°.

---

In a trapezium with the parallel sides horizontal, two base angles are 135° and 105°. Find the other two.

> [!ANSWER]
> Each pairs with the angle directly above it, summing to 180°:
>
> `180 − 135 =` **45°** and `180 − 105 =` **75°**.
>
> Check: `135 + 105 + 45 + 75 = 360` ✓

---

A trapezium has one angle of 100°. What is the angle at the other end of the same slanted side?

> [!ANSWER]
> `180 − 100 =` **80°** — they are co-interior angles between the two parallel sides.

---

What is an **isosceles trapezium**, and why are its base angles equal?

> [!ANSWER]
> One whose two **non-parallel** sides are equal.
>
> **Why the base angles match:** drop perpendiculars XY and WZ from the top corners to the base. XWZY has right angles at Y and Z, and since `XW || UV` the angles at X and W are also 90° — so **XWZY is a rectangle**, giving `XY = WZ` (equal heights).
>
> Now `∆UXY ≅ ∆VWZ` by **RHS** (equal hypotenuses UX = VW, equal heights) → **∠U = ∠V**. ✓

---

Is an isosceles trapezium a parallelogram?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Only **one** pair of its sides is parallel; the other pair is equal but slanting towards each other. A parallelogram needs **both** pairs parallel.

---

<!-- ===== Two triangles → a quadrilateral ===== -->

You have two identical cardboard triangles. What are the only two ways to join them along a matching side, and what do you get each time?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Turn one 180°** about the midpoint of the shared side → always a **parallelogram**, whose side lengths are the triangle's **other two sides**.
> - **Flip one over** (mirror it across the shared side) → always a **kite**, made of two adjacent equal pairs.

---

Two equilateral triangles of side 4 cm are joined along a side. Find all sides and angles of the quadrilateral.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **rhombus** with all sides **4 cm** and angles **60°, 120°, 60°, 120°**.
>
> **Why:** each 60° corner of the triangles stays a corner of the rhombus at one end, while at the other end **two** 60° corners meet to make 120°.

---

Two isosceles triangles with sides 8 cm, 8 cm, 6 cm are joined. What quadrilaterals are possible?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Joined along the **6 cm** side → the other two sides are 8 and 8 → a **rhombus** of side 8 cm.
> - Joined along an **8 cm** side and **turned** → a **parallelogram** with sides 8 cm and 6 cm.
> - Joined along an **8 cm** side and **flipped** → a **kite** with sides 8, 6, 6, 8.

---

Two scalene triangles with sides 6, 9 and 12 cm are joined along the 12 cm side, mirror-fashion. Name the shape and its sides.

> [!ANSWER]
> A **kite** with sides **6, 6, 9, 9**.
>
> **Why a kite and not a parallelogram:** the mirror image puts 6 next to 6 and 9 next to 9 — **adjacent** equal pairs. The 12 cm join becomes the kite's **diagonal**, not a side.

<!-- ===== The family tree ===== -->

Draw (or describe) a Venn diagram containing parallelograms, rectangles, rhombuses and squares.

> [!ANSWER]
> A big **Parallelogram** oval containing two overlapping ovals, **Rectangle** and **Rhombus**. Their overlap is **Square**.
>
> **Why:** a square has 90° angles (so it's a rectangle) *and* four equal sides (so it's a rhombus).

---

Where do **kites** and **trapeziums** fit into that diagram?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Trapezium** is the biggest oval — it contains all parallelograms, since "both pairs parallel" implies "at least one pair parallel".
> - **Kite** is a separate oval that overlaps the parallelogram oval **only at the rhombus** (and hence the square).

<!-- ===== True or false drill ===== -->

**True or false:** a quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other must be a square.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.** That's a **rectangle**. To force a square you also need the diagonals to cross at **90°**.

---

**True or false:** a quadrilateral with three right angles must be a rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.** The 360° sum forces the fourth angle to be `360 − 270 =` 90°, and "all angles 90°" is the definition of a rectangle.

---

**True or false:** a quadrilateral whose diagonals bisect each other must be a parallelogram.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.** With `OA = OC`, `OB = OD` and vertically opposite angles at O, the two pairs of opposite triangles are congruent by **SAS** — giving both pairs of opposite sides equal, hence parallel.

---

**True or false:** a quadrilateral whose diagonals are perpendicular must be a rhombus.

> [!ANSWER]
> **False.** A **kite** also has perpendicular diagonals but unequal sides.
>
> You need perpendicular **and** bisecting each other for a rhombus.

---

**True or false:** a quadrilateral in which the opposite angles are equal must be a parallelogram.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.** Let the angles be x, y, x, y. Then `2x + 2y = 360`, so `x + y = 180`.
>
> Adjacent angles adding to 180° are co-interior angles ⇒ **each pair of opposite sides is parallel**. ✓

---

**True or false:** a quadrilateral in which all four angles are equal is a rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.** Four equal angles adding to 360° must be `360 ÷ 4 =` **90°** each.

---

What kind of quadrilateral has its **opposite sides equal** (nothing else assumed)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **A parallelogram.**
>
> **Why:** draw a diagonal. The two triangles have three pairs of equal sides (the two pairs of opposite sides, plus the common diagonal) → congruent by **SSS**. The matching alternate angles are then equal, which forces both pairs of sides to be **parallel**.

---

Two rectangles are drawn sharing the same corner point R. One is tilted **30°** away from the other. What is the angle between their two nearest sides at R, and why doesn't the rectangles' shape matter?

> [!ANSWER]
> **30°** — the same as the tilt.
>
> **Why the shape is irrelevant:** every rectangle has a **90° corner**, so at R both shapes contribute exactly 90°. Rotating one rectangle by 30° rotates *all* of its sides by 30°, so the gap between corresponding sides is always the rotation angle itself. The lengths never enter the calculation.

---

Take a quadrilateral that is **concave** — one corner pushed inwards, like an arrowhead. Do its angles still add to 360°?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> **Why:** the diagonal proof still works — you just have to pick the diagonal that stays **inside** the figure. It still splits the shape into two triangles, giving `180 + 180 = 360°`.
>
> (The reflex angle at the dented corner is the one you must measure, and it can be more than 180°.)
