<!-- ===== Counting ===== -->

Give three reasons Stone Age people needed to count.

> [!ANSWER]
> To know **how much food** they had, **how many animals** in their livestock, and to record **details of trade**.
>
> Also for **offerings in rituals**, and to **track passing days** — predicting the new moon, full moon or the onset of a season.

---

What is a **one-to-one mapping**, and how is it used to count?

> [!ANSWER]
> Associating **each object with one item** of a standard sequence, so that **no two objects map to the same item**.
>
> Keep one stick per cow, and the final collection of sticks tells you the number of cows — and reveals at once if one has gone missing.

---

What does any **number system** need in order to be usable for counting?

> [!ANSWER]
> A **standard sequence with a fixed order** — of objects, names or written symbols.
>
> Counting is then just making a one-to-one mapping between the collection and that sequence, following its order.

---

Compare counting with **sticks** and counting with the **letters a–z**. What does each get right and wrong?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Sticks:** the sequence is **unending** ✓ — but you need **as many sticks as objects**, which is hopeless for large collections ✗.
>
> **Letters a–z:** **convenient** to say and use ✓ — but the sequence **runs out at 26** ✗.
>
> **The challenge is to get both at once:** an unending sequence that is also easy to count with.

---

What are **numerals**?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **symbols occurring in a written number system**.
>
> 0, 1, 5, 36 and 193 are numerals of the Hindu number system.

---

<!-- ===== Early systems ===== -->

What are the **Lebombo** and **Ishango** bones?

> [!ANSWER]
> Ancient bones marked with **tally notches** — among the oldest known mathematical artefacts.
>
> The **Lebombo bone** (South Africa) has **29 notches** and is about **44,000 years old**, possibly a tally stick or **lunar calendar**. The **Ishango bone** (D.R. Congo) is 20,000–35,000 years old, with notches in columns possibly indicating a calendrical system.

---

The Gumulgal say *ukasar-ukasar-urapon* for 5. Work out their system.

> [!ANSWER]
> *urapon* = 1, *ukasar* = 2. So the names are built by **counting in twos**:
>
> `3 = 2+1` (ukasar-urapon) · `4 = 2+2` · `5 = 2+2+1` · `6 = 2+2+2`.
>
> Anything greater than 6 was simply called *ras*.

---

Three widely separated peoples developed the same counting-in-twos system. Why is this puzzling, and what is one explanation?

> [!ANSWER]
> The **Gumulgal** (Australia), **Bakairi** (South America) and **Bushmen** (South Africa) are **geographically far apart with no trace of contact** — yet their systems are equivalent.
>
> **One theory:** they had **common ancestors** who used this system, whose descendants later migrated to these places.

---

What limit of human perception probably led people to start **grouping**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Most humans cannot see the size of a collection of 5 or more at a single glance.**
>
> That limit likely prompted people using tally marks to **replace each group of about 5 marks with a new symbol** — the first step towards efficient number representation.

---

<!-- ===== Roman ===== -->

What are **landmark numbers**? List the Roman ones.

> [!ANSWER]
> The numbers given their **own basic symbol**.
>
> **I = 1, V = 5, X = 10, L = 50, C = 100, D = 500, M = 1000.**

---

Write **27** and **2367** in Roman numerals, showing the grouping.

> [!ANSWER]
> `27 = 10 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1` → **XXVII**
>
> `2367 = 1000+1000+100+100+100+50+10+5+1+1` → **MMCCCLXVII**
>
> **Method:** take as many of the largest landmark number as possible, then the next, and so on.

---

Why is the Roman system a real improvement over tally marks?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because it groups a number by **a whole sequence of landmark numbers**, not just one group size.
>
> That makes representation vastly more compact — MMCCCLXVII instead of 2367 scratches.

---

**Why is multiplication so hard in Roman numerals?**

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **the landmark numbers do not step up by a constant factor**: 1→5 is ×5, 5→10 is ×2, 10→50 is ×5, 50→100 is ×2.
>
> So the product of two landmark numbers is often **not a landmark number at all** (`V × L = 250`, which has no symbol), and every regrouping has to be handled as a special case.
>
> This is why users depended on an **abacus** — and only specially trained people could operate it.

---

<!-- ===== Base ===== -->

Describe the **Egyptian** system's landmark numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Each landmark number is 10 times the previous one**, starting at 1 — so they are all **powers of 10**: 1, 10, 100, 1000, …
>
> Each has its own symbol, and a number is written by grouping into as many of the largest as possible.

---

Define a **base-n** number system.

> [!ANSWER]
> A system where **(a)** the **first landmark number is 1**, and **(b)** every next landmark number is the current one **multiplied by a fixed number n**.
>
> Its landmark numbers are the **powers of n**: `1, n, n², n³, …`

---

What are the landmark numbers of a **base-5** system? Write **143** in it.

> [!ANSWER]
> `1, 5, 25, 125, 625, 3125, …` (powers of 5).
>
> `143 = 125 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1` — one 125, three 5s and three 1s.

---

What are the landmark numbers of a **base-7** system?

> [!ANSWER]
> `1, 7, 49, 343, 2401, …` — the **powers of 7**.

---

**Why does having a base make multiplication easy?**

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **in a base-n system, the product of any two landmark numbers is another landmark number**.
>
> **Why:** every landmark is a power of n, and `nᵃ × nᵇ = nᵃ⁺ᵇ` — still a power of n, so still a landmark.
>
> That single property is what the Roman system lacks, and it's the whole reason Roman multiplication is painful.

---

Why is **addition** also easier in a base system than in Roman numerals?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because you always regroup by the **same factor**. In base 10, **ten of any symbol always make one of the next** — every single time.
>
> In the Roman system the regrouping factor **changes** (five Is make a V, but two Vs make an X), so each step needs separate care.

---

Can any number be represented in the base-5 system? Why?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** The landmark numbers `1, 5, 25, 125, …` **grow without limit**, so for any number there is always a largest landmark below it to start grouping from — and the process always terminates.

---

<!-- ===== Shortcoming ===== -->

What was the Egyptian system's **remaining shortcoming**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It needed a brand-new symbol for every higher power of 10.**
>
> To write ever-larger numbers you would need an **unending sequence of symbols** — which is the **original problem of number representation resurfacing** in a new form.

---

Can an Egyptian numeral contain the **same symbol 10 or more times**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** Ten of any symbol would immediately be **regrouped into one symbol of the next landmark** — that's what base 10 means.
>
> So each symbol can appear at most **nine** times.

---

<!-- ===== Place value ===== -->

What base did the **Mesopotamians** use, and where does it survive today?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Base 60** — the **sexagesimal** (Babylonian) system.
>
> It survives in our **units of time**: `1 hour = 60 minutes` and `1 minute = 60 seconds`.

---

Give two theories for why they chose 60.

> [!ANSWER]
> Possible links to **periods of important events** (their lunar month of 30 days, or the Sun's apparent revolution), and the **ease of representing fractions**.
>
> Another is that their **earlier landmark sequence** (1, 10, 60, 600, 3600, 36000…) got **reduced to just the powers of 60**.

---

**How does place value solve the Egyptian problem?**

> [!ANSWER]
> Instead of inventing a new symbol for each power, **the POSITION of a digit tells you which power it stands for**.
>
> So **ten digits suffice for every number, however large** — no unending sequence of symbols is ever needed. That is the final breakthrough in the story.

---

Where does the first known written **zero** appear, and in what form?

> [!ANSWER]
> In the **Bakhshali manuscript** (c. **3rd century CE**), India — the first known instance of numbers written with ten digits **including 0**, which was then notated as a **dot**.

---

What did the **Yajurveda Samhita** contribute?

> [!ANSWER]
> It named numbers **by powers of 10, almost exactly as we say them orally today** — *eka* (1), *dasha* (10), *shata* (100), *sahasra* (1000), *āyuta* (10,000) — continuing up to **10¹² and beyond**.

---

Who was **Aryabhata**, and what was his role?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(c. 499 CE)** — the **first mathematician to fully explain the Indian system of 10 symbols** and to carry out **elaborate scientific computations** with it.

---

Trace how the Indian numerals reached Europe.

> [!ANSWER]
> **To the Arab world by ~800 CE**, popularised by **Al-Khwārizmī** in *On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals* (c. 825) and **Al-Kindi** (c. 830).
>
> **To Europe and parts of Africa by ~1100 CE.** The Italian mathematician **Fibonacci** (c. 1200) argued for adopting them, but **Roman numerals were so ingrained** that widespread use took several more centuries — by the **17th century** not adopting them would have impeded scientific progress.

---

Why are these numerals often mistakenly called **"Arabic numerals"**?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **European scholars learned them from the Arab world**, and named them from their own perspective. **Arab scholars themselves called them "Hindu numerals."**
>
> The European term spread during colonisation; today the accurate terms are **Hindu numerals, Indian numerals** or **Hindu-Arabic numerals**.
>
> Note that **"Hindu" here refers to a geography and people, not a religion**.

---

Which word do we get from **Al-Khwārizmī's** name?

> [!ANSWER]
> **"Algorithm."**

---

Laplace said the significance of the Indian system "is no longer appreciated." What did he mean?

> [!ANSWER]
> That the system of **ten symbols, each having both a place value and an absolute value**, is now **so familiar it seems obvious** — so we forget it was an **invention** at all.
>
> It took **thousands of years** and many partial systems (tallies, Roman, Egyptian, Mesopotamian) to arrive at, and its simplicity is exactly what **placed arithmetic foremost among useful inventions**.

---

Summarise the **four big ideas** in the evolution of number systems.

> [!ANSWER]
> **1. One-to-one mapping** to a standard sequence — the basis of counting itself.
>
> **2. Grouping**, prompted by our inability to see 5+ objects at a glance.
>
> **3. Landmark numbers** (Roman) — grouping by a whole sequence of sizes, not one.
>
> **4. A base** (Egyptian) — landmark numbers as **powers** of one number, making arithmetic tractable — and finally **place value with zero** (India), which removes the need for endless new symbols.
