<!-- ===== Doubling and halving ===== -->

To make a square of double the area, why can't you just double each side?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because doubling the side **quadruples** the area: `2s × 2s = 4s²`.
>
> **Area grows as the SQUARE of the side**, so to double the area you must multiply the side by **√2**, not 2.

---

State Baudhāyana's rule for doubling a square and explain why it works.

> [!ANSWER]
> *"The diagonal of a square produces a square of double the area of the original square."* (*Śulba-Sūtra* 1.9)
>
> **Why:** draw the tilted square on the diagonal and add horizontal and vertical lines. The **original** square is made of **2** congruent right triangles; the **new** square is made of **4** of the same triangles.
>
> `4 ÷ 2 =` **2 times the area** ✓

---

Describe the paper method for doubling a square.

> [!ANSWER]
> Cut **two identical squares**. Leave the first whole. Cut the second along **both diagonals** into 4 triangles.
>
> Fit those 4 triangles around the first square, one on each side, and the result is a tilted square of **double the area** — its side is the diagonal of the original.

---

How do you construct a square with **half** the area of a given square?

> [!ANSWER]
> Join the **midpoints** of the four sides. The tilted square PQRS inside has exactly half the area.
>
> **Why:** the four corner triangles cut off are congruent (SAS — two half-sides with the right angle between), and they exactly fill PQRS when rearranged. So the corners together equal the inner square.

---

Will a square with half the side length have half the area? How many such squares fill the original?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No — it has a QUARTER of the area**, since `(s/2)² = s²/4`.
>
> **Four** of them fill the original.
>
> This is the same trap as doubling: **area scales as the square of the length**.

<!-- ===== Isosceles right triangles ===== -->

Find the hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle with legs of length 1.

> [!ANSWER]
> Two such triangles make a unit square (area 1), so the square built on the hypotenuse has area **2**.
>
> If the hypotenuse is c, then `c² = 2`, so `c =` **√2**.

---

State the general formula linking the legs and the hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle, and say why it's true.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`c² = 2a²`, i.e. `c = a√2`.**
>
> **Why:** two copies of the triangle make a square of area `a²`, and the square on the hypotenuse (the diagonal) has **twice** that area, so `c² = 2a²`.

---

An isosceles right triangle has legs 12. Find bounds on the hypotenuse.

> [!ANSWER]
> `c² = 2 × 144 = 288`, so `c = √288`.
>
> `16² = 256` and `17² = 289`, so the hypotenuse lies **between 16 and 17** — very close to 17.

---

Find the hypotenuse of isosceles right triangles with legs 3, 4, 6 and 9, giving bounds to one decimal place.

> [!ANSWER]
> - legs 3 → `√18`; `4.2² = 17.64`, `4.3² = 18.49` → **4.2 to 4.3**
> - legs 4 → `√32`; `5.6² = 31.36`, `5.7² = 32.49` → **5.6 to 5.7**
> - legs 6 → `√72`; `8.4² = 70.56`, `8.5² = 72.25` → **8.4 to 8.5**
> - legs 9 → `√162`; `12.7² = 161.29`, `12.8² = 163.84` → **12.7 to 12.8**

---

The hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle is √72. Find the other two sides.

> [!ANSWER]
> `c² = 2a²` → `72 = 2a²` → `a² = 36` → **a = 6**.
>
> Both equal sides are **6**.

---

The hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle is 10. Find the legs.

> [!ANSWER]
> `100 = 2a²` → `a² = 50` → `a = √50`.
>
> Since `7² = 49` and `8² = 64`, each leg is a bit over **7** (about 7.07).

<!-- ===== √2 ===== -->

Show that √2 lies between 1 and 2, and then between 1.4 and 1.5.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `1² = 1 < 2` and `2² = 4 > 2`, so **1 `<` √2 `<` 2**.
> - `1.4² = 1.96 < 2` and `1.5² = 2.25 > 2`, so **1.4 `<` √2 `<` 1.5**.
>
> **The method:** square your candidates and see which side of 2 they fall on. Each round of squeezing gives one more decimal place.

---

Narrow √2 down to three decimal places.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `1.41² = 1.9881`, `1.42² = 2.0164` → **1.41 to 1.42**
> - `1.414² = 1.999396`, `1.415² = 2.002225` → **1.414 `<` √2 `<` 1.415**

---

Prove that √2 cannot be a **terminating** decimal.

> [!ANSWER]
> Suppose it were, say `1.414…d` with a **non-zero last digit** d.
>
> Squaring it gives a decimal whose **last digit is the last digit of `d × d`** — and `d × d` never ends in 0 for `d = 1…9`. So the square would have a **non-zero digit** at its end.
>
> But `2 = 2.000…` has only zeros after the point. **Contradiction** — so no terminating decimal squares to 2.

---

Prove that √2 cannot be written as a fraction m/n.

> [!ANSWER]
> Suppose `√2 = m/n`. Then `2 = m²/n²`, so **`2n² = m²`**.
>
> **In the prime factorisation of any square, every prime appears an EVEN number of times.**
>
> - On the right, `m²` has an **even** number of 2s.
> - On the left, `2n²` has an even number of 2s (from n²) **plus one more** — an **odd** number.
>
> A number can't have both. **Impossible**, so √2 is not a fraction. (This proof is Euclid's, *Elements*, c. 300 BCE.)

---

What kind of number is √2, then?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Irrational** — neither a terminating decimal nor a ratio of whole numbers.
>
> Its decimal goes on forever without repeating: `√2 = 1.41421356…`

<!-- ===== The theorem ===== -->

State Baudhāyana's rule for combining two different squares into one.

> [!ANSWER]
> *"The area of the square produced by the diagonal is the sum of the areas of the squares produced by the two sides."* (*Śulba-Sūtra* 1.12)
>
> **In practice:** make a right-angled triangle whose two perpendicular sides are the sides of the two squares. The square on the **hypotenuse** has area equal to their sum.

---

Sketch Baudhāyana's construction showing why the new four-sided figure really is a square.

> [!ANSWER]
> Put the two squares (sides a and b) side by side. Inside the bigger one, mark an `a × b` rectangle and draw its **diagonal** — a right triangle with legs a and b. Build four congruent copies of that triangle around the diagonal.
>
> - **All four sides equal** — each is a hypotenuse of a congruent triangle.
> - **All four angles are 90°** — at each corner the acute angles `x` and `90 − x` flank the joint, leaving `180 − x − (90 − x) =` **90°**.
>
> Equal sides + right angles = **square**, and its pieces are exactly the two original squares rearranged. ✓

---

State the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem, and say who stated it first.

> [!ANSWER]
> **In a right-angled triangle with legs a, b and hypotenuse c: `a² + b² = c²`.**
>
> **Baudhāyana** stated it in full generality around **800 BCE** — the first person in history to do so. **Pythagoras** (c. 500 BCE) came about 300 years later, which is why both names are used.

---

A right triangle has legs 5 cm and 12 cm. Find the hypotenuse.

> [!ANSWER]
> `25 + 144 = 169 = c²` → **c = 13 cm**.

---

A right triangle has one leg 8 cm and hypotenuse 17 cm. Find the third side.

> [!ANSWER]
> `b² = 17² − 8² = 289 − 64 = 225` → **b = 15 cm**.
>
> **Note you SUBTRACT here** — the hypotenuse is the biggest side, so finding a leg must shrink the number.

---

When do you add and when do you subtract in `a² + b² = c²`?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Finding the hypotenuse:** `c = √(a² + b²)` — **add**.
> - **Finding a leg:** `a = √(c² − b²)` — **subtract**.
>
> **The check:** the hypotenuse is always the longest side. If your answer for a leg comes out bigger than the hypotenuse, you added when you should have subtracted.

---

Find the missing side: (i) a = 5, b = 7; (ii) a = 8, b = 12; (iii) a = 9, c = 15; (iv) a = 1.5, b = 3.5.

> [!ANSWER]
> - (i) `c = √(25 + 49) = √74 ≈` **8.6**
> - (ii) `c = √(64 + 144) = √208 ≈` **14.4**
> - (iii) `b = √(225 − 81) = √144 =` **12** (a scaled (3,4,5)!)
> - (iv) `c = √(2.25 + 12.25) = √14.5 ≈` **3.81**

---

Is the hypotenuse always the longest side? Justify.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> `c² = a² + b²`, and both `a²` and `b²` are positive. So `c² > a²` and `c² > b²`, which means **c is bigger than both legs**.

<!-- ===== Triples ===== -->

What is a Baudhāyana triple? List those Baudhāyana himself recorded.

> [!ANSWER]
> A triple of **whole numbers** `(a, b, c)` with `a² + b² = c²` — the side lengths of a right triangle.
>
> Baudhāyana's list (*Śulba-Sūtra* 1.13): **(3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25), (12, 35, 37), (15, 36, 39)**.

---

Prove that if `(a, b, c)` is a triple, so is `(ka, kb, kc)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(ka)² + (kb)² = k²a² + k²b² = k²(a² + b²) = k²c² = (kc)²` ✓
>
> The `k²` factors out of everything. So **every triple generates infinitely many** by scaling.

---

Is (30, 40, 50) a triple? Is (300, 400, 500)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Both yes** — they are (3, 4, 5) scaled by 10 and 100.
>
> Check: `900 + 1600 = 2500 = 50²` ✓

---

List all Baudhāyana triples whose numbers are all ≤ 20. Which is primitive?

> [!ANSWER]
> **(3, 4, 5), (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20).**
>
> Only **(3, 4, 5)** is primitive — the other three all share a common factor (2, 3 and 4 respectively).

---

What makes a triple **primitive**? Is (5, 12, 13) primitive? Is (9, 12, 15)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Primitive** = no common factor greater than 1 among all three numbers.
>
> - **(5, 12, 13)** — primitive ✓ (5 is prime and doesn't divide 12)
> - **(9, 12, 15)** — **not** primitive; all divisible by 3, giving (3, 4, 5)

---

If `(a, b, c)` is a triple with common factor f, is `(a/f, b/f, c/f)` also a triple? Check with (9, 12, 15).

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** `a² + b² = c²` divided through by `f²` gives `(a/f)² + (b/f)² = (c/f)²`.
>
> (9, 12, 15) ÷ 3 = **(3, 4, 5)**, and `9 + 16 = 25` ✓

---

Starting from the identity `(n − 1)² + (2n − 1) = n²`, explain how to generate triples.

> [!ANSWER]
> The identity says **the (n−1)th square plus the nth odd number equals the nth square** — it's just the "sum of odd numbers" fact, split off at the last term.
>
> **Choose n so that `2n − 1` is itself a perfect square.** Then all three terms are squares and you have a triple.
>
> `2n − 1 = 9` → `n = 5` → `4² + 3² = 5²` ✓

---

Generate five triples with this method.

> [!ANSWER]
> | Odd square `2n−1` | n | Triple |
> |---|---|---|
> | 9 | 5 | **(3, 4, 5)** |
> | 25 | 13 | **(5, 12, 13)** |
> | 49 | 25 | **(7, 24, 25)** |
> | 81 | 41 | **(9, 40, 41)** |
> | 121 | 61 | **(11, 60, 61)** |
>
> Check the last: `121 + 3600 = 3721 = 61²` ✓

---

Does this method ever produce a **non-primitive** triple?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> **Why:** in every triple it makes, the **larger leg is exactly 1 less than the hypotenuse** (4 and 5; 12 and 13; 24 and 25; 40 and 41). Two consecutive whole numbers share no common factor greater than 1, so the whole triple can't either.

---

Are there primitive triples this method **misses**? Give an example.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes — (8, 15, 17).**
>
> It is primitive (`64 + 225 = 289` ✓, no common factor), but here the hypotenuse exceeds the larger leg by **2** (17 − 15), not 1.
>
> The odd-square method **always** leaves a gap of exactly 1, so it can never reach this triple. (Same for (20, 21, 29).)

<!-- ===== Fermat ===== -->

What did Fermat claim, and what happened to his proof?

> [!ANSWER]
> That **`xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ` has no solution in positive integers when n `>` 2** — no cube is the sum of two cubes, no fourth power the sum of two fourth powers, and so on.
>
> He wrote in a book margin: *"I have found a truly marvellous proof of this statement, but the margin is too small to contain it."* **Nobody ever found that proof.**

---

Who finally proved Fermat's Last Theorem, and when?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Andrew Wiles, in 1994** — over **300 years** after Fermat's note.
>
> He first read about the problem at **age 10**, in 1963, and resolved then to solve it.

---

Why is the case n = 2 so different from n = 3 and beyond?

> [!ANSWER]
> For **n = 2** there are **infinitely many** whole-number solutions — every Baudhāyana triple and every scaling of one.
>
> For **n `>` 2** there are **none at all**. That sharp break is what makes the theorem remarkable.

<!-- ===== Applications ===== -->

Solve Bhāskarāchārya's lotus problem: a lotus stands 1 unit above the water; a breeze pushes its tip to touch the water 3 units away. How deep is the lake?

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the underwater stem be **x**, so the whole stem is `x + 1`. Bent over, the stem is the **hypotenuse** of a right triangle with legs 3 and x.
>
> ```
> 9 + x² = (x + 1)²
> 9 + x² = x² + 2x + 1
>      9 = 2x + 1
>      x = 4
> ```
>
> **The lake is 4 units deep.** The `x²` cancels from both sides — which is why what looks like too little information is enough.

---

Find the diagonal of a square with side 5 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> `d² = 25 + 25 = 50` → `d = √50 ≈` **7.07 cm**.
>
> (Generally the diagonal of a square of side s is **s√2**.)

---

Find the side of a rhombus whose diagonals are 24 and 70 units.

> [!ANSWER]
> The diagonals **bisect each other at right angles**, so each side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs `24/2 = 12` and `70/2 = 35`.
>
> `side = √(144 + 1225) = √1369 =` **37 units**.

---

Give five rectangles whose sides **and** diagonals are all whole numbers.

> [!ANSWER]
> Any Baudhāyana triple gives one — the legs are the sides and the hypotenuse is the diagonal:
>
> **3×4** (diagonal 5) · **5×12** (13) · **8×15** (17) · **7×24** (25) · **20×21** (29)
>
> Scalings work too: **6×8** (10), **9×12** (15).

---

Find the area of an equilateral triangle of side 6 units.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **altitude bisects the base** (the two halves make congruent right triangles by RHS), so it splits the triangle into two right triangles with hypotenuse 6 and one leg 3.
>
> `h = √(36 − 9) = √27 ≈ 5.196`
>
> `Area = ½ × 6 × 5.196 ≈` **15.59 sq units**.
>
> (General formula: `(√3/4)s²`.)

---

How would you construct a square of **three times** the area of a given square? Five times?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Triple:** first double it (square of side `a√2`, area `2a²`). Then make a right triangle with legs `a√2` and `a` — its hypotenuse gives area `2a² + a² =` **3a²** ✓
> - **Five times:** a right triangle with legs `a` and `2a` gives `a² + 4a² =` **5a²** ✓
>
> This is Baudhāyana's *Śulba-Sūtra* Verse 1.10.

---

Construct a square whose area is the **difference** of squares of side 7 and side 5.

> [!ANSWER]
> Make a right triangle with **hypotenuse 7** and one **leg 5**. The other leg is `√(49 − 25) = √24`.
>
> The square on that leg has area `49 − 25 =` **24 sq units** ✓
>
> **The general trick:** for a sum use the two legs; for a **difference** use the hypotenuse and one leg.

---

On a dot grid, can you draw squares (vertices on dots) of area 2, 3, 4 and 5?

> [!ANSWER]
> - **2** ✓ — tilted square whose side is the hypotenuse of a 1–1 right triangle
> - **3** ✗ — **impossible**; no two whole squares add to 3
> - **4** ✓ — an ordinary 2 × 2 square
> - **5** ✓ — tilted square on a 2–1 right triangle (`4 + 1 = 5`)

---

On an infinite dot grid, which whole-number areas are achievable?

> [!ANSWER]
> Exactly those expressible as **`p² + q²`** with p, q whole numbers — because the tilted square's side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with whole-number legs p and q.
>
> So: **1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 13, 16, 17, 18, 20, 25, …** — and never 3, 6, 7, 11, 12, 14, 15, 19.

---

**True or false:** every Baudhāyana triple is either primitive or a scaled version of a primitive triple.

> [!ANSWER]
> **True.**
>
> **Why:** take any triple and divide all three numbers by their HCF. What's left has no common factor — it's primitive — and the original is that primitive triple scaled by the HCF.
