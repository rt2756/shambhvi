<!-- ===== What area is ===== -->

What does "area" actually count, and why is a rectangle's area `length × width`?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Area counts the number of unit squares that fit inside a region** (fractions allowed).
>
> In a `7 × 4` rectangle you can lay 4 rows of 7 unit squares — `7 × 4 =` **28 unit squares**, so the area is **28 cm²**.

---

Which rectangle needs more rangoli powder: 7 cm × 4 cm or 8 cm × 3 cm?

> [!ANSWER]
> `7 × 4 = 28` versus `8 × 3 = 24`.
>
> **The 7 × 4 rectangle** — even though 8 is the biggest single side, its partner is too small.

---

Can two regions have the same perimeter but different areas?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, easily.** A `1 × 8` rectangle and a `3 × 6` rectangle both have perimeter **18**, but areas **8** and **18**.
>
> **Perimeter measures the boundary; area measures the inside.** They're independent.

---

Find two rectangles where the one with the **bigger** perimeter has the **smaller** area.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `1 × 10`: perimeter **22**, area **10**
> - `5 × 5`: perimeter **20**, area **25**
>
> **Bigger perimeter, smaller area** ✓
>
> **Why:** a long thin shape wastes boundary on a sliver of inside. A spiral tube makes this extreme — an enormous perimeter around almost nothing.

---

Why can't perimeter be used as a measure of area?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **regions with equal perimeter can have any area from nearly zero up to a maximum**, and the maximum is only reached by the "roundest" shape.
>
> Since perimeter doesn't determine area at all, area needs its own measurement — counting unit squares.

---

<!-- ===== Triangles ===== -->

State the triangle area formula and prove it for a triangle inside a rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`Area = ½ × base × height`**, where the height is the **perpendicular** distance from the base to the opposite vertex.
>
> **Why:** enclose the triangle in a rectangle of the same base and height. The triangle's two pieces each pair with a congruent piece of the rectangle, so the triangle is exactly **half** the rectangle: `½ × b × h`.

---

Does the formula still work when the foot of the height falls **outside** the base (an obtuse triangle)?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.** Write ∆ABC as the **difference** of two right-angled pieces:
>
> ```
> Area(ABC) = Area(ADC) − Area(ADB)
>           = ½h·DC − ½h·DB
>           = ½h(DC − DB)
>           = ½ × h × BC   ✓
> ```
>
> So `½ × base × height` holds for **every** triangle.

---

A 7 cm × 4 cm rectangle is cut along a diagonal. Find the area of each triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> The diagonal makes two **congruent** triangles, so each is half the rectangle:
>
> `½ × 7 × 4 =` **14 cm²**.

---

In ∆ABC, `AX = 3` is the height onto base `BC = 5`, and `AC = 4`. Find the altitude BY onto AC.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Area = ½ × 5 × 3 =` **7.5 sq units**.
>
> The **same area** with AC as base: `½ × 4 × BY = 7.5` → `2BY = 7.5` → **BY = 3.75 units**.
>
> **The trick: any side can be the base — compute the area once, then reuse it.**

---

In a triangle, `AX = 4` is the altitude to base `BC = 6`, and `AC = 8`. Find BY, the altitude to AC.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Area = ½ × 6 × 4 =` **12**.
>
> `½ × 8 × BY = 12` → **BY = 3 units**.

---

Find the areas: (i) base 4 cm, height 3 cm; (ii) base 3.2 cm, height 5 cm; (iii) base 3 cm, height 4 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> - (i) `½ × 4 × 3 =` **6 cm²**
> - (ii) `½ × 3.2 × 5 =` **8 cm²**
> - (iii) `½ × 3 × 4 =` **6 cm²**

---

∆SUB is isosceles with `SE ⊥ UB`, and the area of ∆SEB is 24 sq units. Find the area of ∆SUB.

> [!ANSWER]
> **48 sq units.**
>
> **Why:** in an isosceles triangle the altitude from the apex **bisects the base**, so ∆SEU and ∆SEB have equal bases and the same height — they are equal in area. Two halves of 24 make **48**.

---

Two identical rectangles have triangles ∆XDC and ∆YDC drawn on side DC, with X and Y at different points on the opposite side. Which has more area?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Neither — they're equal.**
>
> Both have the **same base DC** and the **same height** (the distance between the two opposite sides of the rectangle). Where along the top X or Y sits makes no difference.

---

State the general rule about triangles on the same base.

> [!ANSWER]
> **All triangles on the same base with their apex on a line PARALLEL to that base have equal area.**
>
> **Why:** base and height are both fixed, and the area formula depends on nothing else — not the slant, not the perimeter.

---

Show that a median divides a triangle into two triangles of equal area.

> [!ANSWER]
> A median joins a vertex to the **midpoint** of the opposite side, so the two smaller triangles have **equal bases**.
>
> They also share the **same height** — the perpendicular from that vertex to the base line.
>
> Equal base + equal height ⇒ **equal area** ✓

---

Do the diagonals of a rectangle create four triangles of equal area? They aren't congruent — so how?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes, all four are equal in area.**
>
> Take two adjacent ones, with OD and OB as their bases. The diagonals **bisect each other**, so `OB = OD`. Both triangles have the **same height** — the perpendicular from A to the diagonal DB.
>
> Equal base + equal height ⇒ equal area, and chaining round covers all four.

---

Among all triangles on base BC with apex on a parallel line, which has the maximum area? The minimum?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They all have the SAME area** — base and height are fixed for every one.
>
> So there is no maximum or minimum; the question about area has no interesting answer, which is exactly why the **perimeter** question is the interesting one.

---

Among those same triangles, which has the **minimum perimeter**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The isosceles one — the apex on the perpendicular bisector of BC.**
>
> **Why (the mirror trick):** BC is common to all, so only `AB + AC` matters. **Reflect C across the line l** to get C′; then `AC = AC′`, so the path `B → A → C` is as long as `B → A → C′`.
>
> The shortest route from B to C′ is a **straight line**, so pick A where BC′ meets l. By symmetry that's the perpendicular bisector of BC.

---

Gopal must walk from his house to the river, fill water, then go to his tank. What is the shortest route?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Reflect the water tank across the river line.** Draw the straight line from the house to that reflection. **Where it crosses the river is the fetching point.**
>
> **Why:** the reflection makes the two-leg path the same length as one straight path, and a straight line is the shortest route between two points.

---

<!-- ===== Any polygon ===== -->

How do you find the area of any polygon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Cut it into triangles.**
>
> Every polygon can be split into triangles by drawing diagonals from one vertex, so the triangle formula handles all of them.

---

In quadrilateral ABCD, `AC = 22 cm`, and the perpendiculars BM and DN onto AC are both 3 cm. Find the area.

> [!ANSWER]
> AC splits it into two triangles with the **same base AC**:
>
> `½ × 22 × 3 + ½ × 22 × 3 = ½ × 22 × 6 =` **66 cm²**
>
> **General formula:** `Area = ½ × diagonal × (sum of the two perpendiculars)`.

---

M and N are the midpoints of XY and XZ in ∆XYZ. What fraction of the triangle is ∆XMN?

> [!ANSWER]
> **One quarter.**
>
> **Why:** ∆XMN has **half the base** (`XM = ½XY`) *and* **half the height** (because N is the midpoint of XZ, so it's half as far from line XY).
>
> `½ × ½ =` **¼**

---

<!-- ===== Parallelogram ===== -->

Derive the parallelogram area formula by dissection.

> [!ANSWER]
> Drop `AX ⊥ DC`, cut off ∆AXD and slide it to the far end.
>
> The piece fits exactly, because **∆AXD ≅ ∆BYC by RHS**: `BY = AX` (opposite sides of a rectangle), right angles at X and Y, `BC = AD` (opposite sides of the parallelogram).
>
> The result is a rectangle of height `AX` and width `XY = DC`, so
>
> **`Area of a parallelogram = base × height`** ✓

---

What is **dissection**?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Cutting a figure into pieces and rearranging them into a different figure of equal area.**
>
> It's the classical way to prove area formulas — the pieces never change size, so the total area can't change either.

---

Find the areas: (i) base 7 cm, height 4 cm; (ii) base 5 cm, height 3 cm; (iii) base 4.8 cm, height 5 cm; (iv) base 4.4 cm, height 2 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> - (i) `7 × 4 =` **28 cm²**
> - (ii) `5 × 3 =` **15 cm²**
> - (iii) `4.8 × 5 =` **24 cm²**
> - (iv) `4.4 × 2 =` **8.8 cm²**

---

In parallelogram PQRS, `SR = 12 cm` with height `QM = 6 cm`, and `PS = 7.6 cm`. Find QN, the height onto PS.

> [!ANSWER]
> `Area = 12 × 6 =` **72 cm²**.
>
> Using PS as the base: `7.6 × QN = 72` → **QN = 9.47 cm**.
>
> **One area, two bases, two heights.**

---

A rectangle and a parallelogram both have sides 5 cm and 4 cm. Which has the greater area?

> [!ANSWER]
> **The rectangle — 20 cm².**
>
> **Why:** build both on the same base of 5. The rectangle's height is the full 4 cm (the side is perpendicular). The parallelogram's side of 4 cm **slants**, so its perpendicular height is **less than 4**.
>
> **Push a rectangle over and it loses area while keeping its perimeter.**

---

Several parallelograms are drawn on the same base with their opposite sides on the same parallel line. Compare their areas and perimeters.

> [!ANSWER]
> **All the areas are equal** — same base, same height.
>
> **The perimeters differ.** The upright rectangle has the **smallest** perimeter; the more slanted the parallelogram, the **longer** its slanting sides and the bigger the perimeter.

---

Give a method to make a rectangle with **twice** the area of a given triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Keep the same base and the same height.**
>
> `Rectangle = b × h` and `Triangle = ½bh`, so the rectangle is exactly **double** ✓
>
> (Alternatively, take two copies of the triangle and rotate one 180° — they join into a parallelogram, which converts to a rectangle.)

---

Give a method to make a rectangle of the **same** area as a given triangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Cut along the mid-height line** (the line halfway up, parallel to the base), and **rotate the top piece down** to fill the gaps at the sides.
>
> You get a rectangle of base b and height `h/2`, whose area is `b × h/2 =` **½bh** ✓

---

An isosceles triangle can be turned into a rectangle more simply. How?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Drop the altitude AD from the apex.** It bisects the base, giving two congruent right triangles ∆ADB and ∆ADC.
>
> **Rotate one of them** so its right angle joins the other's — they assemble into a rectangle of sides `b/2` and `h`, area `½bh` ✓

---

Which is bigger: an equilateral triangle of side s, or a square of side s? What about **two** such triangles versus the square?

> [!ANSWER]
> - Triangle: `(√3/4)s² ≈` **0.433 s²**
> - Square: **s²** → **the square is more than twice as big**
> - Two triangles: `0.866 s² <` s² → **the square still wins**
>
> **Why:** the triangle's height is `(√3/2)s ≈ 0.866s`, already shorter than s — and then you halve it.

---

<!-- ===== Rhombus ===== -->

Derive the rhombus area formula from its two triangles.

> [!ANSWER]
> The diagonals meet at O **at right angles**, so each is a height for the other:
>
> ```
> Area(ADB) = ½ × AO × BD
> Area(CDB) = ½ × CO × BD
> Total     = ½ × BD × (AO + CO) = ½ × BD × AC
> ```
>
> **`Area of a rhombus = ½ × product of the diagonals`** ✓

---

Find the area of a rhombus with diagonals 20 cm and 15 cm.

> [!ANSWER]
> `½ × 20 × 15 =` **150 cm²**.

---

Describe the Śulba-Sūtra dissection of a rhombus into a rectangle.

> [!ANSWER]
> A diagonal splits the rhombus into two **isosceles** triangles. Each can be cut along its mid-height and reassembled into a rectangle (as above).
>
> The two rectangles join into one with sides **`AC`** and **`BD/2`**, so the area is `AC × BD/2 = ½ × AC × BD` ✓ — the same formula, by a completely different route.

---

Give a method to turn a rectangle into a rhombus of equal area.

> [!ANSWER]
> Make a rhombus whose diagonals are **`d₁ = 2 × length`** and **`d₂ = width`**.
>
> Then `Area = ½ × 2l × w =` **`l × w`** ✓ — the rectangle's area exactly.

---

<!-- ===== Trapezium ===== -->

Derive the trapezium formula by cutting it into a rectangle and two triangles.

> [!ANSWER]
> Drop perpendiculars from the short parallel side (length a) onto the long one (length b), height h, with the two triangle bases x and y.
>
> ```
> Area = ½hx + ah + ½hy = ½h(x + y + 2a)
> ```
>
> Since `b = x + a + y`, we have **`x + y = b − a`**. Substituting:
>
> `= ½h(b − a + 2a) =` **`½h(a + b)`** ✓

---

Derive the same formula using **two copies** of the trapezium.

> [!ANSWER]
> Take a second copy, **rotate it 180°**, and join it to the first along a slant side.
>
> - **The join is straight**, because the two angles meeting there sum to `x + y = 180°` (co-interior angles between the parallel sides).
> - **The result is a parallelogram**, because the other two angles also sum to 180°, making both pairs of sides parallel.
>
> That parallelogram has base `a + b` and height h, so the trapezium is **half** of it: `½h(a + b)` ✓

---

Why is the "two copies" trick so useful?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because **doubling a shape often turns it into something whose area you already know**.
>
> The same idea gives the triangle formula (two triangles make a parallelogram) and the sum `1 + 2 + … + n` (two staircases make a rectangle).

---

Find the areas: (i) parallel sides 10 ft and 7 ft, height 16 ft; (ii) 24 m and 36 m, height 14 m; (iii) 14 in and 6 in, height 10 in; (iv) 12 ft and 18 ft, height 8 ft.

> [!ANSWER]
> Using `½h(a + b)`:
>
> - (i) `½ × 16 × 17 =` **136 ft²**
> - (ii) `½ × 14 × 60 =` **420 m²**
> - (iii) `½ × 10 × 20 =` **100 in²**
> - (iv) `½ × 8 × 30 =` **120 ft²**

---

Does the trapezium formula still hold for a "leaning" trapezium, where one slant side sticks out past the base?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> **Approach 1:** `Area ABCD = Area ABED + Area(∆BEC)`, where the first piece is a rectangle minus a triangle. The algebra collapses to `½h(a + b)` again.
>
> **Approach 2:** draw `BG ∥ AD`, splitting it into a **parallelogram** (area `ah`) and a **triangle** (area `½h(b − a)`). Adding: `ah + ½hb − ½ha =` **`½h(a + b)`** ✓

---

Construct a trapezium of area exactly 144 cm².

> [!ANSWER]
> Need `½h(a + b) = 144`, i.e. `h(a + b) = 288`.
>
> Pick **h = 12** and `a + b = 24` — say **a = 10, b = 14**.
>
> Check: `½ × 12 × 24 =` **144 cm²** ✓ (Infinitely many other choices work.)

---

A regular hexagon is divided into a trapezium, an equilateral triangle and a rhombus. Find the ratio of their areas.

> [!ANSWER]
> A regular hexagon of side s is made of **6 identical equilateral triangles** of side s. Counting the pieces:
>
> - the **trapezium** is 3 of them
> - the **rhombus** is 2 of them
> - the **triangle** is 1
>
> Ratio **3 : 1 : 2** (trapezium : triangle : rhombus).

---

What measurements do you need to find the area of a regular hexagon?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Just the side length s.**
>
> **Why:** the hexagon splits into **6 equilateral triangles** of side s, so
>
> `Area = 6 × (√3/4)s² = (3√3/2)s²`

<!-- ===== Real-life units ===== -->

Find the area of an A4 sheet (21 cm × 29.7 cm).

> [!ANSWER]
> `21 × 29.7 =` **623.7 cm²**.

---

How many cm² is 1 in²? How many in² is 1 ft²?

> [!ANSWER]
> - `1 in² = 2.54² =` **6.4516 cm²**
> - `1 ft² = 12² =` **144 in²**
>
> **You square the length conversion, not use it directly.**

---

Why do you square the conversion factor for areas?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because area is a **product of two lengths**, and each of them gets multiplied by the factor.
>
> **Lengths scale by k; areas scale by k².** Double every length and the area **quadruples** — the most common mistake in all of area work.

---

Convert 10 in² to cm², and 161.29 cm² to in².

> [!ANSWER]
> - `10 × 6.4516 =` **64.516 cm²**
> - `161.29 ÷ 6.4516 =` **25 in²**

---

Express 5 in and 7.4 in in centimetres; and 5.08 cm and 11.43 cm in inches.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `5 × 2.54 =` **12.7 cm**
> - `7.4 × 2.54 =` **18.796 cm**
> - `5.08 ÷ 2.54 =` **2 in**
> - `11.43 ÷ 2.54 =` **4.5 in**

---

How many m² are in a km²? Why isn't it 1,000?

> [!ANSWER]
> `1000² =` **10,00,000 m²** (one million).
>
> **Not 1,000** — because 1 km² is a square 1,000 m on **each** side, so you multiply 1,000 by 1,000.

---

A plot is 200 ft by 500 ft. How many acres is that? (1 acre = 43,560 ft².)

> [!ANSWER]
> `200 × 500 = 1,00,000 ft²`
>
> `100000 ÷ 43560 ≈` **2.3 acres**.

---

If a square field's side is doubled, what happens to its area?

> [!ANSWER]
> **It becomes 4 times as big**, not 2.
>
> `(2s)² = 4s²` — this is why doubling the sides of a photo quadruples the paper it needs.

---

A square is drawn with three nested regions. If the side is doubled, by what factor does each region's area grow?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Every region grows by a factor of 4.**
>
> **Why:** doubling the side scales **every length in the figure by 2**, so every area — whatever its shape — scales by `2² =` **4**. The regions keep their relative proportions exactly.
