<!-- ===== The distributive law ===== -->

State the distributive property and prove it by counting dots.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`a(b + c) = ab + ac`**
>
> Draw `a` rows of dots, with `b` dots then `c` more in each row.
>
> - Count by whole rows → `a × (b + c)`
> - Count block by block → `ab + ac`
>
> **Same dots counted two ways, so the two answers must be equal.** ✓

---

Why is `(a + b)c = ac + bc` also true, if the law is stated as `a(b + c) = ab + ac`?

> [!ANSWER]
> Use **commutativity** to swap first:
>
> `(a + b)c = c(a + b) = ca + cb = ac + bc` ✓

---

`23 × 27 = 621`. Without multiplying again, find `23 × 28`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `23(27 + 1) = 23×27 + 23 = 621 + 23 =` **644**
>
> **Why:** increasing one factor by 1 adds **one more copy of the other factor** — one extra column of 23 dots.

---

By how much does `ab` increase when **both** a and b go up by 1?

> [!ANSWER]
> **By `a + b + 1`.**
>
> `(a+1)(b+1) = (a+1)b + (a+1) = ab + b + a + 1`
>
> **Picture:** you add a new row (b dots), a new column (a dots), and **one corner dot** where they meet.

---

What happens to `ab` when a goes up by 1 and b goes down by 1? Does the product always increase?

> [!ANSWER]
> `(a+1)(b−1) = ab + b − a − 1`, so the change is **b − a − 1**.
>
> **It increases only when b `>` a + 1.** If `a = 10, b = 3`: `11 × 2 = 22` vs `30` — it **decreased**.
>
> More examples where it drops: `9×2 < 8×3`, `12×4 < 11×5` reversed, `20×1 < 19×2` reversed — whenever the bigger number is the one you increase.

---

Write down Identity 1 and explain it in words.

> [!ANSWER]
> **`(a + m)(b + n) = ab + mb + an + mn`**
>
> **In words: multiply every term of the first bracket by every term of the second, then add.**
>
> **Why:** distribute twice — `(a+m)(b+n) = (a+m)b + (a+m)n = ab + mb + an + mn`.

---

Why don't you need a separate identity for subtraction?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because subtraction is addition of a negative.
>
> `(a + 1)(b − 1) = (a + 1)(b + (−1))` — take m = 1, n = −1 in Identity 1:
> `ab + b + a(−1) + 1(−1) = ab + b − a − 1` ✓
>
> **The integer sign rules do all the work**, so one identity covers all four sign combinations.

---

Use Identity 1 to say how a product changes when one number is decreased by 2 and the other increased by 3.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(a − 2)(b + 3) = ab + 3a − 2b − 6`
>
> So the change is **3a − 2b − 6** — which can be positive or negative depending on a and b.

---

Find three pairs of numbers whose product is **unchanged** when one is increased by 2 and the other decreased by 4.

> [!ANSWER]
> We need `(a + 2)(b − 4) = ab`, i.e. `−4a + 2b − 8 = 0`, i.e. **b = 2a + 4**.
>
> - `a=1, b=6`: `1×6 = 6` and `3×2 = 6` ✓
> - `a=2, b=8`: `2×8 = 16` and `4×4 = 16` ✓
> - `a=3, b=10`: `3×10 = 30` and `5×6 = 30` ✓

---

What is an **identity**, and how is it different from an equation?

> [!ANSWER]
> An **identity** is true for **every** value of its letters — `a(b + 8) = ab + 8a`.
>
> An **equation** is a condition, usually true only for particular values — `2x = 6` holds only when x = 3.

<!-- ===== Expanding ===== -->

Expand `(3 + u)(v − 3)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `3v − 9 + uv − 3u` — each term of the first bracket times each term of the second.

---

Expand `(10a + b)(10c + d)`. What familiar calculation does this describe?

> [!ANSWER]
> **`100ac + 10ad + 10bc + bd`**
>
> This is exactly **long multiplication of two 2-digit numbers**: `10a + b` has tens digit a, units b. The four products are the four partial products you write down.

---

Expand `(3 − x)(x − 6)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `3x − 18 − x² + 6x = −x² + 9x − 18`

---

Expand `(a + ab − 3b²)(4 + b)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `4(a + ab − 3b²) + b(a + ab − 3b²)`
> `= 4a + 4ab − 12b² + ab + ab² − 3b³`
> `=` **`4a + 5ab + ab² − 12b² − 3b³`**
>
> (`4ab` and `ab` are like terms — same letters, same powers — so they combine.)

---

Expand `(4y + 7)(y + 11z − 3)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `4y(y + 11z − 3) + 7(y + 11z − 3)`
> `= 4y² + 44yz − 12y + 7y + 77z − 21`
> `=` **`4y² + 44yz − 5y + 77z − 21`**

---

Which terms in `(3/2)a² − (3/2)ab + (3/10)a` can be combined?

> [!ANSWER]
> **None of them.**
>
> Like terms need **exactly the same letters with the same powers**. Here we have `a²`, `ab` and `a` — three different things.

---

Expand `(a − b)(a² + ab + b²)` and `(a − b)(a³ + a²b + ab² + b³)`. What's the pattern?

> [!ANSWER]
> **`a³ − b³`** and **`a⁴ − b⁴`**.
>
> The pattern continues: `(a − b)(a⁴ + a³b + a²b² + ab³ + b⁴) = a⁵ − b⁵`.
>
> **Why it works:** every middle term appears **twice, once with + and once with −**, so they all cancel — the sum **telescopes**, leaving only `aⁿ` and `−bⁿ`.

<!-- ===== Fast multiplication ===== -->

Explain the one-line rule for multiplying by 11, and use it on `3279 × 11`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `N × 11 = N × 10 + N`, which lines the digits up **one place apart** — so **neighbouring digits get added**.
>
> **Rule: write the last digit, then each pair of neighbours added, then the first digit — carrying as you go.**
>
> `3279 × 11`: 9 · `7+9=16`→6c1 · `2+7+1=10`→0c1 · `3+2+1=6` · 3 → **36069** ✓

---

Use the same idea for `949 × 101`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `949 × 101 = 949 × 100 + 949 = 94900 + 949 =` **95849**
>
> **The pattern:** with 101 the copies are **two places apart**, so digits **two apart** add. For 1001 they are three apart, and so on.

---

Compute `9734 × 99` and `23478 × 999` in one step each.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `9734 × 99 = 9734(100 − 1) = 973400 − 9734 =` **963666**
> - `23478 × 999 = 23478(1000 − 1) = 23478000 − 23478 =` **23454522**
>
> **Why it's fast:** shifting is free; only one subtraction remains.

---

What did Brahmagupta call the distributive law, and what did his verse say?

> [!ANSWER]
> **khaṇḍa-guṇanam** — "multiplication by parts", in the *Brahmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE).
>
> His verse says the multiplier is broken into parts that add up to it, the other number is multiplied by each part, and the results added — exactly `(a + b)c = ac + bc`.
>
> He called the fast-multiplication versions **iṣṭa-guṇana**.

<!-- ===== Identity 1A ===== -->

State and prove `(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²` with a picture.

> [!ANSWER]
> Draw a square of side `a + b`. Cutting at distance a along both edges gives **four** pieces: `a²`, `b²`, and **two** rectangles each `a × b`.
>
> Total area = `a² + 2ab + b²`, and it is also `(a + b)²`. ✓

---

Why is `(a + b)² ≠ a² + b²`?

> [!ANSWER]
> Because the **two `ab` rectangles are missing**.
>
> **Check with numbers:** `(3 + 4)² = 49`, but `3² + 4² = 25`. The gap is `2 × 3 × 4 = 24`.
>
> This is the most common mistake in all of algebra — always draw the square if unsure.

---

Find `65²` and `104²` using Identity 1A.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `65² = (60 + 5)² = 3600 + 2(60×5) + 25 = 3600 + 600 + 25 =` **4225**
> - `104² = (100 + 4)² = 10000 + 800 + 16 =` **10816**

---

Expand `(6x + 5)²` two ways — by the identity and by full distribution.

> [!ANSWER]
> **Identity:** `(6x)² + 2(6x × 5) + 5² = 36x² + 60x + 25`
>
> **Distribution:** `(6x+5)(6x+5) = 36x² + 30x + 30x + 25 = 36x² + 60x + 25`
>
> **Same answer** — the identity is just the distribution, already done for you.

---

Expand `(3j + 2k)²` and `(6x + 5y)²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `(3j + 2k)² = 9j² + 12jk + 4k²`
> - `(6x + 5y)² = 36x² + 60xy + 25y²`
>
> **Watch the middle term:** it's `2 × (first) × (second)`, so the coefficients multiply too.

---

Is `(a + b)²` always greater than `a² + b²`?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.** The difference is `2ab`, which is **negative when a and b have opposite signs**.
>
> `a = 5, b = −2`: `(a+b)² = 9`, but `a² + b² = 29`. So `(a+b)²` is **smaller**.
>
> It's greater exactly when **a and b have the same sign** (and neither is 0).

<!-- ===== Identity 1B ===== -->

Derive `(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²` from a picture, using the 55-inside-60 square.

> [!ANSWER]
> Start with the 60 × 60 square and cut off **two** strips of 60 × 5 to reach a 55 × 55 square.
>
> But the two strips **overlap** in a 5 × 5 corner, which has been removed **twice** — so add it back once:
>
> `55² = 3600 − 300 − 300 + 25 =` **3025** ✓
>
> Generally: `(a − b)² = a² − ab − ab + b² = a² − 2ab + b²`.

---

Get Identity 1B from Identity 1A without any picture.

> [!ANSWER]
> `(a − b)² = (a + (−b))²`
>
> Apply 1A with b replaced by −b:
> `= a² + 2a(−b) + (−b)² = a² − 2ab + b²` ✓
>
> (Note `(−b)² = +b²` — squaring kills the sign.)

---

Compute `99²`, `58²` and `91²` using Identity 1B.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `99² = (100 − 1)² = 10000 − 200 + 1 =` **9801**
> - `58² = (60 − 2)² = 3600 − 240 + 4 =` **3364**
> - `91² = (90 + 1)²`… or better `(100 − 9)² = 10000 − 1800 + 81 =` **8281**

---

Which is greater, `(a − b)²` or `(b − a)²`?

> [!ANSWER]
> **They are always equal.**
>
> `b − a = −(a − b)`, and squaring a number and its negative gives the same result.
>
> Expanding confirms it: both give `a² − 2ab + b²`.

---

Expand `(−2a + 3)²` and `(7y − 4z³)²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `(−2a + 3)² = 4a² − 12a + 9` — note `(−2a)² = +4a²`.
> - `(7y − 4z³)² = 49y² − 56yz³ + 16z⁶` — the middle term is `2 × 7y × 4z³`, and `(z³)² = z⁶`.

<!-- ===== Identity 1C ===== -->

Prove `(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> Expand: `a² − ab + ba − b²`.
>
> Since `ab = ba`, the two middle terms are `−ab + ab = 0` — they **cancel**.
>
> Leaving **`a² − b²`**. ✓

---

Calculate `98 × 102`, `45 × 55` and `397 × 403` using Identity 1C.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `98 × 102 = (100−2)(100+2) = 10000 − 4 =` **9996**
> - `45 × 55 = (50−5)(50+5) = 2500 − 25 =` **2475**
> - `397 × 403 = (400−3)(400+3) = 160000 − 9 =` **159991**
>
> **Spot the pattern:** whenever two numbers are **equally spaced either side of a round number**, this is the shortcut.

---

Explain Sridharacharya's squaring method and use it on `197²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> Rearranging Identity 1C gives **`a² = (a + b)(a − b) + b²`**.
>
> **Why it's true:** `(a+b)(a−b) = a² − b²`; add `b²` back and you have `a²`.
>
> Choose b to make a factor round. `197² = 200 × 194 + 3² = 38800 + 9 =` **38809**

---

Express 100 as the difference of two squares.

> [!ANSWER]
> `a² − b² = (a+b)(a−b) = 100`. Pick `a+b = 50`, `a−b = 2` → `a = 26, b = 24`.
>
> **`26² − 24² = 676 − 576 = 100`** ✓
>
> (Also `a+b=10, a−b=10` → `a=10, b=0`: `10² − 0² = 100`.)

---

Show geometrically why `a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)`.

> [!ANSWER]
> Cut a small `b × b` square out of a big `a × a` square — you get an **L-shape** of area `a² − b²`.
>
> The L is made of a `b × (a − b)` strip and an `a × (a − b)` strip. Slide the small strip round to the end of the big one and they form a single rectangle of sides **`(a + b)` and `(a − b)`**. ✓

---

Find `406²`, `1097²` and `124²` using whichever identity is easiest.

> [!ANSWER]
> - `406² = (400 + 6)² = 160000 + 4800 + 36 =` **164836** (1A)
> - `1097² = (1100 − 3)² = 1210000 − 6600 + 9 =` **1203409** (1B)
> - `124² = (125 − 1)² = 15625 − 250 + 1 =` **15376** (1B)

<!-- ===== Patterns ===== -->

Explain why `2(a² + b²)` is always a sum of two squares.

> [!ANSWER]
> Add Identities 1A and 1B:
>
> `(a+b)² + (a−b)² = (a² + 2ab + b²) + (a² − 2ab + b²) = 2a² + 2b²`
>
> The `2ab` terms **cancel**. So `2(a² + b²) = (a+b)² + (a−b)²` — a sum of two squares. ✓
>
> Check: `2(6² + 5²) = 122 = 11² + 1²` ✓

---

Do these identity-based patterns hold for negative numbers and fractions?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> The proofs used only **distributivity** and the **sign rules**, both of which hold for every integer, fraction and decimal. Nothing in the argument assumed the numbers were counting numbers.

<!-- ===== Mind the mistake ===== -->

Spot the error: `−3p(−5p + 2q) = −3p + 5p − 2q = p − 2q`.

> [!ANSWER]
> The `−3p` was **copied, not multiplied**, and only its sign was applied.
>
> **Correct:** `−3p × −5p = 15p²` and `−3p × 2q = −6pq`, so the answer is **`15p² − 6pq`**.

---

Spot the error: `2(x − 1) + 3(x + 4) = 2x − 1 + 3x + 4 = 5x + 3`.

> [!ANSWER]
> The multipliers **didn't reach the second term** inside each bracket.
>
> **Correct:** `2x − 2 + 3x + 12 =` **`5x + 10`**.

---

Spot the error: `(5m + 6n)² = 25m² + 36n²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> The **middle term is missing** — `(a+b)² ≠ a² + b²`.
>
> **Correct:** `25m² + 2(5m)(6n) + 36n² =` **`25m² + 60mn + 36n²`**.

---

Spot the error: `3a(2b × 3c) = 6ab × 9ac = 54a²bc`.

> [!ANSWER]
> Distributivity works over **addition**, not over multiplication. The `3a` was multiplied in **twice**.
>
> **Correct:** `3a × 6bc =` **`18abc`**.

---

Spot the error: `5w² + 6w = 11w²`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `w²` and `w` are **not like terms** — they have different powers, so they cannot be combined at all.
>
> **Correct:** `5w² + 6w` is already in simplest form.

---

Spot the error: `(a + 2)(b + 4) = ab + 8`.

> [!ANSWER]
> Only the **first and last** terms were multiplied; two of the four products are missing.
>
> **Correct:** `ab + 4a + 2b + 8`.

---

Spot the error: `y + 2(y + 2) = (y + 2)² = y² + 4y + 4`.

> [!ANSWER]
> `y + 2(y + 2)` is **not** `(y + 2)(y + 2)` — the leading `y` is being **added**, not multiplied.
>
> **Correct:** `y + 2y + 4 =` **`3y + 4`**.

Four identical `m × n` rectangles surround a square hole inside a square of side `(m + n)`. Tadang writes the hole's area as `(m + n)² − 4mn`; Yusuf writes `(n − m)²`. Are they both right?

> [!ANSWER]
> **Yes.**
>
> `(m + n)² − 4mn = m² + 2mn + n² − 4mn = m² − 2mn + n² = (n − m)²` ✓
>
> Tadang subtracted; Yusuf measured the hole directly. Algebra confirms they agree.

Three identical `y`-wide rectangles sit inside an `x` by `x` square. One student gets `x² − xy`, another `x(x + 2y) − 3xy`, a third `x(x − y)`. Check they agree, and evaluate for `x = 8, y = 3`.

> - `x(x + 2y) − 3xy = x² + 2xy − 3xy = x² − xy` ✓
> - `x(x − y) = x² − xy` ✓

<!-- ===== Consequences ===== -->

In any 2×2 block of a calendar, why is one diagonal product always 7 less than the other?

> [!ANSWER]
> Label the block `a`, `a+1` / `a+7`, `a+8` (a week is 7 days).
>
> `a(a + 8) = a² + 8a`
> `(a+1)(a+7) = a² + 8a + 7`
>
> The second is **always exactly 7 more** — the `a` cancels completely, so it never depends on which block you pick.

---

Show that the square of an even number is a multiple of 4, and the square of an odd number is 1 more than a multiple of 8.

> [!ANSWER]
> - **Even:** `(2n)² = 4n²` — a multiple of 4 ✓
> - **Odd:** `(2n+1)² = 4n² + 4n + 1 = 4n(n+1) + 1`.
>   `n(n+1)` is the product of two consecutive numbers, hence **even**, so `4n(n+1)` is a multiple of **8**. ✓
>
> Check: `7² = 49 = 8×6 + 1` ✓

---

Square the middle of three consecutive numbers and subtract the product of the outer two. What always happens?

> [!ANSWER]
> **You always get 1.**
>
> `n² − (n−1)(n+1) = n² − (n² − 1) =` **1**
>
> The `(n−1)(n+1)` is Identity 1C in disguise.

---

Without computing, which is larger: `14 × 26` or `16 × 24`? What about `25 × 75` or `26 × 74`?

> [!ANSWER]
> Both pairs have the **same sum**, so write each as `(mid − d)(mid + d) = mid² − d²`.
>
> - `14×26 = 400 − 36 = 364`; `16×24 = 400 − 16 =` **384 is larger**
> - `25×75 = 2500 − 625 = 1875`; `26×74 = 2500 − 576 =` **1924 is larger**
>
> **The rule: for a fixed sum, the closer the two factors, the bigger the product** — because you subtract a smaller square.

---

Is `(2q + 1)(2q − 3)` a multiple of 4?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> Expand: `4q² − 6q + 2q − 3 = 4q² − 4q − 3 = 4(q² − q) − 3`.
>
> The `4(q² − q)` is a multiple of 4, but **3 is subtracted**, so the result is always 3 short — never a multiple of 4. (It's odd × odd, so it's odd!)

---

Is `(k + 1)(k + 2) − (k + 3)` always 2?

> [!ANSWER]
> **No.**
>
> `k² + 3k + 2 − k − 3 = k² + 2k − 1`, which changes with k: `k=1` gives 2, but `k=2` gives **7**.
>
> Testing one value is never enough — **expand and look**.

---

A number leaves remainder 3 on ÷7 and another leaves 5. What remainders do their sum, difference and **product** leave?

> [!ANSWER]
> Write `M = 7p + 3`, `N = 7q + 5`.
>
> - **Sum:** `7(p+q) + 8 = 7(p+q+1) + 1` → **1**
> - **Difference (N − M):** `7(q−p) + 2` → **2**
> - **Product:** `49pq + 35p + 21q + 15 = 7(7pq + 5p + 3q + 2) + 1` → **1**
>
> **The shortcut:** just multiply the remainders (`3 × 5 = 15`) and take that mod 7 → `15 − 14 = 1` ✓

---

Add two numbers, then multiply the result by half of the same sum. Prove the answer is half the square of the sum.

> [!ANSWER]
> Let the numbers be a and b, and `S = a + b`.
>
> The steps give `S × (S/2) = S²/2` — **half the square of the sum**. ✓
>
> Written out: `(a + b) × (a + b)/2 = (a + b)²/2`.

---

Two square plots of area `g²` sit side by side inside a rectangular park, separated and surrounded by a path `w` ft wide. Write an expression for the tiled path area.

> [!ANSWER]
> The whole park is `(2g + 3w)` long by `(g + 2w)` wide — two plots of side g plus three widths of path across, and one plot plus two widths down.
>
> **Path area = total − green =** `(2g + 3w)(g + 2w) − 2g²`
> `= 2g² + 4gw + 3gw + 6w² − 2g²` `=` **`7gw + 6w²`**
>
> The `2g²` cancels, which makes sense — the path is exactly what's left after removing the plots.
