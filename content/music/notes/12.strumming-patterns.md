# 12. Strumming patterns

## The beat grid — eight boxes per bar

In 4/4, split each beat into "number" and "&". That gives **8 slots** per bar:

```
Count:  1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
Hand:   ↓   ↑   ↓   ↑   ↓   ↑   ↓   ↑     ← the hand ALWAYS moves like this
```

A strumming pattern is just a choice of **which slots touch the strings**. The hand never stops — on the
other slots it swings past without hitting.

**Why can every pattern be written on the same grid?** Because the hand is a pendulum (lesson 5): down on
numbers, up on "&". Once that motion is automatic, learning a new pattern is just learning **where to touch**.

## Patterns, easiest first

In these grids, a dot `.` = the hand moves but **misses** the strings.

### 1. Quarter notes (Week 3)

```
1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
↓   .   ↓   .   ↓   .   ↓   .
```

### 2. Eighth notes — every slot

```
1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
↓   ↑   ↓   ↑   ↓   ↑   ↓   ↑
```

### 3. Down, down-up (Week 4)

```
1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
↓   .   ↓   ↑   ↓   .   ↓   ↑
```

### 4. The "everyday" strum — D, DU, UDU

```
1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
↓   .   ↓   ↑   .   ↑   ↓   ↑
```

Say it: "**down**, **down-up**, — **up-down-up**". The **miss on 3** is what makes it bounce.

**Why does missing beat 3 sound good?** Your ear expects a strum on a strong beat. When it doesn't come,
the up-strums around it suddenly stand out — that surprise is called **syncopation**, and it makes the
rhythm feel like it's dancing.

> [!TIP]
> This pattern fits a huge number of pop songs — and a lot of Hindi film songs in **Keherwa** (8 beats,
> lesson 6). One Keherwa cycle = two bars of this pattern.

### 5. 3/4 waltz strum

```
1   &   2   &   3   &
↓   .   ↓   ↑   ↓   ↑
```

Use it for *Happy Birthday* and other songs in 3 (lesson 6).

## Accents — the backbeat

An **accent** `>` means strum **louder**. In rock and pop, the snare drum hits on **beats 2 and 4** — the
**backbeat**. Accent them on guitar too:

```
     >         >
1 &  2 &  3 &  4 &
↓ ↑  ↓ ↑  ↓ ↑  ↓ ↑
```

**Why 2 and 4?** Beat 1 is already strong on its own (lesson 6). Hitting 2 and 4 harder balances it out
and gives the "ONE-*two*-three-*four*" swing that makes people nod their heads.

## Electric-guitar strums

### Palm-muted chugging (power chords)

```
PM - - - - - - - - - - - - - |
1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
↓   ↓   ↓   ↓   ↓   ↓   ↓   ↓
```

All **down-strokes** on eighth notes, with the side of your picking hand resting on the strings right
by the bridge.

**Why all downs?** Down-strokes hit the low strings harder and more evenly — that gives the tight,
heavy "chug". (At fast speeds players switch to down-up to keep up.)

**Why does the palm mute sound chunky?** Your hand stops the strings from ringing long and soaks up the
bright high overtones. What's left is a short, low thump.

> [!TIP]
> Too far onto the strings → a dead thud with no note. Too far back on the bridge → no muting at all.
> Slide the hand until you hear a short note with a clear pitch.

### The "chuck" — a percussive strum

Lay your fretting fingers **lightly** across the strings (no pressing) and strum: you get a dry **chk** like
a snare drum. Written **x** in a pattern:

```
1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
↓   .   x   ↑   ↓   .   x   ↑
```

**Why use it?** A chuck on 2 and 4 *is* the backbeat — your guitar plays the drum part too.

## How to learn a new pattern

1. **Say it** first ("down, down-up, up-down-up") while clapping the beat.
2. **Mute the strings** with your fretting hand and strum the pattern on dead strings — only rhythm, no chords.
3. Add **one** chord. Only when it's steady, add the chord changes.
4. Start at **60 BPM**; speed up 5 at a time (Week 1 rule).

**Why mute first?** It takes chords out of the picture so your whole brain is on the rhythm. Then adding
chords is just one new thing, not two.
