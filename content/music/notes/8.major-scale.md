# 8. The major scale

## Do re mi — Sa Re Ga

Sing "**do re mi fa so la ti do**" (or "**Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni Sa**"). You already know the major
scale — it is the most-used set of notes in the world. Now let's see what it is made of.

Start on **C** and play only the letters — no sharps or flats:

**C D E F G A B C**

That is the **C major scale**. Count the steps between the notes:

| C → D | D → E | E → F | F → G | G → A | A → B | B → C |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **W** | **W** | **H** | **W** | **W** | **W** | **H** |

(W = whole step = 2 frets, H = half step = 1 fret.)

## The rule: W W H W W W H

**Every** major scale uses this **same step pattern**: **whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half**.
Start on any note, follow the pattern, and you get that note's major scale.

**Why does C major have no sharps or flats?** The half steps in the pattern fall at steps 3→4 and
7→8. Starting on C, those land on **E→F** and **B→C** — exactly the two natural half steps
(lesson 2). Everything fits with plain letters. Start anywhere else and they don't line up, so you
need a sharp or flat to fix it.

**Why this pattern sounds "right":** it is mostly whole steps, with two half steps spread apart.
Most of its notes make smooth intervals (lesson 7) with the starting note — the 5th, the 4th, the
major 3rd — so the scale sounds bright and settled.

## Building G major

Start on **G** and follow W W H W W W H:

| Step | W | W | H | W | W | W | H |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Notes | G → A | A → B | B → C | C → D | D → E | E → **F♯** | **F♯** → G |

**G major: G A B C D E F♯ G** — one sharp.

**Why F♯, not F?** Step 6 → 7 must be a **whole** step. E → F is only a half step, so we go one
further, to F♯. Then F♯ → G is the half step the pattern needs at the end. The rule **forces** the sharp.

## More scales

| Scale | Notes | Sharps |
|---|---|---|
| C major | C D E F G A B C | none |
| G major | G A B C D E F♯ G | F♯ |
| D major | D E F♯ G A B C♯ D | F♯, C♯ |
| A major | A B C♯ D E F♯ G♯ A | F♯, C♯, G♯ |
| E major | E F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯ E | F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯ |
| F major | F G A B♭ C D E F | B♭ (a flat) |

> [!TIP]
> Each scale uses **every letter once**. That's why F major has **B♭**, not A♯ — it already has an
> A, so the next note must be some kind of B.

## Numbers and names for each step

| Step | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 (=1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solfège | do | re | mi | fa | so | la | ti | do |
| Sargam | Sa | Re | Ga | Ma | Pa | Dha | Ni | Sa |
| In C major | C | D | E | F | G | A | B | C |

Step 1 is the **home note** (also called the **tonic**). A tune in a major key usually **ends** there.

**Why does the scale want to go home?** Step 7 (ti / Ni) is only a **half step** below home. A half
step is the tensest small gap (lesson 7), so it sounds like it is leaning on the door — it "wants"
to move up to do / Sa. Play C D E F G A B and stop: you'll feel it pulling to C.

## The G major scale on the guitar

```
e|-----------------|
B|-----------------|
G|-----------------|
D|-----------2-4-5-|
A|-----2-3-5-------|
E|-3-5-------------|
   G A B C D E F♯G
```

Fingers: fret 2 → finger 1, fret 3 → finger 2, fret 4 → finger 3, fret 5 → finger 4.

**Moveable!** Slide this exact shape up **2 frets** (start on fret 5 of the low E) and you get **A
major**. Because the step pattern is the same for every major scale, the **shape** is the same too —
only the starting fret changes.

> [!WARNING]
> This shape stays on the E, A and D strings. If you move it onto the G and B strings, remember the
> G–B exception (lesson 3) — the shape shifts by one fret there.

## The C major scale — one octave on the guitar

```
e|-----------------|
B|-----------------|
G|-----------2-4-5-|
D|-----2-3-5-------|
A|-3-5-------------|
E|-----------------|
   C D E F G A B C
```

Play it up, then back down, saying the note names — and then saying "Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni Sa".
