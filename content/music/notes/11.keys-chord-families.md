# 11. Keys & chord families

## Why some chords "belong together"

Play **G → C → D → G**. It sounds like a complete little story. Now play **G → F♯ → B♭ → G** (if you can find
them) — it sounds lost. The first set **belongs together**; the second doesn't.

A song's **key** is its home scale. If a song is "in G", it mostly uses the notes of the **G major scale**,
and its chords are built **only from those notes**. That family of chords is why some chords sound right together.

## Building the chord family of a key

Take the scale and build a triad on **every step**, using **only scale notes** — skip one note each time
(1-3-5, 2-4-6, 3-5-7 …).

**C major scale: C D E F G A B**

| Step | Notes (every other note) | 3rd is… | Chord |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | C E G | C→E = 4 (major) | **C** |
| **ii** | D F A | D→F = 3 (minor) | **Dm** |
| **iii** | E G B | E→G = 3 (minor) | **Em** |
| **IV** | F A C | F→A = 4 (major) | **F** |
| **V** | G B D | G→B = 4 (major) | **G** |
| **vi** | A C E | A→C = 3 (minor) | **Am** |
| vii° | B D F | B→D = 3, B→F = 6 | **B dim** (rarely used) |

**The pattern for every major key: Major – minor – minor – Major – Major – minor – (diminished).**

**Why the mix of major and minor?** We only use scale notes, and the scale's steps aren't all the same
size (W W H W W W H). So the gap from each root to its 3rd sometimes comes out as 4 half steps (major)
and sometimes 3 (minor). The scale pattern **decides** each chord's mood.

> [!NOTE]
> **Roman numerals** name chords by step: **capitals = major** (I, IV, V), **small = minor** (ii, iii, vi).
> A ° means diminished. Musicians say "a I–IV–V song" — it works in any key.

## Chord families for guitar-friendly keys

| Key | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C** | C | Dm | Em | F | G | Am |
| **G** | G | Am | Bm | C | D | Em |
| **D** | D | Em | F♯m | G | A | Bm |
| **A** | A | Bm | C♯m | D | E | F♯m |
| **E** | E | F♯m | G♯m | A | B | C♯m |

> [!TIP]
> **Key of G is the beginner's best friend**: G, Am, C, D, Em — five open chords you know (lesson 10).
> Key of **A** gives A, D, E (all open); key of **C** gives C, Dm, Em, G, Am (F is the hard one).

## The jobs: home, away, and pulling back

| Chord | Job | Feels like |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | home | resting, finished |
| **IV** | moving away | lifting, hopeful |
| **V** | tension | "about to come home" |
| **vi** | the sad cousin of I | gentle, thoughtful |

**Why does V pull back to I?** In C, the V chord is G B D. It contains **B — step 7**, only a half step
below home (lesson 8). That B leans hard towards C. So when G moves to C, the tension is released and the
music feels "home". Try stopping a song on G — it hangs in the air.

## Famous progressions

| Progression | In G | In C | Feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I – IV – V – I** | G C D G | C F G C | complete, classic — folk, old rock |
| **I – V – vi – IV** | G D Em C | C G Am F | the "pop" progression — thousands of songs |
| **vi – IV – I – V** | Em C G D | Am F C G | the same chords, starting sad |
| **I – vi – IV – V** | G Em C D | C Am F G | the "50s" sound |

**Why can so many songs share the same chords?** The chords are only the **floor** — the melody, rhythm and
words on top are what make each song different. Four chords from one family always fit together, so writers
keep coming back to them.

## Relative minor — same notes, sad home

**Am** uses the notes A C E. **C major** uses C D E F G A B. Start the C major scale on A instead:
**A B C D E F G A** — same notes, new home. That's the **A minor scale**.

**Am is the relative minor of C**: same family, same notes, but the music treats **A** as home, so it sounds
darker. Every major key has one — its **vi** chord (G → Em, D → Bm, A → F♯m).

## Finding the key of a song

1. Look at the **first and last chords** — very often the key (home).
2. Check that the other chords are in that key's family.

**Example:** a song uses G, C, D, Em, ends on G → key of **G** (all in the G family).
**Example:** a song uses A, D, E, F♯m → key of **A**.
