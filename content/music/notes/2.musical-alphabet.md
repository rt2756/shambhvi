# 2. The musical alphabet

## Only seven letters

Music names its notes with just **seven letters**:

**A  B  C  D  E  F  G** — then back to **A** again.

After G comes A — but a **higher** A, one octave up (lesson 1). The names go round in a circle,
like the days of the week: after Sunday comes Monday again, but it is a new week.

**Why it works:** an octave sounds like "the same note, higher" (twice the shakes per second),
so it gets the same letter. Seven letters are enough because the pattern repeats every octave.

## The notes in between — sharps and flats

Between most letters there is **one extra note**:

- **Sharp ♯** = one step **up**. C♯ ("C sharp") is just above C.
- **Flat ♭** = one step **down**. D♭ ("D flat") is just below D.
- C♯ and D♭ are **the same note** with two names — like a person who has a nickname at home and a
  full name at school.

All the notes in one octave, going up:

| A | A♯/B♭ | B | C | C♯/D♭ | D | D♯/E♭ | E | F | F♯/G♭ | G | G♯/A♭ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Count them: **12 notes**. Then A comes round again.

## Half steps and whole steps

- **Half step** = from one note to the very next one in that list. On the guitar = **1 fret**.
- **Whole step** = two half steps. On the guitar = **2 frets**.

C → C♯ is a half step. C → D is a whole step.

## B–C and E–F: no note in between

Look again at the list: there is **no sharp between B and C**, and **none between E and F**.
B → C is already a half step. E → F is already a half step.

**Why it works:** the seven letters were given to a 7-note scale hundreds of years before
the extra notes were added. In that scale, the gaps were not all the same size — most were
whole steps, but **B–C and E–F were already half steps**. Later, the sharps and flats were
fitted into the big gaps. The small gaps had no room, so nothing went there.

> [!TIP]
> On a piano you can **see** this: the black keys come in groups of 2 and 3, and there is no
> black key between B–C or E–F. White keys = the 7 letters, black keys = the sharps/flats.

> [!WARNING]
> "E♯" and "B♯" are not new notes — E♯ is just F, and B♯ is just C. Beginners often add a
> note between E and F by mistake. Remember: **B–C and E–F are neighbours.**

## Counting up and down

- Going **up**, name the in-between notes as **sharps**: C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F …
- Going **down**, name them as **flats**: E, E♭, D, D♭, C …

**Example:** what is 3 half steps above G? G → G♯ (1) → A (2) → A♯ (3). Answer: **A♯**.
**Example:** what is 2 half steps above E? E → F (1) → F♯ (2). Answer: **F♯** (not F — E to F is only one step).

## Why 12 notes?

After 12 half steps you are back at the same letter, an octave higher, with **twice** the
shakes per second. So each half step multiplies the shakes by about **1.06**.

**Why 12 and not 10 or 15?** With 12 equal steps, some notes land almost exactly on very
"sweet" mixes. 7 half steps up makes the shakes **× 1.5** (3 shakes for every 2). Notes in a
simple ratio like that line up often, so they blend beautifully. This 7-step jump is the sound
of a rock **power chord** — you will play it in lesson 9.
