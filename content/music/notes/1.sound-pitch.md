# 1. Sound & pitch

## Sound is something shaking

- Pluck the thickest string and **look** at it — it blurs. It is swinging back and forth very fast.
- Touch it lightly — you can **feel** it buzz. Stop it with your hand — the sound stops.

**Sound is a vibration.** The string shakes, it pushes the air, the air pushes on your
eardrum, and your brain hears a sound.

**Why it works:** no shaking → no push on the air → nothing reaches your ear. That is why
holding a string still (called **muting**) silences it at once.

## Pitch — high or low

**Pitch** is how high or low a note sounds. It depends on **how many times the string
shakes in one second**. This number is called the **frequency**, measured in **hertz (Hz)**.
1 Hz = one shake per second.

| String (thickest → thinnest) | Shakes per second |
|---|---|
| Low E | about 82 |
| A | 110 |
| D | about 147 |
| G | 196 |
| B | about 247 |
| High E | about 330 |

More shakes per second → **higher** pitch. Fewer → **lower** pitch.

## Three things change the pitch of a string

| Change | What happens | Where you see it on the guitar |
|---|---|---|
| **Shorter** string | higher | pressing a fret makes the shaking part shorter |
| **Thinner / lighter** string | higher | the 6 strings are different thicknesses |
| **Tighter** string | higher | turning a tuning peg |

**Why it works:** a short, light or tight string springs back to the middle **faster**, so it
makes **more swings per second**. Think of a ruler hanging off a desk: flick it with a long
piece hanging off — slow *wobble-wobble*. Pull it in so only a little hangs off — fast *brrrr*.

> [!TIP]
> **Try it:** pluck the A string open, then press it at the 5th fret and pluck again, then the
> 12th fret. Each time the shaking part is shorter, and the note goes up.

## The octave — half the string, same note

- Press the **12th fret** (the double dot). It sits **exactly halfway** along the string.
- Pluck it, then pluck the open string. They sound like **the same note**, one higher and one lower.

That jump is called an **octave**. Half the length → **twice** the shakes per second.
The open A shakes 110 times a second; the A at the 12th fret shakes **220** times.

**Why it works:** when one note shakes exactly twice as fast as another, their shakes line up
perfectly every second swing — so our ears hear them as "the same note, higher". That is
why music gives them **the same name**.

## Loud is not the same as high

- Pluck **gently**, then **hard**. The note gets **louder**, but **not higher**.
- **Loudness** = how **wide** the string swings. **Pitch** = how **fast** it swings.

**Why it works:** plucking harder pulls the string further, so it swings wider — but it still
springs back at the same speed, so the number of swings per second does not change.

## How an electric guitar makes sound

Unplugged, an electric guitar is **quiet**. Its body is solid wood, so it does not boom like
the hollow box of an acoustic.

1. Under the strings are **pickups** — magnets wrapped in thin wire.
2. The **steel** strings shake over the magnets. This makes a tiny **electric signal** in the wire.
3. The cable carries the signal to the **amplifier (amp)**, which makes it big enough to drive
   a **speaker**. The speaker shakes the air — and you hear it.

**Why it works:** a magnet only notices metal moving near it. That is why electric guitars
need **steel** strings — nylon strings would make no signal at all.

| Knob or switch | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Volume** knob (guitar) | how strong a signal goes to the amp |
| **Tone** knob (guitar) | turn down → softer, warmer sound |
| **Pickup selector** | **neck** pickup = warm and round · **bridge** pickup = bright and sharp |
| **Gain** (amp) | low = clean sound · high = distorted "rock" sound |

> [!WARNING]
> Turn the **amp volume down** before you plug in or unplug the cable. Plugging in makes a
> loud *pop* through the speaker — that can hurt your ears and the speaker.

> [!NOTE]
> Why does a guitar sound different from a piano playing the same note? Each instrument also
> makes quieter, higher "extra" vibrations on top of the main one (called **overtones**). The
> mix of these is the instrument's **tone colour** — the same way you can tell two people
> apart when they say the same word.
