# 13. Minor & pentatonic scales

## The natural minor scale

In lesson 11 you met **Am**, the relative minor of C: the same notes as C major, but with **A** as home.
Play them from A to A:

**A B C D E F G A**

```
e|-----------------|
B|-----------------|
G|---------------2-|
D|---------2-3-5---|
A|-0-2-3-5---------|
E|-----------------|
   A B C D E F G A
```

That is the **A natural minor scale**. Its step pattern is **W H W W H W W**.

**Why a different pattern from the same notes?** The notes haven't changed, but you start in a different
place — so the half steps (B–C and E–F) now fall at steps **2→3** and **5→6** instead of 3→4 and 7→8.
Moving the half steps changes the mood: **A → C** is now only 3 half steps — a **minor 3rd** — so the scale
sounds dark, just like a minor chord (lesson 9).

| Scale | Pattern | Step 1 → 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Major | W W H W W W H | 4 half steps (major 3rd) — bright |
| Natural minor | W H W W H W W | 3 half steps (minor 3rd) — dark |

## The pentatonic scale — five notes, no wrong notes

**Penta** = five. A pentatonic scale keeps **five** notes and drops two.

**A minor pentatonic: A C D E G** — the A natural minor scale **without B and F**.

**Why drop B and F?** They are the two notes that sit only **a half step** from a neighbour (B–C, E–F).
Half steps are the tense, clashing gaps (lesson 7). Take them out and every remaining pair of neighbours
is at least a whole step apart — so **almost any note sounds good** over the song's chords. That's why
guitarists improvise with it: it's very hard to hit a "wrong" note.

## Box 1 — the most famous shape on the guitar

A minor pentatonic at the **5th fret**. Two notes on every string:

```
e|---------------------5-8-|
B|-----------------5-8-----|
G|-------------5-7---------|
D|---------5-7-------------|
A|-----5-7-----------------|
E|-5-8---------------------|
```

Fingers: **finger 1 plays every fret-5 note**. Fret 7 → finger 3, fret 8 → finger 4.

| String | Fret 5 | Fret 7/8 |
|---|---|---|
| Low E | **A** (root) | C (8) |
| A | D | E (7) |
| D | G | **A** (7) |
| G | C | D (7) |
| B | E | G (8) |
| High e | **A** | C (8) |

**Why is it 8 on the B string, not 7?** The **G–B exception** again (lesson 3): B is only 4 half steps above G,
so the shape shifts up one fret when it reaches the B string.

> [!TIP]
> The **roots** (A) are your home base — on the low E (fret 5), the D string (fret 7) and the high e (fret 5).
> When you improvise, ending a phrase on a root sounds "finished".

## It moves!

The root is the fret-5 note on the low E string. Slide the whole box, and the root changes:

| Box starts at fret | 0 | 3 | 5 | 7 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minor pentatonic of | E | G | A | B | D | E |

**E minor pentatonic in open position** — great with distortion:

```
e|---------------------0-3-|
B|-----------------0-3-----|
G|-------------0-2---------|
D|---------0-2-------------|
A|-----0-2-----------------|
E|-0-3---------------------|
```

(E G A B D — fret 3 on the B string, the G–B shift once more.)

## Major pentatonic — the same shape, a different home

**C major pentatonic: C D E G A** — the C major scale without F and B. Look: it's the **same five notes** as
A minor pentatonic, just with **C** as home instead of A.

**Why?** C major and A minor are relatives (lesson 11) — they share all their notes, so their pentatonics
share all their notes too. The box is identical; what changes is **which note you treat as home** (start and
end your phrases on C instead of A).

## Indian link: Raag Bhupali

The major pentatonic is also a raag: **Raag Bhupali** uses **Sa Re Ga Pa Dha** — no Ma, no Ni. That's steps
1, 2, 3, 5, 6 of the major scale: exactly the major pentatonic. (More in lesson 14.)

## Making it sound like lead guitar

| Technique | TAB | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Hammer-on** | `5h7` | pick fret 5, slam finger 3 onto fret 7 |
| **Pull-off** | `7p5` | from fret 7, flick finger 3 off so fret 5 sounds |
| **Bend** | `7b` | push the string up (toward the ceiling) so the note rises |
| **Vibrato** | `~` | wiggle a held note slightly up and down, like a singer's voice |

**Why does a bend raise the note?** Pushing the string sideways **stretches it tighter** — and tighter means
higher (lesson 1). Bend the G string at fret 7 until it matches fret 9: that's a whole-step bend.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A first lick in Box 1 — say "down from the top":
> ```
> e|---------|---------|-5-------|-----------|
> B|---------|-8-5-----|---8-5---|-----------|
> G|-7-5-----|-----7-5-|-------7-|-5---------|
> D|-----7-5-|---------|---------|---7-5-----|
> A|---------|---------|---------|-------7-5-|
> E|---------|---------|---------|-----------|
> ```
> It ends on **D** (A string fret 5). Now end it on **A** (D string fret 7) instead — hear how much more "finished" it sounds.
