# 9. How chords are built

## A chord is notes played together

You already play Em: six strings ringing at once. But look at which **notes** they are:

| String | E | A | D | G | B | e |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Em (0 2 2 0 0 0) | **E** | **B** | **E** | **G** | **B** | **E** |

Six strings — but only **three different notes**: **E, G, B**. The others are the same notes
repeated in other octaves.

A **chord** is **three or more different notes** played together. The simplest kind, with three
notes, is a **triad**.

## The power chord — two notes, the electric favourite

Start even simpler: just a **root** (the note that names the chord) and its **perfect 5th** (7 half steps up,
lesson 7). That's a **power chord**, written with a **5**: E5, G5, A5.

```
   E5         G5         A5         C5
e|-----|    |-----|    |-----|    |-----|
B|-----|    |-----|    |-----|    |-----|
G|-----|    |-----|    |-----|    |-----|
D|-----|    |-----|    |-----|    |-5---|
A|-2---|    |-5---|    |-7---|    |-3---|
E|-0---|    |-3---|    |-5---|    |-----|
```

**Shape:** root on one string, **next string up, 2 frets higher** — the perfect-5th shape from lesson 7.
Finger 1 on the root, finger 3 on the 5th. Slide it anywhere: the fret of the root names the chord.

**Why is it the rock-guitar chord?** Distortion adds many extra overtones to every note. With a
3 : 2 ratio (the 5th), those overtones still line up neatly, so the chord stays **clear and heavy**.
Add more notes, and under distortion their overtones clash into a muddy roar.

> [!TIP]
> A power chord is **neither major nor minor** — it has no 3rd. That's why it fits under almost any
> rock song: it never clashes with the mood.

## Major and minor triads — add the 3rd

The 3rd is the note that decides the **mood**:

| Chord | Built from root | Example on C | Example on A |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Major** | root + **major 3rd** (4) + perfect 5th (7) | C **E** G | A **C♯** E |
| **Minor** | root + **minor 3rd** (3) + perfect 5th (7) | C **E♭** G | A **C** E |

So a major triad stacks **4 + 3** half steps; a minor triad stacks **3 + 4**. The outside notes (root and
5th) are the same — **only the middle note moves, by one half step**.

**Why does major sound bright and minor sound sad?** Every note secretly contains quieter overtones
(lesson 1), and one of the strongest is a **major 3rd** above (two octaves up). A major chord **agrees** with
the root's own overtones, so it sounds bright and settled. A minor 3rd doesn't match them, so it sounds
softer and darker. (Part of it is also habit: we hear minor used for sad scenes in films and songs, so we
learn to feel it as sad.)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> You've already met this: **E** and **Em** differ by one fret on the G string — G♯ (major 3rd) vs G
> (minor 3rd). **A** and **Am** differ on the B string (C♯ vs C). **D** and **Dm** differ on the high e
> (F♯ vs F). In every pair, the one note that changes is **the 3rd**.

## Building any triad — steps 1, 3, 5

A major triad is just steps **1, 3 and 5 of the major scale** (lesson 8):

- C major scale: **C** D **E** F **G** A B → C major chord = **C E G**.
- G major scale: **G** A **B** C **D** E F♯ → G major chord = **G B D**.

For a **minor** triad, lower the 3rd by one half step: C minor = C **E♭** G.

**Or count half steps** — root, +4, +3 for major; root, +3, +4 for minor.

**Example:** D major → D, (+4) F♯, (+3) A = **D F♯ A**.
**Example:** E minor → E, (+3) G, (+4) B = **E G B** ✓ (the notes we found in Em above).

| Major | Notes | Minor | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | C E G | Cm | C E♭ G |
| D | D F♯ A | Dm | D F A |
| E | E G♯ B | Em | E G B |
| F | F A C | Fm | F A♭ C |
| G | G B D | Gm | G B♭ D |
| A | A C♯ E | Am | A C E |

## Chord names

| Written | Say | Means |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | C major | just the letter = major |
| **Cm** | C minor | small **m** = minor |
| **C5** | C five | power chord (root + 5th) |
| **C7** | C seven | major chord + a minor 7th (you'll meet these later) |

> [!WARNING]
> A capital **M** (CM, CMaj7) means **major**; a small **m** means **minor**. Read carefully — Cm and CM are opposites.

## Why six strings but three notes?

Guitar chords **double** notes — the same E, G, B repeated in different octaves. Doubling makes the chord
**fuller and louder**, but doesn't change what chord it is. Only the **set of note names** matters.
